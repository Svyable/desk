# The Anxious Generation

Jonathan Haidt is not a newcomer to arguments about declining institutions and their psychological costs; before turning his attention to smartphones and adolescent mental health, the social psychologist had already built a career studying moral reasoning, political polarization, and the fragility he and coauthor Greg Lukianoff argued American universities were cultivating in their students. In 2024, Haidt published *The Anxious Generation*, a book that took the loosest of this book's earlier chapters' threads — the observation that Generation Z reports the highest rates of loneliness of any generation Cigna's index has measured, and that younger workers report higher workplace loneliness in Gallup's data than their older colleagues — and built it into a single, sweeping causal argument: that a specific, dateable shift, roughly coinciding with the introduction of the smartphone and the rise of algorithmically driven social media in the early 2010s, replaced what Haidt calls a "play-based childhood" with a "phone-based childhood," and that this replacement is the primary driver of a documented, sharp rise in anxiety, depression, and self-harm among American and, Haidt argues, international teenagers, concentrated especially among girls.

The book became one of the most commercially and culturally successful works of popular social science in years, and it became, almost as quickly, one of the most scientifically contested. The most substantial published critique came from Candice Odgers, a developmental psychologist at the University of California, Irvine, in a review published in the journal *Nature* on March 29, 2024. Odgers's core objection was not that Haidt had invented his underlying statistics — the rise in teen anxiety, depression, and self-harm rates he documents is itself real and has been independently confirmed by multiple public-health data sources this book is not disputing. Her objection was to the causal architecture Haidt built on top of those real trends: that the book's "repeated suggestion that digital technologies are rewiring our children's brains and causing an epidemic of mental illness," in her phrasing, is not supported by the actual body of published research on the subject, most of which finds correlational rather than causal relationships between social-media use and adolescent wellbeing, and correlational relationships considerably smaller in magnitude than the book's narrative tone implies.

Haidt's own book draws substantially on earlier work by psychologist Jean Twenge, whose research predates *The Anxious Generation* by nearly a decade and supplies much of the underlying time-use data Haidt built his argument on top of. Twenge, analyzing survey data drawn from more than 11 million individuals across multiple large, ongoing, multigenerational studies, found that teenagers she labeled "iGen" — those born from roughly 1995 onward, the first cohort to reach adolescence already carrying a smartphone — spent measurably less time with friends in person than teenagers had in previous decades, on the order of an hour less per day, with the share of teens who reported getting together with friends nearly every day falling by more than 40 percent between 2000 and 2015, a decline Twenge's data showed accelerating sharply after 2012, the same rough inflection point Haidt's later book treats as the start of the phone-based-childhood era. Twenge's underlying time-use finding is less contested than Haidt's broader causal architecture built on top of it; even researchers skeptical of a strong causal link between smartphones and mental-health decline have generally not disputed that in-person teen socializing measurably fell over this period, only whether smartphones specifically, as opposed to other candidate causes operating on the same generation during the same years, are the primary explanation for why.

The quantitative core of that objection comes from a separate line of research, led by Oxford Internet Institute researchers Amy Orben and Andrew Przybylski, whose meta-analytic work — beginning with a widely cited 2019 paper and extended in subsequent studies — has consistently found effect sizes for the association between social-media use and adolescent wellbeing in the range of roughly 0.04 to 0.15, using standard statistical measures of correlation strength. That range, in a field where an effect size around 0.10 is often considered small by conventional psychological research standards and where potato-chip consumption and wearing glasses have, in some of Orben's more pointed comparisons, shown correlations with adolescent wellbeing of similar or larger magnitude, sits considerably below the effect size implied by some of the more dramatic framings of the phone-based-childhood thesis that circulated in the book's marketing and in subsequent media coverage, though smaller than what Haidt's own more careful within-book statistical claims assert. Przybylski has been publicly blunt in characterizing the disagreement, describing the underlying scientific support for Haidt's strongest causal claims as "not scientific" in interviews conducted around the book's release, a characterization Haidt has disputed directly, including through a public response contesting Odgers's characterization of his evidence and publishing supplemental data intended to answer his critics on their own methodological terms.

This book is not going to attempt to resolve a dispute that active, credentialed researchers on both sides continue to litigate in real time, in peer-reviewed journals and in public forums, without having reached anything resembling consensus. What this book can do, consistent with the approach it has tried to take toward every contested claim in its earlier chapters, is describe accurately what is and is not settled. What appears well established, and is not seriously disputed by either side of the Haidt-Odgers exchange: rates of reported anxiety, depression, and self-harm among American teenagers, particularly girls, rose measurably beginning in the early-to-mid 2010s, a timeline that does overlap with the mass adoption of smartphones and algorithmically curated social-media feeds among that age group. What remains genuinely contested: whether that overlap in timing reflects smartphones and social media as the primary causal driver of the mental-health shift, as Haidt argues at length and with considerable rhetorical force, or whether the timing overlap instead reflects one contributing factor among several plausible candidates operating on the same cohort during the same period — including the same erosion of unpaid, in-person social and civic infrastructure this book has traced across its earlier chapters, economic precarity and rising costs facing young adults entering an increasingly difficult housing and labor market, heightened academic and college-admissions pressure, and broader shifts in how mental-health conditions are diagnosed, discussed, and destigmatized, which some researchers argue could itself partly explain rising reported rates independent of any underlying change in actual psychological distress.

South Korea, already a recurring case study in this book for its godoksa solitary-death statistics and its honjok solo-living culture, offers a data point on the smartphone question that cuts in a genuinely complicated direction rather than cleanly supporting either side of the Haidt-Odgers dispute. South Korea recorded the highest age-standardized suicide rate among OECD member countries as of 2019, at 24.6 per 100,000 against an OECD average of roughly 11, and suicide has been the leading cause of death among Korean adolescents and young adults since 2011, accounting for just over half of all youth deaths in the country by 2020. Korean research has found a real statistical association between high-risk smartphone use and both suicidal ideation and suicide attempts among adolescents, with one study finding adolescents in the highest-risk smartphone-use category roughly two and a half times more likely to report suicidal ideation than typical users, and researchers separately estimating that by 2023 roughly 28 percent of Korean adolescents met criteria for smartphone overdependence. On its own, this pattern might seem to hand a decisive point to Haidt's causal framing. But the same body of Korean research consistently emphasizes a confounding factor specific to South Korea's own social context that complicates any clean smartphone-to-suicide causal story: the country's unusually intense, high-stakes academic competition, which researchers studying Korean adolescent mental health describe as an independent, powerful source of chronic stress that itself predicts both depression and smartphone overdependence, raising the live possibility that heavy smartphone use and suicidal ideation are, in the Korean case, both downstream symptoms of the same underlying academic-pressure system rather than the phone use directly causing the distress. South Korea's case, read carefully rather than reached for as a convenient confirming data point, illustrates exactly the kind of causal tangle chapter sixteen's skeptics would predict: a real, serious, statistically robust association between smartphone use and adolescent distress, occurring inside a specific national context with its own severe, well-documented, entirely separate source of youth mental-health strain, making it genuinely difficult to isolate how much of the association reflects smartphones' own causal contribution versus how much reflects a shared underlying cause operating on both variables at once.

There is a version of this debate directly relevant to this book's broader thesis that neither side of the Haidt-Odgers exchange, in the material this book reviewed, has fully foregrounded: the possibility that smartphones and social media are not competing with a healthy, robust in-person social infrastructure for teenagers' attention, but are instead filling a vacuum that this book's earlier chapters have already documented opening up for entirely separate reasons. A teenager growing up in a neighborhood with thinning third places, in a family with fewer nearby extended relatives, attending a school with declining extracurricular and civic participation rates that mirror the adult associational decline Putnam documented, and living in a country where religious congregation membership has fallen below half the adult population for the first time on record, is encountering a social landscape already thinner than the one earlier generations grew up inside, independent of any phone in their pocket. If that thinner landscape is real and is itself part of what is driving rising adolescent distress, then a smartphone arriving into that vacuum might function less as the primary cause of the harm and more as a symptom of the same underlying erosion this book has traced everywhere else — an imperfect, sometimes actively harmful substitute reaching for a gap that already existed before the device did, in the same structural relationship AI companion apps bear to the erosion of adult social infrastructure examined in chapter eight. This reframing does not resolve the empirical dispute between Haidt and his critics, and this book is not claiming it does; it is offered as a hypothesis this book's broader argument suggests is worth taking seriously, one that would predict a more complicated, harder-to-isolate causal picture than either "phones caused this" or "phones are innocent" — closer, in fact, to the finding Buecker and colleagues' 2021 cross-temporal meta-analysis reached about rising loneliness among young adults more broadly, discussed further in chapter sixteen: a real rise, concentrated unevenly by country and region in ways that a single universal explanatory mechanism, whether smartphones or anything else, struggles to account for cleanly on its own.

Not every response to this chapter's underlying concern has come from parents or legislators; a smaller, teen-led countermovement has emerged from the same generation Haidt's book is ostensibly trying to protect. The Luddite Club, founded at Edward R. Murrow High School in Brooklyn by students Logan Lane and Jameson Butler when they were fourteen and fifteen, brings together teenagers who have voluntarily given up smartphones and social media in favor of what members describe as more analog activities — reading, drawing, simply talking to each other without a screen present — becoming enough of a cultural phenomenon in its own right to inspire a documentary film, *Luddite*, directed by former teacher Amanda Hanna-McLeer. The club's significance for this chapter's argument lies less in its scale, which remains modest, than in what it demonstrates about teenage agency in a debate this chapter has otherwise framed almost entirely around adult intervention — parent pledges, school bans, state legislation. A club of teenagers choosing, on their own initiative rather than under any adult mandate, to opt out of the phone-based social landscape Haidt describes complicates any account of teenage smartphone use as simply an addiction imposed on a powerless population; it suggests at least some meaningful number of teenagers experience the same dissatisfaction with phone-mediated social life that adult researchers and parents have been documenting from the outside, and are capable of organizing their own peer-led alternative to it without waiting for a law or a parental pledge to make the choice for them.

Haidt's book spawned a parallel grassroots parent movement moving considerably faster than the underlying scientific dispute has been resolved, and its growth curve is itself a data point about how urgently the concern has been felt by parents regardless of where the research eventually lands. Smartphone Free Childhood began in early 2024 as a single WhatsApp message between two British mothers looking for others who shared their instinct to delay smartphone access for their children; within a week the group had grown to 10,000 members, and by March 2025 it counted more than 300,000 parents across 29 affiliated groups worldwide. A parallel American initiative, built around the "Wait Until 8th" pledge, asks parents to commit to delaying smartphone access until the end of eighth grade, using a threshold mechanism specifically designed to solve a coordination problem familiar to anyone who has studied collective action: once ten families in a given school grade sign on, the pledge organizers circulate the full list of committed families to each other, letting individual parents credibly commit to a restriction they might otherwise abandon out of fear their child would be the only one in the friend group without a phone. Legislatures have moved alongside the grassroots pressure rather than only ahead of it: California Governor Gavin Newsom announced the state's public schools would go fully phone-free by July 2026, following the Los Angeles Unified School District's own earlier phone-free announcement, while Vermont passed what commentators described as two of the strictest state laws in the country regulating children's technology access. Haidt himself has continued refining his practical prescription for parents into four specific, memorable norms — no smartphone before age fourteen, no social media before age sixteen, phone-free schools, and more unsupervised outdoor play — a formulation simple enough to organize a mass movement around regardless of how the underlying academic dispute with Odgers and Przybylski ultimately resolves.

The CDC's most recent Youth Risk Behavior Survey data, released in 2024 and covering the 2023 survey year, adds a genuinely encouraging wrinkle to a chapter that has otherwise focused heavily on decline, and it deserves reporting in full rather than being lost beneath the more dramatic longer-term trend. After the sharp rise Haidt's book documents — persistent sadness or hopelessness among high school students climbing from roughly 30 percent in 2013 to 42 percent in 2021 — the 2023 data showed a modest reversal, down to roughly 40 percent overall, with the improvement concentrated specifically among teenage girls, the population Haidt's book identifies as bearing the heaviest burden of the phone-based-childhood effect: the share of girls reporting persistent sadness or hopelessness fell from 57 percent in 2021 to 53 percent in 2023, and the share seriously considering suicide fell from 30 percent to 27 percent over the same two years. The improvement was not uniform across every measure the survey tracked, however, and this book is reporting the complication alongside the good news rather than only the good news: reported school bullying rose from 15 to 19 percent over the same period, and the share of students missing school specifically due to safety concerns rose from 9 to 13 percent, suggesting whatever forces are driving the modest mental-health improvement are not simultaneously improving school safety, and may in fact be moving in the opposite direction on that specific dimension. This two-year reversal is far too short a window to resolve the Haidt-Odgers dispute in either direction — a single two-year uptick does not undo a decade-long decline, and this book is not suggesting otherwise — but it is worth reporting as the most current available data point, and as a reminder that the trend this chapter has spent its length examining is not a monotonic, ever-worsening slide with no exceptions, but a genuinely dynamic pattern still actively being written as this book goes to press.

Whatever the scientific dispute's eventual resolution, American state legislatures have not waited for it to conclude before acting, and the resulting policy wave offers this book an unusually fast-moving, still-unfolding natural experiment worth reporting even in its incomplete state. Between 2024 and 2025, following two additional Surgeon General advisories specifically addressing youth mental health and the harms of social media, alongside UNESCO recommendations urging school phone restrictions internationally, a rapid succession of American states adopted policies restricting cell phone use during the school day: eight states acted in 2024, and by the end of 2025 thirty-five states plus the District of Columbia had signed or enacted some form of K-12 restriction, with twenty-two of those specifically enacted within 2025 alone — a pace of policy diffusion across the notoriously slow-moving landscape of American state legislation that itself signals how seriously state lawmakers, across a politically diverse range of states, had come to treat the underlying concern. The early evidence on what these bans actually accomplish, gathered as this book was being researched and therefore necessarily provisional, is more encouraging on one dimension than another in a way that maps suggestively onto this chapter's central unresolved question. Academic outcomes show comparatively clear early gains: a study tracking Florida's 2023 distraction-free learning policy, one of the first statewide bans and now carrying the longest track record, found improved test scores and attendance, with the gains especially pronounced among low-income students, alongside a short-term increase in disciplinary suspensions as students adjusted to the new restriction. Mental-health outcomes specifically, by contrast, show a more mixed picture in the available early research, with some studies suggesting indirect wellbeing benefits tied to improved academic performance and reduced classroom disruption, but without yet the same clarity of direct effect that the academic-performance data shows. That gap — clearer evidence for phone bans improving attention and test scores than for phone bans directly relieving anxiety or depression — is itself informative for the Haidt-Odgers dispute this chapter has tried to present even-handedly: if removing phones from the school day measurably helps academic outcomes but has not yet shown an equally clear, equally direct effect on the anxiety and depression Haidt's book centers, that pattern is at least as consistent with Odgers's more cautious, correlational reading of the underlying mental-health data as it is with Haidt's stronger causal claims, though this book stresses again that the research remains too early and too preliminary, on the mental-health dimension specifically, to treat as a real resolution of the dispute in either direction.

What is not in serious dispute, whatever the eventual resolution of the causal debate, is that this generation — the one Cigna's survey found reporting loneliness at nearly double the rate of the oldest cohort it measured, the one Gallup found reporting the highest workplace loneliness, the one Haidt's critics and defenders alike agree is showing real, measurable increases in reported anxiety and depression — is also the generation inheriting every one of the eroded institutions this book has documented across its earlier chapters, with none of them meaningfully rebuilt in time for that inheritance. Whatever role smartphones specifically played in producing that outcome, the young adults now navigating a thinned social landscape without the reliable third places, workplace stability, congregation membership, or dense courtship infrastructure that shaped earlier generations' expectations of ordinary adult life are, by nearly every metric this book has assembled, having a measurably harder time finding and keeping the kind of regular human contact this book's opening chapters described as a largely unplanned, largely guaranteed feature of twentieth-century life. The next chapter turns to the physical environment underneath all of these institutional trends — the specific, deliberate choices about how American cities and suburbs have been built, block by block, that this book has referenced in passing throughout and now examines directly as a cause in its own right.
