# Research Brief — Built to Be Alone

## Purpose

This book argues that the twentieth century's unpaid social infrastructure — workplaces with stable coworkers, religious congregations, unions and fraternal lodges, dense walkable neighborhoods, extended families living close together — thinned out across the wealthy world for reasons mostly unrelated to any deliberate plan, and that the resulting gap in ordinary human connection is now large enough, and durable enough, to register as a public-health statistic, a line item in government budgets, and a market opportunity. The book tries to hold two things at once: the decline is real and worth taking seriously, and several of the most viral statistics describing it have been simplified past what the underlying research supports. Both halves of that sentence need real citations.

## Evidence hierarchy

Prefer, in order:

1. Original peer-reviewed studies, meta-analyses, and government advisories.
2. Primary government and international-body sources (Census Bureau, CDC/NCHS, Surgeon General, WHO, NHS England, UK Parliament, Japanese Cabinet Office, National Police Agency).
3. Court dockets and regulatory orders for legal/regulatory claims.
4. Company financial filings and investor materials for business claims.
5. High-quality secondary reporting (Reuters, AP, major national papers, established trade press) when the primary document could not be directly accessed.
6. Company or advocacy self-reporting (press releases, vendor case studies) — used only when labeled as such, never presented as independent evidence.

## Claim rules

- Distinguish correlation from mechanism, especially for loneliness/mortality and smartphone/mental-health claims.
- Do not repeat the "loneliness is equivalent to smoking 15 cigarettes a day" comparison without its origin and its own authors' 2023 caveat that the analogy oversimplifies the evidence.
- Separate objective social isolation (network size, contact frequency) from subjective loneliness; treat them as related but distinct constructs, following the research that distinguishes them.
- Label vendor and government self-reported outcome figures (an app maker's own usage numbers, a state agency's own press release about its own pilot) as self-reported, not as independent findings.
- Where a widely repeated statistic could not be traced to a specific primary document in this research pass, either omit it or state the uncertainty in the prose itself rather than presenting it as settled.
- Preserve genuine scholarly disagreement (Fischer vs. Putnam; Odgers/Przybylski/Orben vs. Haidt; Cox vs. the "epidemic" framing) rather than resolving it artificially for narrative convenience.

## Chapter 1 source trail — The Man Nobody Missed

- National Police Agency of Japan figures on solitary deaths, as reported in *The Japan Times*, "Some 77,000 people found dead alone in their homes in 2025," April 17, 2026. https://www.japantimes.co.jp/news/2026/04/17/japan/society/japan-solitary-deaths/
- Corroborating figures reported in *The Nation* (Thailand) and *The Star* (Malaysia), citing the same National Police Agency dataset, April 2026.
- Wikipedia, "Kodokushi" — used only as an orientation/overview source, cross-checked against the news reporting above and the academic source below rather than cited alone for numbers. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kodokushi
- Suzuki, "From dreadful shame to manageable incident: how post-mortem cleaning workers' narratives change the feeling rules about 'lonely deaths' in Japan," *Social Science History*, Cambridge Core, 2021 — used for the *tokushu seisou* (special cleaning) industry's professional norms and framing practices. https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/social-science-history/article/from-dreadful-shame-to-manageable-incident-how-postmortem-cleaning-workers-narratives-change-the-feeling-rules-about-lonely-deaths-in-japan/32D8AB36742C1F33E666C50B19E5A837
- Quartz, "The growing industry of cleaning up after elderly Japanese die alone" — used for descriptive detail on the *tokushu seisou* trade. https://qz.com/380685/photos-cleaning-up-after-japans-lonely-deaths
- Reporting on *jiko bukken* ("stigmatized property") disclosure law and rent discounts (20–50%), and on the emerging Japanese insurance market covering landlord losses from solitary deaths, drawn from Japanese real-estate industry reporting surfaced in search results; treated as descriptive of an active, reported market trend rather than a precise, single-sourced percentage.

### Falsification note

This chapter does not claim Japan's kodokushi rate is representative of, or directly comparable to, rates elsewhere; no comparable national tracking exists in the U.S. or most of the EU, and the chapter says so explicitly. The chapter does not attribute any single named person's death to loneliness as a sole cause; it describes the *tokushu seisou* trade and the *jiko bukken* insurance market as aggregate, industry-level responses to a well-documented pattern, not as claims about any individual case.

## Chapter 2 source trail — Bowling Alone, Twenty-Five Years Later

- Putnam, "Bowling Alone: America's Declining Social Capital," *Journal of Democracy* 6(1), 1995. https://www.journalofdemocracy.org/articles/bowling-alone-americas-declining-social-capital/
- Putnam, *Bowling Alone: The Collapse and Revival of American Community* (Simon & Schuster, 2000).
- Putnam and Garrett, *The Upswing: How America Came Together a Century Ago and How We Can Do It Again* (Simon & Schuster, 2020).
- Fischer, "Bowling Alone: What's the Score?," *Social Networks* 27, 2005, and *Still Connected: Family and Friends in America Since 1970* (Russell Sage Foundation, 2011) — primary counter-evidence to the decline thesis. https://www.russellsage.org/publications/book/still-connected
- Lemann, "Kicking in Groups," *The Atlantic*, April 1996 — early skeptical response.
- Pew Research Center, "The Internet, Cities, and Social Capital," November 2001 — early evidence that internet use correlated with higher, not lower, associational involvement. https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2001/11/20/the-internet-cities-and-social-capital/

### Falsification note

The chapter states Fischer's and Lemann's critiques in their own terms rather than dismissing them, and flags that Pew's early-2000s finding about the internet's positive association with civic involvement is itself dated and contested by this book's later chapters on smartphones and remote work. The chapter treats Putnam's own cyclical revision in *The Upswing* as changing the shape of the claim (decline vs. cycle), not as invalidating the 1995/2000 data.

## Chapter 3 source trail — The Advisory

- U.S. Surgeon General, "Our Epidemic of Loneliness and Isolation," HHS, May 2023. https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/surgeon-general-social-connection-advisory.pdf (mirrored at NCBI Bookshelf: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK595227/)
- Holt-Lunstad, Smith, and Layton, "Social Relationships and Mortality Risk: A Meta-analytic Review," *PLoS Medicine* 7(7), 2010 — origin of the smoking-equivalence comparison, based on aggregate social-relationship effect sizes, not loneliness alone. https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1000316
- Holt-Lunstad, Smith, Baker, Harris, and Stephenson, "Loneliness and Social Isolation as Risk Factors for Mortality: A Meta-Analytic Review," *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 10(2), 2015 — separates loneliness (OR 1.26), social isolation (OR 1.29), and living alone (OR 1.32) as distinct, smaller effect sizes than the aggregate 2010 figure. https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/1745691614568352
- Smith, Holt-Lunstad, and Kawachi, "Benchmarking Social Isolation, Loneliness, and Smoking: Challenges and Opportunities for Public Health," *American Journal of Epidemiology* 192(8), 2023 — the effect size's own co-originator's published caution that the smoking analogy "often oversimplifies the evidence." https://academic.oup.com/aje/article/192/8/1238/7172779
- WHO, launch of the Commission on Social Connection, November 15, 2023, and flagship report "From Loneliness to Social Connection," June 30, 2025. https://www.who.int/news/item/15-11-2023-who-launches-commission-to-foster-social-connection and https://www.who.int/news/item/30-06-2025-social-connection-linked-to-improved-heath-and-reduced-risk-of-early-death

### Falsification note

The chapter distinguishes the 2010 pooled effect size (built from social integration broadly) from the 2015 paper's separated, smaller odds ratios for loneliness specifically, and states plainly that the popular "15 cigarettes a day" phrasing is a simplification the effect size's own authors have since publicly cautioned against. The WHO's "100 deaths per hour" global estimate is presented as a commission-modeled estimate, not a directly measured count.

- Oldenburg, *The Great Good Place* (Paragon House, 1989).
- Rhubart, Sun, Pendergrast, and Monnat, "Sociospatial Disparities in 'Third Place' Availability in the United States," *Socius*, 2022, and related 2025 follow-up tracking third-place categories 2010–2021. https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/23780231221090301

The chapter treats the Fortune reporting as contemporary journalism describing a real, ongoing closure trend rather than as peer-reviewed data, and treats the Rhubart et al. findings — that closures concentrate in rural areas and in Black, Hispanic, and less-educated communities — as evidence that third-place decline is unevenly distributed rather than a uniform national pattern.

## Chapter 6 source trail — The Loneliest Job Is Remote

- Gallup, "State of the Global Workplace: 2024 Report." https://www.gallup.com/workplace/645566/employees-worldwide-feel-lonely.aspx

### Falsification note

Gallup's data is correlational: fully remote workers report loneliness at a higher rate (25%) than hybrid (21%) or fully on-site (16%) workers, but the survey does not establish whether remote work causes loneliness or whether people already prone to loneliness self-select into remote roles. The chapter states this limitation directly rather than implying causation.

## Chapter 7 source trail — Swipe Fatigue

- Match Group SEC filings and earnings releases, FY2024 and subsequent quarters — Tinder payer decline (roughly 7–8% year over year across recent periods) and revenue-per-payer trends. https://www.sec.gov/cgi-bin/browse-edgar?action=getcompany&CIK=0000891103

## Chapter 8 source trail — Her, For Real This Time

- *Garcia v. Character Technologies, Inc.*, No. 6:24-cv-01903, M.D. Fla., filed October 22, 2024; docket via CourtListener. https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/69300919/garcia-v-character-technologies-inc/
- Order denying motion to dismiss on First Amendment grounds, May 21, 2025, discussed in Akin Gump's AI Law and Regulation Tracker. https://www.akingump.com/en/insights/ai-law-and-regulation-tracker/district-court-denies-first-amendment-free-speech-rights-for-ai-chatbot

### Falsification note

This chapter concerns a real minor's death and a live product-liability case; it states only what is documented in the docket and in reputable reporting, does not speculate about the settlement's confidential terms, and does not present Character.AI's chatbot behavior as the sole or legally established cause of Sewell Setzer III's death — that causal question is precisely what the litigation concerned and what the confidential settlement did not adjudicate. Replika's own user and revenue figures, circulating widely in secondary and low-quality aggregator sources, are not treated as verified in this chapter; only the regulator's own order is treated as a hard fact. Xiaoice's 660-million-user figure is explicitly labeled a 2020 spinoff-era snapshot, not a current number.

## Chapter 9 source trail — The Herbivores and the Shut-Ins

- Japanese Cabinet Office survey (fielded November 2022, released 2023): 1.46 million people aged 15–64 (about 2 percent of that population) meeting the government's hikikomori criteria, reported by Nippon.com, the Asia Pacific Foundation of Canada, and the *Inquirer*. https://www.nippon.com/en/japan-data/h00463/survey-findings-suggest-japan-has-more-than-1-million-hikikomori.html
- *The Japan Times*, "Japan needs to rethink how it helps hikikomori," June 16, 2025.
- PMC, "Shifting the paradigm of social withdrawal: a new era of coexisting pathological and non-pathological hikikomori" — clinical/academic framing of hikikomori as a heterogeneous phenomenon rather than a single diagnosis. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10990035/
- PARO robot: development at Japan's AIST under Takanori Shibata; peer-reviewed effectiveness evidence including a systematic review/meta-analysis in *International Journal of Nursing Studies* (2023) and an RCT in *JAMDA* (2024).
- ElliQ / New York State Office for the Aging: partnership announced May 2022; state press release (August 1, 2023) reporting a self-measured "95% reduction in loneliness" among participants. https://aging.ny.gov (release dated August 1, 2023)
- Lovot (GrooveX, Japan): launched 2018–2019; secondary reporting (kr-asia.com) on unit sales and financials, treated as moderate-confidence.

### Falsification note

Hikikomori is presented as a heterogeneous, contested clinical and social category, not a single uniform diagnosis, following the PMC source's own framing; the most commonly cited reason respondents gave for withdrawal was job loss, not a simple "shyness" narrative, and the chapter says so. The ElliQ "95% reduction in loneliness" figure is explicitly labeled a state agency's own self-reported program result, not an independently peer-reviewed finding, and is presented alongside the more rigorously studied PARO evidence rather than given equal evidentiary weight.

## Chapter 10 source trail — The Cost of Being Alone

- Flowers, Houser, Noel-Miller, et al., "Medicare Spends More on Socially Isolated Older Adults," AARP Public Policy Institute, November 2017 — the $6.7 billion/year and $1,608-per-person figures, and the finding that objective isolation, not subjective loneliness, predicted the excess spending. https://www.aarp.org/content/dam/aarp/ppi/2017/10/medicare-spends-more-on-socially-isolated-older-adults.pdf
- "An Updated Systematic Literature Review of the Economic Costs of Loneliness and Social Isolation and the Cost Effectiveness of Interventions," peer-reviewed, 2025, finding a wide range of national cost-of-illness estimates (roughly $2 billion to $25.2 billion per year across differing countries and methods). https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12370830/
- Cigna, "U.S. Loneliness Index," 2018 and 2020 reports, using the UCLA Loneliness Scale via Ipsos surveys. https://legacy.cigna.com/static/www-cigna-com/docs/about-us/newsroom/studies-and-reports/combatting-loneliness/loneliness-survey-2018-full-report.pdf

### Falsification note

The AARP figure specifically measured objective social isolation, not loneliness, as the spending predictor — a distinction the chapter preserves rather than collapsing the two terms. The 2025 systematic review's wide range across studies is presented as evidence the field has not converged on one authoritative dollar figure, not as a single settled number. Cigna's index is labeled as an insurer-sponsored, Ipsos-fielded survey using a specific (and debated) loneliness cutoff, not as an independent epidemiological study.

## Chapter 14 source trail — The Anxious Generation

- Haidt, *The Anxious Generation* (Penguin Press, 2024).
- Odgers, review in *Nature* 628, March 29, 2024 — the principal published scientific critique, arguing the evidence for a causal, technology-driven mental-health crisis is largely correlational. https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-024-01488-5
- Orben and Przybylski, meta-analytic work (2019 and subsequent) finding small effect sizes (r roughly 0.04–0.15) for social-media/wellbeing associations, smaller than the effect sizes implied in some popular treatments of the topic.

### Falsification note

This chapter presents the Haidt/Odgers dispute as a live, unresolved scientific disagreement over correlation versus causation, explicitly declining to declare a winner, because that is an accurate description of where the published record currently stands.

### Falsification note for the expansion pass

Several figures encountered during this expansion were explicitly excluded rather than cited: a widely circulated "several independent teams reanalyzed the ATUS data" claim (noted in Ch. 16 as unverified beyond a single blog post); specific AI-companion market-size totals in the billions of dollars (excluded in Ch. 17 as untraceable to a primary report); and a range of U.S. workplace-loneliness cost totals ($150–300 billion) that this book treats explicitly as less reliable than the UK's peer-reviewed and NEF-modeled equivalent (Ch. 11). Where a figure came from a company's own press release, a state agency's self-reported pilot outcome, or an industry-report aggregator rather than a primary or peer-reviewed source, the relevant chapter says so in the prose itself, not only here.
