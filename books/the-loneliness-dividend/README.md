How Modern Life Engineered Away Community, and Who's Selling It Back.

The working thesis is that the unpaid social infrastructure of the twentieth century — a stable workplace with the same coworkers for years, a weekly religious congregation, a union hall or fraternal lodge, a walkable neighborhood with reliable third places, an extended family living close enough to visit without booking a flight — supplied most people's ordinary human connection almost as an accident of pursuing other, unrelated purposes. That infrastructure has thinned across the wealthy world for a wide range of separate, mostly unplanned reasons: rising commercial rents and insurance costs, remote and gig work, a saturated dating-app market, declining formal religious membership, and a built environment still legally designed, in most of the United States, around the automobile rather than the sidewalk. The resulting gap in ordinary human contact is now large enough to register in mortality statistics, healthcare and employer budgets, government ministries on three continents, and a fast-growing, unevenly evidenced market of businesses and technologies built to sell some version of connection back to the people the first wave left without it.

The book traces that argument from a solitary death in a Japanese apartment building and the insurance industry that has grown up around it, through Robert Putnam's original data on American associational decline and its contested aftermath, the U.S. Surgeon General's 2023 loneliness advisory and the research behind its most quoted (and most oversimplified) statistic, the erosion of the third place, the workplace, the dating market, and religious congregation, into the AI companion apps and therapeutic robots built in response — including a landmark product-liability lawsuit and a federal antitrust investigation — and out into government loneliness ministries in Britain, Japan, and beyond, a full chapter of scholarly pushback against the book's own "epidemic" framing, and a closing look at which interventions the evidence actually supports. Real counter-evidence is preserved throughout rather than argued away, and no invented statistic or quotation appears anywhere in the text.
