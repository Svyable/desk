# Notes, chronology, and source trail

This book uses public, lawfully available material only. No claim of access to non-public classified information is made or implied.

## Working chronology

**1950–1953 — MiG-15 surprise and acquisition.** Soviet-designed MiG-15s challenge UN aircraft in Korea. In April 1953 U.S. Far East Command publicly offers $100,000 for the first intact MiG delivered. On September 21, 1953, North Korean pilot No Kum-Sok lands a MiG-15bis at Kimpo. The aircraft is moved to Okinawa, flight-tested by U.S. pilots, disassembled, shipped to Wright-Patterson, and tested further.

**1966–1968 — MiG-21 access and HAVE DOUGHNUT.** An Iraqi MiG-21 reaches Israel through pilot defection in 1966. The United States later gains access. DIA/Air Force/Navy exploitation at Groom Lake becomes HAVE DOUGHNUT. Related HAVE DRILL and HAVE FERRY efforts exploit MiG-17s.

**1968–1974 — K-129 and Project AZORIAN.** Soviet submarine K-129 is lost in the Pacific. U.S. intelligence locates the wreck. CIA, with Defense Department support and private contractors, develops the Hughes Glomar Explorer under a deep-sea-mining cover story. A 1974 lift recovers part of the submarine; part falls back to the seafloor.

**1975 — AZORIAN exposure.** Reporting connects Hughes, CIA, and the Glomar Explorer. The Ford administration uses the formulation that it can neither confirm nor deny, giving the later “Glomar response” its name. A follow-on recovery is abandoned after cover is lost.

**1977–1988 — CONSTANT PEG.** The 4477th Test and Evaluation Squadron operates MiG-17, MiG-21, and MiG-23 aircraft at Tonopah Test Range. Thousands of U.S. aircrew receive realistic training against Soviet-designed fighters.

**1983 — Grenada captured-materiel exploitation.** Army technical-intelligence analysts deploy during Operation URGENT FURY, establish a captured-materiel collection function, and ship selected Soviet-origin equipment for deeper analysis.

**2007–2012 — AAWSAP period.** DIA sponsors the Advanced Aerospace Weapon System Applications Program. Its scope and relationship to UAP/paranormal research later become central to modern UAP program narratives and AARO’s historical analysis.

**Post-AAWSAP — KONA BLUE proposal.** Supporters seek a Department of Homeland Security prospective special access program. AARO later reports the program was proposed but never approved, funded, or supplied with recovered material.

**2021 — IC controlled-access expansion.** AARO later reports that one intelligence controlled-access program was unnecessarily expanded to include a UAP reverse-engineering mission, never recovered or reverse-engineered technology, and was disestablished.

**July 26, 2023 — House UAP hearing.** David Grusch, Ryan Graves, and David Fravor testify before the House Subcommittee on National Security, the Border, and Foreign Affairs. Grusch publicly alleges a multi-decade crash-retrieval and reverse-engineering effort and attributes non-human biological claims to people with direct knowledge whom he interviewed.

**March 2024 — AARO historical report Volume I.** AARO says it found no empirical evidence that the U.S. government or private companies possessed or reverse-engineered off-world technology. It provides specific resolutions for several alleged programs and claims while stating some matters remained under evaluation.

**April 2024 — ORNL/AARO material analysis.** Oak Ridge National Laboratory’s public synopsis reports that a layered magnesium/zinc/bismuth specimen associated with a 1947 crash claim is terrestrial and does not support the proposed terahertz-waveguide/antigravity interpretation. AARO notes the specimen’s long chain of custody cannot be verified.

**2024 onward — continuing UAP records and oversight.** AARO continues publishing case records, annual reports, information papers, and declassification material; Congress continues oversight. These activities do not, by themselves, establish the origin of unresolved UAP.

## Evidentiary caution

Official histories are authoritative evidence for what the issuing institution publicly acknowledges; they are not complete archives of still-classified activity. AARO’s report is evidence of its investigation and findings, not an omniscient proof of absence. Grusch’s public testimony is verified evidence that he made the allegations under oath and described his claimed sourcing; it is not physical proof of the underlying craft/biologics claims. ORNL’s analysis applies to the specimen tested and does not resolve every historical material allegation.

The book’s synthesis—the expected institutional “surface area” of a decades-long retrieval program—is an inference from documented exploitation practice, not an established intelligence finding.
