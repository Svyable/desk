# Have Doughnut

A MiG-21 is small enough to hide if you own enough desert.

In 1967, the United States gained access to a Soviet-built MiG-21 through Israel. The aircraft had reached Israeli hands after an Iraqi pilot defected the year before. American intelligence had spent years studying the MiG from photographs, combat reports, signals, and whatever technical reporting could be assembled. Now an actual example was available.

The exploitation program was called HAVE DOUGHNUT.

The name is almost offensively ordinary. This is a recurring feature of classified work. Grand claims attract grand nouns in retrospect; working compartments often accumulate language that sounds like somebody had to fill a blank on a form before lunch.

The aircraft went to Groom Lake. It was given U.S. markings and a cover designation. Pilots flew it against American fighters. Engineers inspected its systems and handling. According to a declassified Foreign Technology Division briefing, the test effort accumulated more than a hundred sorties. DIA’s current museum summarizes the work more simply: the MiGs in the HAVE programs were flown, analyzed, disassembled, and studied.

The results corrected fantasies in both directions.

The MiG-21 had real strengths. Its small size made visual acquisition difficult. It could be dangerous in a turning fight. It had performance regimes American pilots needed to respect. It also had limitations that mattered once the aircraft was no longer an adversary-shaped rumor. Testers found handling constraints, visibility issues, engine-response characteristics, and other practical vulnerabilities that could be turned into tactics.

This is what possession buys: not omniscience, but resolution.

A remote intelligence picture often produces a smooth enemy. One knows maximum speed, ceiling, radar estimates, missile envelopes, maybe doctrine. The actual machine is less smooth. It has a switch that is awkward with gloves. A control force becomes unpleasant above a certain speed. The engine takes longer than expected to respond from idle. A mirror blocks a useful angle. A maintenance panel is clever. A hydraulic line is stupidly placed. A pilot can exploit one characteristic and be trapped by another.

Machines are provincial. They live in conditions.

That is why HAVE DOUGHNUT matters beyond aviation history. It demonstrates that reverse engineering is not a mystical transfer of “technology.” It is the production of conditional knowledge. The question is not merely what a system can do, but under which temperatures, speeds, loads, frequencies, maintenance states, operator skill levels, and failure modes it does it.

If the United States possessed a genuinely anomalous craft, the same intellectual discipline would be unavoidable.

Imagine, for the sake of argument, that an intact vehicle lacked conventional wings, intakes, exhaust, fuel volume, or obvious reaction mass. That observation would be astonishing, but it would not constitute understanding. Analysts would need to establish whether the object ever operated in the recovered configuration. Crash damage could remove conventional components. A fragment might be mistaken for a complete subsystem. Apparent absence could reflect unfamiliar packaging. A claimed propulsion effect might arise from test equipment rather than the object. Every spectacular interpretation would create a queue of boring controls.

The work would become slower as the claim became stranger.

This is the opposite of the way stories tend to evolve. In stories, one extraordinary property licenses the next. No visible engine becomes antigravity. An unusual alloy becomes extraterrestrial manufacture. A strange isotopic ratio becomes proof of off-world origin. In an exploitation lab, each step should make the chain more fragile. Unusual isotope ratios require contamination analysis, reference populations, instrument calibration, geological alternatives, industrial feedstock histories, and chain-of-custody confidence. A material can be weird for many terrestrial reasons.

HAVE DOUGHNUT did not need to prove the MiG was Soviet. Its identity was known. That made analysis easier.

A supposed non-human object reverses the burden. Origin is the hardest claim. The physical specimen might establish properties long before it establishes provenance.

Consider a piece of magnesium alloy with layered structure. It may be manufactured. It may be unusual. It may even contain isotope ratios outside common commercial ranges. None of those observations individually identifies a civilization. Before origin, there are mines, enrichment processes, laboratory fractionation, industrial waste streams, experimental metallurgy, hoaxes, mislabeling, contamination, and ordinary variation poorly represented in a comparison database.

AARO’s 2024 report describes one material sample alleged to have come from an off-world craft. The office says its characterization found a terrestrial manufactured alloy without exceptional qualities. That conclusion does not resolve every historic material allegation. It resolves, if the analysis and custody are sound, that sample.

The specificity is the point.

One sample should die one death.

The UAP field has often had trouble letting claims die locally. A failed specimen becomes evidence that the real specimen was switched. A mundane explanation for one event becomes irrelevant because other events remain. Skeptics commit the inverse error: one fraud or misidentification becomes grounds to treat an entire class as solved. Foreign-materiel exploitation offers a healthier model. Every object gets its own file. Every configuration has its own limits. Every conclusion is bounded by what was actually tested.

HAVE DOUGHNUT also teaches us that access to a foreign system can be highly restricted without making the program institutionally inert. The aircraft was secret, but its lessons were eventually translated outward. Pilots who would never be told the full acquisition story could still receive better tactics. Training programs could incorporate weaknesses. Weapons development could respond to measured characteristics. Intelligence gained value by escaping its compartment in sanitized form.

This creates another prediction for alleged exotic exploitation.

If a hidden program had achieved meaningful technical breakthroughs, some effects should leak into capability even if the source remained protected. Not necessarily a flying saucer built by Lockheed in 1962. Technology transfer is rarely that clean. But a novel manufacturing method, sensing principle, energy-storage architecture, field-control technique, material, or computational method might influence adjacent programs through sanitized requirements or discoveries.

The difficulty is attribution. Modern defense technology advances for many reasons, and secrecy makes genealogies incomplete. Stealth, composite materials, phased arrays, fly-by-wire, precision guidance, high-temperature materials, superconducting devices, additive manufacturing, and advanced propulsion all have visible terrestrial research histories. It is methodologically weak to point to any dramatic advance and call it reverse-engineered alien technology because the advance looks impressive in hindsight.

A stronger claim would require an anomalous discontinuity: a capability appearing without a plausible research lineage, tied by records or witnesses to an exploitation compartment, with technical features matching measured properties of recovered material.

That is a very high bar.

It should be.

HAVE DOUGHNUT clears its own bar beautifully because the chain is concrete. We know there was an aircraft. We know where it came from. We know who exploited it institutionally. We know what kinds of tests were conducted. We know the knowledge affected tactics and training. The secrecy protected the advantage; it did not substitute for the evidence.

This distinction will haunt the rest of the book.

A black program is not evidenced by blackness.

If anything, the history of real classified exploitation should make us more demanding. Governments capable of hiding MiGs at Groom Lake are also capable of generating exactly the atmospherics that later become false UFO stories: restricted airspace, strange silhouettes, cover designations, misleading explanations, unusual security, and people who know they saw something but do not know what.

The desert did not merely hide secrets. It manufactured ambiguity around them.

HAVE DOUGHNUT was one of the secrets.
