# Black Programs Have Surface Area

The easiest way to imagine a black program is as a room with no windows.

The harder and more accurate image is a building with too many doors, each door opening onto a different ordinary system.

Security classification can restrict information. It cannot repeal procurement, electricity, gravity, occupational safety, human turnover, geography, or the need to move objects through space. Every classified program negotiates with the unclassified world whether it likes it or not.

Project AZORIAN needed shipyards, contractors, steel, hydraulic systems, a port, a cover corporation, maritime operations, and a public story plausible enough to explain all of those things. CONSTANT PEG needed runways, fuel, maintenance, spare parts, test pilots, airspace, and a way to keep Soviet aircraft flying without Soviet support. Even a much smaller laboratory program needs instruments, technicians, calibration standards, waste disposal, access control, communications, and somebody authorized to pay for them.

I call this surface area.

Surface area is not the number of people who know the deepest secret. It is the number of interfaces the secret creates with systems that do not know the whole thing.

A hypothetical crash-retrieval program could keep origin tightly compartmented while still possessing enormous surface area. A driver moves a sealed object without knowing what is inside. A machinist fabricates a vacuum fixture without knowing what it will hold. A laboratory technician receives a coded sample. A security officer sees names but not results. A contractor accountant sees a cost center but not the technical objective. A congressional staffer sees an access-controlled budget line. A facilities engineer installs unusual shielding.

No single person outside the core compartment can reconstruct the program.

But the program still exists as a pattern of interfaces.

This distinction explains how a secret can remain hidden from the public while becoming vulnerable to partial observation. It also explains why witness testimony in classified-program controversies is so difficult to interpret. A peripheral witness can be perfectly truthful and deeply wrong about the whole.

Suppose an engineer is asked to characterize an alloy under a special access program. He is told only that it is a foreign aerospace sample. The specimen has an unusual layered structure. Security is extreme. Years later he learns that colleagues believe the program involved UAP material. His original memory is real. The later interpretation may be right, wrong, or partly right.

Compartmentation preserves secrets by creating epistemic amputees.

That sounds cruel. It is also deliberate. Need-to-know structures are meant to prevent any one compromise from revealing an entire program. The security architecture therefore produces exactly the kind of fragmented testimony that later investigators find frustrating.

This gives believers a legitimate point. “No single witness knows everything” is not evidence against a compartmented program. In fact, it may be what one should expect.

But the same structure gives skeptics an equally legitimate point. Fragmentation makes sincere misassembly likely. A network of people with adjacent pieces can construct a coherent story that no one personally observed end to end.

The question becomes whether the pieces correlate independently with the same hidden object.

Surface-area analysis helps.

Start with the alleged physical inventory. One small sample produces little surface area. An intact craft produces more. Multiple intact craft produce much more. Bodies produce medical and preservation requirements. A flightworthy vehicle creates range and safety requirements. A power system creates electrical and hazard requirements. A decades-long program creates retirement, records, contractor, and oversight interfaces.

Then ask what kinds of traces should exist without assuming they are public.

Facility modifications.

Specialized handling equipment.

Unusual procurement.

Access rosters.

Shipping records.

Security classification guides.

Environmental or occupational monitoring.

Program-element funding.

Contractor subcontracts.

Test instrumentation.

Incident reports.

Intellectual-property disputes.

Records-management schedules.

Inspector-general complaints.

Congressional notifications.

Again: no one public artifact must exist. Some may be classified. Some may be legally destroyed. Some may be obscured in broader programs. But a serious oversight investigation can search for the categories.

This is more useful than asking witnesses to remember code names.

Code names are fragile. They change. Different compartments can reuse similar language. People mishear them. Nicknames become formal names in retelling. A program can have an unclassified nickname, a classified nickname, a contract title, a budget line, a compartment, and an internal shop label. Investigators who obsess over the perfect magic word risk missing the institutional body.

A machine is harder to rename than a program.

How big was it?

What did it weigh?

Where could it fit?

How was it transported?

What kept it from corroding?

What hazard perimeter did it require?

Which loading dock could accept it?

These questions are embarrassingly physical. That is why they are good.

Secrecy narratives become strongest when they float above geometry. “The material was moved to a secure facility” sounds plausible almost anywhere. “A nine-meter object weighing approximately fourteen tonnes was moved in one piece from this region to a facility with a door no smaller than these dimensions during this week” creates a testable claim. Roads, bridges, aircraft, cranes, witnesses, and facilities enter the picture.

Specificity creates enemies for falsehood.

It also creates risks for truth. A genuine witness may be unable to provide dimensions or dates publicly without revealing classified details. This is why secure investigative channels matter. Congress and authorized offices can receive specificity without publishing it. The public may only be told later that a claim was or was not corroborated.

Trust then shifts to the investigating institution, which is another problem.

AARO says it established secure processes with special-access and controlled-access program offices and was granted access to pertinent sensitive U.S. programs. Some critics dispute whether that access was practically sufficient or whether relevant witnesses trusted the office enough to participate. Those disagreements cannot be settled by repeating either side’s assurance.

An independent review architecture would therefore verify access itself.

Which SAP control offices were queried?

Were contractor-owned facilities included?

Were waived or unacknowledged programs within scope?

Were historical records searched after reorganizations?

Were allegations mapped to physical requirements as well as names?

Did investigators have authority to compel records or rely on voluntary cooperation?

Were refusals documented?

Oversight should leave its own surface area.

That is one of the lessons of the last several years of UAP politics. A government can say it found nothing. A whistleblower can say the search missed the right compartment. Neither statement resolves the dispute unless the search process is independently intelligible.

The public does not need every classified detail.

It needs confidence that the doors were actually opened.
