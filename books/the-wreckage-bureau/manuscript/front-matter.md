# The Wreckage Bureau

## What real reverse engineering teaches us about crash-retrieval claims

**Sven Hardy Benson**

There are two bad ways to approach a secret.

The first is to assume the government could never hide anything large, technologically difficult, or morally consequential. History disposes of that confidence quickly. Intelligence agencies have built front companies, hidden aircraft fleets, recovered foreign weapons, intercepted communications under cover missions, and constructed the Hughes Glomar Explorer to raise part of a Soviet ballistic-missile submarine from the Pacific while the public was told the ship was mining the deep sea.

The second bad way is to treat every demonstrated secret as credit in an account that can be spent on an unrelated claim. Project AZORIAN was real; therefore Roswell. MiGs flew at Groom Lake; therefore alien craft flew there too. Special access programs exist; therefore an unnamed special access program contains bodies. This is not inference. It is atmosphere.

I wrote this book because the documented world of foreign-materiel exploitation gives us a better instrument.

Governments really retrieve technology they did not build. They hide the acquisition. They move the machines through compartments. They disassemble them. They test them. They bring in contractors. They learn how the systems fail. They sanitize the lessons and push those lessons into tactics, countermeasures, engineering, and training.

That history gives modern crash-retrieval allegations a control group.

The comparison does not decide the non-human question in advance. It makes the question harder to ask badly.

Throughout the book I keep four evidence classes separate.

**Documented / verified.** Declassified records, official histories, physical artifacts with established provenance, public program documentation, sworn testimony as evidence that the testimony occurred, and scientific results within the scope actually tested.

**Credible but incomplete.** Reporting or testimony from people plausibly positioned to know, official assessments whose underlying classified evidence cannot be independently inspected, and technical findings whose provenance or replication remains incomplete.

**Disputed allegation.** Claims of hidden programs, recovered craft, biological remains, origin, or technical capability without independent confirmation sufficient to establish the proposition.

**Speculative synthesis.** My own attempt to infer what a real program would require, what traces it should leave, and how investigators could distinguish possession from planning, secrecy from evidence, and anomalous material from anomalous provenance.

The classes attach to propositions, not people. A witness can be right about a secure facility and wrong about what was inside it. A government office can document that it conducted an investigation without its conclusion becoming infallible. A laboratory can establish the composition of a sample without establishing where the sample came from.

The discipline of this book is to keep those sentences from merging.

The central proposition is narrow:

**If a state has operated a multi-decade retrieval and reverse-engineering program involving extraordinary physical objects, the program should exhibit an institutional metabolism broadly comparable to documented foreign-materiel exploitation: acquisition, custody, logistics, technical measurement, security interfaces, records, personnel succession, and downstream consequences. The more objects, years, bodies, contractors, and claimed technical success involved, the larger that expected footprint becomes.**

This is a prediction, not a conclusion.

It could fail. A tiny compartment may have found ways to hold material with much less surface area than historical analogies suggest. Records may remain inaccessible. Technical exploitation may have produced no useful results. A private custodian may have insulated the material from normal systems. There may be firsthand evidence in classified channels that the public simply cannot see.

But the prediction gives us something better than posture.

It tells investigators where to look.

The chapters begin with machines whose histories are settled enough to teach us: the MiG-15 that landed at Kimpo; HAVE DOUGHNUT and the secret MiG-21; CONSTANT PEG’s hidden Soviet-aircraft school; Project AZORIAN; Army captured-materiel exploitation. From there the book turns toward the evidentiary machinery itself—chain of custody, materials characterization, contractor records, black-program surface area, and the difference between a proposed program and an operational one.

Only then do we enter the modern claim directly: David Grusch’s 2023 testimony, AARO’s 2024 historical countercase, KONA BLUE, material analysis, and the possibility that authentic classified programs can generate sincere false provenance when witnesses see only pieces.

I do not assume AARO is correct because it is official.

I do not assume Grusch is correct because he was cleared and testified under oath.

I treat both as evidence-producing institutions with different access, incentives, constraints, and claims.

The unresolved question deserves more than belief on either side.

It deserves an inventory.
