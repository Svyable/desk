# The MiG at Kimpo

On the morning of September 21, 1953, a Soviet-designed MiG-15 came down at Kimpo Air Base outside Seoul and rolled into American possession. The airplane had not been shot down. It had not been recovered from a crater by a night convoy. It arrived under its own power, flown by a twenty-one-year-old North Korean pilot named No Kum-Sok, who asked for asylum after landing.

The American reaction is useful because nobody had to invent a doctrine for what happened next. The machine was foreign, valuable, intact, and frightening enough that the United States had already offered $100,000 for one. Technicians secured it. Pilots examined it. It was moved to Okinawa, flown by American test pilots including Chuck Yeager, disassembled, shipped to Wright-Patterson, rebuilt, and tested again. The Air Force wanted the thing not because it was mysterious but because mystery was expensive. Pilots were dying in Korea while intelligence estimates and combat reports argued about what the MiG could really do.

A captured machine can settle certain arguments brutally well.

Before Kimpo, the MiG-15 existed for American planners as a collection of encounters, photographs, fragments, radar plots, pilot impressions, intelligence estimates, and guesses about Soviet engineering. After Kimpo, the Americans could put fuel in one. They could measure acceleration. They could inspect the gunsight. They could look at the welds. They could ask where the design was elegant and where it was crude. They could find out how long the engine took to spool up, how the controls loaded at speed, what the canopy permitted a pilot to see, and what happened when an American aviator flew the aircraft at the edge of its envelope.

This book begins there because the United States has, beyond dispute, operated programs whose purpose was to acquire foreign machines, hide the fact of acquisition, exploit those machines technically, and distribute the resulting knowledge to people who could use it. There is no need to infer that such organizations might exist. They did. Versions of them still do. The U.S. Army today describes technical intelligence as beginning with the acquisition of foreign equipment or scientific information and continuing through specialized exploitation meant to identify capabilities and vulnerabilities. The 203rd Military Intelligence Battalion trains to collect captured enemy materiel, catalog it, secure it, analyze it, and move the useful pieces into deeper exploitation.

That fact should make the modern argument over alleged UAP crash-retrieval programs more serious, not less.

A lazy skeptic hears “crash retrieval” and rejects the category as cinematic. A lazy believer hears that the government once hid Soviet aircraft at Groom Lake and treats the precedent as a blank check for every later story. Both reactions waste the most valuable evidence. Real foreign-materiel exploitation gives us a control group. It shows what secret possession of an adversary’s technology actually looks like when it happens inside a large state.

It looks like custody.

It looks like transport.

It looks like engineers arguing with pilots. It looks like warehouses, instrumentation, budgets, safety problems, replacement parts, cover stories, accident reports, security compartments, test ranges, contractor interfaces, manuals, dissemination rules, and downstream changes in tactics. It looks like a machine being slowly translated from an enemy object into American knowledge.

The translation leaves marks.

No Kum-Sok’s MiG is almost too clean an example because the acquisition became public immediately. The plane landed in daylight on a runway full of people. But even this uncomplicated case reveals the anatomy of exploitation. The object had to be isolated. It had to be made safe. Its origin and configuration had to be established. Test pilots had to be selected. The airframe had to be transported without destroying what made it valuable. Engineers had to decide what to measure. Results had to be written down in forms that could change training and procurement. Eventually, the machine itself became an artifact in a museum, which is what happens when secrecy ages and the reason for secrecy does not.

Later cases were far stranger.

In the late 1960s, the United States obtained Soviet MiG-21 and MiG-17 fighters through a partner nation and flew them secretly at Groom Lake under programs including HAVE DOUGHNUT, HAVE DRILL, and HAVE FERRY. The Defense Intelligence Agency now says plainly that the aircraft were flown, analyzed, disassembled, and studied. The results changed air-to-air combat doctrine and fed training institutions. A decade later, CONSTANT PEG turned a hidden fleet of Soviet-designed aircraft into a secret school where thousands of American aircrew learned what it felt like to fight the real thing.

Then there was Project AZORIAN, which makes almost every fictional retrieval program look administratively timid. The CIA and Department of Defense spent years building a cover enterprise around a giant ship ostensibly intended to mine manganese nodules from the deep ocean. Its actual purpose was to raise a wrecked Soviet ballistic-missile submarine from more than three miles down. The operation required a purpose-built vessel, a giant capture mechanism, private contractors, Howard Hughes as cover, a fabricated commercial rationale, and an extraordinary amount of engineering conducted under secrecy. The CIA got part of the submarine. The rest fell back to the seafloor.

This is the uncomfortable precedent at the center of the book: states really do things that sound ridiculous before the documents are declassified.

The lesson, however, is not that ridiculous-sounding claims are therefore true. AZORIAN teaches almost the opposite. Extraordinary secret programs are hard to erase because they are physically greedy. They consume steel, money, labor, specialized facilities, access, logistics, industrial capacity, and human attention. They generate interfaces with the ordinary world. Cover stories have to be built because the operation has a surface area.

A secret can be deep and still be wide.

That distinction matters when we reach claims of recovered non-human craft. In July 2023, former intelligence officer David Grusch told a House subcommittee that he had been informed of a multi-decade UAP crash-retrieval and reverse-engineering program and that “biologics” associated with some recoveries were non-human, according to people with direct knowledge whom he had interviewed. He did not present recovered material publicly. He did not claim to have personally touched a craft or body. His testimony was evidence that a cleared official had made serious allegations under oath based substantially on information supplied by others.

In 2024, the Pentagon’s All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office published a sharply different account. AARO said it found no empirical evidence that the United States or private companies possessed off-world technology. It argued that some alleged programs were misidentified real national-security programs, that some stories were circularly repeated within a network of believers, and that KONA BLUE—a proposed Department of Homeland Security special-access effort explicitly designed in part to receive and reverse-engineer hoped-for off-world material—was never approved and never acquired such material. AARO also acknowledged something crucial: authentic sensitive programs existed behind some of the names and descriptions it investigated.

That is precisely why this subject cannot be handled by attitude.

The existence of real black programs does not prove the extraordinary program. The existence of false identifications does not prove that every witness is mistaken. The fact that a proposal contemplated off-world material does not mean the material existed. The fact that Congress heard sworn testimony does not convert secondhand testimony into physical evidence. The fact that AARO denies finding such evidence does not logically prove that no compartment could ever have escaped its search, although it does create a substantial evidentiary burden for anyone alleging exactly that.

What we can do is study how possession behaves.

If a government had recovered a device whose materials, propulsion, energy system, manufacturing methods, or control architecture lay far outside known human technology, it would face the same broad problems that confronted the men around No Kum-Sok’s MiG, multiplied grotesquely. The object would need custody. Safety. Measurement. Expertise. Interfaces. It might require new tools merely to cut it open, power it, image it, or distinguish structure from residue. If it contained unfamiliar isotopic compositions or manufacturing signatures, laboratories would need reference standards. If it produced fields or radiation, somebody would build detectors. If it could fly, a test program would need range space, telemetry, crash response, and a philosophy of acceptable loss. If it could not fly, materials and subsystem teams would still produce a forest of observations.

And if the object were genuinely beyond the state of the art, the exploitation program might fail for decades.

Failure would also leave marks.

Engineers keep notebooks because memory is not engineering. Programs reproduce measurements because a single instrument lies in mundane ways. Managers commission parallel teams because one interpretation can become a religion. Safety officers worry about unknown hazards. Security officers worry about what the safety officers must know. Contractors invoice. Facilities get modified. Samples move. People retire with peculiar fragments of experience that may later become stories, sometimes accurately and sometimes not.

The central question is not whether a government could keep a crashed machine secret. It can.

The question is what kind of secret a machine forces you to keep.

That is a much narrower problem, and therefore a more useful one.

The MiG at Kimpo is where the evidentiary standard begins. It is a real foreign machine. It entered custody by a known path. We can trace its movement. We can identify some of the people who tested it. We can see the institutional consequences. We can visit the aircraft today. There are missing details and once-classified periods, but the object eventually accumulated a documentary shadow proportionate to its physical existence.

When we later examine allegations of stranger wreckage, I will not ask whether the witnesses sound sincere. Sincerity matters, but machines are not obliged to respect it. I will ask what was acquired, by whom, from where, under what authority, with what chain of custody, moved through which physical interfaces, measured by what means, and with what downstream effects.

A retrieval story becomes an exploitation story only when the object begins to resist us.

That resistance is where reality usually enters.
