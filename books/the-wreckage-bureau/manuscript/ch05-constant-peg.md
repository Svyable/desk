# Constant Peg

The first secret was the airplane. The second secret was the school.

By the late 1970s, the United States had moved beyond occasional exploitation flights of Soviet fighters. At Tonopah Test Range in Nevada, the Air Force operated a clandestine unit with actual MiG-17s, MiG-21s, and MiG-23s. The program was CONSTANT PEG. The 4477th Test and Evaluation Squadron became the Red Eagles. American fighter crews came to fight machines they might otherwise encounter only in war.

The National Museum of the United States Air Force now describes the program openly. From 1977 to 1988, the Red Eagles flew thousands of sorties and trained thousands of aircrew. The airfield itself concealed two classified worlds: Soviet-designed aircraft by day and, later, F-117 training activity at night.

There is a special kind of historical irony in that arrangement. One site held foreign technology acquired for exploitation and an indigenous American aircraft whose existence was itself hidden. From outside the fence, a witness could have seen something true and assigned it to the wrong secret.

That is not a theoretical problem in UAP history. It is one of the central mechanisms by which classified aerospace and anomalous-aerospace folklore become entangled.

The popular imagination treats Area 51 as evidence because it is secret. The historical record is more interesting: secrecy there protected specific aircraft, test methods, signatures, tactics, and vulnerabilities. Some of those aircraft were American prototypes. Some were Soviet designs in American hands. Some flights were meant to make a machine understood; others were meant to keep a machine from being understood by adversaries. The same restricted sky served opposite epistemic purposes.

CONSTANT PEG also shows what successful exploitation looks like after the laboratory.

The United States did not acquire MiGs merely so a few engineers could produce classified reports. Pilots needed embodied knowledge. They needed to see how small a MiG-21 looked head-on. They needed to feel how quickly visual geometry changed. They needed to discover which American tactics failed against the real airplane rather than a simulator or a friendly aircraft pretending to be one. The exploitation program therefore grew into training infrastructure.

This transition—from object to doctrine—is one of the strongest signatures a real reverse-engineering effort can leave.

When a machine yields useful knowledge, institutions change around the knowledge. Manuals change. Countermeasures change. procurement priorities change. Exercises change. Safety rules change. Language changes. A new threat system may produce a new checklist or a new piece of test gear long after the original acquisition remains classified.

If alleged non-human technology has been exploited since the 1940s, what changed?

That question is often answered too quickly. Believers point to postwar advances in transistors, fiber optics, lasers, integrated circuits, night vision, advanced materials, or stealth. The implication is that modernity itself is the leakage pattern.

The problem is that these technologies have rich, traceable terrestrial histories involving known laboratories, theoretical work, patents, prototypes, failures, industrial competition, and incremental improvements. One can always insert a hidden cause behind a visible history, but doing so explains nothing unless the hidden cause predicts details the visible history cannot.

A reverse-engineering claim should earn explanatory necessity.

CONSTANT PEG earned it. If you want to explain why American fighter training suddenly included actual Soviet aircraft under extreme secrecy, the acquired MiGs are not decorative. They are the mechanism. Remove them and the program is no longer the same program.

By contrast, if semiconductor history remains coherent from solid-state physics through Bell Labs and industrial development without exotic input, adding a recovered craft is not an explanation. It is fan fiction attached to a functioning causal chain.

This does not mean a hidden program could never seed a technology while allowing a conventional development history to grow around it. Intelligence services routinely sanitize sources. A foreign design insight can be translated into a requirement without telling the contractor where the requirement came from. Signals intelligence can guide a countermeasure program while the engineers see only a threat specification. An exploitation finding can become a target performance number.

If an exotic program were competently run, sanitized transfer would be expected.

But sanitized transfer still generates temporal structure. A requirement appears. Funding shifts. A technical problem gains urgency. A small group starts pursuing an approach it previously ignored. The lineage may be obscured, but the downstream work is not magic.

This suggests a better historical method. Instead of asking which modern technologies look futuristic enough to have alien ancestry, identify documented discontinuities in government requirements and technical programs, then test whether conventional intelligence or scientific developments explain them. Only after those explanations are exhausted does an exotic source become useful.

The method is slower. It produces fewer revelations. That is usually a good sign.

CONSTANT PEG also exposes the personnel problem. The program required pilots, maintainers, range staff, security personnel, logisticians, intelligence officers, and leadership. Soviet aircraft were difficult and dangerous to operate without normal manufacturer support. Parts had to be acquired, fabricated, cannibalized, or improvised. Maintenance itself became exploitation because keeping an enemy system alive teaches you how it is built.

A decades-long program involving recovered anomalous craft would face an even stranger sustainment problem. If the object were damaged, there may be no supply chain. Every fastener could be irreplaceable. Every destructive test could permanently reduce the specimen. If multiple craft existed, analysts could compare them. If they differed, the program would need configuration control. If biological material were involved, preservation protocols would create a separate institutional ecology.

These are the kinds of details that make large claims testable.

How many specimens? In what condition? Are they parts of one design family or multiple? Which components were removed? Were any destroyed during testing? What environmental conditions are required for storage? What contamination controls exist? Which laboratories received aliquots? Were duplicate samples retained? Did anyone attempt replication? What happened when replication failed?

Public crash-retrieval narratives rarely stabilize around such questions because the evidence available publicly is dominated by testimony about program existence rather than technical records. That may be an artifact of classification. It may also indicate that the narrative is being assembled above the level where hardware would constrain it.

CONSTANT PEG had another useful feature: danger.

MiG-23s were not museum pieces while the Red Eagles flew them. They were high-performance aircraft with unfamiliar characteristics, operating in a program that could not rely on normal Soviet technical support. The secret had safety consequences. Safety creates paperwork because dead pilots are not an acceptable form of compartmentation.

Unknown technology would intensify this issue. A recovered energy system might expose personnel to ionizing radiation, toxic substances, high voltage, intense fields, vacuum hazards, biological agents, or something initially unrecognized. Even if security records were perfectly buried, occupational medicine might become an unintended archive.

One should therefore be cautious with stories of strange illnesses around alleged programs, but one should not dismiss the category. A serious investigation would seek patterns tied to specific facilities, tasks, exposure windows, and measurable agents. Anonymous claims of sickness are weak. Cohort-level medical anomalies with occupational linkage are stronger. Contemporaneous dosimetry or environmental records are stronger still.

The advantage of studying real black programs is that they teach us where to look without telling us what we will find.

At Tonopah, the secret eventually became history. The Air Force Museum can now display the MiGs. Pilots have names. The program has dates. The number of sorties can be estimated. The institutional consequences can be discussed in public.

A secret does not become unreal because it was once denied or invisible.

But when it becomes real to history, it acquires edges.
