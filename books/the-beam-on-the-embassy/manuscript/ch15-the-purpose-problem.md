# The Purpose Problem

Even a perfectly measured beam does not announce why it exists.

This is the piece of the Moscow Signal story that biomedical debate tends to erase. The Soviet Union directed radiofrequency energy at the U.S. Embassy. That fact establishes targeting in a physical sense. It does not establish the target function.

Three broad explanations appear repeatedly in the record and later analysis. The signal may have supported technical surveillance, including activation or interrogation of implanted devices. It may have interfered with American electronics or intelligence collection. Or it may have been intended to affect the people in the building. These categories are not mutually exclusive, and none can be inferred from frequency alone.

The surveillance explanation has unusually strong historical context. Soviet services had already used externally energized passive listening technology against the United States. The Great Seal device demonstrated that radiofrequency illumination could be part of an espionage architecture. The embassy was saturated with technical-security problems. U.S. memoranda in the 1970s explicitly considered the possibility that at least some Moscow signals were associated with audio devices.

That does not prove that every transmission served this purpose. It gives the hypothesis a concrete mechanism and precedent.

The biological explanation has a different evidentiary profile. Soviet literature and restrictive standards showed interest in low-level RF effects. U.S. officials took that interest seriously enough to establish PANDORA. But the American experiments did not validate a reliable behavioral impairment at the actual Moscow exposure. No declassified Soviet operational document in the source base for this book establishes that the embassy transmission was deployed as a health or mind-control weapon.

This means purpose remains underdetermined.

Under ordinary historical standards, we would search the operator's archive. Orders, technical manuals, design requirements, unit histories, intelligence directives, procurement records, or testimony from personnel involved could identify the mission. For many Soviet intelligence operations, those records remain inaccessible, incomplete, or never produced publicly. The American archive therefore tells us mostly what the target thought the signal might mean.

That is a dangerous source asymmetry.

Threat assessments are designed to consider worst cases. They are not neutral descriptions of adversary intent. If an American memorandum says officials were concerned that the beam might alter behavior, the document proves the concern. It does not prove the Soviet purpose. If a biomedical program is authorized, it proves the hypothesis reached an institutional threshold. It does not prove the hypothesis was correct.

This distinction should be obvious, yet it collapses constantly in classified-adjacent history.

One reason is that the target's reaction becomes more richly documented than the attacker's plan. We can follow PANDORA meetings, blood contracts, diplomatic protests, Senate hearings, and health studies in detail. The Soviet side is quieter. The documentary density itself makes the American hypothesis feel substantial.

Absence of Soviet records is not evidence of innocence. It is simply a limit.

There are ways to reason inside that limit. We can ask which hypothesis best explains the observed technical characteristics. A signal designed to energize a passive device might require certain geometry and power at the target. A jammer should correlate with frequencies or systems being disrupted. A biological weapon should be evaluated against dose-response data and expected effects. If the transmission changed when diplomatic pressure increased or when embassy equipment changed, those correlations could help.

But historical data are not complete enough to make every test decisive.

The purpose problem is therefore a Bayesian one whether we use that language or not. We begin with priors based on known Soviet technical intelligence practice, biomedical research, and strategic incentives. We update on signal measurements, source locations, modulation, timing, experimental results, and diplomatic behavior. The posterior may favor surveillance over biology without reaching certainty.

What we should not do is set the prior to one because the most dramatic hypothesis is possible.

The same lesson applies to contemporary directed-energy allegations. Finding that a foreign service researched a technology raises the prior that it could attempt to use it. It does not identify the cause of a specific incident. Finding a suspicious RF signal near a facility raises the prior further. Demonstrating that the signal had the correct waveform and delivered a relevant dose raises it again. Finding a biomarker consistent with the proposed mechanism helps. Connecting the emitter to an actor and timing it to symptoms helps more.

This is how evidence should accumulate: link by link.

The Moscow Signal is unusual because one early link is so strong. There was a real external source aimed at a real embassy. That makes later weak links feel stronger by association.

But causation is multiplicative in an unforgiving sense. If the beam exists but the dose is wrong, a specific injury hypothesis fails. If the dose is plausible but the biological endpoint is not reproducible, the chain weakens. If the effect is real but the operator's intent is unknown, we cannot call it a weapon rather than an incidental exposure. If intent is hostile but the mechanism is ineffective, we may have an attempted weapon that did not work.

Those are different historical outcomes.

One of the strangest possibilities is that the Soviet purpose may have been mundane within the world of espionage and extraordinary only from the perspective of the people being exposed. An interrogation signal for a hidden device could be technically routine for an intelligence service while creating a genuine involuntary microwave exposure that American medicine had never planned to study. The health question would then be real even if health was irrelevant to Soviet intent.

This possibility dissolves the false choice between “spy beam” and “health beam.”

A system can be both an espionage instrument and an environmental exposure.

The government's duty to employees depends on the latter. The intelligence interpretation depends on the former. PANDORA tried to infer one from the other because both were present in the same field.

The archive never gives us the clean confession that would end the argument.

That may be why the episode remains useful. It forces us to practice history without the luxury of final intent.
