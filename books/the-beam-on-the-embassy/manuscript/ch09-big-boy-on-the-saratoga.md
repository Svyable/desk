# Big Boy on the Saratoga

The aircraft carrier USS *Saratoga* entered PANDORA because it offered something Moscow could not: people who had been exposed to strong radiofrequency equipment as part of ordinary work.

Radar ships are electromagnetic environments by design. Sailors serve near transmitters, antennas, cables, rotating arrays, maintenance spaces, and equipment that can produce fields far above the background encountered by civilians. If chronic microwave exposure produced a recognizable medical or psychological pattern, a naval population might reveal it.

ARPA called the effort BIG BOY.

A January 1969 memorandum by Herbert Pollack described plans to review medical records and compare experienced crew members with new personnel who lacked the same exposure history. Observers would establish baseline readings. Psychological, genetic, and physical measures were contemplated. The logic was attractive: rather than create a risky human exposure from scratch, use an occupational setting in which exposure already existed.

Natural experiments are seductive for exactly this reason. They appear to solve the ethics and scale problems at once. The exposure is already happening. The people are available. Records exist. A control group may be nearby.

Then the confounding begins.

A veteran sailor differs from a new recruit in more ways than cumulative microwave exposure. Age, rank, job specialty, sleep history, deployment experience, stress, hearing damage, chemical exposures, smoking, fitness, and selection effects can all matter. Personnel assigned to radar-related duties may differ systematically from the rest of the crew. Exposure itself is difficult to reconstruct: a job title is not a dosimeter, and a ship's electromagnetic environment changes with equipment, watch station, maintenance status, antenna operation, distance, shielding, and time.

BIG BOY was therefore not a clean substitute for a controlled experiment. It was a second imperfect window.

The early results reported back to PANDORA were negative. Meeting minutes from April 1969 recorded no significant differences in psychological tests between apparently exposed and control groups and no significant genetic or physical differences. Those findings mattered because they weakened the expectation that chronic occupational RF exposure would produce a large, easily detected syndrome across broad endpoints.

Again, the result was narrower than either camp wanted.

Negative psychological and physical comparisons aboard one ship do not establish that all radiofrequency exposures are harmless. Radar workers may experience very different frequencies and peak powers from embassy personnel. Exposure reconstruction may be crude. A subtle effect can be missed in a small study. Healthy-worker selection can hide morbidity. The study may be underpowered for rare cancers or long-latency disease.

But a negative study still counts. If a hypothesis predicts broad chronic impairment, an exposed occupational group that fails to show it should reduce confidence in the broadest version of the hypothesis.

This is one of the places where PANDORA's history becomes useful far beyond microwaves. Anomalous research often handles negative evidence badly. Positive results are treated as specific: this experiment saw the effect. Negative results are treated as nonspecific: perhaps the frequency was wrong, the timing was wrong, the subject population was wrong, the effect was too subtle, the control group was contaminated.

Sometimes those objections are scientifically justified. If they are available after every null finding, the hypothesis can never lose.

A threat program needs a rule for losing.

BIG BOY should not have been expected to settle the Moscow problem. It could, however, constrain it. If sailors with occupational RF exposure did not show a large pattern of psychological or genetic abnormality, then any Moscow-specific effect would need to depend on something more particular than “microwaves are bad”: waveform, frequency, chronicity, tissue coupling, intensity regime, or some interaction not represented aboard ship.

That move makes the hypothesis more specific. It also makes it harder to test.

There is a cost to every rescue.

The *Saratoga* study also shows why epidemiology would eventually dominate the embassy question. Animal experiments could simulate exposure, but translation to people remained uncertain. Controlled human experiments raised ethical barriers. Occupational cohorts offered real human exposure but poor control. The actual Moscow employees had the most relevant exposure, yet they were a small and complicated population whose dose history was spatially and temporally uneven.

No study design could provide everything.

The problem then becomes triangulation. If animal studies at realistic intensity are negative, occupational cohorts show no broad syndrome, embassy epidemiology shows no convincing mortality signal, and physical models place exposure below established injury thresholds, the combined case against a strong harmful effect becomes stronger even if no single study is decisive. Conversely, if several independent methods point to the same endpoint with a coherent dose-response relationship, confidence rises.

PANDORA never obtained that convergence.

This is why the program's breadth should not be mistaken for depth. It touched primates, blood, sailors, medical literature, electrophysiology, pathology, and planned human exposure. A long list of research activities can sound like corroboration. In fact, they were attempts to find an endpoint robust enough to survive movement between systems.

The endpoint did not emerge.

By 1969, the PANDORA advisory process was already confronting the problem directly. The BIG BOY results were negative. The primate data were inconclusive. Yet some officials still wanted human data. The reasoning was understandable: perhaps humans were the only relevant model.

That is also the moment at which the program approached its ethical edge.

When animal evidence is weak, moving to human exposure can be framed as the necessary next experiment. It can also be the point at which scientific frustration begins to outrun justification.

The meeting minutes show PANDORA standing exactly there.
