# The Number That Changed the Experiment

There is a particular kind of scientific mistake that becomes more dangerous when the experiment is secret: the wrong number survives longer because fewer people can attack it.

Project PANDORA began with a problem of that kind. Early American estimates of the microwave power density at the Moscow embassy were too high. The error mattered because the entire biological question depended on dose. A rhesus monkey exposed to one field strength is not evidence about a diplomat exposed to another merely because both exposures are called microwaves.

By 1967, new measurements had forced a substantial revision. CIA scientist Joseph Johnston summarized the change in a memorandum on Project BIZARRE. There was, he wrote, reasonable certainty that the signal was not above roughly fifty microwatts per square centimeter and was closer to two microwatts per square centimeter at its average high level. Richard Cesaro's ARPA update likewise acknowledged that improved on-site measurements had driven the estimate downward.

The National Security Archive's reconstruction makes the consequence plain. An early experiment that appeared to show behavioral degradation in a monkey had been conducted under assumptions based on a much stronger field than the one subsequently believed to exist at the embassy. When the experiment was brought closer to the corrected Moscow level, the reported behavioral degradation did not persist.

This is not a minor methodological wrinkle. It is the story.

Dosimetry is the bridge between an observed field and a biological claim. Without it, the phrase low-level microwave effect can hide orders of magnitude. Frequency matters because tissue absorption changes with it. Modulation matters because a pulsed field can produce different temporal peaks from a continuous wave with the same average power. Geometry matters because an external field is not identical to energy absorbed in a particular organ. Duration matters because intermittent occupational exposure and continuous laboratory exposure are different regimes. Body size matters. Orientation matters. Reflections inside a building matter.

The easiest way to make an invisible exposure frightening is to talk about the source before talking about the dose.

The easiest way to make it harmless is to compare only average power against a thermal standard and ignore waveform, local coupling, or nonthermal hypotheses.

Good analysis has to survive between those temptations.

The 1967 revision is especially useful because it lets us see a hypothesis change under measurement pressure. Johnston did not simply declare the earlier positive finding invalid and walk away. He noted the behavioral effect seen in one animal at the earlier regime and considered the possibility that modulation, rather than carrier energy alone, might explain it. He wanted independent confirmation. At the same time, he concluded that at the power levels then reported for TUMS, exposed people were not at risk of injury.

That combination is intellectually cleaner than many later accounts. A result can be interesting without being operationally relevant. A mechanism can be worth testing without being established. A safety judgment can be provisional without becoming meaningless.

Cesaro's September 1967 report was similar. Corrected measurements had been made at the embassy. At the lower level, the primate experiment showed no overt performance degradation. Yet the program continued to explore whether subhuman primate data could be related to people and whether direct human testing might eventually be necessary.

Why continue after the dramatic result weakens?

Because one negative condition does not exhaust the hypothesis space. Perhaps a different modulation mattered. Perhaps the relevant endpoint was neurological rather than gross task performance. Perhaps chronic exposure mattered more than acute exposure. Perhaps Soviet researchers had found effects American protocols were not capturing. Perhaps the signal varied over time. Perhaps the technical characterization was still incomplete.

All of those are scientifically possible. They are also how a research program can become difficult to kill.

When an adversarial capability is the object of study, the hypothesis is not merely 'does this exposure produce this effect?' It becomes 'is there any plausible configuration the adversary might be using that we have failed to test?' That is an open-ended question. Every null result can lead to a new parameter choice.

The only defense is an explicit evidentiary boundary. What result would make us reduce confidence in the feared explanation? The corrected-dose BIZARRE work should have done exactly that. It did not prove that microwaves could never produce a nonthermal biological effect. It did not prove that no Soviet program had biomedical ambitions. It did not even prove that no embassy worker could experience any effect under any circumstances. What it did was narrower and more useful: it weakened the claim that the measured Moscow exposure was producing an obvious behavioral decrement of the kind the early primate result had seemed to suggest.

Later summaries sometimes flatten this into either triumph or debunking. Both lose the measurement lesson.

Imagine a modern sensor report. A directional radiofrequency burst is detected near an incident. The first engineering estimate places the field in a regime where a known biological effect is plausible. A laboratory team reproduces something at that regime. Later calibration shows the actual exposure was ten or a hundred times lower. At that point the burden shifts. The lab effect does not vanish as physics, but it stops explaining the incident unless a coupling mechanism can restore the missing dose.

This sounds elementary. In contested fields it is routinely ignored.

One reason is that biological research often cites exposure in the language of the transmitter while health effects occur in the receiver. Power density outside the body is not the same as specific absorption rate inside tissue. A measured field at one point in a room is not a personal dosimeter. A peak is not an average. A nominal frequency does not describe modulation. A building can create reflections and standing-wave patterns. If an epidemiological study later classifies exposure simply by whether someone served in Moscow, it inherits all of those simplifications at once.

The embassy case therefore contains two different exposure problems. The first is reconstructing what fields existed in the building. The second is reconstructing what energy individual people absorbed.

PANDORA had difficulty with the first before it could solve the second.

There is a broader Cold War irony here. The American government was investigating a Soviet signal partly because Soviet biomedical standards and literature appeared to take low-level exposure more seriously than American practice did. Yet the Americans initially modeled the threat with an inaccurate American estimate of the signal. The adversary's reported science widened the hypothesis; the U.S. measurement error distorted the first test of it.

Once the estimate came down, the story should have become less dramatic.

Instead, it became more interesting. The absence of clear primate degradation at the corrected level did not end the program. It forced researchers to ask a harder question: if the gross effect was gone, what exactly were they looking for?

That is where PANDORA began to open.
