# The Building Was Already Listening

The microwave signal did not enter an innocent building.

That fact changes the psychology of the entire episode. The American embassy in Moscow was already a place where walls, fixtures, construction materials, telephone systems, windows, and nearby buildings had to be treated as possible components of an intelligence contest. Soviet technical surveillance was not hypothetical. In 1964, Jerome Wiesner's security review followed the discovery of listening devices embedded in the embassy. His report recommended defensive measures against acoustic and electromagnetic collection and separately complained that too little effort had been made to understand the microwave signal.

A beam aimed at a normal office building is an engineering anomaly. A beam aimed at a building already known to contain hostile technical devices is immediately entangled with espionage.

This is the strongest competing explanation to the biological-weapon story and it should not be saved for a skeptical appendix. Electromagnetic energy can be useful for surveillance without being intended to harm a person. It can illuminate a passive or semi-passive device, interrogate electronics, reveal emissions, or interfere with collection systems. Cold War technical intelligence was full of devices that made ordinary architecture electrically interesting.

The famous Great Seal bug is the obvious precedent. In 1945 Soviet schoolchildren presented U.S. Ambassador Averell Harriman with a carved wooden Great Seal. Hidden inside was a passive resonant cavity microphone later nicknamed the Thing. It did not require the kind of internal power supply American technicians were accustomed to hunting. An external radiofrequency signal could energize the device, which then reradiated information from the room. The device was discovered in 1952.

The Thing does not prove that the later Moscow Signal activated another bug. It proves something narrower and more important: Soviet services had already demonstrated an operational interest in illuminating a U.S. diplomatic space with radiofrequency energy to extract information. That makes a surveillance hypothesis historically grounded rather than ad hoc.

It also makes the later biomedical concern more complicated. If a signal is being used to activate or interrogate a device, human exposure can be a by-product of the intelligence operation. The beam may still matter medically. But harmful intent cannot be inferred from exposure alone.

Several declassified assessments preserved this ambiguity. A 1976 White House memorandum, written after stronger or additional signals had been detected, noted Defense Department concern that at least some transmissions might be associated with the activation or interrogation of implanted audio devices. Other theories involved jamming or interference with American technical operations. The biological hypothesis existed beside these rather than replacing them.

Why, then, did the biological branch become culturally dominant?

Partly because a secret beam aimed at people is narratively stronger than a secret beam aimed at electronics. Partly because health effects are personal in a way that counterintelligence mechanisms are not. And partly because the U.S. government's own behavior supplied the ingredients: animal experiments, hidden blood sampling, discussion of unwitting human tests, secrecy from embassy personnel. Once those facts became public, it was reasonable for employees and journalists to ask whether officials knew more about danger than they were admitting.

But an institution can behave secretively because it does not know enough, not only because it knows too much.

The building's history made that distinction hard to trust. If officials already knew Soviet technicians had hidden devices in walls and illuminated passive bugs, then why not tell employees that another unexplained radiofrequency signal was present? The intelligence answer is that disclosure could compromise collection and countermeasures. The ethical answer is that workers cannot make informed decisions about a hazard they are not told exists. The scientific answer is messier: informing people might change symptom reporting, but not informing them destroys informed consent and complicates later reconstruction.

All three answers can be true at once.

This is where secrecy begins to create its own evidentiary ecology. Suppose an employee later develops headaches, depression, leukemia, or another illness. If the exposure had been disclosed prospectively, researchers might have contemporaneous symptom logs, location data, exposure maps, and medical measurements designed around a defined protocol. Because it was not, the later investigator inherits records created for other purposes. The absence of a clean signal in those records can then be interpreted either as evidence of no effect or as evidence that the monitoring was inadequate.

The argument becomes difficult to close because the original secrecy damaged the experiment no one knew they were running.

There is another lesson in the building itself. Technical systems rarely respect the boundaries we draw between intelligence, safety, and biology. A radiofrequency transmitter can be a communications device, a radar, a jammer, an interrogator, a heater, a sensory stimulus under some pulsed conditions, or simply background exposure depending on frequency, waveform, geometry, power, duration, and the receiving system. Asking what a beam is for without specifying those parameters is like asking what electricity is for.

The Moscow Signal's purpose problem was therefore not going to be solved by the fact that it was microwave energy. The frequency band narrowed possibilities. The measured field strength narrowed them further. Modulation could matter. Directionality and source location mattered. The presence of known Soviet technical-collection practice mattered. So did the failure to find a clear biological effect at reconstructed exposure levels.

The building was already listening. That does not solve the case.

It does, however, prevent us from pretending that biology was the only mystery the beam was capable of serving.
