# Notes on Evidence and Sources

The Moscow Signal is unusually vulnerable to a particular historical mistake: using the existence of secret research as evidence that the research succeeded.

The source trail for this book is organized to resist that move. Declassified memoranda establish what U.S. officials detected, feared, proposed, funded, measured, or concluded at a given time. They do not automatically establish the underlying Soviet intent or the truth of a biomedical hypothesis. A memorandum saying an official worried about behavioral effects is evidence of the worry. A program authorization is evidence that the question crossed a funding threshold. Neither is proof of a weapon.

The reverse rule also applies. A later negative review does not erase the original exposure. The microwave signal directed at the embassy is not a rumor invented by PANDORA enthusiasts. It is a documented technical event. The ethical problems surrounding employee notification, cover stories, and contemplated human experimentation are likewise independent of whether the radiation caused a measurable health effect.

The manuscript therefore uses four evidence classes throughout its research notes: verified/declassified/documented facts; credible but incomplete reporting or testimony; disputed allegations or claims without independent confirmation; and speculative synthesis or open conjecture. Readers should be suspicious whenever a sentence crosses from one class to another without announcing the move.

The most important public documentary collection used in this draft is the National Security Archive's 2022 briefing book, “The Moscow Signals Declassified,” which assembles and links a series of primary U.S. records obtained from archives and FOIA releases. The next research pass should replace summary dependence with page-indexed use of the underlying documents wherever possible.

For the health question, the 1978 Foreign Service Health Status Study remains central. Later methodological discussion is not unanimous in emphasis. J. Mark Elwood's 2012 review supports the broad conclusion of the original report and argues that some alarming reinterpretations misuse small-number findings and multiple comparisons. José A. Martínez's 2019 review argues that unresolved questions remain. IARC's later treatment of the cohort provides an additional standardized epidemiological context. The manuscript does not treat any of these as an oracle.

For anomalous health incidents, the source hierarchy is deliberately split. The 2020 National Academies report and the 2022 Intelligence Community Experts Panel addressed mechanism plausibility. The 2023 and January 2025 Intelligence Community assessments addressed adversary attribution and intelligence evidence. GAO work addresses treatment and administration. These documents answer different questions. Combining them into one government “position” would be inaccurate.

No nonpublic classified material was used or claimed. No anonymous intelligence access is implied. All classified-adjacent claims in this draft derive from lawfully public declassified records, official public assessments, congressional/GAO material, published science, or identified secondary historical work.
