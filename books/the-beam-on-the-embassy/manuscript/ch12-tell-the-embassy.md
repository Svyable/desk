# Tell the Embassy

For years, the government knew there was an unusual microwave exposure in the embassy and most of the people working there did not.

By 1975, that arrangement became untenable.

New or stronger signals were detected. Ambassador Walter Stoessel pushed Washington to confront the Soviet government and to tell embassy personnel. The internal argument was not only about health. Disclosure could reveal U.S. technical knowledge, complicate diplomacy, invite press attention, and create fear before officials could offer a firm medical conclusion. But the alternative was becoming indefensible: continue monitoring an unexplained exposure while the exposed people remained outside the decision.

A briefing was finally held in early 1976.

The secrecy did not survive contact with the workforce. News leaked rapidly, and the Moscow Signal became public controversy. Once that happened, every earlier institutional choice was reinterpreted through a new question: if the government had believed the signal harmless, why had it kept the signal secret?

That question is powerful and incomplete.

Governments classify technical intelligence for reasons unrelated to health. Revealing that a signal has been detected can tell the transmitter what the receiver can measure. Countermeasures can expose the parameters of the threat. An unexplained Soviet transmission aimed at a sensitive building would have been classified even if officials were certain it had no biological effect.

But the health research made the secrecy harder to defend. PANDORA existed precisely because officials were not certain. Blood had been collected under a cover story. Animal exposure work had been conducted. Human experimentation had been discussed. The employees were not simply being denied intelligence trivia. They were being denied information about a physical exposure their government considered important enough to study as a possible hazard.

The 1979 Senate review later stated the point directly: personnel should have been informed earlier.

This conclusion does not require proving that the exposure caused disease. Risk communication is not a criminal trial. The threshold for telling someone that an unusual, persistent, involuntary exposure exists can be lower than the threshold for establishing that the exposure caused a specific injury.

The distinction is central to modern controversies. Institutions often fear that communicating uncertainty will be heard as confirming danger. Employees may hear “we are investigating” as “we know something is wrong.” Officials respond by waiting until they have a cleaner answer. The wait itself becomes evidence of concealment when the issue eventually emerges.

Moscow is an unusually pure example of that failure mode.

The government had two risks: the physical risk from the signal and the institutional risk from disclosure. It managed the second by imposing the first uncertainty on employees without their knowledge. Once the secret broke, the institution inherited a third risk: distrust.

Trust is often discussed as if it were public relations. Here it had technical consequences. A retrospective health survey depends on people answering questions about symptoms, work history, and medical events. Those people now knew that relevant information had been withheld from them. Some might overattribute ordinary symptoms to the exposure. Others might distrust reassurance. Both responses complicate interpretation without making the symptoms unreal.

Expectation effects are especially easy to weaponize rhetorically. Skeptics can dismiss post-disclosure symptom reports as anxiety. Advocates can dismiss any discussion of expectation as an attempt to psychologize injured people. Neither position is adequate. Knowing about an exposure can change symptom attention and reporting. It can also reveal symptoms that were previously ignored. A rigorous study has to account for the timing of knowledge rather than choosing the explanation that flatters its conclusion.

The Moscow cohort offered a rare opportunity because much of the exposure preceded public awareness. Medical records existed from the period when employees did not know about the beam. That reduces one kind of reporting bias. It does not solve exposure misclassification, small numbers, multiple comparisons, or long latency.

The public disclosure also changed diplomacy. U.S. officials had already raised the matter privately with Soviet counterparts, including during the 1967 Glassboro summit. The Soviet side reportedly denied or deflected the concern and suggested comparable American activities against Soviet facilities. Once the issue became public, the beam acquired propaganda value for both sides.

This is another reason intent remains difficult to infer. An intelligence service caught running a technical collection operation has every incentive to deny its function. A government embarrassed by years of exposure has incentives to emphasize the seriousness of the adversary's act while minimizing its own failure to inform employees. Journalists have incentives to choose the most vivid interpretation. Scientists enter a field already saturated with institutional motives.

None of those motives make the evidence false.

They make source criticism mandatory.

The strongest documented facts after disclosure were still the same: the signal had existed; its levels had varied; the U.S. had studied possible biological effects; no convincing evidence of a major health effect had been established; and the employees had been kept uninformed for too long.

The public wanted one answer: was it dangerous?

The government could offer only a hierarchy. Established high-level microwave exposure can heat tissue and injure. Some pulsed RF conditions can produce sensory effects such as microwave auditory perception. The embassy levels, as later measured and reconstructed, were far below the regimes associated with obvious thermal injury. PANDORA had not demonstrated behavioral impairment at realistic levels. Epidemiology was still needed for longer-term outcomes.

That hierarchy is scientifically responsible and politically terrible.

People do not experience a hierarchy of evidence. They experience the fact that a beam was aimed at their workplace and someone else decided not to tell them.

The later health study would have to carry not only the statistical burden of the exposure but the moral burden of the secrecy.

No epidemiologist can adjust for that with a regression coefficient.
