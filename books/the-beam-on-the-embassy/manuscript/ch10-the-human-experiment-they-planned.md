# The Human Experiment They Planned

On April 21, 1969, a group of officials and scientists discussing Project PANDORA reached the point every uncertain animal program eventually approaches: perhaps the model is the problem.

The declassified meeting minutes are blunt. After years of primate work, there was still insufficient evidence to draw conclusions about the effect of the Moscow-type signal on human behavior. BIG BOY had not produced significant differences in the exposed naval groups. The animal data had not closed the question. The committee discussed developing a human program.

Subjects, the minutes suggested, might be obtained from Fort Detrick. Exposure could continue for six months. The study should be double-blind. Eyes and gonads should be protected. Shielding of the testicles was recommended.

The document is disturbing partly because of its tone. A proposal that reads grotesquely now appears in the language of protocol design: duration, blinding, shielding, subject source. The bureaucratic form makes the ethical leap look like another technical parameter.

A May meeting went further into questions of cover and classification. The Defense Department regarded human-based data as a high priority. ARPA believed the effort should be classified. The discussion included how subjects might be kept unaware of the true nature of the work.

The experiment, according to the available record, was not carried out before PANDORA ended in 1970.

That boundary must be stated precisely. Planning an unethical or ethically dubious experiment is not the same as performing it. No manuscript about secret programs earns credibility by converting a proposal into an event.

The planning still matters.

It reveals how uncertainty and national security can combine to invert normal research logic. In civilian medicine, weak animal evidence about a poorly characterized hazard usually makes deliberate human exposure harder to justify. Inside PANDORA, the same weakness became part of the argument for moving toward people: the animal model had not answered the operational question, and only human data might.

This is not unique to microwaves. Military medicine has repeatedly faced situations in which the relevant population is human and the threat cannot be modeled perfectly. Chemical agents, altitude, acceleration, sleep deprivation, blast, radiation, and infectious disease all create pressure for controlled exposure or challenge studies. Ethical research is possible in some of those domains. The question is what subjects know, what risks are justified, what safeguards exist, and whether the science is strong enough to make the exposure informative.

PANDORA's proposed unwitting design fails at the first requirement.

Blinding is not consent. A subject can agree to a randomized blinded experiment without knowing whether they are in the treatment arm. They still know the nature of the intervention, the relevant risks, and the fact that they are research participants. A cover story that conceals the exposure itself is a different thing.

The distinction is elementary now because human-subject research has spent decades learning it the hard way.

The PANDORA minutes belong to that history. They sit in the same era in which U.S. institutions were being forced to confront abuses in medical experimentation and the inadequacy of informal professional ethics. The National Research Act and modern institutional review structures were still ahead. The Belmont Report would not appear until 1979. But Nuremberg was already history. The idea that voluntary consent mattered was not unavailable.

What national-security officials possessed was a competing moral frame. If an adversary might be using a new physical mechanism against American personnel, then a controlled experiment could protect far more people than it exposed. Secrecy could be treated as part of operational necessity. The anticipated benefit was not merely scientific knowledge but defense against an unknown attack.

That logic has force. It is also the logic by which emergency programs justify crossing boundaries.

The way to test it is to ask whether the study could actually have answered the question. What waveform would be used? What dose? Why six months? What endpoint? How would the protocol account for the fact that realistic Moscow exposure had not produced clear behavioral effects in primates? What would a positive result mean if subjects were exposed at a higher field than the embassy? What would a negative result mean if the modulation differed? How many subjects would be required? Were there credible priors for the expected effect size?

Without answers, the ethical cost rises while scientific value falls.

This is where the earlier dosimetry problem returns. Human experimentation looks like the most direct route only if the exposure being tested is known. If the actual Moscow field remains uncertain in waveform and individual dose, then moving from monkey to human does not solve the central identification problem. It merely moves the uncertainty into a more morally expensive subject.

PANDORA's planned human work therefore illuminates the difference between relevance and validity. Humans are more relevant to human risk than rhesus monkeys. But an uncontrolled or badly specified human experiment can be less valid than a careful animal one.

The episode also helps explain why later claims about secret microwave research acquired such staying power. Here, in declassified government minutes, are officials discussing covert human exposure to electromagnetic energy. Anyone encountering that record after decades of official denial or minimization of unusual bioeffects could reasonably ask what else remained hidden.

The disciplined answer is: perhaps other things did remain hidden. The document itself cannot tell us what they were.

Classification creates an evidentiary asymmetry that conspiracy narratives exploit naturally. The government can prove the existence of one secret only by declassifying it. The public cannot prove the nonexistence of an undisclosed successor. This means every revealed program can be used to support an indefinitely larger invisible one.

The antidote is not trust. It is claim discipline.

We know PANDORA discussed unwitting human exposure. We know the public record reviewed here does not show that the proposed six-month Fort Detrick experiment occurred. We know PANDORA ended in 1970. We know later U.S. agencies continued to study radiofrequency bioeffects in other contexts because radar, communications, occupational safety, and weapons research made the field important regardless of Moscow.

Those facts leave room for unanswered archival questions. They do not authorize invention.

There is a final irony in the planned experiment. PANDORA was trying to determine whether a foreign government might be exposing Americans without their knowledge. To answer that question, American officials considered exposing other Americans without their knowledge.

The symmetry is not exact. Purpose, dose, safeguards, and institutional role differ. But from the perspective of consent, it is close enough to be uncomfortable.

The program did not cross that final line, at least on the evidence now public.

Outside reviewers were about to give it another reason not to.
