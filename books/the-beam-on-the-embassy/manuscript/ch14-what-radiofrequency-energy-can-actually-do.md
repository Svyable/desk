# What Radiofrequency Energy Can Actually Do

The argument becomes cleaner once the word microwave is stripped of its mythology.

Microwaves are electromagnetic waves. They occupy a portion of the radiofrequency spectrum. At sufficiently high absorbed power, they heat tissue. That is not controversial. Microwave ovens work because electromagnetic energy couples into material and produces heating. Radar and high-power transmitters can create occupational hazards for the same reason. Eyes and testes have historically drawn special concern because of their thermal characteristics and limited ability to dissipate heat.

None of that tells us what happened at the Moscow embassy.

The dose matters.

At low incident power densities, bulk heating may be too small to explain symptoms. That is where the argument moves into nonthermal or microthermal effects, pulsed-field interactions, and sensory phenomena. One of the most important established examples is the microwave auditory effect, often associated with Allan Frey, in which brief pulses of radiofrequency energy can produce an auditory sensation. The prevailing physical explanation involves rapid thermoelastic expansion from tiny temperature changes that launch pressure waves inside the head. The effect is real without requiring mystical brain coupling.

That mechanism is instructive because it breaks a common rhetorical shortcut. A nonobvious biological or sensory effect at low average power does not imply that all low-power exposures are biologically potent. The relevant pulse characteristics, peak power, absorption, repetition rate, and geometry still matter. Average power can conceal high peaks; peak power can sound alarming while total deposited energy remains small. A waveform is not adequately described by one scalar.

PANDORA investigators understood part of this problem when they became interested in modulation. If the Moscow signal's carrier power was too low to produce the feared gross heating, perhaps temporal structure mattered. But “modulation matters” is not a mechanism. It is a research direction.

A robust mechanism has to connect field to tissue and tissue to outcome.

For an RF hypothesis, that chain might begin with incident electric and magnetic fields, continue through coupling and specific absorption, produce a physical change in tissue, perturb a biological process, and generate a measurable symptom or injury. Each link has its own scale and uncertainty. A field measured outside the body is not the biological dose. A biological change is not necessarily harmful. A transient symptom is not necessarily structural injury.

This hierarchy sounds fussy only because public discussion usually skips it.

The thermal framework remains powerful because it supplies a well-understood route from absorbed electromagnetic energy to tissue temperature and damage. Claims of reliable effects far below thermal thresholds need equally disciplined evidence. Some laboratory studies report changes in cells, animals, or neurological measures under low-intensity RF exposure. The literature is heterogeneous. Exposure systems differ. Reproducibility varies. Endpoints are numerous. Mechanisms remain contested for many claimed effects.

That does not justify saying “nonthermal effects are impossible.” Physics does not grant that shortcut. It just means a claim should be tied to the exact regime in which it was demonstrated and should survive replication.

The Moscow case was badly suited to this precision. Historical documents often give power density ranges and carrier frequencies but not the full exposure map an engineer would want today. Building reflections, source geometry, duty cycle, modulation, occupancy, and individual position would all affect absorbed dose. The signal also changed over time. An employee working on an upper floor near a window could have had a different exposure from a dependent living elsewhere in the complex.

This makes retrospective statements such as “the staff were exposed to five microwatts per square centimeter” convenient and misleading. A maximum measurement is not a personal dose.

The same discipline applies to weapons feasibility. A device capable of producing an acute effect at a target must deliver sufficient energy with the required waveform after propagation losses, antenna geometry, obstacles, orientation, and tissue coupling. A mechanism demonstrated with the head next to a laboratory source may be irrelevant at standoff distance. A pulsed effect with high peak power may impose engineering signatures that are detectable. Conversely, a directional antenna can concentrate energy and make distance less decisive than intuition suggests.

Engineering decides which intuitions survive.

This is why the later anomalous-health-incident debate produced apparently conflicting official statements. The 2020 National Academies committee judged directed pulsed radiofrequency energy a plausible mechanism for the distinctive acute symptoms described in some cases. A 2022 Intelligence Community experts panel likewise considered pulsed electromagnetic energy plausible for a subset of core characteristics. Those conclusions concerned physical and biological plausibility.

The intelligence assessments asked a different question: did a foreign actor actually cause the reported incidents? By 2023, and again in the updated assessment released in January 2025, most U.S. intelligence agencies judged foreign adversary responsibility very unlikely for the reported AHI population as a whole. Some components retained lower-confidence dissent or concern around a small subset and around foreign research capabilities.

There is no logical contradiction between “a mechanism could produce these symptoms” and “we do not assess that an adversary used such a mechanism in these cases.”

Moscow helps explain why people confuse the two. There, an adversary unquestionably did direct microwave energy at a U.S. diplomatic facility. That historical fact supplies intent-shaped context to a later scientific plausibility argument. It can make the modern hypothesis feel half-proved before incident-specific evidence is examined.

But a mechanism has no memory. The fact that Soviet intelligence used radiofrequency energy in Moscow does not cause a later patient's symptoms.

The strongest scientific posture is almost annoyingly conditional. At high absorbed power, RF heating is real and hazardous. Under some pulsed conditions, sensory phenomena such as microwave auditory perception are established. Other low-intensity biological effects remain an active and uneven research field. A particular incident requires source characterization, dosimetry, a plausible coupling mechanism, compatible symptoms, timing, exclusion of stronger alternatives, and ideally instrumented detection or reproducible biomarkers.

The farther we move from measured source to inferred injury, the more evidence we need.

The Moscow Signal gave investigators the source but not the injury.

Many later AHI cases gave investigators the injury report but not the source.

That reversal is one reason analogy between them is so tempting and so treacherous.
