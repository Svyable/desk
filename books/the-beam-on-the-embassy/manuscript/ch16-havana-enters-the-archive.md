# Havana Enters the Archive

When American diplomats in Havana began reporting sudden auditory phenomena, pressure, pain, dizziness, disequilibrium, cognitive symptoms, and other health problems in 2016 and 2017, the Moscow Signal returned before anyone invited it.

The analogy was irresistible. American personnel. A hostile capital. Unusual sensory experiences. Possible directed energy. A government slow to explain what it knew. A history of Soviet microwave research sitting in declassified archives.

But analogy is not evidence, and the differences are not small.

In Moscow, the radiofrequency source was measured. The argument was over purpose, dose, and biological consequence. In Havana and later anomalous health incidents, the core problem ran in the opposite direction: people reported acute symptoms and sensory phenomena, but investigators generally lacked a contemporaneously measured external source that could be tied to the event.

The evidentiary chains therefore begin at different ends.

This did not stop serious scientists from considering radiofrequency energy. In 2020, a National Academies committee concluded that directed, pulsed radiofrequency energy appeared to be the most plausible mechanism among those it considered for the distinctive acute clinical features in some cases, while emphasizing information gaps and heterogeneity. In 2022, an Intelligence Community expert panel similarly judged pulsed electromagnetic energy, particularly in the RF range, to be a plausible mechanism for the core characteristics of a subset of incidents. Ultrasound was also considered plausible in more limited circumstances.

Those assessments mattered because they rejected an easy dismissal. They did not say every symptom was psychogenic. They did not say a microwave weapon had been detected. They said the physics and biology of certain external stimuli could plausibly produce features described in some reports.

Then intelligence analysis asked a different question.

The March 2023 Intelligence Community assessment concluded that most agencies judged it very unlikely a foreign adversary was responsible for the reported incidents overall. Investigators had looked for patterns of targeting, foreign programs, device feasibility, and connections among cases. The assessment argued that many reports were likely attributable to preexisting medical conditions, environmental factors, or other conventional causes and that the geographic and temporal distribution did not fit a single adversary campaign.

The updated assessment released on January 10, 2025 did not simply erase dissent. Most of the Intelligence Community retained the judgment that foreign responsibility was very unlikely overall. At least some components adjusted their confidence or remained open to the possibility that a foreign actor had developed or could have used a novel capability in a small subset. The public document also distinguished evidence about foreign research from evidence tying that research to a reported incident.

This is exactly the distinction Moscow teaches us to preserve.

A state can research a technology without using it in a specific case. A device can be physically feasible without being present. A symptom pattern can be consistent with an exposure without identifying its source. A historical precedent can raise prior plausibility without proving recurrence.

The temptation to collapse these categories has been enormous because the stakes are personal. People reporting AHIs have experienced real suffering and, in many cases, long medical journeys. Government employees who believe they were attacked have had to navigate agencies simultaneously responsible for care, security, intelligence analysis, and institutional reputation. A causal debate can easily become a loyalty test: believe the mechanism or disbelieve the patient.

That is a category error.

A person can be genuinely injured while the cause remains unknown. An intelligence agency can judge foreign responsibility unlikely without claiming symptoms are invented. A scientific panel can find pulsed RF plausible without claiming a Russian emitter was present outside a particular residence.

The Moscow record helps precisely because it contains the inverse combination. There we know an emitter was present, yet a clear health effect was not demonstrated.

Put the two episodes together and the lesson becomes almost embarrassingly simple: source evidence and health evidence are separate dimensions.

Imagine a two-axis chart. One axis asks how strongly an external source is established. The other asks how strongly a specific biological effect is established. Moscow sits high on source evidence and lower on demonstrated health consequence. Many AHI cases sit higher on reported clinical consequence and lower on source identification. Neither can fill the other's missing axis by analogy.

This matters for the public debate because Moscow is often invoked as if it supplies the missing emitter for Havana. “They did it before” becomes a substitute for “we measured it here.” Intelligence history can inform what to look for; it cannot replace incident evidence.

There is a second historical carryover: secrecy.

In Moscow, the government withheld knowledge of a measured signal from employees. In the AHI era, the criticism has often been different: employees and advocates argued that agencies were too slow to accept a hostile-actor hypothesis or too fragmented in responding to reports. The moral memory of Moscow makes underreaction look especially dangerous. Nobody wants to be the official who dismisses an invisible exposure and discovers decades later that it was real.

That institutional fear can rationally broaden investigation. It can also create a bias toward threat explanations.

The opposite bias exists too. Intelligence agencies are reluctant to attribute attacks without evidence because attribution can have diplomatic and military consequences. A history of weak microwave bioeffects claims can encourage analysts to treat RF hypotheses as fringe even when a particular waveform is physically plausible. The organization is pulled in both directions: avoid missing a novel weapon; avoid inventing one.

The 2025 assessment should therefore be read as an intelligence judgment, not the final word of physics. It says what the agencies assess about adversary responsibility given the available collection. It does not repeal microwave auditory effects, close low-intensity bioeffects research, or prove that no directed-energy device could ever cause acute neurological symptoms.

Likewise, the National Academies plausibility conclusion should be read as a scientific mechanism assessment, not an attribution finding. It does not identify an operator, device, or deployment history.

The difference between these documents is not bureaucratic confusion. It is the difference between questions.

Moscow is useful when it makes those questions sharper. It becomes dangerous when it is used to answer them in advance.

The responsible historical statement is modest: the Soviet Union did direct microwave energy at the U.S. Embassy in Moscow for years, and the United States secretly investigated possible biological effects without establishing the feared behavioral harm. That episode proves that covert RF irradiation of a diplomatic facility has historical precedent.

It does not prove what happened in Havana.

The archive can raise suspicion.

It cannot emit the missing signal.
