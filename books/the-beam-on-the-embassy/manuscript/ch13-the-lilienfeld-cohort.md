# The Lilienfeld Cohort

By the time Abraham Lilienfeld and his colleagues were asked to study the health of American personnel who had served in Moscow, the clean experiment had already been lost.

Nobody had assigned diplomats randomly to irradiated and nonirradiated embassies. Nobody had equipped each employee with a personal dosimeter. Exposure had varied with building location, time, and changes in the Soviet transmissions. The relevant population included employees and dependents with different patterns of presence. The government had withheld knowledge of the beam for most of the exposure period and then disclosed it dramatically.

Epidemiology arrived afterward, as it often does, to reconstruct what engineering and policy had failed to measure prospectively.

The State Department commissioned a large retrospective study comparing people who had worked or lived at the U.S. Embassy in Moscow with personnel and dependents at other Eastern European posts. The 1978 report became the principal human-health evidence in the Moscow Signal debate. It did not find a persuasive increase in overall mortality among the Moscow group or a coherent pattern of serious disease that could be attributed to the microwave exposure.

That broad conclusion has survived later review better than the mythology around it.

The study is also a perfect object lesson in how rare outcomes and multiple comparisons can keep an argument alive.

When researchers examine many diseases and symptoms in a relatively small exposed group, some outcomes will differ by chance. A leukemia estimate can look elevated while containing only a few cases and a confidence interval wide enough to include very different true risks. A cluster of nonspecific complaints can appear in questionnaire data without demonstrating a mechanism. An absence of brain-cancer deaths can coexist with concern about other endpoints. The temptation is to select the interesting cells from a large table and treat them as the study's hidden truth.

Later writers have done exactly that in opposite directions.

Critics of the original reassurance have pointed to particular symptom patterns, blood findings, or cancer categories and argued that the study underplayed them. Defenders have emphasized the lack of consistent mortality or dose-response patterns and the statistical instability of small subgroups. A 2012 review by epidemiologist J. Mark Elwood concluded that the original report's overall interpretation was supported and that some contrary readings depended on misinterpreting the data. A 2019 review by José A. Martínez revisited unresolved questions and argued that the episode still deserved careful attention.

This is what a mature dispute looks like when the underlying study cannot be rerun.

The cohort has strengths. It used a comparison population drawn from other Foreign Service posts rather than comparing diplomats with the general public alone. It could examine medical records from periods before the signal became public, reducing some awareness bias. It covered thousands of employees and dependents across the comparison groups and attempted mortality follow-up.

Its weaknesses are equally structural. Assignment to Moscow was not random. Exposure was largely ecological: being in Moscow served as a proxy for a highly variable electromagnetic dose. The actual field changed over the decades. Many diseases of interest were rare. Latency could exceed the observation window. Questionnaire response and medical-record completeness could vary. The study tested many outcomes. Employees at different embassies faced different environmental, occupational, and cultural conditions unrelated to microwaves.

No statistical technique can create a personal exposure record that never existed.

This is why claims about the study should be proportional. It is strong evidence against a large, common, easily detectable health catastrophe caused by the Moscow exposure. If the signal had produced a dramatic mortality effect across the workforce, the cohort should have had a reasonable chance of seeing it. It is weaker evidence against rare outcomes, small risk increases, very long-latency effects, or effects confined to a subset with unusual exposure.

Those residual possibilities matter scientifically. They are also infinitely expandable if no prior is specified.

A rare effect in a subset exposed to an unknown waveform at an unknown local field can survive almost any negative study. At some point the question changes from “has harm been demonstrated?” to “can harm be ruled out absolutely?” The second question is nearly impossible for any environmental exposure.

Policy and history should not confuse them.

The Senate's 1979 review reached a similarly cautious position. It criticized the government's secrecy but concluded that no convincing evidence directly implicated the microwave exposure in adverse health effects as of that analysis, while noting that some long-term mortality questions could not yet be closed.

That is less satisfying than either certainty.

For people who became ill after serving in Moscow, population-level reassurance could never answer the personal counterfactual: would this have happened if I had not been there? Epidemiology rarely can. It estimates patterns across groups. A disease with ordinary background incidence can be caused by an exposure in one individual and still leave no detectable cohort signal, especially in small populations. Conversely, the occurrence of disease after exposure is not evidence that the exposure caused it.

The human mind dislikes this asymmetry. Suffering is specific. Statistics are collective.

The Moscow debate often moved from one scale to the other without warning. An individual's leukemia became evidence for the cohort. The cohort's null result became reassurance for the individual. Neither translation is automatic.

There is also the issue of exposure standards. Later summaries of the cohort describe maximum measured field levels in the range of a few to tens of microwatts per square centimeter during different periods, with lower levels at other times. Those numbers are useful only when tied to duration, location, frequency, and the distinction between incident field and absorbed dose. They cannot be turned into a binary exposed/unexposed switch without losing information.

Yet the epidemiology had little choice. Historical reconstruction often works with the categories history leaves behind.

The correct posture is therefore neither dismissal nor rescue. The Lilienfeld study did not validate a microwave sickness syndrome in the embassy population. It also did not provide the kind of high-resolution dosimetry that would let us exclude every subtle or subgroup effect. The broad health catastrophe feared in some accounts failed to appear. The narrowest possibilities remained difficult to test.

This matters when Moscow is invoked as precedent for later anomalous health incidents. The historical argument sometimes runs: embassy personnel were exposed to microwaves and suffered symptoms; therefore later diplomats with symptoms may have been exposed to microwaves. But the first premise silently overstates causation. Moscow gives us a documented exposure and a disputed health interpretation, not a solved exposure-disease pair.

A precedent with an unresolved causal chain cannot close a later causal chain.

It can, however, tell us what measurements we should wish we had taken the first time.
