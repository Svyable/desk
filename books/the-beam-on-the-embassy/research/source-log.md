# Source log

This log favors primary/declassified and authoritative sources. Secondary work is used to locate records, reconstruct debate, or provide later methodological review. A patent, program, or foreign paper is evidence that a claim or research direction existed, not that the claimed capability worked.

## Declassified / archival spine

### National Security Archive — The Moscow Signals Declassified (2022)

https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/briefing-book/intelligence-russia-programs/2022-09-13/moscow-signals-declassified-microwave

The strongest single public collection for the book. It reproduces and summarizes declassified records including:

- Jerome Wiesner's June 24, 1964 report to Clark Clifford on embassy security and the unexplained microwave signal.
- Samuel Koslov's May 1965 ARPA memorandum, “Biomedical Phenomena.”
- Richard S. Cesaro's October 15, 1965 “Justification Memorandum for Project PANDORA.”
- State Department medical cables and the 1966 George Washington University cytogenetics contract.
- the June 25, 1967 Rusk-Gromyko memorandum of conversation concerning signals against the Moscow chancery.
- CIA representative Joseph Johnston's September 25, 1967 summary of Project BIZARRE results and revised TUMS power levels.
- Cesaro's September 27, 1967 BIZARRE progress report.
- Herbert Pollack's January 2, 1969 report on the USS Saratoga / BIG BOY study.
- April-May 1969 PANDORA meeting minutes discussing human experimentation, cover, classification, six-month exposure, and shielding.
- Samuel Koslov's November 4, 1969 RAND review of PANDORA animal data.
- the August 16, 1971 RAND-led review of Navy chronic low-level primate work.
- George H. Heilmeier's September 15, 1977 DARPA letter summarizing the program and its cost.
- the 1979 Senate Committee print reviewing embassy irradiation and health studies.

The Archive's editorial framing is secondary; individual documents are treated as primary evidence for what the originating institution recorded.

## Government and congressional review

### U.S. Senate Committee on Commerce, Science, and Transportation, 1979

“Microwave Irradiation of the U.S. Embassy in Moscow (Review of its History and Studies to Determine Whether or Not Related Health Defects were Experienced by Employees Assigned in the Period 1953-1977).”

A scan is linked as Document 15 in the National Security Archive collection above. Critical for two separate conclusions that must not be collapsed: the committee criticized the failure to inform embassy employees promptly, while finding no convincing evidence at that time directly implicating the exposure in adverse health effects. It also preserved uncertainty about long-latency outcomes.

## Epidemiology

### Lilienfeld et al. (1978), Foreign Service Health Status Study

The original contract report is the central retrospective cohort study. A useful later review with detailed description and references:

J. Mark Elwood (2012), “Microwaves in the cold war: the Moscow embassy study and its interpretation.” *Environmental Health* 11:85.

https://doi.org/10.1186/1476-069X-11-85

https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3509929/

Elwood concludes that the original report's broad interpretation is supported and argues that contrary claims often misread small-number findings and multiple comparisons.

### Martínez (2019)

José A. Martínez, “The ‘Moscow signal’ epidemiological study, 40 years on.” *Reviews on Environmental Health* 34(1):13–24.

https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30676008/

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1515/reveh-2018-0061

Useful counter-reading that revisits uncertainties and specific findings rather than accepting the historical reassurance uncritically.

### IARC / NCBI exposure review

IARC Monographs material on radiofrequency electromagnetic fields includes the Moscow cohort in its discussion of human evidence.

https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK304633/

Useful for exact study design summary, exposure categories, and context. Do not infer that IARC's Group 2B classification establishes that Moscow exposure caused disease in the embassy cohort.

## Anomalous Health Incidents — authoritative current record

### National Academies (2020)

*An Assessment of Illness in U.S. Government Employees and Their Families at Overseas Embassies.*

https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25889/chapter/2

The committee found directed, pulsed RF energy consistent with distinctive acute signs and symptoms in some reported cases and considered it the most plausible mechanism among those reviewed, while stressing data limitations. This is mechanism plausibility, not attribution.

### ODNI / IC Experts Panel (2022)

https://archive.dni.gov/index.php/newsroom/reports-publications/reports-publications-2022/3591-complementary-efforts-on-anomalous-health-incidents

The panel judged pulsed electromagnetic energy, particularly RF, plausible for the core characteristics of a subset of AHIs, with information gaps. Ultrasound was also considered plausible in close-access scenarios. Again: physical plausibility, not a finding that a foreign actor used a device.

### ODNI Intelligence Community Assessment (March 1, 2023)

https://www.dni.gov/files/ODNI/documents/assessments/NIC-Unclassified-ICA-Updated-Assessment-AHI-March2023.pdf

Most agencies assessed that a foreign adversary was very unlikely to be responsible for reported AHIs as a whole. Important to distinguish this intelligence attribution question from mechanism studies.

### ODNI Updated Assessment (January 10, 2025; information through December 2024)

https://www.dni.gov/files/ODNI/documents/assessments/NIC-Unclassified-ICA-Updated-Assessment-AHI-December2024.pdf

Fresh current anchor for the manuscript. Most of the IC continued to assess foreign responsibility as very unlikely overall, while the public assessment preserved some interagency differences and ongoing concern about foreign research/capability questions.

### GAO care / governance record (2024)

https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-24-106593

Useful for the fact that causal uncertainty did not eliminate a government duty to provide care and improve access for affected personnel.

### Senate intelligence legislation / declassification direction (2025)

https://www.intelligence.senate.gov/2025/07/17/intelligence-authorization-act-for-fiscal-year-2026-as-reported-on-july-17-2025/

Relevant to the continuing policy demand for review of AHI, known-cause comparison incidents, foreign weapons research, and declassification. Legislative proposal/report language is evidence of oversight interest, not evidence that a specific foreign weapon exists.

## Secondary historical synthesis worth consulting in expansion

- Nicholas H. Steneck, *The Microwave Debate* (MIT Press, 1984). Important historical treatment of standards, PANDORA, and RF controversy.
- Sharon Weinberger, *The Imagineers of War* (Knopf, 2017), especially ARPA/PANDORA context.
- National Security Archive editorial essays by Peter Kornbluh and William Burr, used as guideposts to originating records rather than replacements for them.
