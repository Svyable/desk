# The Embassy That Glowed

In 1953, the United States moved into a chancery building in Moscow and discovered that the building was already inside an experiment nobody had agreed to join.

The experiment did not look like an experiment. There was no apparatus in the lobby, no Soviet technician standing beside a rack of instruments, no consent form, no unmistakable injury. There was a radio-frequency signal. It arrived from outside. It bathed portions of the embassy for years. American officials measured it, argued about it, tried to infer its purpose, complained about it privately, and kept the people working inside the building on a need-to-know diet that often did not include the fact that they were the ones being exposed.

Later, the signal acquired the wonderfully bureaucratic name TUMS: the Technically Unidentified Moscow Signal. The research program that grew around one possible interpretation became PANDORA. Those names are useful because they prevent hindsight from improving the story. Nobody in the beginning knew that there was a secret Soviet mind-control weapon. Nobody knew that there was a harmless surveillance beam. Nobody knew that the relevant question was even biological. They knew a beam was there. The rest was inference.

That distinction is the spine of this book.

A physical signal existed. Its purpose was uncertain. Its biological significance was uncertain. The secrecy surrounding the investigation was real. The later mythology was enormous. All four facts can occupy the same room without one canceling the others.

The Moscow Signal is one of those historical objects that becomes less useful the more eagerly it is recruited. People who want proof of invisible weapons point to it as though the Soviet Union had left a notarized user manual. People who want the entire subject to disappear point to uncertainty about its purpose as though uncertainty erased the transmitter. Neither move is serious enough. The beam deserves the same treatment we would give a strange mineral pulled from a drill core or an unfamiliar trace in a particle detector: establish what was measured, preserve the limits of the measurement, separate mechanism from intention, then keep going.

The official record gives us more than rumor. A 1964 review by Jerome Wiesner, then an MIT provost and former presidential science adviser, warned that more effort should be made to understand the microwave signal directed at the embassy or stop it. By 1965, the Advanced Research Projects Agency had organized PANDORA around the possibility of effects on people. The program included animal work intended to simulate aspects of the Moscow exposure. In 1967, Secretary of State Dean Rusk raised the matter directly with Soviet Foreign Minister Andrei Gromyko. A 1972 State Department press-guidance cable acknowledged the signal and described PANDORA as an effort to simulate it and investigate possible health effects. In 1975, a Moscow embassy cable again discussed newly detected radio-frequency interference, previous PANDORA work, exposure levels, safety standards, and plans for additional measurements.

This was not an internet rumor assembled backward from Havana in 2016. It was a Cold War technical problem recorded by the bureaucracy while it was happening.

The obvious temptation is to jump from there to a weapon. Resist it.

A beam aimed at a diplomatic building can have many uses. Radio-frequency energy can illuminate structures. It can interact with electronic equipment. It can be part of surveillance or counter-surveillance systems. It can support passive listening techniques by providing energy to resonant devices. It can interfere with communications. It can be used to test how a target reacts. It can be a harassment tool. It can be a technical operation whose biological consequences are incidental. Even when a state knows that a signal affects tissue, that does not tell us whether the exposure was designed to do so.

This is where the Moscow Signal becomes more interesting than the weapon story. American officials did not know what the Soviets intended, yet they considered biological effects plausible enough to fund experiments. That tells us something about a recurring intelligence problem: governments do not wait for scientific consensus when the cost of being wrong appears asymmetric. If an adversary might possess an unfamiliar effect, the threshold for investigation falls. The result can be prudent. It can also create a literature warped by threat perception, secrecy, poor replication, and classified null results.

The beam was therefore two things at once. It was an external physical exposure, and it was a machine for producing uncertainty inside institutions.

That second effect lasted longer.

Consider what a physician in the embassy needed to know. If an employee complained of headaches, fatigue, dizziness, irritability, poor sleep, or another common symptom, was the microwave field relevant? The answer required dosimetry, frequency characterization, modulation data, exposure maps, baseline measurements, animal evidence, epidemiology, and some account of individual susceptibility. But much of the technical information belonged to security channels rather than medicine. The people responsible for protecting diplomatic secrets did not share the same incentives as the people responsible for interpreting symptoms. Compartmentation solved one problem by creating another.

This is not unusual. Secrecy rearranges evidence. It can keep a capability hidden from an adversary while also keeping the relevant variables apart from one another inside the same government. A radio engineer knows the field strength but not the clinical complaints. A doctor sees the complaints but not the waveform. An intelligence officer sees adversary reporting but not the raw laboratory notebooks. A policy official sees a conclusion stripped of the uncertainty that produced it. Each person can be competent. The system can still be epistemically stupid.

The Moscow case is an early demonstration of this failure mode.

It is also an early demonstration of another one: standards can masquerade as biology.

In the 1975 cable, officials noted that measured power levels appeared low relative to contemporary U.S. safety standards and also referred to lower Soviet standards. That sounds reassuring until one remembers what a safety standard is. A standard is not a law of nature. It is an administrative boundary built from evidence, assumptions, uncertainty factors, practical constraints, and a theory about which effects matter. If the relevant endpoint is heating, one exposure threshold follows. If a reproducible sensory or neurological effect occurs through another mechanism, the threshold has to be reconsidered for that endpoint. Conversely, the mere existence of a stricter standard does not prove that weaker fields cause disease. A national standard can reflect precaution, institutional history, measurement conventions, or different judgments about uncertainty.

The mistake is to treat the line as a discovery.

Radio-frequency biology is full of lines: thermal versus nonthermal, ionizing versus non-ionizing, safe versus unsafe, communication versus weapon, sensory effect versus injury. Some are physically meaningful. Some are useful approximations. Some are policy categories that become metaphysics through repetition.

The body does not know which side of a regulatory chart it occupies.

At sufficiently high power, radio-frequency energy heats tissue. That is not controversial. At particular pulsed conditions, radio-frequency energy can also produce an auditory perception through thermoelastic mechanisms in tissue around the head; that effect was reported in the open literature by Allan Frey in the early 1960s and later investigated in considerable detail. Millimeter-wave systems can deliberately produce a rapid painful heating sensation at the skin, which the United States eventually developed openly as Active Denial Technology. High-power microwave systems can disrupt or damage electronics. None of these established facts proves that an unknown adversary can secretly cause the full range of symptoms later grouped under anomalous health incidents. They do destroy the lazy premise that invisible radio-frequency energy is incapable of producing acute, targeted biological sensation unless it cooks the entire body.

Physics is more specific than slogans.

The specificity matters because the Moscow Signal became a template for stories long after the original facts blurred. The Soviet Union had secret microwave research. Therefore, the story goes, Soviet scientists must have discovered exotic mind effects. The U.S. investigated Soviet work. Therefore the American government must have confirmed it. PANDORA was classified. Therefore the results must have been extraordinary. The embassy exposure was hidden from employees. Therefore officials must have known it was dangerous.

Every “therefore” in that chain asks the evidence to do work it cannot do.

Classified research is not automatically successful research. A secret program can be confused, duplicated, badly designed, politically driven, inconclusive, or simply wrong. Governments classify capabilities, vulnerabilities, sources, collection methods, diplomatic embarrassments, and sometimes the fact that they do not know what is happening. The classification stamp measures sensitivity, not truth.

This principle will become increasingly important as we move toward the twenty-first century. Anomalous health incidents, the episodes commonly called Havana Syndrome, sit at the intersection of real symptoms, disputed mechanisms, incomplete attribution, directed-energy science, intelligence collection, and enormous institutional pressure. In 2020, a National Academies committee concluded that directed pulsed radio-frequency energy appeared to be the most plausible mechanism among those it considered for the distinctive early cases, while emphasizing limitations in the available information. In 2022, an Intelligence Community experts panel said pulsed electromagnetic energy could plausibly explain the core characteristics of a subset of cases. In 2023, most intelligence agencies concluded that foreign-adversary responsibility for the reported incidents was very unlikely. In January 2025, most of the Intelligence Community retained that judgment, while two components shifted somewhat because of new reporting about foreign scientific research and weapons development. The 2025 assessment still did not establish deployment of a weapon in a reported incident.

That sequence is not a contradiction to be solved by choosing the sentence one prefers. It is the subject.

Mechanism is not attribution. Capability is not use. Symptoms are not a weapon signature merely because a weapon could cause symptoms. Failure to find a foreign operation is not proof that every biological mechanism considered was impossible. A negative MRI is not a negative experience. A plausible mechanism is not an identified device. A classified adversary research program is not an attack.

These distinctions sound pedantic until a government employee wakes up unable to stand.

The language we use then becomes operational. If we call the event psychological too quickly, we may fail to measure an exposure that will never happen the same way again. If we call it an attack too quickly, we can generate diplomatic crisis, contaminate witness memory, produce reporting cascades, and force every ordinary illness into an adversarial frame. If we define a syndrome so broadly that incompatible events belong to the same bucket, the average case can erase the rare case. If we define it so narrowly that only one mechanism is allowed, the mechanism becomes true by taxonomy.

The first obligation is measurement before narrative.

Moscow teaches this in a particularly unforgiving way because the crucial measurements were taken inside a political machine. In 1967, Rusk did not approach Gromyko as a laboratory collaborator. The issue was embedded in espionage, diplomacy, hidden microphones, technical surveillance, reciprocal accusations, and a superpower relationship in which neither side could interpret an unexplained signal innocently. The same field strength means something different when measured in a university anechoic chamber than when measured through the wall of an embassy surrounded by intelligence services.

Nature does not care. Institutions do.

This book is about the gap.

It is not a book arguing that Havana Syndrome was a Russian microwave weapon. The public evidence does not establish that. It is not a book arguing that low-level electromagnetic exposure secretly explains modern chronic illness. The evidence does not support that either. It is not a book claiming that every classified directed-energy program contains hidden biological breakthroughs. That is precisely the kind of inference the record warns us against.

The narrower claim is stranger and, I think, harder to dismiss.

For more than seventy years, governments have encountered, investigated, engineered, feared, and sometimes deliberately produced biological effects using electromagnetic energy. Parts of this history are ordinary physics. Parts are military engineering. Parts are medical uncertainty. Parts remain classified. Parts became folklore because secrecy created empty spaces that certainty rushed to fill. The resulting subject cannot be understood by asking only whether “microwave weapons” are real. Some are. The useful questions are which effects, under which waveforms, at what intensities, with what geometry, through what tissue, at what distance, producing what measurable endpoint, and leaving what trace.

The Moscow Signal is where those questions first became politically expensive.

For years, people went to work inside a building receiving energy from an unknown external source. Their government knew enough to measure it, not enough to explain it, and enough to keep much of the problem secret.

That is the scene to remember. Not because it proves what came later. Because it tells us what an invisible weapons problem looks like before anybody knows the answer.
