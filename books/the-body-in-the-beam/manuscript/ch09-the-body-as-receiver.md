# The Body as Receiver

A human being is a terrible antenna and an excellent collection of accidental ones.

That sentence needs immediate repair before it becomes a slogan. The body is not a radio receiver in the ordinary engineering sense. It does not demodulate every broadcast into meaning. It is, however, matter containing water, ions, membranes, conductive paths, excitable cells, layered tissues, cavities, boundaries, and structures whose interaction with electromagnetic fields changes with frequency and geometry.

The question is never whether the body “receives energy.” It does.

The question is what the energy becomes.

At some frequencies and intensities, the result is predominantly heat. At sufficiently low frequencies and strong fields, induced currents can stimulate nerves and muscles. In the microwave auditory regime, brief heating can generate a thermoelastic pressure wave and therefore sound. At millimeter-wave frequencies used by Active Denial Technology, shallow absorption can drive an intense surface heat sensation before deeper tissue warms much. At ordinary environmental exposure levels, none of this authorizes the claim that familiar wireless devices are secretly rearranging cognition.

The transfer is conditional.

This is basic enough that specialists may find it tedious. The public debate repeatedly proves otherwise.

Take specific absorption rate, or SAR. In radio-frequency safety discussions, SAR is a way to quantify the rate at which energy is absorbed per unit mass of tissue. It is useful because the same external field does not necessarily produce the same internal absorption in different bodies or at different frequencies. But SAR is not a universal biological oracle. A metric chosen to capture heating-related exposure may not by itself describe every pulsed transient or every localized sensory endpoint. Engineers use quantities appropriate to the frequency range and mechanism under consideration, including absorbed or incident power density at higher frequencies.

Metrics are models of relevance.

The body complicates them because it is not a bag of uniform saline. Skin, fat, muscle, bone, blood, eye tissue, and neural structures have different dielectric properties. Those properties vary with frequency. Shape matters. Orientation matters. A limb is not a torso. An eye is not a thigh. The head has boundaries that can reflect or refract energy and convert a rapid thermal event into mechanical pressure.

Even the word penetration needs care. A frequency whose energy is absorbed near the surface can still produce a profound sensation because sensory receptors live near the surface. Deep penetration is not synonymous with stronger biological significance. Conversely, a field that penetrates more deeply may deposit less energy per unit volume under a given condition.

There is no hierarchy in which deeper automatically means more sinister.

This matters when claims migrate from one regime to another. Active Denial proves a shallow skin effect. It does not prove deep brain coupling. Microwave auditory perception proves a pathway from pulsed RF absorption to an internally generated acoustic wave. It does not prove direct synaptic modulation. Transcranial magnetic stimulation proves that time-varying magnetic fields can noninvasively influence neural activity when delivered with purpose-built coils at close range and appropriate field strengths. It does not prove that a distant microwave transmitter can do the same thing.

The body contains many couplings. A claim must choose one.

This is also where the word “frequency” becomes misleading. People hear that the brain has alpha, beta, theta, or gamma rhythms and then imagine that transmitting electromagnetic radiation at the same number of hertz will tune the brain like a radio. The analogy ignores what is oscillating, how the field couples to tissue, field strength, waveform, spatial distribution, and the difference between an observed neural rhythm and an externally addressable control channel.

A shared number is not a mechanism.

The same mistake appears in more sophisticated clothes when a carrier frequency and a low-frequency modulation are conflated. A microwave carrier can be amplitude-modulated at a lower rate. Whether that modulation produces a biologically meaningful effect depends on how the energy is absorbed and transduced. It is not enough to say that a modulation frequency “matches” a biological rhythm.

Biology has thresholds, filters, adaptation, noise, nonlinearities, and anatomy.

So does perception.

The auditory system is a particularly useful case because it demonstrates that the route from energy to experience can be indirect. A person hears something. The stimulus is electromagnetic. The proximate input to the cochlea may nevertheless be mechanical. The description “hearing microwaves” is phenomenologically reasonable and mechanistically incomplete.

This should make us cautious when people report pressure, vibration, heat, tingling, or sound during anomalous events. Those descriptions are evidence about experience. They do not tell us which stage of the transduction chain generated the experience. A sound could be airborne, bone-conducted, internally generated through thermoelastic expansion, produced by an electronic device, or perceived through another auditory phenomenon. Pressure could arise from acoustic energy, vestibular disturbance, muscle contraction, sinus effects, vascular changes, anxiety, or an external field under an as-yet-unspecified mechanism.

The symptom is where inquiry begins.

The body’s variability is another problem. A covert weapon is useful only if its effect is predictable enough for the operator’s purpose. A sensory threshold that varies by anatomy, orientation, prior exposure, hydration, movement, medications, hearing status, or unrelated illness makes targeting more difficult. An effect that works beautifully on a stationary subject in a calibrated chamber may become unreliable in an occupied building.

This is where engineering constraints become evidentiary constraints.

If a proposed mechanism is extremely sensitive to head orientation, then reports that remain identical while subjects walk around may count against it. If a beam must illuminate a particular anatomical region, geometry should matter. If the effect depends on line of sight, barriers should matter. If it depends on a high peak field, sensors with sufficient bandwidth should have a chance to capture it. If it produces a characteristic auditory phenomenon, carefully collected descriptions should cluster in relevant ways.

Good hypotheses make the world narrower.

Bad ones explain anything after it happens.

The body also repairs, compensates, and remembers imperfectly. A transient physiological disturbance can leave symptoms after the initial mechanism has disappeared. A vestibular insult can create prolonged disequilibrium through both tissue effects and maladaptive compensation. Pain can alter sleep; poor sleep can alter cognition; anxiety can amplify vigilance; persistent symptoms can become a network of consequences no longer requiring the original trigger.

This makes delayed investigation difficult without making the original event unreal.

It also means biomarkers have to be interpreted with humility. A scan that looks normal months later may fail to capture a transient injury. A scan that looks different may reflect preexisting variation. A blood marker can be nonspecific. A group average can hide subgroups. A small case series can exaggerate noise. A sophisticated imaging method can produce impressive pictures without a validated diagnostic signature.

The 2024 NIH studies of personnel reporting anomalous health incidents are important because they attacked this problem with unusually broad clinical and imaging evaluation. Researchers did not find a consistent MRI-detectable brain injury pattern or most other biological differences that distinguished affected participants from matched controls. The symptoms were real and often severe. The tested markers did not yield the kind of objective lesion pattern some earlier interpretations had encouraged.

That negative result should reduce confidence in claims requiring a persistent injury that those methods ought to detect.

It should not be exaggerated into proof that no external event occurred.

The authors themselves noted timing and methodological limitations. If a mechanism causes transient physiological changes that resolve before imaging, later MRI may be normal. If only a subset of heterogeneous cases shares one cause, pooling can dilute a signal. Conversely, invoking hidden transient effects every time a test is negative can make a theory unfalsifiable.

This is the edge we have to walk.

The body is both evidence and noise.

That sounds cold when discussing people who have suffered. It is not meant to be. Medicine depends on the distinction. A person can be disabled by vertigo while the cause remains uncertain. The ethical duty to treat does not wait for intelligence attribution. The scientific duty to avoid inventing a cause does not disappear because the symptoms deserve care.

This separation became difficult in the Havana debate because cause acquired moral weight. To doubt a weapon could sound like doubting the patient. To accept the patient could sound like accepting the weapon. That is a category error with human consequences.

Pain does not authenticate a theory.

A theory does not authenticate pain.

The body as receiver therefore needs two kinds of respect: physical and clinical. Physical respect means specifying fields, doses, tissue interactions, and mechanisms. Clinical respect means taking symptoms seriously without forcing them into a geopolitical story before the evidence is ready.

There is also a third kind: adversarial respect.

If a state wanted to engineer an electromagnetic effect against a person, it would not necessarily use parameters familiar from civilian exposure. Safety guidelines are designed around protection, not offense. A weapon designer can exploit high peaks, unusual pulse structures, frequencies selected for absorption, repeated short exposures, or combinations of stimuli that civilian standards were never meant to optimize. This is one reason the absence of harm in ordinary wireless environments cannot by itself settle the military-capability question.

But the adversary remains bound by physics.

Offensive parameters make detection more important, not less. If the system relies on unusual pulses, capture the pulses. If it requires high peak power, design sensors that do not average the peak away. If the beam is narrow, deploy spatially distributed detectors. If it is mobile, preserve direction-of-arrival data. If a proposed mechanism depends on acoustic byproducts, collect synchronized audio and ultrasound. If the body is the target, pair exposure measurements with immediate standardized clinical testing.

The body should not have to be the only detector in the room.

This is perhaps the most avoidable failure in the history we are tracing. The Moscow embassy had a known external signal but incomplete biological understanding. Havana produced a contested pattern of symptoms without a captured causal signal. Between those episodes, sensor technology became radically better. Yet the core evidentiary complaint remained: we lacked the right measurements at the right moment.

That is not a mystery about biology.

It is an institutional design failure.

The body is complicated. The instrument can be simpler.
