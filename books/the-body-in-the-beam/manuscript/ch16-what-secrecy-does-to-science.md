# What Secrecy Does to Science

Classification does not make a discovery true.

It does something more complicated: it changes who is allowed to be wrong about it.

Open science distributes error correction. A researcher publishes a result and people with no loyalty to the laboratory try to break it. Competitors repeat the experiment. Methods are criticized. Instruments are recalibrated. Nulls arrive. The effect shrinks, survives, changes mechanism, or dies.

Classified science can contain excellent researchers and better equipment. It can also prevent the error-correction network from forming around a subtle claim.

This is not a moral accusation. It is a structural fact.

Some work has to be secret. A country does not owe an adversary the waveform that defeats its radar, the vulnerability of a satellite sensor, or the detailed performance of a new source. Intelligence agencies cannot publish the identity of a human source so a university seminar can assess reliability. Military medicine may receive exposure information whose disclosure reveals a capability.

The cost is epistemic isolation.

Radio-frequency bioeffects are unusually sensitive to that cost because many interesting claims live near the boundary between robust effect and experimental artifact. Small behavioral changes, transient physiological responses, subjective sensations, and heterogeneous symptoms are exactly the endpoints that benefit most from independent replication.

A secret nuclear yield announces itself to instruments.

A secret five-percent change in a cognitive task can disappear into protocol choices.

PANDORA illustrates the problem. The program arose from a genuine external signal and a legitimate national-security concern. But the biological question required expertise across dosimetry, physiology, behavioral science, statistics, and engineering. Classification could protect Moscow collection details while simultaneously restricting the pool of critics able to inspect whether the simulation matched the field.

The harder the effect is to reproduce, the more secrecy can resemble preservation fluid.

An ambiguous result that would die quickly in open literature can remain institutionally alive because only a few people know the nulls. A positive report can migrate into a threat assessment while replication failures remain buried under another compartment. A program continues because the adversary might have something, and continued funding later becomes retrospective evidence that the effect must have been real.

This is how bureaucracies create fossils of uncertainty.

The opposite can happen too. A real effect can remain scientifically underdeveloped because the researchers most capable of reproducing it do not know it exists. Classification can slow civilian safety standards, medical recognition, or countermeasure design. Workers can be exposed without receiving the technical information their physicians need.

The Moscow embassy carried this tension in human form.

Officials knew of the signal before many employees understood their own exposure. Security logic favored concealment. Health logic favored disclosure. Diplomacy added another constraint because public accusation could alter superpower relations. The result was not a clean conspiracy to hide harm. It was a system in which no office owned the whole truth.

This pattern matters more than any villain.

Modern special-access systems can fragment knowledge even further. A physicist may understand the source but not the target set. A physician may know the exposure envelope but not the operational purpose. A contractor may build a subsystem without knowing the final platform. Oversight officials may see budgets and milestones without raw experiment data. Compartmentation reduces leak risk by reducing shared context.

It can also reduce shared error correction.

This is one reason alleged deeply classified programs are difficult to evaluate from testimony. A credible witness may have genuine access and still hold a local model of the system. Someone who saw unusual medical protocols can infer a human-effects weapon. Someone who saw source hardware can infer a mission. Someone who saw a threat briefing can infer adversary capability. Each may be accurate about the artifact they encountered and wrong about the larger interpretation.

Access is not omniscience.

The problem becomes severe when a program is organized around a threat whose existence is uncertain. Imagine a classified effort to defend against a suspected foreign electromagnetic effect. The program funds sensors, animal studies, protective equipment, intelligence collection, and prototype countermeasures. Decades later, the program’s existence leaks. Outsiders see millions of dollars, code names, restricted access, technical reports, and specialized hardware.

The archive looks exactly like evidence that the threat was real.

Yet the whole enterprise may have been precaution.

This is not hypothetical as an institutional pattern. Governments routinely fund defenses against low-probability threats. Missile-defense programs model adversary systems not yet deployed. Pandemic programs study pathogens that may never spread. Cyber teams build exploits against architectures they may never encounter. Nuclear emergency plans exist for scenarios everyone hopes are imaginary in practice.

Preparation is not confirmation.

Suppressed-knowledge culture gets this wrong because it treats secrecy as a one-way evidentiary multiplier. A Top Secret document feels more true than an open paper. Sometimes the opposite is rational. The open paper has survived replication and criticism; the classified memo may be one analyst’s interpretation of one source.

Classification describes damage from disclosure.

It is not a peer-review score.

There is another asymmetry. Public science publishes methods because reproducibility is a value. Intelligence reports often conceal methods because source protection is a value. This means outsiders cannot distinguish a high-confidence conclusion built from exquisite technical collection from one built from a source who later proved unreliable. The confidence language helps, but it cannot substitute for the missing provenance.

That is why declassification matters so much over time.

Old programs can be re-examined after sources and methods lose sensitivity. The Moscow Signal documents are richer now because diplomatic cables, memoranda, and program records can be placed beside open biophysics. The result is neither the old official reassurance nor the later mythology. It is a messier, more useful history.

The same process should eventually happen with anomalous health incidents. The public 2025 assessment tells us that two components saw enough new reporting about foreign research and weapons development to adjust their views. Years from now, portions of that reporting may become releasable. When they do, the scientific question should be revisited without embarrassment if the evidence changes.

Declassification should be designed for learning, not only history.

Agencies could preserve technical metadata in forms intended for eventual release even when operational details remain protected. Exposure studies could separate classified waveform parameters from publishable biological methods so civilian laboratories can reproduce the underlying effect. Independent cleared review panels could include adversarial statisticians and biophysicists not tied to the sponsoring program. Null results could be cataloged rather than disappearing into contractor archives.

This would not make secret science open.

It would make it less lonely.

There is a precedent in cryptography. For years, governments kept important cryptographic methods secret, and public researchers sometimes rediscovered ideas independently. Over time, open cryptographic science proved that algorithms exposed to sustained attack can become stronger than systems whose security depends on nobody inspecting them. The key remains secret; the algorithm can be public.

Bioeffects research may admit a similar separation. Keep operational parameters classified where necessary. Expose enough mechanism and protocol that independent researchers can test whether the biology exists.

If the effect disappears without the secret parameter, then the parameter is scientifically important and the classified community bears a larger replication burden. If the effect can be reproduced openly, medicine and protection improve.

The worst configuration is a claim that remains both consequential and permanently untestable because every relevant detail is classified.

Such a claim can govern policy without ever becoming knowledge.

This is where human oversight matters. Congressional committees, inspectors general, cleared scientific boards, and internal red teams are not decorative constitutional furniture. They are substitute peer networks when ordinary peer review is impossible. Their job is not merely to prevent illegal programs. It is to ask whether the hidden evidence is as strong as the hidden conclusion.

The questions should be embarrassingly basic.

Was the effect replicated?

Were sham exposures blinded?

Was temperature controlled?

Were nulls retained?

Were statistics specified before results?

Can another cleared laboratory reproduce the finding?

Does the operational system deliver the same exposure as the laboratory system?

What observation would make the program conclude the effect is not useful?

Secret science needs falsification more, not less.

The greatest danger is not that every classified laboratory is hiding miracles.

It is that secrecy can preserve both miracles and mistakes longer than the outside world knows which is which.
