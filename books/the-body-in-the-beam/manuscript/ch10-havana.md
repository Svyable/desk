# Havana

The first mistake was naming it before knowing what it was.

“Havana Syndrome” sounds like a diagnosis. It is a place name attached to a contested cluster of experiences that began among U.S. government personnel in Cuba in 2016 and later expanded across countries, agencies, and symptom patterns. The label was useful because people needed a way to refer to the problem. It was dangerous because labels accumulate implied mechanisms even when they are only filing systems.

Some early personnel described abrupt episodes involving unusual sounds or pressure, followed by symptoms that could include headache, dizziness, balance problems, tinnitus, visual complaints, cognitive difficulty, fatigue, and sleep disruption. Later reports varied. The U.S. government eventually adopted the less committal term anomalous health incidents, AHI.

That bureaucratic renaming was scientifically healthy.

A syndrome ordinarily implies a recognizable grouping of signs and symptoms. An incident is an event. “Anomalous” admits that the event does not fit comfortably into established explanation. None of those words tells us whether a foreign actor was present or whether energy was directed at anyone.

The distinction became hard to maintain almost immediately.

Cuba carried history. American diplomats and intelligence personnel worked inside a hostile counterintelligence environment with deep Russian connections. Acoustic descriptions invited acoustic-weapon hypotheses. The Moscow Signal sat in the institutional memory. Directed-energy research existed. Some clinicians reported findings they interpreted as unusual. Employees became sick in ways that disrupted careers and families. The event was therefore never only medical.

It became an attribution problem before it became a solved exposure problem.

This is the worst possible ordering for science.

An attribution problem asks who did it. An exposure problem asks what happened physically. If “who” enters first, every observation is filtered through adversary capability. Russia researched microwaves. Cuba has intelligence services. Therefore an external device becomes salient. Once salient, witness interviews include questions shaped by that model. Other employees learn what sensations have been reported. Security bulletins teach a phenomenology. New cases are compared to old ones that may not share a cause.

None of this requires mass suggestion. It is how classification works.

A medical category changes what clinicians ask. A security category changes what employees notice. A news story changes which experiences people recognize as reportable. A compensation law changes the institutional consequences of classification. An intelligence hypothesis changes collection priorities. These feedback loops can exist alongside genuine external attacks.

The possibility of social amplification is not an accusation that patients invented symptoms.

This point deserves repetition because the Havana debate has punished nuance. A person can experience sudden, frightening, disabling symptoms whose cause remains unknown. Another person can develop similar symptoms through a different medical pathway. A third can have an ordinary illness that becomes grouped with the first two because the category is broad. If all three are analyzed as one mechanistic syndrome, the data may become less informative as the sample grows.

Heterogeneity is not exoneration. It is a statistical problem.

The most consequential early scientific assessment came from the National Academies in 2020. The committee reviewed available clinical information and considered possible mechanisms. It concluded that directed pulsed radio-frequency energy appeared to be the most plausible mechanism among those it examined for the distinctive initial presentations, while emphasizing substantial information gaps and limitations. The report did not identify a device, an adversary, or a captured emission.

That conclusion mattered because it moved directed RF from internet speculation into a serious institutional mechanism assessment.

It also became stronger in retelling.

“The National Academies said microwave weapons caused Havana Syndrome” is not an accurate summary. The committee ranked plausibility among mechanisms using incomplete data. That is a different epistemic act from causal identification. If a doctor says one diagnosis is the leading possibility while awaiting tests, the statement is not a biopsy.

In 2022, an Intelligence Community experts panel reached another carefully bounded conclusion. It found the signs and symptoms in the cases it considered genuine and compelling, judged that a subset could not be easily explained by known environmental or medical conditions, and said pulsed electromagnetic energy, especially in the radio-frequency range, plausibly explained the core characteristics, with information gaps. Ultrasound was also considered plausible in close-access scenarios.

Again: mechanism plausibility.

Not deployment proof.

The panel’s recommendations are more important than the headlines. It called for better data, biomarkers, detectors, clinical measurements, biological-effects research, communications, and devices to aid investigation. In other words, the experts concluded that the existing evidence was not instrumented well enough.

That is the recurring sentence of this book.

By 2023, the Intelligence Community’s attribution assessment moved in a different direction. Most agencies judged it very unlikely that a foreign adversary was responsible for the reported incidents. The assessment pointed to intelligence collection, technical analysis, and medical evidence that weakened the idea of a sustained global adversary campaign. It also noted that many reported incidents could be explained by preexisting conditions, conventional illnesses, or environmental factors.

A mechanism can remain physically plausible while an adversary campaign becomes less likely.

This distinction confused nearly everyone because the public expects institutions to announce one answer. Yet it is normal Bayesian behavior. You can believe a pistol could produce a wound while concluding the person was not shot. You can believe a pathogen could cause a fever while concluding a particular outbreak had another cause. Physical possibility and historical attribution are different variables.

In January 2025, the Intelligence Community updated its judgment again. Most components still assessed foreign responsibility as very unlikely. New reporting, however, caused two components to shift somewhat on whether foreign actors might possess relevant capabilities and whether a small number of events might involve them. The public assessment said those components had low confidence in the changed judgments. The overall record remained against a broad foreign campaign.

This is not bureaucratic indecision. It is what an unresolved low-base-rate problem looks like when new intelligence arrives unevenly.

The hard question is whether the case population itself prevented resolution.

If the original Havana events had a distinctive external cause and later reporting expanded to hundreds of heterogeneous cases, aggregate analysis could wash out the signal. Imagine a rare poisoning event followed by a broad reporting category that includes migraine, viral illness, anxiety, vestibular disorders, environmental exposures, medication effects, and unrelated neurological conditions. The average patient would eventually stop resembling the original poisoned group even if the first event were real.

The opposite possibility is equally serious: investigators may have privileged the earliest cases because they fit the dramatic hypothesis better, then treated later ordinary explanations as contamination. That can preserve a false origin story indefinitely.

The only safe move is case-level evidence quality.

What exactly happened in the strongest cases? How quickly did symptoms begin? Was there a directional sensation? Did movement alter it? Were multiple people affected in the same space? What environmental data were captured? Were there contemporaneous medical findings? Were there plausible conventional explanations? Was the witness exposed to media descriptions before reporting? Was an adversary present with access and motive? Did technical collection reveal a relevant capability? Did the event leave any independent physical trace?

A thousand weak cases do not equal one strong instrument reading.

This principle matters because intelligence systems are trained to aggregate reports. Repetition can indicate pattern. But repeated reports can also be correlated through shared information, shared definitions, shared workplaces, and shared expectations. Evidentiary redundancy requires independent contact with the underlying event, not simply multiple copies of a story.

The Havana problem therefore has an information architecture hidden inside the medical one.

Employees needed warning. Warning changed awareness. Awareness changed reporting. Reporting changed the case definition. The case definition changed collection. Collection changed assessment. Assessment leaked into media. Media changed awareness again.

This does not make the phenomenon social. It makes the dataset endogenous.

Every serious investigation of rare anomalous events has this problem. UFO waves, disease clusters, product side effects, crime panics, battlefield sightings, and intelligence defectors all create feedback between the phenomenon and the system observing it. The better the reporting campaign, the more denominator and selection problems matter.

Havana added another complication: geopolitical incentives.

A foreign adversary accused of attacking diplomats has reason to deny it. A government responsible for employees has reason to demonstrate care. Intelligence agencies have reason to protect sources. Affected personnel have reason to seek recognition, treatment, and compensation. Skeptics have reputational incentives to puncture extraordinary claims. Advocates have incentives to keep neglected cases visible. Journalists have incentives to find both scandal and certainty.

These incentives do not tell us who is right.

They tell us that no party’s confidence should substitute for data.

The symptoms themselves also invite overinterpretation because neurology contains many subjective and semi-objective endpoints. Dizziness is real but nonspecific. Tinnitus is real but common. Headache is real but common. Cognitive difficulty can be disabling and arise through many pathways. Vestibular testing can find abnormalities that require careful interpretation. Imaging can produce group differences that do not necessarily identify cause.

The question is not whether the body can be hurt in these ways.

It is whether the pattern contains a fingerprint.

A fingerprint could be a distinctive waveform captured during an event. It could be a reproducible acute biomarker tightly linked to timing. It could be a unique injury pattern with a mechanism consistent with a known exposure. It could be a device recovered or observed. It could be intelligence documenting an operational program with technical parameters matching the cases. It could be a controlled reproduction of the core event under safe conditions.

Public evidence has not delivered that fingerprint.

This is why both certainty camps feel unsatisfied. The directed-energy hypothesis has enough physical plausibility and historical context to resist casual dismissal. The attribution evidence has enough negative weight to resist confident accusation. The medical literature has enough suffering to make “nothing happened” obscene and enough heterogeneity to make one syndrome questionable.

The answer may not be one answer.

Some incidents may be conventional illness. Some may be environmental. Some may be psychologically mediated without being fabricated. Some may remain unexplained. A small subset could conceivably involve an external energetic stimulus without proving a global campaign. The categories are allowed to be ugly.

Nature often is.

The policy mistake would be waiting for a single narrative before building better defenses. If an external-energy mechanism is remotely plausible and the cost of monitoring is manageable, instrument sensitive facilities. If symptoms are disabling regardless of cause, improve clinical pathways. If broad case definitions degrade analysis, preserve high-resolution subgroups. If adversaries are researching relevant technologies, collect on capabilities without telling patients that collection equals attribution.

We do not need to know who did it to learn how not to be blind next time.

Havana should have ended the era in which the first sensor is a person saying, Something just happened to my head.
