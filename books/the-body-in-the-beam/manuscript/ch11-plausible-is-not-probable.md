# Plausible Is Not Probable

The word plausible has ruined more public arguments than almost.

When the National Academies committee examined the illnesses reported by U.S. government employees, it had a problem unlike the one a laboratory scientist would choose. The committee did not control the exposure. It did not design the data collection before the events. It did not have complete clinical records for every person. It did not possess a captured waveform from the onset of a strong case. It was asked to reason backward from a limited, heterogeneous record.

That is forensic science, not experimental science.

For the distinctive early cases, the committee found directed pulsed radio-frequency energy to be the most plausible mechanism among those it considered. The conclusion was cautious and bounded. Chemicals, infectious disease, psychological and social factors, and other physical mechanisms were evaluated. The committee also emphasized limitations in access to information and the difficulty of fitting all cases into one explanation.

Then the sentence entered public life.

Plausible became likely. Likely became probably happened. Probably happened became foreign weapon. Foreign weapon became Russia. By the time the claim reached some retellings, a mechanism-ranking exercise had become proof of an attack program.

This is not unique to Havana. It is what humans do with ranked uncertainty.

A scientist says, “Of the mechanisms we evaluated, this one best fits a subset of observations.” A headline needs a subject and a verb. The conditional structure collapses.

The opposite camp performs its own collapse. Because the committee lacked direct exposure evidence and later intelligence assessments weakened attribution, critics sometimes treat the radio-frequency mechanism as discredited in physics. That does not follow either. A mechanism can be physically plausible and historically absent.

Plausibility belongs to a layer.

The first layer is physical possibility. Can electromagnetic energy under some realizable conditions produce an acute sensory or physiological effect relevant to the reports? The answer is clearly yes in broad categories. Microwave auditory perception exists. Directed millimeter-wave skin pain exists. Strong fields can heat tissue or stimulate excitable systems depending on frequency. The disputed question is not whether electromagnetic fields interact with bodies. It is whether a specific exposure can reproduce the specific event pattern.

The second layer is engineering feasibility. Can a device generate the required waveform at useful distance, through realistic geometry, with manageable power, cooling, antenna size, targeting, and concealment? A laboratory effect can fail here.

The third layer is adversary capability. Has any state or organization actually developed that device? Publications, intelligence reporting, procurement, testing, and technical collection matter here.

The fourth layer is deployment. Was such a device brought to the relevant place at the relevant time?

The fifth is event attribution. Did it cause this person’s symptoms?

Public debate routinely uses evidence from layer one to answer layer five.

The Intelligence Community experts panel in 2022 also used the language of plausibility with care. The panel concluded that signs and symptoms in a subset of cases were genuine and compelling, that some were difficult to explain through known environmental or medical conditions, and that pulsed electromagnetic energy—especially radio-frequency energy—could plausibly explain core characteristics. Ultrasound remained plausible in close-access scenarios. Several other proposed mechanisms were judged less consistent.

The panel then did something that should have dominated coverage: it asked for instruments.

Better data. Biomarkers. Detectors. Clinical measurements. Biological-effects research. Devices to aid investigation.

A group convinced it had solved the mechanism would not make the absence of measurement infrastructure central to its recommendations.

The detector recommendation is the tell. The experts were saying, in institutional language, that the next event had to be less anecdotal than the last.

This is what plausibility is for. It guides measurement.

If pulsed RF energy is plausible enough to deserve attention, a facility can deploy detectors capable of preserving relevant transient signals. If ultrasound is plausible only under close-access conditions, physical security and acoustic instrumentation can be designed accordingly. If environmental toxins remain possible in some cases, air and surface sampling protocols can improve. If conventional vestibular or neurological conditions explain other cases, early standardized clinical workup can separate them faster.

A plausible mechanism earns a test, not a verdict.

The same standard should be applied to more exotic claims. Suppose a whistleblower says an adversary uses a waveform that interacts with the vestibular apparatus through an unfamiliar mechanism. We can assign the claim to an evidence class without mocking or endorsing it. Testimony may be credible. The mechanism may be incompletely specified. The claimed program may be classified. What observable predictions follow? Does the source require a field detectable outside the body? Does orientation matter? Does the effect occur in shielded rooms? Is there a frequency window? Does it produce a consistent acute biomarker? Is there foreign technical literature or procurement pointing toward the enabling hardware?

Plausibility should become experimental pressure.

Instead it often becomes narrative comfort.

The human mind likes a mechanism that makes symptoms legible. Unexplained illness is humiliating to institutions. A foreign weapon has moral clarity. It creates an aggressor, a victim, a budget, and a response. A conventional diagnosis can feel like demotion. A multifactorial explanation can feel like abandonment.

Those feelings are politically important and scientifically useless.

The National Academies report deserves another kind of respect because it preserved uncertainty under pressure. Its committee did not have to believe that every case shared a single cause in order to identify a mechanism that fit some distinctive features. That is a better model than syndrome thinking.

Subsets are allowed.

This becomes crucial in rare-event analysis. If ten cases share abrupt onset, localized sensory phenomena, a strong directional component, immediate vestibular disruption, and close temporal clustering at one facility, while two hundred later cases involve gradual headache and fatigue across dozens of locations, combining them may destroy the signal. Conversely, carving out the first ten because they fit a favored mechanism can manufacture a subgroup.

Subgroups should therefore be defined by features specified independently of the mechanism where possible.

This is difficult. It is also the difference between discovery and storytelling.

A prospective protocol could predefine high-priority features: sudden onset within a narrow time window; spatial localization; unusual auditory or pressure sensation; immediate objective vestibular or neurological findings; corroborated co-exposure; environmental sensor event; rapid symptom change with movement; and absence of a conventional acute explanation. These features would not assume radio-frequency causation. They would identify incidents worth urgent instrumented investigation.

The protocol should also specify what lowers priority. Long delays between event and report. Symptoms beginning gradually over days. Prior exposure to detailed case narratives. Strong conventional diagnosis. No temporal or spatial localization. Contradictory accounts that evolve with questioning. None makes a person dishonest. They affect how much a case can reveal about a transient external stimulus.

Evidence quality is not moral worth.

This distinction may be the most important clinical lesson of the entire controversy. People deserve care based on need. Cases deserve causal weight based on evidence. Confusing those two creates perverse incentives in both directions. If compensation depends on proving attack, patients are pushed toward certainty. If skepticism about mechanism threatens care, investigators become ethically reluctant to narrow the hypothesis.

Separate the systems.

Treat first. Attribute separately.

The same separation protects national security. An intelligence agency should be free to conclude that a foreign attack is unlikely without implying that affected employees imagined symptoms. A medical team should be free to document disabling symptoms without implying that Russia, China, Cuba, or anyone else caused them. An engineering team should be free to investigate pulsed RF mechanisms without being accused of endorsing a geopolitical theory.

Institutional boundaries usually get blamed for fragmentation. Here we need better ones.

Plausibility also changes with time. A mechanism considered plausible in 2020 can become less or more plausible as biomedical studies, intelligence collection, source technology, and incident data change. This does not mean the earlier committee failed. Science is not a contract to keep the first ranking forever.

The 2025 intelligence update is a good example. Most agencies retained a very-low likelihood of foreign responsibility for the incident set. Yet new intelligence led two components to adjust their judgments about foreign capability and a possible role in a small number of cases. This did not restore the broad attack hypothesis. It showed that one layer of the model—capability—could move while another—overall attribution—remained largely negative.

That is how a multidimensional uncertainty should behave.

The public often interprets changing confidence as evidence of incompetence or cover-up. Sometimes it is. More often it means the underlying variables were never one variable.

Imagine a table rather than a verdict. Rows are cases. Columns are physical plausibility, engineering feasibility, adversary capability, source access, environmental detection, clinical specificity, corroboration, conventional alternatives, and intelligence linkage. Most cells are not yes or no. They carry confidence and provenance.

Now ask which rows remain unusual after the table is filled.

That is less thrilling than “Havana Syndrome solved.”

It is also how one might actually solve it.

The word plausible should never be the end of a sentence.

Plausible enough to do what?

To study? To instrument? To protect against? To accuse a country? To compensate an employee? To redesign an embassy? To fund a source-development program? To tell the public an attack occurred?

Those decisions require different thresholds.

The National Academies gave directed pulsed RF energy enough plausibility to remain in the scientific investigation. It did not give anyone permission to skip the rest of the chain.
