# Build the Detector Before the Story

The next anomalous health incident should begin with a file, not a memory.

Not a medical file. A data file.

Seconds of raw environmental measurements captured before anybody has had time to decide whether the event was a weapon, an illness, an equipment fault, or nothing at all.

That sounds less heroic than a counterintelligence operation. It is probably more useful.

The central failure running from Moscow to Havana is not that governments refused to investigate. They investigated repeatedly. The failure is that the physical event and the biological event were rarely captured together at sufficient resolution. In Moscow, there was a known radio-frequency environment but uncertain biological significance. In Havana, there were striking reported experiences but no publicly established causal waveform captured at onset.

We kept getting half the experiment.

A serious detection architecture would begin by refusing the word microwave as a specification. The electromagnetic spectrum is too broad and the possible mechanisms too different. Fixed sensors in sensitive facilities should cover frequency ranges selected from explicit threat models, with enough instantaneous bandwidth and dynamic range to preserve both ordinary background and unusual transients. Where continuous raw capture is impractical, ring buffers can retain a rolling window and write it permanently when triggered.

The trigger should not depend on one threshold.

A high peak field may matter even when time-averaged exposure remains modest. A narrowband carrier may be ordinary while an unusual pulse train is not. A directional source may be visible to one antenna and absent from another. Saturation itself can be data if the system records it rather than flattening the event into a clipped maximum.

Three cheap sensors placed badly are not redundancy.

Geometry matters.

A monitored site should have spatially separated antennas where security permits, enough to compare arrival times, amplitude, direction, and local reflection patterns. Sensors should be located near workspaces, not only at the roofline where they characterize a different field. Building materials and known emitters should be mapped. Normal radio systems need identifiers and schedules so a maintenance test does not become an incident.

The environment should have a fingerprint before anyone claims it changed.

Radio frequency is only one channel. The 2022 Intelligence Community experts panel also considered ultrasound plausible in close-access scenarios. A complete package should therefore include synchronized acoustic and ultrasonic sensing, vibration, magnetic-field channels where relevant, temperature, pressure, and ordinary environmental variables. A strange event can begin by eliminating whole mechanism classes.

Time synchronization is the unglamorous miracle.

If every sensor, security camera, access-control log, device fault, and clinical report shares trustworthy time, investigators can reconstruct an event across systems. Without synchronized clocks, a five-second RF transient and a person’s report that “it happened around three” may never meet.

The system should preserve pre-event data because people notice after onset. By the time a person presses an alarm, the relevant pulse may have ended. A sixty-second or several-minute rolling buffer could capture what happened before conscious recognition.

This is ordinary engineering borrowed from aircraft flight recorders, security systems, and network monitoring.

The harder part is deciding who owns the data.

A health detector inside an embassy will inevitably collect information about local communications and security systems. Raw RF recordings can reveal sensitive operations. Location and timing data can reveal personnel behavior. A medical trigger can expose private health information. If everything flows into one intelligence database, employees may reasonably distrust the program. If everything stays in a medical system, technical analysts may lack access to the waveform.

The architecture needs deliberate separation.

Environmental data can be stored under security controls with technical custodians. Clinical data can remain under medical privacy. A neutral incident identifier can allow blinded correlation. Investigators should be able to ask whether a sensor event occurred near symptom onset without browsing an employee’s entire health history. Clinicians should be able to request exposure characterization without learning sensitive source intelligence irrelevant to treatment.

The goal is a bridge, not a merger.

A priority incident protocol should be written before the first alarm.

The employee reports sudden onset. The system timestamps the report and freezes local sensor buffers. A trained clinician records phenomenology before offering mechanism language: sound, pressure, heat, vibration, direction, onset, movement, duration, associated symptoms. Technical staff examine the data without seeing the witness narrative initially. Security staff preserve access logs and relevant camera data. If predefined criteria are met, rapid clinical testing begins on a fixed schedule.

Blinding matters because everybody involved has a favorite story.

The technical analyst who believes in an RF mechanism can find interesting pulses in noisy spectra. The clinician who knows the patient heard a click can overinterpret an auditory measure. The patient who has read about Havana can unconsciously fit experience to the template. A protocol cannot eliminate expectation. It can keep expectation from touching every piece of evidence at once.

The system also needs negative controls from real life.

Record periods when no incidents occur. Log unusual RF events that produce no symptoms. Analyze symptom reports when the sensors show nothing abnormal. Run scheduled harmless transmitter tests unknown to the analysts scoring the data, within ethical and safety constraints. Validate that detectors can actually capture the classes of waveforms threat models predict.

Otherwise the first anomaly will be uninterpretable because nobody knows the false-positive rate.

This is where the subject can learn from astronomy. A telescope does not become credible because it sees one strange point of light. Astronomers characterize noise, instrumental artifacts, detector defects, atmospheric effects, cosmic rays, calibration sources, and survey selection. An anomaly earns attention after the instrument’s ordinary lies are understood.

Embassy sensors need the same humility.

The program should publish as much as security permits. Detector performance specifications, anonymized incident criteria, calibration methods, and negative-result statistics could be open. If a classified waveform signature is used internally, the public system can still disclose that a protected detection channel exists and that it has been independently validated.

Secret threat signatures do not require secret methodology.

A detector network also creates deterrence. A covert energetic attack is more attractive when the victim’s body is the only witness. The moment facilities can capture and geolocate emissions, the operator faces a different risk. Even if the system never identifies a weapon, adversaries have to plan around the possibility that a pulse will become diplomatic evidence.

Detection makes secrecy expensive.

This is the lesson of nuclear-test monitoring, submarine acoustics, missile warning, cyber intrusion detection, and air defense. You do not need certainty that an attack will occur before building the sensor. You need a plausible enough threat and a monitoring cost proportional to the stakes.

The AHI debate has already crossed that threshold.

Even if every historical case ultimately receives a conventional explanation, the detector infrastructure would still have value. Sensitive facilities face electronic surveillance, jamming, malicious RF activity, equipment interference, acoustic intrusion, and other environmental threats. A well-designed system can support counterintelligence and occupational safety without being dedicated to a single exotic hypothesis.

That is how speculative-risk infrastructure should be built: useful even if the strangest theory is wrong.

The same principle applies to clinical preparation. Rapid vestibular testing, hearing assessment, neurological examination, standardized symptom collection, and preserved biological samples can improve understanding of ordinary acute events too. A protocol built around the edge case can make routine care better if it is not allowed to become a diagnostic hammer.

There should be no “Havana positive” button.

The data should remain granular.

One sensor detected a transient between these frequencies with these pulse characteristics. One person reported an internal clicking sensation beginning two seconds later. A second person in the adjacent room reported nothing. The first person had an acute vestibular abnormality that resolved within twelve hours. The transmitter was geolocated to this sector with this uncertainty. A known communications test does or does not account for it.

That paragraph would be worth more than a thousand pages of retrospective argument.

The design also clarifies what cannot be hidden behind unknown technology. If a future claim proposes an effect using no detectable electromagnetic, acoustic, mechanical, chemical, radiological, or biological intermediary, then it has left the domain of the current threat model. Perhaps new physics will someday require new sensors. Until then, the investigator should resist letting “classified” become a synonym for “causally exempt.”

Physical influence creates physical relations.

The instrumentation challenge is therefore not only technical. It is cultural. Agencies have to become comfortable preserving data before they know whether it will embarrass their preferred explanation. Medical teams have to tolerate technical uncertainty. Security teams have to let scientists see enough to challenge methods. Employees have to trust that reporting symptoms will not end careers. Analysts have to record nulls.

Nulls may be the most important requirement.

A sensor network that only preserves data when someone believes an incident occurred will become a confirmation machine. It needs ordinary days, unexplained technical transients, false alarms, and clinical episodes with known conventional causes. The denominator is part of the instrument.

So is version control. Detector firmware changes. Calibration changes. Thresholds change. Algorithms improve. Every incident record should preserve which configuration was active at the time. A later analyst should be able to reconstruct not merely what the sensor saw but what the sensor was capable of seeing.

This is tedious enough to be trustworthy.

The dream in anomalous research is a revelation: a leaked document, a captured weapon, a confession, a biological signature so strange that the argument ends overnight.

Build for the less cinematic possibility.

Build a system that makes the next event ten percent less ambiguous.

Then another ten.

Science often reaches the impossible by removing ignorance in increments too boring for mythology to notice.

The body has carried this mystery long enough.

Give the room a memory.
