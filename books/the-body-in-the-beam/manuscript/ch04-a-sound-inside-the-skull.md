# A Sound Inside the Skull

In 1961, Allan H. Frey published a short technical note with a title that sounds almost dull enough to hide what it said: “Auditory System Response to Radio Frequency Energy.”

People exposed to certain pulsed radio-frequency fields could perceive sound.

No loudspeaker was required. No conventional acoustic wave had to travel through the room and enter the ear in the usual way. The perception was generated through the interaction of electromagnetic energy with the body. Later work developed a thermoelastic account: a short radio-frequency pulse deposits a tiny amount of energy, producing rapid thermal expansion and launching a pressure wave in tissue that reaches the auditory apparatus. The average heating can be small. The sensory event can still be real.

This is the kind of fact that should make a person both more open-minded and more difficult to fool.

More open-minded, because ordinary intuition is a poor guide to the ways physical energy can become perception. If someone in 1960 had said that a person could “hear microwaves” without a normal external sound source, the phrase would have been easy to mock. A phenomenon can sound occult while being an engineering problem waiting for the right measurements.

More difficult to fool, because once the mechanism is understood the dramatic phrase loses much of its freedom. Microwave hearing is not evidence that arbitrary voices can be projected into anyone’s mind at any distance under any conditions. It does not establish thought reading, behavioral control, memory implantation, or a general-purpose neurological weapon. It establishes a coupling pathway with constraints.

Constraints are where reality lives.

The effect depends on pulse characteristics, frequency, absorbed energy, geometry, and the sensitivity of the auditory system. The relevant acoustic event is generated inside tissue through rapid expansion. The mechanism implies thresholds and dose relationships. It predicts that different head dimensions and tissue properties can change the percept. It offers ways to test whether the perceived sound is consistent with the proposed exposure. It belongs to biophysics, not to a category labeled “weird things microwaves can do.”

That last category is where bad arguments go to reproduce.

When the Havana reports became public, descriptions of strange sounds naturally pulled the Frey effect into discussion. Some affected personnel described localized noises or pressure sensations associated with acute symptoms. A known radio-frequency auditory mechanism therefore seemed relevant. Relevant is the correct word. It is not the same as explanatory.

A click generated by thermoelastic expansion does not by itself explain dizziness, cognitive complaints, headache, sleep disturbance, visual symptoms, persistent imbalance, or the diversity of later cases grouped under anomalous health incidents. Any proposed external-energy explanation has to account for the whole pattern it claims to explain, including timing and variation between cases. The microwave auditory effect can establish physical plausibility for a class of sensory events without identifying the cause of a historical incident.

This is an unusually important distinction because humans overweight vivid correspondences. A witness reports a sound. Science contains a microwave-induced sound effect. The two facts snap together in the mind with a satisfying click of their own.

Causation requires more.

It requires a source capable of delivering the relevant pulses to the relevant place. It requires geometry compatible with the exposure. It requires enough energy at the target. It requires timing. It requires some account of why other people nearby did or did not experience the effect. It requires a pathway from the known sensory phenomenon to any claimed injury. Ideally it requires direct environmental measurement during an event, which is exactly the evidence that retrospective anomalous cases rarely provide.

The Frey effect therefore belongs in this book as an anchor against two kinds of laziness.

The first laziness says non-ionizing radio-frequency energy cannot produce an acute sensation except by obvious bulk heating. That is false. The auditory effect is real enough to have a literature, physical models, and experimental history.

The second laziness says because one surprising sensory effect exists, broad secret capabilities become likely. That is also false. One mechanism does not license another.

The history of science is full of discoveries that briefly create intellectual credit for their neighbors. X-rays made invisible radiation real and immediately attracted claims about every imaginable hidden ray. Radio created action at a distance and fed dreams of universal wireless influence. Nuclear physics made transmutation real and gave old alchemical intuitions a misleading afterlife. Quantum mechanics broke classical expectations and has been conscripted ever since to explain almost anything that refuses ordinary explanation.

The cure is not cynicism. It is resolution.

Name the effect precisely enough that it can fail.

Frey’s work is also a reminder that “thermal” is an inadequate insult. Public debates about electromagnetic exposure often use thermal and nonthermal as though they were rival religions. At one extreme, any effect below obvious heating is declared impossible. At the other, any subtle effect is called nonthermal and treated as evidence of unknown biology.

Thermoelastic microwave hearing complicates both camps. The energy deposition is thermal in a physically meaningful sense: a rapid temperature increase produces expansion. Yet the phenomenon does not require the kind of sustained temperature rise people imagine when they hear “heating.” A tiny, rapid thermal transient can become a mechanical wave. The biological endpoint is auditory because anatomy and timing convert the energy.

The body is a transducer.

That sentence is simple enough to become dangerous if left alone. The body transduces light into neural signals, mechanical pressure into hearing, molecules into smell, temperature into pain, acceleration into vestibular information. Electromagnetic energy outside the visible range can interact with tissue in many ways depending on frequency and power. But a transducer has a transfer function. It does not turn every input into every output.

The practical question is always the transfer function.

For radio-frequency auditory perception, what pulse energy and width produce a detectable acoustic pressure wave? How does frequency change absorption? Where is the wave generated? What is the threshold relative to exposure limits? What does a subject actually hear? What other sensations appear? Can shielding interrupt it? Can a microphone placed outside the head detect the same sound? Can hydrophones or other sensors measure the internally generated pressure wave in models? How does the effect scale?

When these questions are answered, mystery shrinks into parameters.

Intelligence work has the opposite temptation. Parameters can be classified, incomplete, or deliberately obscured. An analyst may know that an adversary published research on pulsed radio-frequency effects but not whether the laboratory achieved useful range. A technical collection report may indicate a device exists without revealing its purpose. A human source may describe a weapon using words chosen for a nontechnical audience. The analyst must decide how much weight to give a capability whose mechanism is partly known and whose implementation is hidden.

That is where established effects like microwave hearing can mislead even competent people. They reduce the prior improbability of a narrow claim—radio-frequency energy can indeed create an auditory percept—but can be overextended into confidence about a specific adversary device.

The same problem appears in patents. A patent can describe a technique involving radio-frequency-induced sound or neural effects. The patent proves that somebody filed a claim to an invention. It does not prove that the invention works as described, was built, reached useful range, entered government service, or caused a reported event. Patent literature is an index of ideas, not an arsenal inventory.

The same is true of military research solicitations. Funding proves interest. A prototype proves more. Controlled human testing proves more again. Fielding proves another level. Operational attribution is still separate.

This evidence ladder is tedious. That is why it works.

There are openly acknowledged directed-energy systems whose human effect is far less mysterious than the Frey effect. Active Denial Technology uses millimeter-wave energy around 95 gigahertz to deposit energy very shallowly in the skin, producing an intense heating sensation that prompts rapid movement away from the beam. The U.S. military has publicly demonstrated and described the system. We do not have to infer its existence from witnesses. We can discuss frequency, intended effect, testing, range claims, and safety engineering.

Place Active Denial beside microwave hearing and a useful spectrum appears.

At one end is an overt system designed to produce pain through shallow heating. In the middle is an established radio-frequency auditory phenomenon with a relatively well-developed physical mechanism. Further out are disputed claims about neurological injury from remote pulsed energy at unknown parameters. Beyond those are claims of behavioral control, thought manipulation, or other capabilities for which public evidence is far weaker.

All use electromagnetic language. They do not occupy the same evidentiary ground.

This is what a mature field would make visible.

Instead, public conversation tends to split into two tribes. One treats every unusual electromagnetic claim as proof that secret weapons have outrun public science by fifty years. The other treats any association with conspiratorial claims as a reason to avoid the underlying physics. Both abandon territory.

The Frey effect sits on that abandoned ground waving a small flag.

It says: an effect that violates naive intuition can be real. It also says: once real, the effect becomes narrower, not broader.

There is a human lesson here that reaches beyond radio-frequency biology. We are bad at living beside phenomena during the interval between impossible and ordinary. Before explanation, a strange effect attracts mythology. After explanation, we retroactively decide it was never strange. The interesting moment disappears.

Imagine being one of Frey’s early subjects without knowing the mechanism. You stand in a room. The apparatus emits no ordinary sound you can identify. Then something clicks inside your head. You move; the perception changes. The sensation appears tied to equipment that people around you describe in the language of microwave radiation.

Your report would sound unreliable before the paper existed.

Afterward it would sound obvious.

The experience did not change. The available model did.

This matters when evaluating witnesses to anomalous events. A witness can accurately report a perception while being wrong about its cause. A witness can also report a real effect before the mechanism is accepted. These are not contradictory possibilities. Testimony is strongest about experience and weaker as it climbs toward mechanism, source, and intention.

“I heard a clicking sound that seemed to come from inside my head” is one claim.

“A microwave weapon caused neurological injury” contains several more.

The first may be true even when the last is false.

Investigators should preserve that granularity. Record the sensation before supplying vocabulary. Record where the person stood, which direction they faced, whether covering the ears changed it, whether movement altered the perception, whether another person could hear anything, what electronics were present, and the exact timing relative to other symptoms. Do not teach the witness a mechanism before collecting the phenomenology. Memory is not a spectrum analyzer, but it can be contaminated by one.

The phrase “Havana Syndrome” itself became a contaminant once it acquired a public template. A label helps coordinate care, but it can also cause heterogeneous experiences to converge in description. This does not mean symptoms are imagined. It means classification changes reporting. The same thing happens in medicine whenever a new diagnosis gains salience.

If a future external-energy event occurs, the most valuable evidence may therefore be boring: a timestamped waveform captured by a sensor nobody is emotionally invested in.

Frey gave us a known effect. The lesson is not to turn it into a universal explanation.

The lesson is to build the instrument that would tell us when it happened.
