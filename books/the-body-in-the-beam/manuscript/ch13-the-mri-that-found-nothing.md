# The MRI That Found Nothing

In March 2024, the National Institutes of Health published the kind of result that anomalous subjects need more often: a sophisticated study that did not find the thing people had been looking for.

Researchers evaluated more than eighty U.S. government personnel and adult family members who had reported anomalous health incidents. The work included advanced MRI, clinical assessments, auditory and vestibular testing, neuropsychological measures, and biomarkers. Compared with matched controls, the investigators did not find a consistent pattern of MRI-detectable brain injury or most other biological abnormalities that could explain the reported symptoms.

The symptoms were not dismissed. The lesion was not found.

Those two sentences should be allowed to stand together.

Instead, negative medical findings tend to be recruited immediately. Skeptics say the scans prove there was no attack. Advocates say the absence proves the weapon is subtle enough to escape imaging. Both answers make the test less useful than it was.

A negative result updates a model according to what the test could reasonably have detected.

MRI is not a time machine. Participants were often scanned after the acute event, sometimes substantially later. A transient physiological disturbance could resolve without leaving the sort of structural or functional difference the study was powered to identify. Heterogeneous causes could average away. Individual preexisting variation can complicate group comparisons. The NIH team acknowledged limitations like these.

But limits are not permission slips.

If a proposed theory requires persistent structural brain injury, a high-quality study failing to find a consistent injury pattern should hurt that theory. If advocates respond by shifting to a transient injury that leaves no trace, the revised theory owes a mechanism explaining persistent symptoms. If they shift again to a functional disturbance below current detection, the theory becomes harder to falsify and should lose confidence unless independent evidence improves.

This is how evidence is supposed to work. The target moves only when the evidence buys the movement.

The 2024 NIH studies are particularly important because earlier discussion had been influenced by imaging findings from smaller groups that some interpreted as evidence of brain injury. Neuroimaging is seductive. A color map of statistical differences looks like a photograph of causation even when it is nothing of the kind. Group-level differences can arise from analysis choices, small samples, scanner variation, multiple comparisons, selection, or real but nonspecific biological variation.

Replicability is the antidote.

The NIH work used multiple imaging methods and analytical approaches and reported highly reproducible imaging metrics within its own framework. What it did not find was a reliable signature separating affected participants from controls in a way that established an injury pattern.

This does not make earlier investigators foolish. Early case series often operate under emergency conditions. They are good at generating hypotheses and poor at ending them. The mistake occurs when provisional findings acquire forensic certainty before larger, more controlled work arrives.

Havana moved faster than medicine normally likes.

A diplomatic crisis does not wait for replication.

The same time pressure affects patient experience. Imagine developing severe dizziness, head pressure, tinnitus, visual difficulty, and cognitive problems while assigned overseas. You are told the event may have national-security significance. Specialists examine you. News stories appear. Coworkers report similar experiences. Years later, a large study says it cannot identify a consistent objective brain abnormality.

The result may feel like erasure even when the researchers explicitly say the symptoms are real.

Medicine has trained patients to equate objective findings with legitimacy. A scan shows the thing; therefore the thing exists. Yet many disabling conditions do not produce a clean imaging marker. Migraine can be real without a lesion. Vestibular disorders can be difficult to capture between episodes. Pain can be severe without a structural abnormality visible on routine imaging. Functional neurological symptoms are not imaginary merely because their mechanism differs from a focal injury.

The ethical response to a negative scan is not disbelief.

It is diagnostic humility.

For intelligence, however, the same scan has another meaning. If a hypothesized weapon is supposed to produce lasting tissue damage of a kind MRI ought to detect, the absence matters operationally. It narrows the mechanism. A weapon program is an engineering claim. Engineering claims need endpoints.

This is where the clinical and intelligence questions diverge again.

A patient asks: why am I still sick?

An analyst asks: does the illness pattern support a foreign capability?

The same MRI can be weak evidence for the first question and stronger evidence against one version of the second.

Conflating them damages both.

The NIH studies also expose the importance of baseline data. Most people do not have extensive pre-incident neuroimaging, vestibular testing, cognitive batteries, and blood biomarkers sitting in a secure archive. After an event, investigators compare the person with controls or population norms. That is useful but not the same as knowing what changed in that individual.

Sensitive occupations could do better without turning employment into perpetual medical surveillance.

A voluntary, privacy-protected baseline protocol for personnel assigned to high-risk posts might include standardized hearing, vestibular, vision, neurological, and selected cognitive measures, with strict separation between clinical care and performance evaluation. The purpose would not be to search for hidden disease. It would create a before-state for rare acute events.

The design would be ethically difficult. Baseline testing can produce incidental findings. Employees may fear career consequences. Intelligence agencies have poor histories when medical data and operational interests mix. Data retention creates privacy risks. A program intended to protect people can become coercive if assignments depend on participation.

These objections are not reasons to give up. They are design constraints.

The same is true for environmental baselines. If a building’s normal radio-frequency spectrum is unknown, investigators cannot easily decide whether a later recording is anomalous. A secure facility has changing transmitters, access points, radios, electronics, and external sources. Baseline characterization would allow future transients to be compared against actual local conditions rather than a generic safety table.

The human and environmental baselines belong on the same clock.

This is the future study the NIH result points toward. Not a larger retrospective argument about whether scans are normal. A prospective architecture capable of capturing exposure, immediate physiology, and later recovery from the same timestamped event.

Imagine a priority incident at a monitored facility. A wearable or room sensor triggers on an unusual pulsed RF event. The system preserves raw data from seconds before and after the trigger. Acoustic and ultrasonic channels capture simultaneously. The affected employee receives standardized clinical testing within hours, not months. Blood samples are taken according to a predefined research protocol. Vestibular and auditory testing follows. Imaging occurs on a schedule selected before anyone knows the result. Investigators compare the person with their own baseline and matched controls. Technical teams analyze the field without being told the clinical outcome. Clinicians review the patient without being told whether the RF detector triggered until initial observations are recorded.

Now the evidence can cross-examine itself.

If no signal appears, the external-RF hypothesis loses weight for that incident. If a signal appears without symptoms in anyone present, the proposed bioeffect may weaken. If a distinctive signal repeatedly coincides with acute symptoms and objective changes under blind analysis, the field changes overnight.

The key is prospectivity.

Retrospective anomaly research suffers from a cruel asymmetry. Positive anecdotes are durable. Missing measurements are permanent. Each new retelling adds narrative detail while the physical scene recedes.

The NIH studies did what retrospective medicine could reasonably do with a difficult cohort. Their negative findings should be taken seriously. They are not the final word because no retrospective study can recreate the initiating event.

That limitation is itself a finding.

We have spent years scanning bodies for a signal that may have existed for milliseconds in a room.

Next time, scan the room too.
