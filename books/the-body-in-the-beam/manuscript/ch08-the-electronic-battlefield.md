# The Electronic Battlefield

The easiest target for a microwave weapon is not a brain. It is a circuit.

Modern war has made this obvious enough that the phrase high-power microwave no longer belongs only to speculative literature. Directed-energy programs openly pursue ways to disrupt electronics, defeat drones, interfere with sensors, and attack systems whose usefulness depends on semiconductors behaving inside narrow electrical tolerances.

Electronics are vulnerable for a simple reason: we built them to respond to small electrical signals.

A biological organism is wet, heterogeneous, adaptive, and annoyingly variable. A circuit board contains conductors, junctions, traces, antennas both intended and accidental, clock lines, protection components, and logic thresholds. Couple a sufficiently strong transient into the right path and the machine can misbehave long before any person nearby experiences a dramatic biological effect.

This is why a discussion of “microwave weapons” that begins with Havana Syndrome is historically upside down. The military importance of radio-frequency energy is already enormous without any covert neurological mechanism. Radar, communications, jamming, electronic attack, electromagnetic pulse effects, counter-drone systems, and high-power microwave research all live inside a mature strategic domain.

The spectrum is contested terrain.

The human body entered that terrain long before anyone coined anomalous health incidents. Personnel work beside transmitters, radar arrays, communications systems, electronic-warfare equipment, and industrial RF sources. Militaries therefore have practical reasons to understand exposure even when the intended target is hardware. A source powerful enough to disrupt electronics can create an occupational-safety problem. A system designed to affect people can create electromagnetic-compatibility problems. The engineering worlds touch.

They are still not interchangeable.

A high-power microwave system designed to upset a drone may exploit coupling through antennas and electronics. A biological target has no USB port. Energy reaches tissue according to dielectric properties, geometry, and frequency. To claim a biological weapon by pointing at an electronic-attack capability is like proving poison gas by displaying a solvent. Both involve chemistry; the causal chain is missing.

This matters especially when discussing China and Russia because public defense reporting confirms serious investment in directed-energy and electronic-warfare capabilities. The U.S. Department of Defense has repeatedly described Chinese work in directed energy for counterspace and other military purposes. Recent public assessments continue to discuss Chinese laser and electromagnetic capabilities, while Russia has long maintained sophisticated electronic-warfare systems and a history of research in high-power sources.

These are real strategic facts.

They create a prior that sophisticated states are interested in manipulating the electromagnetic environment. They do not tell us that a hotel-room neurological weapon exists.

The gap between those propositions is where intelligence analysis earns its salary.

Suppose an analyst receives reporting that a foreign laboratory has developed a compact high-power microwave source. That is important. The same source technology might support radar, electronic attack, counter-drone use, accelerator research, or a biological experiment. The source is a component. Capability appears only when the source is integrated with beam control, target acquisition, power, cooling, platform, and an effect that survives range.

The target determines the rest of the machine.

Counter-electronics weapons can sometimes be tested with satisfying brutality. Put the target system in a controlled environment, expose it, and see whether it resets, corrupts, degrades, or dies. Measure the field. Repeat. Open the box. Identify the failed component. Improve shielding. Test again.

Human effects are harder because the desired endpoint may be subjective, variable, ethically constrained, or slow to appear. If the endpoint is pain at the skin, Active Denial gives a tractable case. If it is an auditory click, the Frey effect gives another. If the endpoint is persistent dizziness or cognitive dysfunction after a brief remote exposure, the chain becomes longer and each link needs evidence.

This asymmetry has a strategic consequence: militaries may become very advanced in electromagnetic source technology without having equally advanced understanding of subtle human bioeffects.

Technology does not progress as one front.

A country might know how to build a compact pulsed source before it knows what waveform would produce a particular neurological outcome. It might discover an effect accidentally during safety testing. It might fund biological programs because the source exists, not because the biology is mature. Conversely, biomedical researchers might observe an effect that cannot be operationalized because the required exposure is impractical outside a laboratory.

This is why “they have directed-energy weapons” is the beginning of a question.

The electronic battlefield also teaches us about signatures. A weapon that throws a physical projectile leaves obvious evidence. A weapon that couples energy into electronics may leave failures that look like ordinary faults unless the timing and pattern are recognized. A device reboots. A camera glitches. A communications link drops. A sensor saturates. A power supply fails. The forensic clue may be distributed across machines rather than lodged in one piece of tissue.

If an external energetic event affects both people and electronics, the electronics may be better witnesses.

That possibility is underused in public discussion of anomalous health incidents. Investigators understandably focus on medical symptoms, but a physical exposure strong enough to produce a biological effect may also interact with nearby systems depending on frequency and field strength. Did phones behave unusually? Did wireless links drop? Did security cameras record artifacts? Did access-control systems log faults? Did a spectrum monitor see a transient? Did a building-management sensor capture anything? Were clocks synchronized well enough to compare records?

Most ordinary electronic anomalies are ordinary. The point is not to turn every frozen laptop into evidence of attack. The point is to treat machines as environmental sensors when the mechanism under consideration is electromagnetic.

The best evidence may already exist accidentally.

A modern embassy is a forest of receivers. Wi-Fi access points, cellular equipment, radios, satellite terminals, security systems, electronic locks, building controls, laptops, phones, spectrum-management devices, and counter-surveillance equipment all interact with the electromagnetic environment. Some log data. Some do not. Some are designed to detect interference. Some may saturate before providing useful information. A deliberate monitoring architecture could turn this accidental sensor network into a forensic one.

The engineering challenge is not trivial. High-power transients can be brief. Sensors have limited dynamic range. A detector may miss a frequency outside its band. A close source can create near-field conditions poorly represented by simple far-field power-density assumptions. Shielding can protect occupants while also complicating source localization. Capturing raw high-bandwidth data continuously is expensive. Security requirements make cloud logging unattractive. False alarms can be operationally disruptive.

But these are design problems, not mysteries.

The electronic-warfare community already knows how to think about uncertain emitters. It uses direction finding, time difference of arrival, spectral fingerprints, modulation recognition, geolocation, signal libraries, and distributed sensors. A health-protection system does not need the full machinery of a national signals-intelligence platform, but it can borrow the logic.

Detect first. Characterize second. Attribute last.

That ordering is almost the reverse of public rumor.

A person experiences a strange event. Social or organizational context supplies an adversary. The adversary supplies a rumored technology. The rumored technology supplies a mechanism. Then evidence is searched for backward.

Signals intelligence tries, at its best, to begin with the emission.

What is there?

This matters because hostile activity can be real without being the hostile activity you expected. The Moscow Signal may have been connected to surveillance rather than health effects. A modern anomalous emission around a sensitive building might be part of electronic intelligence, communications interception, device exploitation, jamming, or counter-surveillance. A person’s symptoms could be incidental, unrelated, or caused by a secondary effect.

If investigators begin with “weapon against the body,” they may miss the operation against the building.

This is one of the book’s broader propositions: the most interesting anomalous events often sit at category boundaries created by our institutions rather than by nature. Medicine asks whether a person was injured. Counterintelligence asks whether an adversary acted. Electronic warfare asks what signal was present. Occupational safety asks whether an exposure exceeded limits. Diplomacy asks whether responsibility can be assigned. Each office sees a different target.

The field itself does not care which office owns the case.

The problem becomes even sharper with dual-use technology. A microwave source can be a research instrument, communications component, radar element, industrial heater, electronic-attack device, or part of a human-effects system. Export controls and intelligence collection may focus on components whose end use is ambiguous. A foreign program can hide inside ordinary technical development because the same advances—solid-state sources, phased arrays, high-speed switching, power electronics, thermal management—serve many purposes.

This is where genuine secrecy becomes possible without requiring a giant black hangar.

The enabling technologies are public enough to have civilian markets. The integration is what matters.

That is also why capability assessments should resist the fantasy that a single leaked document will resolve everything. The truth may be distributed across mundane records: a power-module procurement, a biological test contract, an antenna patent, a medical safety protocol, an unusual facility, a budget line, a doctrine paper, a personnel transfer, a countermeasure solicitation. None proves the system. Together they can outline one.

Intelligence analysts call this all-source work. Historians call it archives. Scientists call it triangulation when they remember to.

The electronic battlefield gives us another useful constraint: adversaries defend themselves too.

If Russia, China, or the United States believes radio-frequency systems can threaten personnel or equipment, one should expect countermeasures. Shielding, detection, exposure standards, hardening, operational spacing, waveform management, medical monitoring, and doctrine become part of the ecology. Sometimes the defensive system is easier to observe than the offensive one.

A secret weapon creates public shadows.

The stronger the claimed capability, the larger the shadows should become.

That principle will matter when we return to Havana. By then the question will not be whether high-power microwave technology exists. It does. The question will be whether the specific pattern of reported events fits any capability strongly enough to survive the competing evidence.

The circuit board is the easy target.

The human case is where certainty becomes expensive.
