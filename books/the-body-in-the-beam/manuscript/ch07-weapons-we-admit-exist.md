# Weapons We Admit Exist

A useful antidote to speculation is a weapon with a brochure.

The Active Denial System has been publicly demonstrated by the United States for years. Its operating principle is not hidden behind anonymous witnesses or leaked acronyms. A directed beam around 95 gigahertz deposits energy very shallowly in exposed skin and produces a rapid, intolerable heating sensation. The person moves away. The intended military value lies precisely in creating a strong behavioral effect at range while reducing the likelihood of lasting injury compared with lethal force.

There is something clarifying about an invisible beam whose purpose the government openly explains.

It shows what directed energy looks like after the mystery has been engineered out.

There is a source. There is a frequency regime. There is an aperture. There is a target. There is an absorption depth. There is a physiological endpoint. There are human-effects tests. There are operating procedures, safety limits, failure modes, range considerations, and tactical arguments about when the system is useful. One can disagree about ethics, crowd-control doctrine, injury risk, or deployment without having to decide whether physics allows the basic effect.

The weapon is real because the chain is complete.

That completeness matters when discussing less visible claims. A known directed-energy weapon does not prove an unknown one, but it teaches us what evidence an unknown one eventually has to acquire.

Active Denial also corrects a persistent error about remote biological effects. Range does not require magic. It requires enough energy to arrive at the target, a beam geometry that keeps it concentrated, and a tissue interaction that produces the desired endpoint efficiently enough. In this case, shallow absorption is a feature. The system is not trying to reach the brain. It is trying to activate skin pain pathways.

That makes the engineering easier in one sense and harder in another. Easier because the target is at the surface. Harder because the effect has to be strong enough to compel movement without producing unacceptable burns under operational variation. Exposure time, beam control, clothing, weather, movement, target vulnerability, reflections, and operator discipline all matter.

A weapon is not a physics paper with wheels.

Military utility appears only after the effect survives logistics.

The history of Active Denial is therefore more instructive than the usual public summary. The system spent years in development and testing. It was publicly demonstrated, evaluated, miniaturized in solid-state variants, and discussed as a nonlethal option. Yet the existence of a working effect did not automatically produce universal fielding. Tactical usefulness competes with size, cost, power, rules of engagement, perception, countermeasures, weather, platform integration, and the fact that an adversary may respond with lethal force while being repelled nonlethally.

This is a general rule for secret-weapon claims: if the effect is real, ask why it is useful.

A laboratory capability can die between the bench and the battlefield.

High-power microwave systems aimed at electronics illustrate another branch of the same family. Here the desired endpoint may be disruption, upset, degradation, or damage in electronic systems rather than sensation in a person. The coupling problem changes. Cable runs, apertures, shielding, circuit vulnerabilities, antenna structures, and field strength become central. A high-power pulse may be devastating to an inadequately protected electronic target while producing no exotic biological effect at all.

Again, “microwave weapon” is too coarse.

The phrase can refer to a crowd-control system that heats skin, a high-power source intended to upset electronics, a laboratory apparatus studying auditory perception, a radar operating at high peak power, or a hypothetical covert system designed around a specific biological effect. A person who says microwave weapons are real is correct in the way a person who says chemical weapons are real is correct. The statement tells you almost nothing about whether a particular chemical in a particular concentration caused a particular illness.

Mechanism is the missing noun.

The public existence of Active Denial also gives us a useful bound on secrecy. Governments can keep some details classified while acknowledging a capability. They can reveal a system’s broad physical principle without publishing every engineering parameter. They can conduct human-effects research because safety and doctrine demand it. The program generates a visible institutional trail.

A truly covert anti-personnel directed-energy system would generate less public material by design. But it would still have to solve analogous problems: source generation, beam formation, power, thermal management, human-effects characterization, target access, safety for operators, discrimination, and countermeasures.

The more ambitious the claimed effect, the larger this hidden engineering burden becomes.

Suppose the goal is not pain at the skin but a reproducible vestibular or neurological effect without obvious tissue heating. The source must couple energy into the relevant structure at a dose and waveform that reliably produces the endpoint. If the mechanism is narrow, individual anatomy may matter. If the target is behind walls, propagation through construction materials matters. If the device must be covert, the antenna and power system must be concealed. If the event is to look like illness, the operation must avoid sensors and bystanders. If the effect is meant to be persistent, the biological pathway must explain persistence.

None of these requirements is impossible merely because it is difficult. Together they form a signature.

This is why the 2025 Intelligence Community assessment on anomalous health incidents is more interesting than a yes-or-no headline. Most agencies continued to judge foreign-adversary responsibility very unlikely. At the same time, two components changed part of their assessment because reporting suggested foreign actors were making progress in scientific research and weapons development relevant to potential causal mechanisms. Even those components did not conclude that such a weapon had been deployed in reported incidents.

Capability and attribution moved independently.

That is exactly how they should move.

An adversary can develop a class of technology without using it against the people under investigation. A mechanism can become more technically plausible while the evidence for a specific attack weakens. Conversely, failure to identify a known adversary program does not prove the mechanism physically impossible.

Public argument hates this because it cannot be printed on a bumper sticker.

The military already lives there.

Every weapons office understands technology readiness levels even if it uses different language in practice. An effect in a paper is not a component. A component is not a prototype. A prototype is not a system. A system is not a fielded capability. A fielded capability is not evidence of use in a particular event. Each step can fail for reasons that have nothing to do with the underlying science.

The active-denial story is a rare opportunity to see the ladder from the outside.

It also shows how language changes once a system becomes legitimate. Nobody needs to call the skin effect “mysterious bioenergy.” The Army can describe a 90–95 gigahertz prototype and its intended repel effect in an ordinary portfolio book. The vocabulary is frequency, target, effect, integration, operator, range, and safety. Strangeness evaporates under procurement.

This suggests a rule for reading leaked or declassified descriptions of more exotic programs. The closer a document is to actual engineering, the more physical nouns it should contain.

A proposal full of “cognitive influence,” “neurological disruption,” “novel field effects,” or “behavioral modification” without dose, source, waveform, distance, endpoint, or apparatus may be an idea trying to become a program. A test plan with calibrated instruments, exposure geometry, sham conditions, thermal measurements, and predefined physiological endpoints is closer to reality. A maintenance manual is better still.

Paper has technological depth.

This is one reason procurement records are often more valuable than visionary memoranda. Dreams use adjectives. Machines require part numbers.

The same principle applies to foreign systems. Public U.S. defense assessments describe China and Russia pursuing directed-energy capabilities, especially for counterspace and electronic-warfare missions. Those assessments matter. They indicate state-level investment in source technology, beam control, lasers, high-power microwaves, jamming, and related systems. They do not automatically validate every report of anti-personnel energy attacks.

A country that can damage a satellite sensor is not necessarily able to cause a covert vestibular injury through a hotel wall. The engineering problems overlap in some components and diverge in others.

This is a particularly important correction in discussions of China. The country’s published technical literature is enormous, its defense research ecosystem is broad, and open-source analysts can find papers on directed energy, bioelectromagnetics, neural interfaces, high-power microwaves, and electronic warfare. The sheer number of papers can feel like a capability proof. It is not. A large scientific system explores many things that never become weapons.

The United States does the same.

One can find American patents, military solicitations, laboratory studies, and program documents describing concepts that were never operationally successful. If we judged U.S. capability by everything an American engineer proposed, the Pentagon would already possess half the technologies in science fiction.

The correct comparison is ecosystem to ecosystem, evidence class to evidence class.

Active Denial belongs in the documented fielded-or-prototyped human-effects class. The microwave auditory effect belongs in the established biological-effect class. High-power microwave electronic attack belongs in the established military-technology class. Claims of covert neurological weapons belong elsewhere until a comparable evidentiary chain appears.

Keeping the boxes separate does not make the last box empty.

It makes it inspectable.

There is another uncomfortable lesson in the overt weapon: a capability can be ethically troubling without being medically mysterious. Directed pain at range raises questions about consent, crowd control, escalation, misuse, accountability, vulnerable populations, and what happens when a supposedly nonlethal system is operated outside its intended envelope. Those questions do not require speculative brain effects.

Technology does not become morally simple once the physics is known.

Nor does it become strategically simple. An invisible beam can be hard for a target to attribute in the moment even when the system itself is publicly known. People feel pain and move. They may not see what touched them. This perceptual asymmetry is part of directed energy’s appeal. Bullets announce direction through damage and sound. A field can act without carrying an obvious object into the body.

That feature is real enough to deserve sober attention.

It is also why extravagant claims find such fertile ground here. Invisible causes are cognitively generous. They can be placed anywhere the evidence is thin.

The defense is not disbelief.

It is demanding the same engineering depth from the weapon we cannot see as from the one we can.
