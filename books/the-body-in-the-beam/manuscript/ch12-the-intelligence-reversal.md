# The Intelligence Reversal

In intelligence work, a negative judgment is not the same thing as an empty file.

The 2023 U.S. Intelligence Community assessment on anomalous health incidents is a useful example. Most agencies concluded that it was very unlikely a foreign adversary was responsible for the reported incidents. That conclusion came after years of collection and investigation, not before them. The government had looked for links between cases and foreign actors, examined technical feasibility, revisited medical assumptions, and searched for evidence of a sustained campaign.

The broad adversary hypothesis weakened.

For people who had spent years believing they were attacked, the assessment could feel like abandonment. For people who had always regarded the weapon theory as absurd, it looked like vindication. Both reactions were too simple.

An intelligence assessment is a judgment about the best explanation given available collection, not a metaphysical ruling about what technology can exist.

The 2023 assessment made several things harder for the attack hypothesis. Investigators did not find the pattern of adversary activity expected from a global campaign affecting hundreds of U.S. personnel. Agencies found no consistent intelligence linking a foreign actor to the broad incident set. Medical and technical analysis weakened some assumptions that had shaped early understanding. Many incidents appeared explainable through ordinary medical, environmental, or technical factors.

Those are consequential negatives.

They do not mean every case was solved. They mean the proposed campaign had acquired a serious burden it was not meeting.

This is where hidden-weapon reasoning usually escapes. Lack of evidence becomes evidence of sophistication. No detected operators? They were elite. No captured device? It was compact. No emissions? The waveform evaded sensors. No common injury? The weapon was tunable. No intelligence reporting? The program was deeply compartmented. No repeated pattern? Deliberate deception.

A sufficiently secret adversary can explain any absence.

That is exactly why intelligence tradecraft requires alternatives that can lose.

A covert program can evade collection. History contains plenty of surprises. The United States missed major aspects of Soviet and Chinese programs before. Compartmented development can keep large numbers of people ignorant of a project’s purpose. Deception can work. Sources can be wrong. Sensors can be in the wrong place.

But concealment has a cost curve.

The more events attributed to a program, across more countries, over more years, involving more operators, more target access, more equipment, and more repeated exposure, the harder it becomes to explain complete collection failure. A small number of operations by a mature service can hide. A worldwide campaign against hundreds of government employees is a different logistical object.

The 2023 judgment drew strength from that scale mismatch.

Then January 2025 complicated the story.

The updated Intelligence Community assessment retained the central conclusion: most components continued to judge foreign responsibility very unlikely. But two components shifted somewhat because new reporting suggested foreign actors were making progress in scientific research and weapons development relevant to possible causal mechanisms. One of those components judged it possible that a foreign actor might have played a role in a small number of events; the public assessment described low confidence around the shifted views. The update still did not conclude that a foreign state had deployed an AHI-causing weapon.

This is what an honest unresolved assessment looks like.

The broad case can weaken while a narrow tail becomes more interesting.

That structure should sound familiar from statistics. Most observations come from ordinary distributions. A few outliers remain. The mistake is either to force the outliers into the average or to let the outliers redefine the whole population.

The 2025 assessment implicitly resisted both.

It is worth noticing what changed the judgments: reporting about capability, not public discovery of a device at an incident scene. That moves one layer of the model. If foreign actors are making progress in relevant technology, the prior probability of some mechanism rises. It does not identify an operation.

This is an important lesson for every classified-adjacent claim in this project. New evidence has to be attached to the proposition it actually supports.

A foreign laboratory researching pulsed RF bioeffects supports interest and perhaps capability development. A procurement supports hardware acquisition. A test site supports experimentation. Doctrine supports contemplated use. A source reporting deployment supports an operation, with source reliability caveats. A waveform captured at the scene supports exposure. A clinical signature supports biological consequence.

Do not let one row fill the table.

The word “intelligence” itself creates problems because outsiders imagine a unified hidden view. There is no single mind behind the classification curtain. Agencies have different sources, technical competencies, missions, and confidence thresholds. An assessment is coordinated precisely because the components may disagree.

Those disagreements are data.

If one component with a particular collection portfolio shifts while others do not, the difference may reflect access to a source, a distinct technical interpretation, or a different weighting of evidence. The public version cannot always explain which. That opacity is frustrating, but it should not be replaced by invented certainty.

This is one place where suppressed-knowledge culture has a legitimate complaint and a bad solution.

The legitimate complaint: citizens cannot independently audit classified evidence that materially shapes official conclusions. “Trust us” is not scientific replication. The public receives confidence language without the underlying collection. Intelligence agencies sometimes have histories of deception, politicization, overconfidence, and failure. Skepticism is warranted.

The bad solution: assume the hidden evidence says whichever extraordinary thing the public report does not.

Secrecy creates an unknown, not a confession.

A disciplined reader can do more. Compare what changed across assessments. Look at which judgments are unanimous and which are split. Track confidence levels. Separate mechanism from actor. Examine what public technical evidence can test independently. Ask what observable consequences follow from the hidden claim. Watch declassification over time.

The AHI record rewards this approach because the government has released enough material to expose genuine evolution.

In 2022, an experts panel emphasized mechanism plausibility for a subset and recommended better detection. In 2023, most agencies judged a foreign campaign very unlikely. In 2025, that broad conclusion held, but two components adjusted views on capability and a narrow possible role. This is not a straight line from belief to disbelief or back again.

It is a matrix moving cell by cell.

The public has another reason to care about this distinction. Threat assessments affect policy. If the government falsely attributes an illness cluster to a hostile state, it can escalate conflict. If it falsely dismisses a real covert capability, personnel remain exposed. The cost function is asymmetric in both directions.

That is unusual.

Many scientific questions can tolerate years of uncertainty. Whether a foreign state is attacking diplomats cannot. Decision-makers must act before certainty arrives. They may add shielding, change assignments, issue warnings, fund detectors, or confront another government under conditions of incomplete evidence.

This is why precaution and accusation must be separated.

You can harden a facility against directed energy without publicly claiming an adversary used it. You can issue personnel protocols for acute anomalous symptoms without declaring a syndrome. You can collect on foreign source technology without telling every affected employee they were targeted. You can provide treatment and compensation without requiring geopolitical proof.

These options reduce the pressure to make science answer policy questions prematurely.

The Intelligence Community also has to protect against its own narrative inertia. Once a major hypothesis takes hold, collection can become self-reinforcing. Analysts ask sources about microwave weapons. Sources learn that microwave weapons are valuable reporting. Technical teams search bands associated with the hypothesis. Medical investigators interpret symptoms through the same frame. Absence of evidence from other mechanisms receives less attention.

Then a reversal requires more than new facts. It requires permission to disappoint the institution.

The 2023 assessment did that publicly. Whether it went too far in the other direction is still a legitimate question, especially given the 2025 adjustments. The point is not to crown either assessment. It is to watch the system revise itself.

The most interesting intelligence question may therefore be procedural: what evidence would cause each component to move again?

A captured device would do it. Technical collection on a foreign program with parameters matching incidents would do it. A high-confidence human source with access might do it, depending on corroboration. Repeated instrumented events would do it. A reproducible biomarker tied tightly to a known exposure could do it.

What would move confidence the other way?

More high-quality prospective monitoring with no relevant emissions during strong incidents. Better conventional diagnoses for priority cases. Demonstration that proposed devices cannot meet range or concealment requirements. Foreign program reporting showing research failure rather than success. A case-definition analysis demonstrating that the supposed distinctive cluster dissolves under blinded review.

A hypothesis that cannot name its losing evidence is not an intelligence hypothesis. It is loyalty.

The 2025 update leaves us in an uncomfortable place, which is probably the correct one. The United States does not publicly assess a broad adversary campaign. It does publicly acknowledge that some components see enough foreign technical progress to be less dismissive about capability and a narrow set of events.

That is not the revelation either side wants.

It is the kind of edge on which future surprises occur.
