# What We Still Have Not Sampled

The most surprising fact about milky seas is no longer that they exist. It is that modern oceanography has not yet deliberately driven into one with a full instrument suite.

That is partly logistics. Detection occurs at night, often far offshore. Cloud can interrupt remote sensing. Ships are slow relative to satellite alerts. A luminous event may evolve while an expedition is still being mobilized. Oceanographic cruises are scheduled months or years ahead; anomalies do not respect calendars.

But persistence changes the equation. The 2019 Java event lasted long enough that a standing response plan might have reached it. If the satellite system can identify a candidate within hours and transmit coordinates to vessels of opportunity, the field no longer has to depend entirely on luck.

What remains unmeasured is embarrassingly basic: the full microbial community, the actual concentration and spatial arrangement of luminous cells, the chemistry of the particles or films they inhabit, the vertical thickness of the emitting layer, the spectrum and absolute photon flux, the physical circulation maintaining the patch, and the difference between glowing and adjacent non-glowing water.

A single expedition could fail by arriving too late or sampling the wrong depth. That is why the design has to preserve negative data. If a satellite says the water is luminous and the ship measures no bacterial light at the surface, the mismatch is not an embarrassment to discard. It may reveal a vertical layer, an atmospheric confound, a rapidly changing event, or a flaw in the remote-sensing classifier.

Rare-event science matures when misses become interpretable.
