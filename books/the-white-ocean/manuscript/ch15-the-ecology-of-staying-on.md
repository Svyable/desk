# The Ecology of Staying On

A flash can be accidental. A glow that lasts for weeks needs an economy.

Continuous bacterial luminescence consumes reducing power and substrate. The population has to remain alive, oxygenated and sufficiently fed while physical processes keep the relevant cells or particles concentrated. Persistence therefore turns the milky-sea problem into ecology rather than optics.

One hypothesis is that light recruits higher trophic levels. Luminous bacteria eaten by small grazers may make those grazers visible to larger predators, indirectly increasing transport of the bacteria into nutrient-rich guts. Variants of this “burglar alarm” logic have been discussed for marine bioluminescence more broadly. It is plausible enough to test and too adaptive-sounding to assume.

Another possibility is less elegant: luminescence may be coupled to metabolic states selected for reasons having little to do with photons. A trait can be conspicuous to us without being the primary object of selection. In a huge event, the light might be the visible exhaust of a microbial transition whose important business is carbon, oxygen and competition.

These possibilities predict different relationships among grazing, bacterial fitness, nutrient state and light output. A future expedition should not merely identify luminous taxa. It should ask what grows better, what gets eaten, what disappears at dawn, and whether luminous and dark strains occupy different niches.

The event's duration also makes viruses relevant. Bacteriophages can restructure microbial blooms rapidly. So can protistan grazing. A milky sea that remains luminous for weeks may be continuously regenerated rather than populated by the same cells throughout. Satellite persistence does not imply organismal persistence.

The white ocean may be stable in the way a flame is stable: the shape persists while the material passes through it.
