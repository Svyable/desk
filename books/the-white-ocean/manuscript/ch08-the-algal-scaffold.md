# The Algal Scaffold

The bacteria need somewhere to stand.

Not literally. But the open ocean is an unforgiving place for any theory that depends on local chemical accumulation. Water moves. Molecules diffuse. Cells separate. A quorum-sensing explanation for milky seas therefore becomes much more plausible if the bacteria are attached to something that concentrates both organisms and food.

The 1985 observations implicated algal material, and later discussion often focused on *Phaeocystis*, a colonial phytoplankton capable of forming large blooms and producing mucilaginous structures. The attraction is obvious. A colonial alga offers surface area, organic exudates and a spatial scaffold. If luminous bacteria colonize those structures densely, the ocean can be full of local quorums without becoming a uniform bacterial broth.

But *Phaeocystis* is a candidate, not a conviction.

The field evidence linking one specific algal host to milky seas is sparse. The same macroscopic glow might arise on other particles, detritus, marine snow or surface films produced as blooms decay. In fact, decomposition may be as important as growth. A collapsing phytoplankton bloom releases reduced carbon that heterotrophic bacteria can exploit. Under calm or convergent surface conditions, that material can accumulate where oxygen, light and bacterial density favor sustained luminescence.

This suggests an ecological sequence rather than a single trigger. Nutrients support phytoplankton. Physical circulation gathers or retains biomass. The bloom ages. Organic substrates become available. Luminous bacteria expand on concentrated material. Signaling molecules accumulate locally. Light switches on across many microcolonies. The satellite sees the sum.

It is a satisfying story. Satisfaction is a reason to become suspicious.

Every step needs direct measurement in the same event. Ocean ecology is full of narratives assembled from processes that are individually real but not demonstrated to occur in the required order at the required scale. A milky sea could involve a different substrate entirely. The luminous bacteria could be passengers rather than drivers of the ecological transition. The algal association might be common in one region and absent in another.

A targeted expedition should therefore sample across gradients, not only at the brightest point. Water outside the luminous boundary is as important as water inside it. If the proposed scaffold is causal, its abundance, chemistry or microbial colonization should change systematically across the edge. If the difference is merely photon output with no corresponding ecological boundary, the theory has a problem.

The boundary is where explanation can finally stop being picturesque and become comparative.
