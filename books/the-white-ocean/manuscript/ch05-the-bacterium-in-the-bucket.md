# The Bacterium in the Bucket

A bucket is a crude scientific instrument until it is the only one that reaches the thing you need.

The romantic image of a milky sea is horizon scale. The decisive biological questions are millimetres wide. Which cells are making the light? Are they free in the water, attached to particles, living on phytoplankton, or concentrated in a surface film? Do they remain luminous when removed from the event? What happens when the sample is stirred? What is the oxygen concentration? What are the nutrients? Which organisms are present but dark?

The 1985 field work could answer only a subset of those questions, and the techniques available then were not today's metagenomic toolkit. Culturing luminous colonies biases toward organisms that tolerate the chosen media and laboratory conditions. A plate can reveal a luminous bacterium without establishing its abundance in situ. Taxonomic assignments can later shift as molecular classification improves. If a modern expedition reaches an event, it should treat the old identification as a hypothesis to test, not a result to reproduce.

Still, the bacterial explanation has a strong phenomenological advantage. It predicts steady light.

The luminous vibrios carry the enzymatic machinery for continuous emission, and their light production is connected to density-dependent regulation. That creates a plausible bridge from microscopic population structure to macroscopic glow. If enough bacteria occupy enough dense microhabitats across a surface layer, the sea can look continuously lit without requiring every cell in the water column to glow.

The word “enough” hides most of the science.

Remote-sensing work on the 2019 Java event estimated that an event of roughly 100,000 square kilometres could imply on the order of 10^22 to 10^23 luminous bacteria under assumed cell brightness and distribution. The number is useful as scale, not census. It depends on how bright individual cells are, how densely they are packed, how much light the water absorbs, how the emitting layer is structured, and how the satellite's spectral response maps onto bacterial emission.

There is a tendency, when confronted by a number that large, to let awe replace mechanism. Ten to the twenty-third sounds like explanation by magnitude. It is not. A bacterial population that large is conceivable because the ocean is large. The hard part is explaining coherence: why so much of that population enters a luminous state in the same broad place for so long.

One candidate is attachment. If bacteria colonize algal cells, detrital particles or mucous-rich aggregates, they can experience locally high density even when the bulk water remains dilute. Autoinducers accumulate within those microenvironments. The surface ocean can contain immense numbers of such particles. A satellite would integrate across them and see a continuous field.

Another candidate is a surface film rich in organic material from a phytoplankton bloom entering senescence. Dying or stressed algae release compounds that feed heterotrophic bacteria. Surface convergence can gather both substrate and cells. In that version, the milky sea is not primarily a bloom of luminous bacteria arriving from nowhere. It is a phase transition in an existing microbial food web when physical concentration and carbon supply cross a threshold together.

These models make different predictions. Attachment should produce spatial relationships between bacterial cells and specific particles or hosts. A degrading surface film should have characteristic dissolved and particulate organic chemistry. A host-specific model should recover the same partner repeatedly. A generic substrate model might not.

The next bucket, if we are fortunate enough to lower one, should therefore be much more demanding than the first.
