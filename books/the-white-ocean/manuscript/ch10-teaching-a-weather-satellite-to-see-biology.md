# Teaching a Weather Satellite to See Biology

The Day/Night Band on VIIRS is one of those instruments that becomes more interesting when people misuse it carefully.

Its job is not to identify bacteria. It measures faint nighttime radiance from the Earth. That includes city lights, fires, gas flares, fishing fleets, aurora, reflected moonlight, lightning-lit cloud and other sources that are usually much brighter than the ocean glow under discussion. Hunting milky seas therefore means constructing an exclusion problem around a weak signal.

Steven Miller and colleagues developed a set of practical tests. Candidate features had to sit over water, survive scrutiny against cloud and lunar contamination, lack a corresponding daytime reflective feature, persist across successive nights when geometry allowed, and behave like something coupled to the ocean rather than fixed infrastructure. They also used thermal and visible channels to understand cloud context and background conditions.

The approach found a dozen candidate events between 2012 and 2021 in the Indian Ocean and western Pacific. That count should not be treated as an incidence rate without qualification. Detection opportunities depend on cloud cover, moon phase, satellite overpass, sensor sensitivity and analyst criteria. An event can occur and remain invisible to the method.

The geographical pattern was nevertheless suggestive. Candidate events appeared in regions already prominent in maritime accounts, especially the northwestern Indian Ocean and Maritime Continent. That convergence is stronger than either archive alone because the biases differ. Shipping routes shape eyewitness reports; satellite geometry and atmospheric conditions shape remote detections.

A good anomaly becomes less mysterious when independent biases point toward the same place.

The 2019 Java event was the prize. It appeared enormous, bright enough to persist through difficult observing conditions, and stable enough to return through multiple moon-free windows. Satellite imagery suggested an event lasting far longer than one night and covering roughly 100,000 square kilometres.

Even then, one uncertainty remained embarrassingly simple.

Was anyone actually there?
