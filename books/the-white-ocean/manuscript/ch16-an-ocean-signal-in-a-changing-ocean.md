# An Ocean Signal in a Changing Ocean

Once a rare biological event can be detected repeatedly from orbit, the obvious temptation is to turn it into an environmental indicator.

That would be premature.

Milky seas may depend on temperature, nutrient supply, plankton community, calm surface conditions, convergence, oxygen, organic matter and microbial composition. Climate change can alter every one of those variables. It does not follow that milky seas will simply become more common or less common as the ocean warms.

The observational record is too short and the detection function too uneven. Modern satellite capability changes through time; cloud cover and moon phase alter opportunities; historical reports are shaped by shipping routes. A trend in detections could therefore arise from better looking rather than more glowing.

Still, the question is worth preparing for. A consistent satellite classifier could build an event climatology prospectively. Each candidate could be linked to sea-surface temperature, chlorophyll, productivity, currents and atmospheric forcing. If events repeatedly occupy a narrow physical envelope, that would constrain mechanism. If their envelope shifts over decades, the change could then be interpreted against a stable observing system.

The important thing is to resist the order in which climate stories are often told. First establish the ecology. Then establish the detection denominator. Only then ask whether the phenomenon is changing.

A mysterious light does not become a climate proxy because climate is important. It becomes one only if the causal and observational bookkeeping survives.
