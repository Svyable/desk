# Chapter 4 — The Magnetite War

Magnetite became the hardest surviving piece of the ALH84001 case because some bacteria make exquisite magnetite crystals on purpose. Magnetotactic bacteria build chains of magnetic particles that help orient them along geomagnetic field lines. Their crystals can occupy narrow ranges of size, purity and morphology.

Kathie Thomas-Keprta and colleagues argued that a subpopulation of ALH84001 magnetites was physically and chemically similar to magnetite produced by the terrestrial bacterium MV-1. Their 2001 PNAS paper emphasized single-domain behavior, chemical purity and a truncated hexa-octahedral crystal habit. If a morphology had no known inorganic route, it could function as a presumptive biosignature.

The phrase 'no known inorganic route' is always temporary.

Critics attacked the morphology, the population statistics, the imaging and the assumption of exclusivity. Laboratory and theoretical work explored whether carbonate decomposition and shock heating could generate small, pure magnetite grains with relevant forms. Golden and colleagues described an inorganic shock/thermal history in which iron-rich carbonate decomposed, magnetite nucleated rapidly, and subsequent heating and equilibration refined crystal chemistry.

The important scientific move was not that one side produced a more confident adjective. It was that a biosignature claim became an experimental materials-science problem.

Could an abiotic process reproduce not merely magnetite, but the exact size distribution, shape population, purity, crystallographic defects, orientation and spatial relationship to carbonate? Could a biological process do so under plausible early Martian conditions? Which prediction differed?

That last question is where many anomaly debates fail. Two stories are allowed to explain the same observation indefinitely. A decisive program instead seeks a property on which they disagree.

Cheyava Falls now confronts us with vivianite and likely greigite. Both can be associated with microbial processes on Earth. Both can also form abiotically. The task is therefore not to add them to a checklist called 'life.' It is to determine whether their crystal chemistry, isotopes, microtextures and spatial coupling to organic matter encode a reaction path that one hypothesis cannot cheaply imitate.

Alien life will not be established by a mineral's name. It will be established, if we are lucky, by a mineral's biography.
