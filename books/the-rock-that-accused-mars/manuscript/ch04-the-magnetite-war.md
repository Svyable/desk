# Chapter 4 — The Magnetite War

The magnetite argument eventually came down to a shape small enough to fit comfortably inside a virus: a crystal a few tens of nanometres long, stretched along a particular crystallographic axis, its faces clipped into what the researchers called a truncated hexa-octahedron.

That shape mattered because some magnetotactic bacteria manufacture magnetite with remarkable control. In 2001 Kathie Thomas-Keprta and colleagues compared a subset of ALH84001 grains with crystals made by the terrestrial strain MV-1. The proposed assay was stricter than “magnetite exists.” It combined six properties: a narrow single-domain size range, restricted width-to-length ratios, chemical purity, few crystal defects, the unusual truncated hexa-octahedral morphology, and elongation along a [111] axis. Their later review estimated that roughly a quarter of the meteorite's magnetites met the assay. Even MV-1 did not make qualifying crystals every time; about thirty percent of its magnetites failed the authors' own test.

That is a much better claim than the usual cartoon of the controversy. It was not that bacteria make magnetite and a Martian rock contained magnetite. The claim was that a particular population occupied an unusually specific corner of mineralogical possibility and that nobody had yet produced that population without biology.

The trouble was hidden in the word *yet*.

The decisive counterattack did not merely point out that impacts get hot. Golden and colleagues compared about a thousand magnetite crystals drawn from three populations: ALH84001, MV-1 bacteria, and laboratory products made by thermally decomposing hydrothermally precipitated iron-rich carbonate. They reconstructed three-dimensional crystal shapes from transmission-electron-microscope views taken at multiple angles. All three samples contained [111]-elongated forms. More damagingly, the most common elongated morphology in ALH84001 matched the inorganic laboratory products rather than MV-1. Their conclusion was that the morphology advertised as a robust biosignature could be reproduced by an abiotic process and that an exclusively inorganic origin was consistent with their measurements.

That did not make the biological argument silly. Thomas-Keprta's group had already made the strongest version of the objection: reproducing one attractive projection or one elongated crystal was not the same as reproducing the full six-property population, and carbonate decomposition had chemical as well as morphological burdens to meet. The disagreement therefore became unusually useful. One side was no longer saying “this looks bacterial.” It had written down an assay. The other side could attack the assay property by property and, crucially, manufacture comparison material.

There is also an experimental embarrassment buried in the history. A two-dimensional image of a three-dimensional nanocrystal can flatter the hypothesis. Different shapes can cast similar projections. Both sides therefore had to care about viewing angle, population fractions and which crystals were being counted, not merely the five beautiful particles that fit on a figure. Once the denominator became roughly a thousand grains instead of a handful of exemplars, the argument changed character.

This is the standard Cheyava Falls will eventually have to survive. Vivianite and likely greigite are interesting partly because terrestrial microbes can participate in the redox chemistry that produces them. But a mineral name is cheap evidence. The expensive evidence is a population: crystal chemistry, isotopes, defects, grain-size distributions, spatial relationships to organic matter, and a geological sequence that competing abiotic experiments fail to reproduce together.

The strongest counterargument cuts both ways. An abiotic laboratory analogue is not proof that Mars used that route; it establishes a physically available rival. Conversely, a terrestrial bacterium that makes a beautiful mineral analogue does not prove that Martian biology made the sample. The experiment has to force the histories apart.

ALH84001's magnetite dispute got closer to that standard than the slogan “life in a Mars rock” suggests. Its most valuable result may be the humiliating one: a biosignature became weaker when researchers learned how to make more convincing rocks.
