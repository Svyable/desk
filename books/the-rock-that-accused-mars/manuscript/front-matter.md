# The Rock That Accused Mars

## ALH84001, Cheyava Falls, and the Rules for Recognizing Alien Life

Sven Hardy Benson

## Author's note

There are rocks that become evidence and rocks that become characters. Allan Hills 84001 did both. It was four and a half billion years old, had spent millions of years on Mars, was punched into space by an impact, crossed interplanetary darkness, fell onto Antarctica, sat in ice, and then accused a planet of once being alive.

The accusation did not stick. It did something better. It taught us how difficult an accusation like that should be.

In 2024, Perseverance drilled another Martian rock. The core is called Sapphire Canyon. The outcrop is Cheyava Falls. NASA's instruments found organic carbon, iron phosphate, iron sulfide, bleached reaction fronts, dark rims, and a sedimentary environment in which microorganisms on Earth would know what to do. In September 2025 a Nature paper called the associations potential biosignatures. That wording is doing serious work.

This book is about the distance between potential and proof. It is also about a dangerous habit in astrobiology: counting clues without asking whether the clues are causally independent. If one hydrothermal, impact, or water-rock process can make the organic molecules, the magnetite, the carbonate and the texture together, then four observations may contain less information than one thinks.

The same problem now returns at Jezero Crater with better instruments, better geology and better sample provenance. We are closer to the right rock. That does not mean we are closer to an easy answer.

The rule throughout is simple. 'Unexplained' is not a synonym for 'biological.' 'Abiotic pathway known' is not a synonym for 'abiotic pathway occurred.' The task is not to defend one side. The task is to construct experiments in which Mars has fewer places to hide.
