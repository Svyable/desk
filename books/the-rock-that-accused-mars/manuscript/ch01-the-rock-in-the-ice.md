# Chapter 1 — The Rock in the Ice

ALH84001 was found in Antarctica in 1984, which is a fitting place for an argument about contamination. Antarctica is both a gift and a trap for meteorite science. The ice concentrates dark stones. The cold preserves them. The Earth also touches them.

The rock was eventually recognized as Martian. Its mineralogy and trapped gases tied it to Mars, and its history was already extravagant before biology entered the room. It crystallized in the ancient Martian crust, was altered by fluids, shocked by impacts, ejected into space, traveled for millions of years and landed on Earth. Each stage became a possible factory for one of the observations that would later be called biological.

That is the first fact worth keeping. A meteorite is not a specimen with one environment. It is a compressed stack of environments.

In 1996 David McKay and colleagues published their Science paper, 'Search for Past Life on Mars: Possible Relic Biogenic Activity in Martian Meteorite ALH84001.' The paper did not claim a single fossil had solved Mars. It assembled a case: indigenous polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, carbonate globules, magnetite and iron sulfide phases, and tiny structures that resembled microbial forms. The power was supposed to come from convergence.

The public memory simplified that convergence into a photograph. There were tiny shapes. They looked like bacteria. President Clinton spoke about the possibility of life beyond Earth. A technical argument became a civilizational headline.

But the strongest version of the 1996 case was never 'this shape looks alive.' It was that several observations, none sufficient alone, pointed in the same direction. That is a respectable evidentiary strategy. Medicine uses it. Geology uses it. Intelligence analysis uses it. Astrobiology must use it because fossils smaller than cells and chemistry older than continents rarely arrive with labels.

The vulnerability is correlation. If the evidence streams are not independent, convergence becomes multiplication by the same fact.

Suppose an impact heats carbonate. The carbonate decomposes. Magnetite appears. Carbon-bearing gases react. Organic material appears. Shock changes textures. A single history can produce several clues. The clues remain real. What changes is the arithmetic of confidence.

This book starts there because Cheyava Falls has already tempted us into the same mental move. Organic carbon. Ferrous iron phosphate. Iron sulfide. Redox fronts. Ancient water. A habitable setting. Six clues sound better than one. They are better than one only to the extent that they cannot all be purchased by the same abiotic chemistry.

The oldest mistake in alien-life detection may be assuming that evidence counted separately was caused separately.
