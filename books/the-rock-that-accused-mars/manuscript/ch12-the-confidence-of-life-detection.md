# Chapter 12 — The Confidence of Life Detection

NASA's Confidence of Life Detection framework tries to solve a communication problem science cannot avoid. Life detection is not binary at the first observation. Evidence accumulates through levels: a possible signal, elimination of contamination and abiotic explanations, independent confirmation, broader contextual coherence.

The virtue of a scale is that it makes uncertainty visible. The danger is that a ladder can be mistaken for destiny. Evidence does not always move upward. A better abiotic mechanism can move it down.

ALH84001 is the canonical example. The 1996 evidence deserved serious attention. Subsequent work weakened biological exclusivity for several features. That is not a scandal. It is exactly what a mature confidence framework should permit.

Cheyava Falls entered public discussion with unusually disciplined wording: potential biosignature. The term says both 'look harder' and 'do not cash the check.'

I would add one axis to any life-detection scale: causal independence. How many of the supporting observables require distinct explanations under the abiotic hypothesis? A confidence score that counts organics, minerals and texture separately without modeling their shared geochemistry can overstate the evidence.

The second axis should be adversarial reproduction. Has a skeptical laboratory generated the claimed biosignature package under nonbiological conditions that match the sample's geological history? Has a pro-biological laboratory predicted a feature before looking for it?

The third axis should be transferability. Can an independent team, blind to the preferred interpretation, classify held-out samples correctly?

Alien-life detection should borrow less from courtroom rhetoric and more from medical diagnostics. Sensitivity without specificity creates false positives. A test that recognizes Earth life perfectly may recognize Mars geology too.
