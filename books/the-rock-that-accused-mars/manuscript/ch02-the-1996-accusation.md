# Chapter 2 — The 1996 Accusation

Read the McKay paper now and one notices how conditional it is. The famous conclusion used 'could.' The authors knew inorganic formation was possible. They argued that biogenic processes offered a coherent explanation for a collection of features.

The collection mattered. Carbonate globules suggested aqueous alteration. Their textures invited comparison with terrestrial bacterially influenced carbonates. Magnetite grains and iron sulfides existed in and around the carbonates. Fresh fracture surfaces contained PAHs whose distribution appeared inconsistent with simple Antarctic contamination. Electron microscopy showed ovoid and elongated features small enough to attract the word microfossil.

None of this was invented. That is why the episode remains scientifically useful. Bad evidence is easy to dismiss. ALH84001 was difficult because the evidence was good enough to deserve a fight.

The argument also contained a hidden asymmetry. Biology is extremely good at making disequilibria, selective mineral morphologies, organized textures and complex organic molecules. Geology is also extremely good at embarrassing anyone who declares a morphology exclusive.

A 'biosignature' therefore has two jobs. It must be compatible with life. It must also be difficult to produce without life under the actual planetary conditions. The second job is usually harder.

That distinction was not unique to ALH84001, but the meteorite made it visible. A structure that looks like a bacterium can be a mineral surface. An organic molecule can be indigenous and still abiotic. A chemically pure magnetite crystal can resemble a bacterial magnetosome and still have a high-temperature or shock route. A carbonate can have a low-temperature history in one domain and later experience thermal processing elsewhere.

The 1996 paper became controversial because every line of evidence opened a separate laboratory program. That is exactly what should happen after an extraordinary claim. Skeptics did not merely say 'no.' They tried to manufacture the supposedly diagnostic features without biology. Proponents did not merely say 'yes.' They refined the claimed magnetite subpopulation and tried to specify what, exactly, made it biologically distinctive.

This is the part of the story that public memory loses. Science did not vote on Mars. It decomposed the accusation into mechanisms.

The correct question after a potential biosignature is not 'do you believe it?' It is 'what machine, organism, reaction, temperature path, contamination history or measurement artifact could generate this exact package of observations?'

Belief is cheap. Mechanisms have invoices.
