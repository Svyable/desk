# Chapter 7 — The Rock That Could Have Been Hot

Temperature is an argument disguised as a number.

If ALH84001 carbonates formed at low temperature, biological participation becomes easier to imagine and some thermal decomposition scenarios become harder. If the rock later experienced substantial shock heating, other abiotic routes to magnetite and organics become available. Much of the controversy therefore migrated into thermometry, shock physics and chronology.

This is healthy. A biological claim should be forced to coexist with the complete geological history of its host.

Mars is not obligated to preserve evidence in a laboratory-clean sequence. A rock can form in water, be buried, altered, shocked, fractured, heated, exposed, ejected and weathered on Earth. One event can erase a diagnostic signature; another can manufacture a mimic.

The relevant question is not whether a proposed abiotic pathway is possible in the abstract. It is whether the sample experienced the required conditions.

This distinction matters immediately at Cheyava Falls. The 2025 interpretation notes abiotic mechanisms capable of producing some relevant minerals but also asks whether the Bright Angel rocks show evidence for the required high-temperature or acidic conditions. If the geology excludes those conditions, the abiotic alternatives become less attractive. But exclusion must be measured, not narrated.

Returned-sample work should therefore treat thermal and fluid history as part of biosignature analysis, not preliminary geology. Mineral zoning, fluid inclusions, isotope systems, crystal defects, reaction-front geometry and radiometric constraints are evidence about which chemical pathways were physically available.

The life question sits downstream of the rock-history question.

This sounds conservative. It is actually aggressive. The fastest way to make a biological interpretation stronger is to destroy its best abiotic competitors experimentally.
