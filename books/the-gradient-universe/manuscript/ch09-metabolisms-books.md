# Metabolism's Books

Metabolism is not a random network. It is organized into pathways with clear inputs, outputs, and bookkeeping. Every reaction has a substrate and a product, a free-energy change, and a stoichiometry that must balance. Every pathway has a flux, a rate at which material flows through it, and the flux is regulated by enzyme activity, substrate availability, and feedback inhibition. Metabolism is a set of ledgers, tracking carbon, nitrogen, energy, and electrons as they move through the cell. The books must balance. If they do not, the cell accumulates waste, depletes resources, or loses the gradients that keep it alive.

The central currency of metabolism is ATP, adenosine triphosphate. ATP is a nucleotide with three phosphate groups. The bonds between the phosphates are high-energy bonds, meaning they release a large amount of free energy when hydrolyzed. When ATP is converted to ADP, adenosine diphosphate, plus inorganic phosphate, about 7.3 kilocalories per mole of free energy is released under standard conditions, and more under physiological conditions where the concentrations of ATP, ADP, and phosphate are maintained far from equilibrium. This energy can be coupled to other reactions, driving them forward even when they would otherwise be thermodynamically unfavorable.

ATP is not stored in large amounts. A typical cell contains enough ATP to sustain activity for a few seconds. ATP is continuously synthesized and consumed, turning over hundreds of times per day. The turnover rate in an active muscle cell can exceed the cell's body weight in ATP per day. This is possible because ATP is regenerated from ADP and phosphate using the energy from glucose oxidation or, in the case of photosynthetic cells, from light. ATP is not a long-term energy store. It is a short-term energy shuttle, delivering energy from catabolic pathways to anabolic pathways, transport, and motility.

The synthesis of ATP is coupled to the discharge of a proton gradient. In mitochondria, the electron transport chain uses the energy from oxidizing NADH and FADH₂ to pump protons from the mitochondrial matrix into the intermembrane space, creating a gradient of both pH and voltage. The protons flow back through ATP synthase, a rotary motor embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane. The flow of protons drives the rotation of part of the enzyme, and the mechanical rotation is coupled to the chemical synthesis of ATP from ADP and phosphate. The coupling efficiency is high, around 90 percent, making ATP synthase one of the most efficient molecular machines known.

ATP synthase is not the only molecular machine. The ribosome synthesizes proteins by reading mRNA and linking amino acids in the specified order. The proteasome degrades proteins by unfolding them and cleaving them into peptides. Kinesin and dynein are motor proteins that walk along microtubules, transporting cargo within the cell. Myosin is a motor protein that slides along actin filaments, driving muscle contraction. All of these machines are powered by the hydrolysis of ATP or GTP, guanosine triphosphate, a close analog of ATP. The machines do mechanical work, but they do not violate thermodynamics. They dissipate free energy and increase entropy.

The cell's energy budget can be partitioned. In a resting human, about 20 to 25 percent of the energy expenditure goes to the brain, even though the brain is only about 2 percent of body mass. Another 20 to 25 percent goes to the liver and kidneys, which perform biosynthesis and filtration. Muscle accounts for about 20 percent at rest and far more during exercise. The heart beats continuously and accounts for about 10 percent. The remaining energy goes to other organs, protein synthesis, active transport, and thermogenesis. These numbers vary with activity, but they show that the brain and visceral organs are energetically expensive, not luxuries but necessities.

Biosynthesis requires energy. Synthesizing a protein from amino acids requires breaking and forming peptide bonds, which has a free-energy cost. Synthesizing a nucleotide requires assembling a sugar, a phosphate, and a nitrogenous base, all of which require energy input. Synthesizing a lipid requires reducing acetyl-CoA to fatty acids, which consumes NADPH, a reducing agent that is itself synthesized using energy from glucose oxidation. The cost of biosynthesis is often stated in terms of ATP equivalents. A typical protein of 300 amino acids requires about 1,200 ATP equivalents to synthesize, accounting for transcription, translation, and folding.

Cells regulate metabolism by controlling enzyme activity. Allosteric regulation is common: a molecule binds to the enzyme at a site distinct from the active site, changing the enzyme's shape and activity. Covalent modification is also common: adding or removing a phosphate group can activate or inhibit an enzyme. Gene expression is a slower mechanism: the cell can increase or decrease the number of enzyme molecules by transcribing more or less mRNA. All three mechanisms are used, often in combination, to adjust metabolic flux in response to changing conditions.

Glycolysis is the most studied metabolic pathway. It converts one molecule of glucose into two molecules of pyruvate, producing two molecules of ATP and two molecules of NADH. The pathway has ten reactions, each catalyzed by a specific enzyme. The first half of the pathway consumes ATP to phosphorylate glucose and its derivatives, priming them for cleavage. The second half releases energy, producing ATP and NADH. The net result is a modest energy gain, but glycolysis is fast and does not require oxygen. It is the primary energy source for anaerobic organisms and for tissues operating under low-oxygen conditions, like sprinting muscle.

Pyruvate has three main fates. In aerobic conditions, it enters the mitochondrion and is oxidized to acetyl-CoA, which feeds into the citric acid cycle. In anaerobic conditions, it is reduced to lactate in animals or ethanol in yeast, regenerating NAD+ so that glycolysis can continue. In some organisms, pyruvate is carboxylated to oxaloacetate, which can be used for gluconeogenesis, the synthesis of glucose from non-carbohydrate precursors. The choice of fate depends on the availability of oxygen, the cell's energy state, and the presence of regulatory signals.

The citric acid cycle oxidizes acetyl-CoA to carbon dioxide, producing NADH, FADH₂, and GTP. The cycle has eight reactions, organized in a loop where oxaloacetate is regenerated at the end. The cycle is not merely catabolic. Intermediates are siphoned off for biosynthesis, including amino acids, nucleotides, and porphyrins. The cycle is regulated at several steps, primarily by feedback inhibition. When ATP, NADH, and citrate accumulate, the cycle slows down. When ADP, NAD+, and calcium increase, the cycle speeds up. The regulation matches the cycle's activity to the cell's energy demand.

The electron transport chain is a series of protein complexes embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane. NADH and FADH₂ donate electrons to the chain, which passes them through a series of redox reactions, ultimately reducing oxygen to water. The energy released by the electron transfers is used to pump protons across the membrane, creating the gradient that drives ATP synthesis. The chain is organized into four complexes, plus ATP synthase, and the efficiency of energy conversion is about 38 percent, with the rest dissipated as heat. The dissipation is not waste. It maintains body temperature in warm-blooded animals and satisfies the second law.

Photosynthesis is the inverse of respiration in the sense that it uses light to reduce carbon dioxide and oxidize water. The process has two stages. The light reactions occur in the thylakoid membrane and use photons to split water, producing oxygen, protons, and electrons. The electrons are passed through photosystem II and photosystem I, which pump protons and produce NADPH. The proton gradient drives ATP synthesis. The dark reactions, also called the Calvin cycle, occur in the stroma and use ATP and NADPH to fix carbon dioxide into glucose. The dark reactions do not require darkness. They are light-independent but typically occur during the day when ATP and NADPH are available.

The Calvin cycle has a curious feature: the enzyme that fixes carbon dioxide, RuBisCO, is the most abundant protein on Earth, making up about half the protein mass in leaves. RuBisCO is slow and prone to errors. It sometimes fixes oxygen instead of carbon dioxide, a wasteful process called photorespiration that costs energy and releases carbon dioxide. Despite its inefficiency, RuBisCO has not been replaced by a better enzyme in billions of years. The reason may be that no better enzyme exists under the constraints of biochemistry, or that the evolutionary path to a better enzyme is inaccessible.

Nitrogen fixation is another bottleneck. Nitrogen gas, N₂, is abundant in the atmosphere but chemically inert. Organisms need nitrogen for amino acids, nucleotides, and other biomolecules, but most organisms cannot fix atmospheric nitrogen. Only certain bacteria and archaea have the nitrogenase enzyme, which reduces nitrogen gas to ammonia. The reaction requires a large energy input, about 16 ATP equivalents per molecule of ammonia, and the enzyme is inhibited by oxygen. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria often live in anaerobic environments or form symbiotic relationships with plants, providing ammonia in exchange for sugars. The Haber-Bosch process, invented in the early 20th century, fixes nitrogen industrially, enabling the large-scale production of fertilizer and supporting much of modern agriculture.

Metabolism couples to ecology. Photosynthesis produces glucose, which herbivores consume. Herbivores are consumed by carnivores. Carnivores die and are decomposed by bacteria and fungi, which release nutrients back into the soil. The cycle is not perfect. Some organic carbon is buried in sediments, becoming coal or oil over geological timescales. Some nitrogen escapes as nitrogen gas via denitrification. The leaks are small compared to the flux through the biosphere, but they accumulate over millions of years, changing the composition of the atmosphere and crust.

The ratio of carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus in organisms is roughly 106:16:1 by atoms, a ratio called the Redfield ratio after the oceanographer who discovered it. The ratio is not exact, but it is conserved across many marine organisms, suggesting that it reflects fundamental stoichiometric constraints. The ratio has implications for nutrient cycling. If nitrogen is limiting, photosynthesis slows down even if carbon dioxide and phosphorus are abundant. If phosphorus is limiting, nitrogen and carbon accumulate. The balance determines the productivity of ecosystems and the composition of the biosphere.

Metabolism is constrained by thermodynamics and kinetics. A reaction that is thermodynamically favorable may still be too slow to sustain life without a catalyst. A reaction that is thermodynamically unfavorable cannot proceed unless it is coupled to a favorable reaction, like ATP hydrolysis. The constraints are strict, and they limit the possible designs for life. Different organisms use different metabolic strategies — fermentation, respiration, photosynthesis, chemosynthesis — but all obey the same laws. The books must balance, or the organism dies.

The balance is monitored continuously. Cells sense their ATP levels, their redox state, and their nutrient availability, and they adjust metabolic flux accordingly. When ATP is low, AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) is activated, which upregulates catabolic pathways and downregulates anabolic pathways. When glucose is high, insulin signaling activates pathways that store glucose as glycogen or convert it into fat. When oxygen is low, hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) is stabilized, which upregulates glycolysis and angiogenesis. These regulatory mechanisms are not optional. They are essential for maintaining the cell's energy balance in fluctuating environments.

Metabolic diseases arise when the balance is disrupted. Diabetes is a failure to regulate blood glucose, either because the pancreas does not produce enough insulin or because cells do not respond to insulin properly. The result is chronic hyperglycemia, which damages blood vessels, nerves, and organs. Cancer cells reprogram their metabolism to support rapid growth, often relying on glycolysis even in the presence of oxygen, a phenomenon called the Warburg effect. The shift allows cancer cells to produce biosynthetic precursors more efficiently, but it also makes them vulnerable to metabolic therapies that target glycolysis or other altered pathways.

Mitochondrial dysfunction is implicated in aging and many degenerative diseases. Mitochondria are the primary site of ATP synthesis, but they are also the primary source of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which can damage DNA, proteins, and lipids. As organisms age, mitochondrial DNA accumulates mutations, mitochondrial function declines, and ROS production increases. The decline contributes to sarcopenia, neurodegeneration, and cardiovascular disease. Exercise and caloric restriction have been shown to improve mitochondrial function and reduce ROS production, which may explain their association with longer healthspan and lifespan.

Metabolic flux is not uniform across tissues. The brain has high metabolic demand, consuming about 20 percent of the body's oxygen even though it is only 2 percent of body mass. Neurons rely almost exclusively on glucose for energy, because the blood-brain barrier restricts the passage of fatty acids and other fuels. Muscle can switch between glucose and fatty acids depending on availability and activity level. During rest, muscle preferentially oxidizes fatty acids. During intense exercise, muscle relies on glucose because glycolysis is faster than fatty acid oxidation. The liver acts as a metabolic hub, converting excess glucose into glycogen or fat, breaking down glycogen when blood glucose is low, and synthesizing ketone bodies from fatty acids during prolonged fasting.

Ketone bodies — beta-hydroxybutyrate and acetoacetate — are water-soluble fuels that the liver produces from fatty acids when carbohydrate availability is low. The brain can use ketone bodies for energy, which is how humans survive prolonged fasting or starvation. Ketogenic diets, which are very low in carbohydrates and high in fats, induce ketosis, a metabolic state where ketone bodies become the primary fuel. Ketogenic diets have been used therapeutically for epilepsy since the 1920s and are being investigated for other conditions, including some cancers and neurodegenerative diseases. The mechanisms are not fully understood, but they may involve metabolic reprogramming, ROS reduction, and signaling changes.

Metabolic networks are robust. They tolerate perturbations, reroute flux when pathways are blocked, and maintain homeostasis in the face of changing conditions. But they are not infinitely robust. Extreme starvation depletes ATP, and the cell cannot maintain its ion gradients. Hypoxia shuts down aerobic respiration, forcing the cell to rely on glycolysis, which is far less efficient. Toxins inhibit key enzymes, blocking entire pathways. The cell's survival depends on maintaining the metabolic network in a functional state, which requires a continuous supply of nutrients and energy.

The metabolic networks of different organisms reflect their ecological niches. Obligate anaerobes, which cannot tolerate oxygen, rely on fermentation or anaerobic respiration. Facultative anaerobes can switch between aerobic and anaerobic metabolism depending on oxygen availability. Phototrophs capture light energy to drive metabolism. Chemolithotrophs oxidize inorganic compounds like hydrogen sulfide or ferrous iron to extract energy. The diversity of metabolic strategies is enormous, but all are constrained by the same thermodynamic and kinetic principles. The next question is how individual metabolic networks couple to form ecosystems.
