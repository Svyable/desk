# Civilization's Energy Stack

When someone says that the world “uses” energy, ask where in the stack they are measuring.

The answer can change the story.

A coal seam contains chemical energy. Coal at a mine mouth is a primary energy product in common statistical accounting. Burn it in a power station and some of that energy becomes electricity while a larger fraction leaves as heat. Send the electricity over a grid and some is lost in transmission and distribution. Feed the remainder to a motor and most may become mechanical work. Use that motor to drive a pump and still another fraction becomes useful movement of water. The water itself may then provide an energy service such as irrigation, sanitation, cooling, or drinking water.

At every layer the word *energy* refers to the same conserved physical quantity, but the usefulness, carrier, ownership, location, timing, and accounting category change.

This is civilization’s energy stack.

The most useful vocabulary is not source → consumption. It is **primary supply → transformation → final energy → useful energy → energy service**.

The International Energy Agency’s explanation of [energy balances](https://www.iea.org/commentaries/understanding-and-using-the-energy-balance) makes the distinctions explicit. Primary supply tracks energy products entering the economy before conversion. Final consumption tracks what reaches end users. Useful energy is what remains after conversion at the point of use and is available to provide a service. The service is the thing people actually wanted: a warm room, a tonne of steel, ten kilometers of travel, illumination on a desk, a refrigerated vaccine, a computation.

The stack matters because societies do not demand joules for their own sake.

They demand capabilities.

A candle and an LED can provide light with radically different energy inputs. A resistance heater and a heat pump can provide the same indoor temperature through different physical pathways. A gasoline car and an electric car can move the same passenger while converting and losing energy at different stages. A blast furnace and a hydrogen-based direct-reduction process can make iron through different chemical routes.

The end service can remain similar while the stack beneath it changes.

That fact corrects one of the easiest mistakes in long-run energy comparisons. “Primary energy” is not measured identically in all popular datasets because non-combustible electricity such as wind, solar, and hydro does not pass through a thermal power plant whose rejected heat can be counted. The IEA uses a physical-content method in which wind, solar, and hydro enter the balance as electricity at the point of generation. Older or alternative “substitution” methods estimate how much thermal fuel would have been needed to generate the same electricity.

Those conventions can produce very different apparent primary-energy shares for renewables even when the underlying electricity generation is identical.

A transition toward direct renewable electricity can therefore reduce reported primary energy partly because a large thermal-conversion loss disappears from the accounting.

That is not an accounting trick. The loss really has disappeared.

It is also not proof that civilization is doing less. The same or greater energy service may be delivered with less primary input.

This is why the old statement that economic activity must track gross energy use closely because “thermodynamics requires it” was too strong.

Thermodynamics requires physical processes to obey energy and entropy constraints. It does not say GDP must rise in proportion to primary joules.

Current data make the changing stack visible. The IEA’s [Global Energy Review 2026](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-energy-review-2026) estimates that worldwide energy demand grew about 1.3 percent in 2025, while electricity demand grew nearly 3 percent. Low-emissions sources — solar, wind, hydropower, other renewables, and nuclear — supplied nearly 60 percent of the increase in total energy demand that year. Solar photovoltaics alone accounted for more than a quarter of the growth.

At the same time, all three major fossil fuels still grew in 2025.

There is no clean handoff in which one energy era ends before the next begins.

The electricity system changed faster. According to the IEA, low-emissions sources reached 43 percent of global electricity generation in 2025. Renewables supplied 34 percent. Coal also supplied about 34 percent, remaining the largest single electricity source, while natural gas supplied about 21 percent. Solar PV generation grew by roughly 600 terawatt-hours in one year and exceeded 8 percent of global electricity generation.

These numbers make two apparently conflicting statements true at once.

The power system is decarbonizing at the margin unusually quickly.

The world still operates an enormous fossil-fuel system.

A serious energy chapter has to hold both facts simultaneously.

Electricity is moving toward the center of the stack because many end uses can be electrified efficiently. Electric motors usually convert a large share of electrical input to mechanical output. Heat pumps can move several units of heat for each unit of electrical work, depending on temperatures and equipment. Battery-electric drivetrains avoid much of the rejected heat intrinsic to combustion engines. Arc furnaces and electrolysis can replace some fuel-based industrial steps under suitable conditions.

But “electrify everything” is not itself a physical plan.

Some services are easier to electrify than others. Low-temperature building heat is different from long-distance aviation. A passenger car is different from ocean shipping. Aluminum electrolysis already uses electricity as a core process, while cement emits carbon dioxide partly because limestone is chemically decomposed, not merely because a kiln needs heat. Steelmaking can change routes, but ore quality, hydrogen supply, scrap availability, furnace stock, and product requirements matter.

The stack is sector-specific.

Electricity also has an unusual constraint: in a conventional grid, generation and demand must be balanced continuously within tight frequency and voltage limits. Stored fuels let a thermal plant keep weeks or months of chemical energy nearby. A wind turbine has no fuel tank. A photovoltaic array cannot postpone noon sunlight until evening by itself.

That does not make variable renewables “intermittent” in the sense of simply unreliable devices. Their output is variable and weather-dependent, often forecastable with increasing skill, while thermal and nuclear plants also have forced outages and maintenance. The system problem is portfolio and timing: how generation profiles, demand, transmission, storage, flexible loads, dispatchable resources, and geographic diversity fit together.

A grid is a coordination machine for gradients.

Transmission lets a surplus in one region serve a deficit elsewhere. Storage moves availability across time. Demand response changes when a service is consumed. Fast controls maintain local electrical stability. Markets and operators schedule resources around physical constraints. Backup generation covers combinations of demand and supply that the rest of the portfolio cannot.

The important variable is not simply how much generating capacity exists.

It is whether the portfolio can deliver power at the needed location and time.

This makes capacity and energy different quantities. A one-gigawatt plant describes a maximum rate of power. A gigawatt-hour describes an amount of energy. A battery can have high power and short duration or lower power and long duration. A solar farm may have large nameplate capacity and a production profile set by daylight, weather, orientation, and curtailment. A nuclear plant with the same nameplate capacity may produce for far more hours but has different construction, fuel, maintenance, and flexibility characteristics.

Confusing watts with watt-hours is the energy equivalent of confusing speed with distance.

Storage is expanding quickly because temporal control becomes more valuable as electricity supplies more services and variable generation grows. The IEA reported almost 110 gigawatts of battery-storage additions in 2025, with annual additions rising about 40 percent. Yet a power rating alone does not tell us how many hours the batteries can discharge, what cycling duty they perform, where they are located, or which grid service they provide.

Again: measurement belongs to a boundary and a task.

Fuel storage and electrical storage should also not be compared as if they were interchangeable boxes. Oil products are chemically dense and can sit in tanks. Gas can be stored underground or as LNG with substantial infrastructure. Coal can be piled. Electricity must be converted to another form for storage: electrochemical, gravitational, thermal, mechanical, chemical, or another medium.

Each conversion pays a cost but can buy a different timescale or service.

Industry makes the stack material. Steel, cement, ammonia, aluminum, glass, ceramics, chemicals, and refined fuels all require particular temperatures, pressures, redox conditions, separations, or electrical potentials. Industrial energy demand is therefore partly an inventory of transformations civilization insists on performing.

The old draft assigned one energy number to each material as though the number were a property like atomic mass. Real process energy varies by feedstock, route, plant age, recycling, product quality, fuel, electricity mix, and system boundary.

Steel from an integrated blast furnace is not energetically identical to steel from scrap in an electric arc furnace. Primary aluminum production is not the same as recycling aluminum. Cement grinding is not the same as calcination. Ammonia made from natural-gas-derived hydrogen is not the same process chain as ammonia made from electrolytic hydrogen.

The chemical route is part of the energy stack.

This is where exergy analysis can add something ordinary energy accounting misses. One megajoule of near-ambient heat and one megajoule of electricity contain the same amount of energy. They do not have the same work potential. Using high-quality electricity for a service that can be delivered from low-temperature waste heat may destroy more available work than necessary. Conversely, trying to run an electrolyzer on low-grade heat is not an option just because the joule count matches.

Energy has quantity. Exergy adds quality relative to an environment.

Civilization is full of quality mismatches.

We burn high-temperature fuels to maintain rooms only modestly warmer than outside. We throttle pressurized fluids and destroy pressure exergy. We dissipate braking energy that regenerative systems can sometimes recover. We reject industrial heat because no nearby process needs it at that temperature and time. We transmit electricity because moving the desired physical service itself would be more difficult.

Theoretically avoidable loss and economically avoidable loss are not the same.

A heat-recovery system can be thermodynamically attractive and economically pointless if the heat source and sink never coincide, if pipes are too expensive, or if reliability requirements dominate. Exergy can identify opportunity. Engineering and economics decide whether the opportunity is usable.

Transportation makes this especially visible. The useful service is not “burn gasoline.” It may be passenger-kilometers, tonne-kilometers, speed, flexibility, range, or access. Much of the energy in a combustion vehicle becomes engine and exhaust heat. Some moves the vehicle itself rather than the passenger or cargo. Aerodynamic drag rises steeply with speed. Braking deliberately destroys kinetic energy unless regeneration captures part of it.

An electric drivetrain removes some conversion losses but cannot remove tire deformation, air resistance, vehicle mass, road friction, or the need to accelerate the vehicle.

Efficiency gains occur at particular layers.

The same is true in buildings. Good insulation lowers unwanted heat transfer. Air sealing reduces infiltration. Heat pumps change the conversion pathway. Passive design changes solar gains and ventilation. Building geometry changes surface area. Controls can avoid conditioning empty spaces. Occupant behavior changes set points and schedules.

A building is less a consumer of energy than a device for maintaining selected internal conditions against weather.

Agriculture combines biological and industrial stacks. Sunlight drives crop photosynthesis, but agricultural production also uses land, water, nutrients, labor, machinery, fuels, electricity, pesticides, refrigeration, processing, storage, and transport. The old claim that industrial agriculture universally requires “ten fossil calories per food calorie” is one of those statistics that travels farther than its measurement basis.

Food-system energy intensity varies enormously by crop, farming system, climate, fertilizer source, irrigation, processing, cold chain, diet, and boundary. Greenhouse-grown vegetables, rain-fed grain, beef, and orchard fruit cannot be compressed honestly into one ratio.

The meaningful question is where each food system’s constraints and losses actually sit.

Direct anthropogenic heat provides a useful reality check on the thermodynamic rhetoric. Almost all commercial energy eventually becomes heat, so civilization does add heat directly to its surroundings. In dense cities this can contribute meaningfully to local heat islands alongside altered surfaces, reduced vegetation, building geometry, and other factors.

Globally, however, direct waste heat is much smaller than the radiative forcing created by greenhouse gases.

Burning fossil fuel changes climate primarily because carbon dioxide and other emissions change how Earth exchanges radiation with space, not because the released combustion heat itself accumulates for centuries. The heat dissipates; the atmospheric composition changes the continuing radiative balance.

This distinction prevents a common category error: entropy production is not the metric that explains contemporary global warming.

Carbon emissions are tied to the carbon content and process chemistry of the energy stack. The IEA estimated that energy-related CO₂ emissions still grew in 2025, though only about 0.4 percent. At the same time, it estimates that deployment since 2019 of solar PV, wind, nuclear power, electric cars, and heat pumps avoided more than 35 exajoules of annual fossil-fuel demand in 2025 and around three billion tonnes of annual CO₂ emissions compared with a counterfactual without that deployment.

A transition can therefore be materially consequential before fossil-fuel use begins falling everywhere.

Counterfactuals matter.

The same principle applies to claims about “peak oil.” Physical reserves are finite, but production is not a simple geological countdown. Price, technology, demand, regulation, geopolitics, substitution, discoveries, depletion rates, and climate policy all shape production. A resource can lose market share before becoming scarce. An expensive resource can become economic after technology changes. A high-cost reserve may never be extracted if substitutes win first.

The gradient thesis does not predict the date of peak oil.

It predicts almost nothing about market timing without economics and institutions.

Decoupling deserves the same restraint. It is perfectly possible for GDP to grow while energy use falls in a country for a period. Efficiency, structural change, electrification, demographic trends, warmer winters, industrial relocation, and changing prices can all contribute. It is also possible for territorial emissions to decline while consumption-based emissions remain higher because energy-intensive production occurs abroad.

A 2024 *Scientific Reports* study of [CO₂ decoupling across 164 countries](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-71101-2) found that some high-income countries had achieved periods of absolute emissions decoupling while a positive relationship between income and emissions remained at the global level. The result is neither “decoupling is impossible” nor “growth has become immaterial.”

Economic value is not measured in joules.

A software bug fix can create large market value with tiny incremental energy use. A barrel of oil can be burned wastefully. A medical treatment can improve life enormously without proportionally increasing national energy throughput. At the same time, every physical economy requires energy and materials, and many valuable services depend on energy-intensive infrastructure.

Thermodynamics supplies lower-level constraints, not a one-to-one equation between GDP and energy.

Energy transitions are therefore not governed by one inevitable timescale. Some infrastructure lasts for decades. Some technologies diffuse remarkably fast. Solar PV manufacturing scaled over a few decades. Smartphone adoption transformed communication in far less than a century. Transmission lines and nuclear plants can take many years to permit and build. Vehicle fleets turn over at rates different from software systems. Furnaces and industrial plants have their own replacement cycles.

“Transitions are slow” is an average that hides the mechanisms that determine speed.

A more useful question is: which stock must turn over, and what constrains the replacement rate?

A coal plant can close early if economics or policy change. A steel mill may operate for decades but undergo retrofit. A transmission bottleneck can delay new generation that is otherwise cheap. A shortage of transformers can bind after turbine supply improves. Permitting can bind after capital becomes available. Public opposition can bind after engineering is solved.

The energy stack is also a constraint stack.

This is why resilience cannot be reduced to fuel diversity alone. A grid can have many generating sources and depend on one vulnerable transmission corridor. A gas system can have abundant production and insufficient pipeline capacity during a cold snap. A solar-heavy system can be robust to fuel-price shocks and vulnerable to a transmission failure. A nuclear fleet can carry large fuel inventories and share a reactor design problem. A refinery network can have crude supply and lack a needed product grade.

Redundancy, storage, diversity, spare capacity, interoperability, maintenance, and black-start capability all create different forms of resilience.

They cost something when nothing goes wrong.

That cost is why optimized systems can become brittle if every visible margin of slack is removed. But the opposite slogan — “natural systems always prefer robustness over efficiency” — is no better. The appropriate redundancy depends on failure probability, consequence, recovery time, and alternatives.

Civilization chooses a reliability level through engineering, markets, regulation, and politics.

There is no thermodynamic optimum for how many hours of outage risk a society should tolerate.

The energy stack thus ends where social choice begins.

Physics tells us that electricity must be balanced, that heat moves down temperature differences without work, that chemical bonds and nuclear binding energies constrain fuels, that conversion creates entropy, and that no storage technology returns all the work put into it.

Engineering tells us what can be built from those constraints.

Economics tells us what people will pay to build and operate.

Institutions determine who may build, who bears risks, how reliability is valued, how pollution is regulated, and how costs are distributed.

Politics decides many of the boundaries the market takes as given.

The gradient framework belongs at the bottom of that stack, not at the top pretending to dictate the rest.

Its contribution is to clarify what civilization is physically doing: finding sources of available energy, converting them into flexible carriers, moving them through infrastructure, storing some for later, transforming them again at end use, and discarding the remainder as lower-quality energy while materials continue through their own cycles.

The current transition changes almost every layer at once.

Generation is shifting toward sources with no fuel combustion. Transport is becoming more electric in some segments. Heat pumps change building heat flows. Batteries alter short-duration balancing. Data centers add fast-growing electrical loads in some regions. Industrial decarbonization experiments with new chemical routes. Transmission becomes more valuable as generation geography changes. Flexible demand becomes an operational resource.

No single source “wins the gradient universe.”

A functioning stack must fit sources to services across time, geography, reliability, cost, and environmental constraint.

That is the physical challenge beneath the political argument.

The next chapter is about what happens when we forget the hierarchy and turn a physical constraint into a metaphysical story. Entropy becomes morality. Dissipation becomes purpose. “Free energy” becomes a bridge between unrelated theories. Physical descriptions become social prescriptions.

The gradient framework can illuminate a civilization only if it knows what it cannot tell us.
