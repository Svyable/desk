# Planets That Leak Heat

A planet is not at equilibrium with space. It absorbs radiation from its star and emits radiation into the void, and the difference determines its internal temperature and surface dynamics. Earth absorbs about 174 petawatts of solar radiation and emits approximately the same amount as infrared radiation. The balance is not exact on short timescales — the planet has thermal inertia, seasons, and transient phenomena like volcanic eruptions and ice-age cycles — but over decades and centuries the average incoming and outgoing energy fluxes are nearly equal. This is not equilibrium. It is a steady state maintained by throughput.

The temperature of a planet in steady state depends on its distance from the star, the star's luminosity, and the planet's albedo and emissivity. A simple calculation assuming a perfect absorber gives the effective radiative temperature: the temperature at which the planet radiates as much power as it absorbs. For Earth, this temperature is about 255 Kelvin, or -18 Celsius. That is the temperature the planet would have if it had no atmosphere and radiated uniformly in all directions. The actual surface temperature is about 288 Kelvin, or 15 Celsius, because the atmosphere traps infrared radiation and warms the surface. The difference, 33 Kelvin, is the greenhouse effect.

The greenhouse effect is not an atmospheric failure. It is a thermodynamic consequence of wavelength-selective absorption. Solar radiation peaks in the visible spectrum, around 500 nanometers, where the atmosphere is mostly transparent. Earth's surface absorbs the visible light and heats up. The surface then radiates infrared photons, around 10,000 nanometers, where the atmosphere is opaque due to water vapor, carbon dioxide, and other greenhouse gases. The infrared photons are absorbed and re-emitted by the atmosphere, some upward and some downward. The downward flux warms the surface beyond the effective radiative temperature. The system reaches a new steady state where the surface is warmer than it would be without an atmosphere, but the total outgoing radiation still balances the incoming solar radiation.

The greenhouse effect creates a thermal gradient between the surface and the upper atmosphere. The surface is warm, roughly 288 Kelvin. The tropopause, the boundary between the troposphere and stratosphere, is cold, roughly 220 Kelvin. This gradient drives convection, evaporation, cloud formation, precipitation, and all the phenomena we call weather. Without the gradient, the atmosphere would be stagnant. With the gradient, it becomes a dissipative structure, redistributing heat from equator to pole, from surface to space, and from day to night.

The gradient is not uniform. The equator receives more solar radiation per unit area than the poles because the Sun's rays strike the surface more directly. This creates a latitudinal temperature gradient that drives atmospheric circulation. Warm air at the equator rises, flows poleward at high altitude, cools, and sinks at mid-latitudes. The sinking air returns to the equator at low altitude, completing the circulation cell. This is the Hadley cell, one of three major circulation cells in each hemisphere. The others are the Ferrel cell, between 30 and 60 degrees latitude, and the polar cell, between 60 degrees and the pole. The cells are not perfect. They are disrupted by continents, oceans, mountains, and the Coriolis effect, which deflects moving air due to Earth's rotation.

The Coriolis effect is central to atmospheric dynamics. Air moving poleward in the Northern Hemisphere is deflected to the right, toward the east. Air moving equatorward is deflected to the left, toward the west. The result is the prevailing westerlies at mid-latitudes and the trade winds at low latitudes. The same effect creates cyclones and anticyclones, rotating systems of low and high pressure. Cyclones rotate counterclockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and clockwise in the Southern Hemisphere. Anticyclones rotate in the opposite direction. The rotation is not imposed by external forces. It emerges from the interaction between the pressure gradient, the Coriolis effect, and friction with the surface.

The oceans also transport heat, and they do so more efficiently than the atmosphere in some respects because water has a higher heat capacity than air. Ocean currents are driven by wind stress, density differences, and the Earth's rotation. The Gulf Stream transports warm water from the Gulf of Mexico to the North Atlantic, warming Western Europe by several degrees Celsius relative to regions at the same latitude in North America. The thermohaline circulation is a global overturning current driven by density differences caused by temperature and salinity variations. Cold, salty water sinks in the polar regions and flows equatorward at depth, while warm, fresh water flows poleward at the surface. The complete cycle takes roughly 1,000 years.

Oceans and atmospheres couple. Wind stress drives surface currents, which transport heat, which changes the temperature of the overlying air, which changes the pressure gradient, which changes the wind. The coupling creates feedback loops. The El Niño-Southern Oscillation is a prominent example. In normal years, trade winds push warm surface water westward across the Pacific, and cold water upwells along the coast of South America. In El Niño years, the trade winds weaken, the warm water sloshes back eastward, and the upwelling diminishes. This changes the temperature and pressure patterns across the Pacific, affecting weather patterns globally. The oscillation has a period of 2 to 7 years, and it is a natural mode of variability in the coupled ocean-atmosphere system.

Planets without atmospheres have no weather. The Moon receives solar radiation on its dayside and radiates infrared from both day and night sides. The dayside heats up to about 400 Kelvin, and the nightside cools to about 100 Kelvin. There is no mechanism to transport heat horizontally or to retain it between day and night. The surface temperature simply responds to the local radiation balance. Mercury is similar, despite being closer to the Sun and having a thin exosphere. Venus has a thick atmosphere, but its slow rotation and strong greenhouse effect create a surface temperature of about 740 Kelvin, hot enough to melt lead. The high temperature makes the greenhouse effect even stronger, creating a runaway positive feedback that has left Venus in a stable but uninhabitable state.

Mars has a thin atmosphere, about 0.6 percent of Earth's atmospheric pressure, composed mostly of carbon dioxide. The weak greenhouse effect and greater distance from the Sun result in a surface temperature averaging about 210 Kelvin, well below the freezing point of water. Liquid water is unstable on the surface; it either freezes or sublimates. But Mars shows clear evidence of ancient river valleys, deltas, and lake beds, indicating that liquid water once flowed on the surface. The early Martian atmosphere was likely thicker and warmer, sustained by volcanic outgassing and possibly a stronger greenhouse effect. Over time, the atmosphere was lost, possibly due to solar wind stripping after Mars lost its global magnetic field. The planet transitioned from a wet, potentially habitable state to the cold, dry desert we see today.

The presence or absence of a magnetic field affects atmospheric retention. Earth has a strong magnetic field generated by a dynamo in its liquid iron core. The field deflects the solar wind, a stream of charged particles flowing from the Sun. Without the magnetic field, the solar wind would directly strike the upper atmosphere, gradually eroding it over geological time. Mars lost its magnetic field early in its history, and the solar wind has been stripping its atmosphere ever since. Venus has no intrinsic magnetic field either, but its atmosphere is so thick that the erosion rate is low. The balance between outgassing and loss determines whether a planet retains a substantial atmosphere over billions of years.

Volcanic outgassing is a major source of atmospheric gases. When magma rises from the mantle, dissolved gases are released, including water vapor, carbon dioxide, sulfur dioxide, and hydrogen sulfide. On early Earth, volcanic outgassing likely produced the first atmosphere, which was very different from the current nitrogen-oxygen atmosphere. Oxygen accumulated only after photosynthetic organisms evolved and began producing it as a waste product. The Great Oxidation Event, around 2.4 billion years ago, marked the transition from a reducing atmosphere to an oxidizing one. The event was catastrophic for anaerobic organisms that could not tolerate oxygen, but it enabled the evolution of aerobic respiration, which is far more energy-efficient than anaerobic metabolism.

The carbon cycle couples atmospheric carbon dioxide to geological processes. Carbon dioxide dissolves in rainwater, forming carbonic acid, which weathers silicate rocks. The dissolved ions wash into the ocean, where they precipitate as calcium carbonate in shells and skeletons. The carbonate eventually subducts at tectonic plate boundaries, melts in the mantle, and returns to the atmosphere via volcanic outgassing. The cycle operates over timescales of hundreds of thousands to millions of years, and it acts as a thermostat, stabilizing Earth's climate. When the Sun is brighter and the surface warms, weathering accelerates, removing carbon dioxide from the atmosphere and cooling the climate. When the Sun is dimmer or volcanic activity decreases, weathering slows, carbon dioxide accumulates, and the climate warms.

This feedback is not instantaneous. Ice ages occur when orbital variations reduce summer insolation at high latitudes, allowing snow and ice to persist year-round. The ice reflects more sunlight, cooling the planet further, and the cooling triggers additional feedbacks involving ocean circulation, carbon dioxide levels, and vegetation cover. The Milankovitch cycles — variations in Earth's orbit, axial tilt, and precession — drive ice-age cycles with periods of 20,000, 41,000, and 100,000 years. The cycles are predictable from orbital mechanics, but the climate response involves complex interactions between the atmosphere, oceans, ice sheets, and biosphere.

Tidal heating is another planetary heat source. When a moon or planet orbits a larger body, gravitational tides deform the object. If the orbit is eccentric, the tidal deformation varies over the orbital period, flexing the interior and generating frictional heat. Jupiter's moon Io is the most volcanically active body in the solar system due to tidal heating from Jupiter and orbital resonances with Europa and Ganymede. Saturn's moon Enceladus ejects water-ice plumes from its south polar region, driven by tidal heating and possibly cryovolcanism. These moons are not heated by solar radiation alone. They are dissipating gravitational gradients created by their host planets.

Europa, another Jovian moon, has a surface of water ice and likely a subsurface ocean kept liquid by tidal heating. The ocean may be 100 kilometers deep, with more water than all of Earth's oceans combined. The ocean floor may have hydrothermal vents, analogous to those on Earth's ocean floor, where chemical gradients could support microbial ecosystems. Europa is a high-priority target for astrobiology because it has liquid water, chemical energy from redox gradients, and geological activity, all of which are thought to be necessary for life. The moon is a dissipative structure, maintained by the orbital gradient between Jupiter and Europa.

Radioactive decay is another heat source. Earth's interior is heated by the decay of uranium-238, uranium-235, thorium-232, and potassium-40. The decay releases energy, which heats the mantle and drives convection. Mantle convection transports heat from the core to the surface, driving plate tectonics, volcanism, and mountain building. The radioactive heat flux is roughly 47 terawatts, about one-third of Earth's total heat loss. The other two-thirds is primordial heat, left over from the planet's formation and the differentiation of the core. Both heat sources are gradients that drive geological activity, and both are slowly declining as the planet cools and the radioactive isotopes decay.

Plate tectonics is a surface expression of mantle convection. The lithosphere, composed of the crust and upper mantle, is broken into plates that move relative to each other. New lithosphere forms at mid-ocean ridges, where upwelling mantle melts and solidifies. Old lithosphere subducts at convergent boundaries, where it sinks back into the mantle and melts. The plates move at a few centimeters per year, driven by the combined forces of ridge push, slab pull, and drag from the convecting mantle. Plate tectonics recycles surface material into the mantle and brings mantle material to the surface, mixing the planet over timescales of hundreds of millions of years.

Plate tectonics is not common in the solar system. Venus and Mars show no evidence of current plate activity, though Mars may have had it early in its history. Venus's surface is geologically young, about 500 million years old, suggesting a global resurfacing event rather than continuous plate recycling. Earth's plate tectonics may be enabled by the presence of liquid water, which weakens the lithosphere and facilitates subduction. Dry rocky planets may have a rigid lid that prevents plates from forming, leaving only localized volcanism driven by mantle plumes.

The interplay of solar heating, greenhouse effect, atmospheric circulation, ocean currents, volcanic outgassing, tectonic recycling, and tidal heating makes planets dynamic dissipative structures. They do not passively absorb and emit radiation. They actively redistribute energy, create gradients, and sustain processes that would be impossible at equilibrium. A planet is a leaky heat engine, and the leak is the source of its organization. The next layer of the cascade is the atmosphere itself, treated as a machine.
