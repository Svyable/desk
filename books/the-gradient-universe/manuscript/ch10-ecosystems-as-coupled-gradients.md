# Ecosystems as Coupled Gradients

A forest does not have one metabolism.

That seems like a small correction, but it prevents a large conceptual mistake. A forest contains millions of organisms with conflicting interests and different metabolisms: trees competing for light, fungi exchanging nutrients with roots, herbivores consuming leaves, predators consuming herbivores, microbes decomposing dead tissue, parasites redirecting host resources, pollinators moving among flowers, and scavengers reopening energy and nutrient pathways after death.

The ecosystem is not one superorganism balancing a common ledger. It is a network of partial couplings among many organisms and abiotic processes.

Energy enters much of the biosphere as sunlight and ultimately leaves as heat. Matter behaves differently. Carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, sulfur, water, and trace elements are reused, sequestered, transported, buried, weathered, dissolved, volatilized, and sometimes lost from a local system entirely. An ecosystem therefore contains directional energy throughput and partially cycling matter at the same time.

That asymmetry is foundational ecology.

Primary producers capture only a fraction of the solar energy incident on their environment and store some of it in chemical form. Herbivores consume only a fraction of plant production. Predators consume only a fraction of prey production. At every stage, organisms respire, excrete, shed tissue, die, or leave material uneaten. Decomposers and detritivores route part of that material through additional pathways.

The familiar “ten-percent rule” compresses all of this into one number: roughly ten percent of production at one trophic level becomes production at the next. It is useful as a classroom intuition and dangerous as a law.

Transfer efficiency varies substantially among ecosystems, taxa, temperatures, body sizes, food quality, and food-web structure. A modern review of [energy transfer through marine ecosystems](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tree.2020.09.014) emphasizes that transfer efficiency is an emergent and highly variable property rather than a fixed constant. Even defining the denominator requires care: are we comparing ingested energy, assimilated energy, biomass production, or trophic-level production?

The broad constraint survives the correction. Trophic transfer is lossy. That is one reason food webs cannot support arbitrary biomass at arbitrarily high trophic positions. But the number of trophic levels is not determined by repeatedly multiplying by 0.1 until the calculator reaches zero. Ecosystem size, productivity, disturbance, omnivory, prey defenses, body size, metabolic rates, detrital pathways, and evolutionary history all matter.

A food web is not a pyramid drawn with a ruler.

Primary production itself is constrained by more than light. A desert receives abundant solar radiation and can remain sparsely productive because water is scarce. A patch of ocean can receive strong sunlight and remain relatively unproductive because nitrogen, phosphorus, iron, or another nutrient limits phytoplankton growth. A shaded forest understory can have water and nutrients but little usable light. Cold temperatures can slow growth and decomposition. Grazing can remove leaf area. Disease can redirect production into pathogens.

The productive capacity of an ecosystem is therefore set by whichever constraints are binding in that place and time.

This is the ecological version of metabolic bookkeeping. Carbon fixation requires energy and carbon dioxide, but biomass also requires nitrogen, phosphorus, sulfur, metals, and water. Increase one input and another may become limiting. Fertilizer experiments often show strong growth responses at first and weaker responses later as limitation shifts.

No single gradient owns the system.

Aquatic ecosystems make the coupling especially visible. In much of the open ocean, photosynthesis is restricted to the illuminated upper layer. Nutrients can be regenerated below that layer as sinking organic matter is decomposed. Upwelling or mixing must return nutrients toward the surface before phytoplankton can use them again. Strong stratification can preserve a warm, sunlit surface while cutting it off from nutrient-rich deeper water.

The same structure can therefore help one process and hinder another. Stratification creates a temperature gradient and suppresses vertical mixing. That can stabilize the water column while limiting nutrient resupply. A thermodynamic description that celebrates the existence of a gradient misses the ecological trade-off.

Coastal upwelling systems show the opposite regime. Winds and rotation bring nutrient-rich water toward the surface, supporting high phytoplankton production and often large fisheries. But intense production followed by decomposition can also contribute to low-oxygen conditions if ventilation cannot keep pace. Productivity and habitability do not move together monotonically.

Coral reefs offer another apparent paradox. They can be highly productive despite growing in nutrient-poor tropical water. The solution is tight recycling and symbiosis. Reef-building corals host photosynthetic dinoflagellates that transfer photosynthetically fixed carbon to the animal host while receiving nutrients and a protected environment. Organisms recycle scarce nutrients rapidly within the reef.

This coupling is also vulnerable. Heat stress can disrupt the coral-algal symbiosis, leading to bleaching. Bleached corals are not necessarily dead immediately, and recovery is possible if stressful conditions subside, but prolonged or repeated bleaching increases mortality and can alter community structure. Ocean warming, acidification, disease, pollution, and overfishing can interact rather than acting one at a time.

An ecosystem under multiple stressors does not have a single “collapse gradient.”

Decomposition is the return path that simple food-chain diagrams often hide. Dead tissue still contains carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, and chemical free energy. Bacteria, archaea, fungi, and detritivores break that material down. Their metabolism returns some nutrients to forms producers can reuse and transfers carbon to atmospheric, aquatic, or soil reservoirs.

Decomposition rates depend on temperature, moisture, oxygen, substrate chemistry, microbial community, and physical protection. Organic matter can persist because it is cold, dry, waterlogged, chemically resistant, bound to minerals, buried, or physically inaccessible. Peatlands accumulate carbon partly because production and decomposition are mismatched over long periods.

That is storage, not failed ecology.

The biosphere contains enormous delays. Carbon can pass through a leaf in weeks, through wood in centuries, through soil pools on multiple timescales, through sedimentary rock in millions of years. Nitrogen can cycle rapidly through organisms and also return to the atmosphere through denitrification. Phosphorus lacks a comparably large gaseous reservoir and is strongly tied to rock weathering, soils, biological uptake, rivers, sediments, and tectonics.

“Cycling” can make these processes sound closed and tidy. They leak.

Rivers export nutrients from watersheds. Fires move carbon and minerals to the atmosphere. Animals migrate across ecosystem boundaries. Dust carries phosphorus across oceans. Nitrogen can leave as N₂ or nitrous oxide. Carbon can be buried in sediment. Geological uplift later exposes old material to weathering. Ecosystem boundaries are analytical conveniences, not walls.

Food webs add another kind of coupling: one species changes the abundance or behavior of another, which changes the conditions faced by a third.

Trophic cascades are real in many systems. Sea otters, sea urchins, and kelp provide a famous marine example. Where otters strongly suppress urchins, kelp forests can persist in places that might otherwise be heavily grazed. But even this relationship varies with wave exposure, disease, predation by other species, climate, recruitment, and local food-web structure.

Ecology becomes misleading when one celebrated cascade is turned into a universal plot.

Yellowstone wolves are the cautionary case. The popular story is wonderfully clean: wolves were reintroduced, elk became fewer or more fearful, willows and aspens recovered, stream banks stabilized, beavers returned, and the wolves “changed the rivers.” Parts of that causal chain have empirical support at particular sites. Other parts remain contested or have been overstated.

Studies have found evidence consistent with wolf-mediated changes in elk behavior and vegetation. Others have found that hydrology, drought, human harvest, other predators, beaver engineering, spatial sampling, and long vegetation legacies are necessary to explain the observed patterns. A 2024 synthesis in *Ecological Monographs*, [“Does restoring apex predators to food webs restore ecosystems?”](https://doi.org/10.1002/ecm.1598), argues that wolf restoration alone has not produced the broad, durable restoration of northern-range willow communities implied by the popular cascade narrative.

This is exactly the kind of case the book needs.

The gradient framework would be weakened if it encouraged us to flatten a complicated ecological history into “predators improve dissipation.” The real system contains multiple feedbacks and historical states. Wolves matter. So do water tables, beavers, elk abundance, drought, hunting, vegetation structure, and the lagged effects of decades without large predators.

Causation is distributed.

Succession provides a second lesson in abandoning equilibrium language. Old ecological descriptions often imagined communities progressing through predictable stages toward a stable climax. Real landscapes are more contingent. Volcanic eruptions, hurricanes, fires, landslides, droughts, pests, grazing, invasive species, and human land use repeatedly interrupt or redirect trajectories.

Mount St. Helens after the 1980 eruption is a classic case because the disturbance created a natural experiment across areas that experienced very different levels of burial, blast, heat, and surviving biological legacy. Recovery did not begin everywhere from sterile blank ground. Buried roots, surviving plants, animal recolonization, lupines, erosion, and topography produced different pathways across the landscape.

History survived the disturbance in patches.

That matters because ecosystems can exhibit hysteresis: the path from state A to state B may differ from the path back. Remove a stressor and the prior ecosystem may not reappear automatically. Soil can be gone. Seed sources can be distant. A key mutualist may be missing. Fire frequency may have changed. An invasive species may now reinforce the new state.

Alternative stable states are a useful model in some systems, especially shallow lakes, reefs, grass-shrub transitions, and other environments with strong feedback. Yet the language can also be overused. Not every slow recovery implies bistability, and not every abrupt change is a mathematical tipping point. Demonstrating alternative stable states requires evidence about feedback and dynamics, not just observing two different-looking places.

The same caution applies to resilience. In engineering, resilience may mean how quickly a system returns to a reference state. In ecology, it can also mean the amount of disturbance a system can absorb before shifting into a different regime. These are related but not identical concepts.

A forest that recovers quickly after a small drought may still be vulnerable to an unprecedented sequence of drought, fire, and logging.

The intermediate disturbance hypothesis illustrates how an appealing ecological rule can become too universal. The classic idea predicts highest diversity at intermediate disturbance: too little disturbance allows competitive exclusion, while too much eliminates sensitive species. Some ecosystems fit that pattern. Others do not. The hypothesis has been repeatedly criticized, refined, and defended. Even a 2026 study finding support in rocky intertidal forests notes the [long-running controversy](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-50481-7).

The correct lesson is not “intermediate disturbance maximizes diversity.” It is that disturbance regime interacts with life history, dispersal, competition, habitat heterogeneity, and scale.

Scale is especially important. A fire can reduce diversity in one local plot while increasing landscape heterogeneity across a region. A flood can destroy a cohort and create new habitat downstream. A fallen tree can kill the organisms beneath it and open a canopy gap that supports new species.

Entropy production does not tell us which scale of biodiversity to care about.

The Amazon makes these questions planetary. Forest trees move vast quantities of water from soil to atmosphere through transpiration. That water contributes to atmospheric moisture transport and rainfall, coupling vegetation to regional climate. Deforestation and degradation reduce that recycling in some regions while also changing surface roughness, albedo, fire risk, carbon storage, and local energy fluxes.

Evidence for declining resilience has grown. A 2022 *Nature Climate Change* analysis found [widespread loss of Amazon forest resilience](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41558-022-01287-8) in satellite-derived vegetation dynamics, especially in drier regions and areas closer to human activity. A 2024 *Nature* synthesis of [critical transitions in the Amazon forest system](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-06970-0) estimated that by 2050 a substantial fraction of Amazonian forests could be exposed to compounding disturbances capable of triggering ecosystem transitions.

The science has moved further since then. A 2026 *Nature* modeling study on [deforestation-induced drying](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10456-0) found that land-use change can lower the warming threshold at which large areas lose stability, with transition risk propagating through atmospheric moisture transport. Its exact thresholds depend on the model and scenario, so they should not be presented as a countdown clock. The robust message is interaction: warming and deforestation can reinforce drought risk across distances.

That is a coupled-gradient story with actual predictive content. Vegetation changes water flux. Water flux changes rainfall. Rainfall changes vegetation viability. Fire changes vegetation and atmospheric composition. Feedback can propagate beyond the location of the original disturbance.

It is also a warning that “the Amazon is close to *the* tipping point” is too singular. Different regions have different soils, water tables, species, dry-season climates, and disturbance histories. A 2024 *Nature* study found that [Amazon forest biogeography predicts contrasting resilience and vulnerability to drought](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07568-w). The forest is a mosaic with coupled regional risks, not one switch.

This layered causation is what a serious gradient framework should illuminate.

Ecosystems alter their physical environment. Forests change roughness and evapotranspiration. Kelp changes local flow and habitat structure. Beavers dam streams. Corals build reefs. Microbes change redox chemistry. Earthworms restructure soil. Termites alter gas exchange and nutrient distributions. Organisms are not just passengers in preexisting gradients; through metabolism and construction they modify the boundary conditions future organisms encounter.

Ecologists call part of this niche construction. The thermodynamic translation is that organisms redirect material and energy flows in ways that can persist beyond the individual that made the change.

Those modifications can accelerate some fluxes and slow others. A beaver dam slows water, traps sediment, raises local water tables, and creates wetlands. A forest canopy slows wind near the ground and greatly increases the path water can take through roots, xylem, leaves, and atmosphere. Soil aggregates can protect organic carbon from decomposition. Shells and woody tissue lock materials into structures that decay slowly. Burrows increase gas exchange in sediments.

An ecosystem is therefore not simply a gradient-dissipation engine.

It is a landscape of conductors and insulators.

That phrase is more useful because it includes both directions of control. Some organisms open pathways. Others close them. Some transform fast pools into slow pools. Others mobilize stored material. Predators alter behavior as well as biomass. Mutualists create access to nutrients neither partner could acquire as effectively alone.

The system-level energy throughput remains physically necessary. But there is no ecological objective function shared by every species.

This is where the book must sharply separate ecosystem thermodynamics from ecosystem evolution. Natural selection acts on organisms and inherited traits, not on the ecosystem as a unit in most ordinary cases. Group- and multilevel-selection questions can become more complicated, but it is still unsafe to say that ecosystems “evolve to dissipate energy” as if the forest had offspring and a genome.

Communities assemble through immigration, extinction, evolution, disturbance, abiotic filtering, historical contingency, and species interactions. The resulting network can display system-level regularities without having been selected as one organism.

That is enough for the gradient thesis.

An ecosystem requires ongoing access to free energy for sustained biological activity. Its organisms couple to chemical, radiative, thermal, and material differences. Those couplings alter one another through food webs, nutrient cycles, habitat modification, and climate feedback. The system can occupy multiple regimes and can respond nonlinearly when constraints change.

What we should not infer without additional evidence is that the realized ecosystem maximizes power, entropy production, complexity, or any other global scalar.

That question belongs in the next chapter because serious researchers have proposed exactly such principles. Maximum entropy production, maximum power, constructal arguments, and dissipation-driven adaptation all try to say something stronger than “open systems require free energy.” They deserve to be judged on what they actually predict, where they work, and where they do not.

The ecosystem chapter has prepared the ground by showing why the stronger claim is tempting.

It has also shown why it is dangerous.
