# A City Beneath the Ice

In the late 1950s and early 1960s, the United States Army built an installation beneath the Greenland ice sheet. It had tunnels, living and working areas, and a small nuclear reactor used to provide heat and electricity. The place was called Camp Century. Officially, it was a demonstration of the possibility of operating in extreme Arctic conditions. It also supported a secret military ambition known as Project Iceworm: investigating whether nuclear missiles could be based beneath the ice. The [U.S. Government Accountability Office](https://www.gao.gov/assets/d24104082.pdf) describes the installation, the reactor, the buried wastes and the Cold War context in a 2024 report.

A city under the ice sounds like the setting of an eccentric adventure novel. The historical version was an engineering project with ordinary human requirements: shelter, power, water, supplies, waste handling, maintenance and access. Operating on an ice sheet meant dealing with a material that moves and deforms over time. The same ice that provided concealment and insulation was not an inert rock foundation. It could change shape and affect the structures installed within it. A plan to establish a network beneath the ice would have to account for that movement.

The choice of Greenland made strategic sense to planners concerned about the shortest routes between the United States and the Soviet Union. The Arctic was not merely a remote wilderness. It sat within the geometry of nuclear deterrence and intercontinental missile flight. A concealed installation in the ice could appear attractive because it might be difficult for an adversary to locate or attack. The same remoteness made construction, operation and support difficult and expensive.

The Army's experiment included construction of trenches cut into compacted snow and ice, later covered to create protected spaces. These were occupied by personnel and supplied through a demanding logistical system. The site demonstrated how much infrastructure was required to make a human workplace inside an environment where ordinary buildings, access roads and utilities could not be assumed. The fact that the installation existed is already astonishing. Its military purpose makes it more complicated than a clever exercise in polar architecture.

The [GAO report](https://www.gao.gov/assets/d24104082.pdf) explains that Camp Century operated from 1959 to 1967, with a reactor used for part of that time. The reactor, the PM-2A, produced thermal energy and electricity, helping reduce dependence on conventional fuel deliveries. The facility supported a population reaching roughly two hundred personnel at points during its operation. It was a genuine demonstration of portable nuclear power in a harsh environment, not a fantastical underground city whose technologies were unknown to the rest of the world.

The reactor was important because energy logistics dominate remote operations. Transporting fuel into the interior of Greenland is costly and subject to weather and access constraints. Solar power is limited by extreme seasonal daylight cycles. Wind and other resources may be variable. A compact nuclear reactor can provide steady energy if it can be deployed, maintained and operated safely. That advantage is real. It comes with requirements for radiation protection, qualified personnel, equipment support and waste management.

The unusual setting made the waste problem especially consequential. During operation, the facility generated ordinary waste and some radioactive materials. The historical record documents the disposal of certain waste streams within the ice sheet under arrangements made at the time. The assumption was that ice could serve as a stable or sufficiently remote storage environment. Decades later, climate change altered the context in which those choices would be understood.

The [GAO's 2024 assessment](https://www.gao.gov/assets/d24104082.pdf) examined contamination associated with Cold War activities outside the United States, including Camp Century. It noted concern that changing environmental conditions may affect how buried materials are managed in the future. The facility's infrastructure and waste remained physically connected to Greenland long after the original military project ended. A temporary installation created a long-lived environmental responsibility.

This is an important distinction from a story in which the ice city was simply abandoned and forgotten. Researchers and officials have documented the site, and there have been investigations into its history and potential environmental effects. The mere existence of buried waste does not establish that a catastrophic release is imminent. Nor does a historical assessment of safe conditions settle what future warming may do. Scientific work is required to understand where materials are, how ice moves and under what conditions exposure or transport could occur.

A [technical report associated with research in Greenland](https://knr.gl/sites/default/files/camp_century_report_20180719.pdf) summarizes the operation of the PM-2A reactor between 1960 and 1964 and describes historical radiological monitoring as well as later investigations. It gives the reactor's thermal output as about ten megawatts and electrical output as about 1.5 megawatts. It also describes sampling and analyses intended to assess whether anthropogenic radionuclides could be detected in relevant ice. The results require careful interpretation because different radionuclides have different sources, including atmospheric nuclear testing as well as local operations.

The distinction between source and presence matters. Detecting radioactive material in ice does not automatically identify Camp Century as the source. Fallout from nuclear weapons testing circulated through the atmosphere and was deposited in many environments. A responsible study must use chemistry, isotopic signatures, historical records and geography to distinguish possible origins. The fact that a story involves a secret missile project should not make every later measurement evidence of something sinister.

Project Iceworm itself was more ambitious than the working Camp Century installation. It envisioned a much broader network of missile-related facilities beneath the Greenland ice sheet. That concept was not fully built. Camp Century provided research on practical feasibility and the effects of operating in the ice. Treating the entire proposed missile network as a completed secret base would confuse planning documents with actual construction. This is one of the book's recurrent distinctions, and the ice makes it unusually visible.

The ice sheet was a dynamic material. Its slow movement could deform tunnels and complicate the maintenance of long-lived spaces. The basic problem differs from digging a tunnel through rock, even when both seem stable on human timescales. A large buried network might require continuous repair or redesign as ice moved. The logistics of maintaining that network would have to be balanced against the supposed advantages of concealment and dispersal. A strategic diagram is easy to draw. A network of working tunnels in flowing ice is another matter.

An extraordinary new view of the installation appeared in 2024, long after the people and machinery of Camp Century had gone. In April that year a NASA Gulfstream III aircraft flew over Greenland carrying instruments for studying the ice sheet. The team was trying to investigate ice structure and the bed beneath it. Their radar measurements revealed artificial-looking features buried within the ice. The researchers recognized the layout of Camp Century, but the observation was not the purpose for which the aircraft had been sent.

The [NASA Earth Observatory's November 2024 account](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/new-view-of-the-city-under-the-ice-153616/) describes the work of scientists including Alex Gardner and Chad Greene. The instrument was an airborne radar system used to investigate the ice, and the resulting image provided a new view of structures that had moved farther below the surface since the base was abandoned. The discovery was not a new underground city. It was a more detailed observation of a known historical installation, produced by technology designed for a different scientific task.

The difference between finding and rediscovering matters. Camp Century had not vanished from historical memory. The Army's operation had been documented, and later researchers had studied its legacy. Radar did, however, reveal the physical arrangement in a way ordinary surface observation could not. Snow and ice had accumulated over the old structures. What once required people walking through tunnels could now be inspected in part through remote sensing from the air. The technology changed the evidence available for a history that was already known.

The [NASA account](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/new-view-of-the-city-under-the-ice-153616/) also emphasizes the challenge of interpreting the radar image. An unfamiliar structure appearing in measurements collected for geological purposes can initially be difficult to identify. Researchers have to compare the signal with known objects, previous surveys and the expected properties of ice. A striking image does not supply its own interpretation. The ability to recognize Camp Century depended on documentary knowledge of its existence.

The radar results make a useful counterpoint to a typical conspiracy narrative. A person could tell the story as a secret city discovered by NASA under the ice. That phrase suggests an unknown civilization or a concealed active base. The factual sequence is much simpler and more fascinating. The U.S. Army built a documented installation during the Cold War. It later shut down. Decades afterward, a research instrument designed to map ice revealed parts of its buried layout. The mystery lies in how the environment and measurement technology interact, not in a newly discovered population hiding underground.

The base has another surprising legacy entirely outside its military purpose. Researchers drilled an ice core at Camp Century in the 1960s, reaching deeply into Greenland's ice sheet. Ice cores are valuable because layers of accumulated snow and ice can preserve evidence of past climate conditions. The scientists also recovered material from beneath the ice. Some of that subglacial sediment was later examined using methods that revealed something extraordinary about Greenland's deeper history.

In a [2021 paper in *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8020747/), researchers analyzed sediment from beneath about 1.4 kilometers of ice at Camp Century. The material contained plant remains and other evidence of environments that existed when the area was not covered by the ice sheet in the way it is today. The analysis drew on several methods, including studies of mineral exposure and sediment chemistry, to infer aspects of past glaciation and vegetation.

The core's history is itself remarkable. A military installation built in part to investigate operating beneath an ice sheet had enabled the collection of material that later became evidence about the ice sheet's instability over geological time. The site was selected for Cold War research and logistics. Its geological record turned out to be useful to scientists asking how Greenland responded to climates very different from the present. A secret military ambition inadvertently contributed to a line of civilian scientific inquiry that its designers could not have anticipated in its later form.

The [2021 study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8020747/) concluded that northwestern Greenland experienced at least some ice-free or reduced-ice conditions during parts of the Pleistocene, with vegetation preserved in sediment that later lay beneath thick ice. The conclusions concerned geological history over very long timescales, not the existence of a hidden warm forest underneath the modern Camp Century tunnels. The plant material was a record of a previous environment, not proof that plants were growing in the darkness at the time the core was recovered.

This distinction makes the finding better science. It is easy to imagine a headline about plants discovered under a mile of Greenland ice and conclude that someone found a living underground ecosystem. The actual result was evidence about past vegetation and ice-sheet extent, preserved in geological material. The methods involved interpreting multiple lines of evidence to reconstruct an earlier landscape. A fossil or plant fragment can be real and still refer to a world that no longer exists.

The chronology also raises a new question about the apparent permanence of ice. When the Army built Camp Century, the ice provided concealment and a physical environment for the installation. The ice was dynamic, and some engineers understood that movement would affect structures. Later climate research demonstrated the sensitivity of major ice sheets to changing environmental conditions over geological and modern timescales. A substance that looks permanent on the scale of a construction schedule can change considerably over centuries and millennia.

The military project and the sediment research illuminate different parts of that problem. The engineers wanted to know whether tunnels could remain usable in moving ice for the duration of a strategic requirement. The geologists wanted to understand whether large regions of Greenland had been ice-free in the distant past. The environmental managers want to know how the buried remnants of the base may be affected by future changes. These questions share a physical location but require different measurements, models and decisions.

The 2024 [Government Accountability Office report](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-24-104082) adds the final institutional dimension. It examined U.S. Cold War contamination in several places abroad, including radioactive liquid waste associated with Camp Century, contamination near Palomares in Spain, and nuclear-test fallout in the Marshall Islands. The cases differed substantially, but they shared a problem of responsibility extending beyond the original military activity. The physical material remained after the program that created it ended.

The GAO reported that radioactive liquid generated by Camp Century's reactor remained entombed under ice. The reactor itself had been removed; the buried liquid and other legacy material were a different category of problem. A statement that a working reactor remains forgotten beneath the glacier would be false. A statement that no radioactive material was left behind would also misrepresent the audit. The distinction between reactor hardware, waste, monitoring and future exposure is essential.

The report did not announce that every contaminated site was experiencing the same hazard or that immediate catastrophe was inevitable in Greenland. It reviewed how changing environmental conditions might affect future management and how different U.S. agencies had responsibilities for assessing or addressing contamination. The evidence included historical disposal, present conditions and uncertainty about future environmental change. It was a policy and risk-management document, not a sensational discovery notice.

That kind of record is one of the least glamorous ways in which a society keeps its obligations. A research installation can close, personnel can be reassigned and the original mission can become irrelevant. The waste does not disappear when the accounting office terminates the project. Somebody has to maintain knowledge about what was left, measure whether conditions change and decide when intervention is warranted. A public agency that documents those obligations may prevent future harm even when no dramatic engineering project follows immediately.

There is an obvious political temptation to describe Camp Century as an abandoned American city whose waste was simply forgotten by everyone. The record is more complicated. Historical and scientific investigations have continued, including the GAO review and radar mapping. The question is not whether anyone knows the facility existed. It is whether the available knowledge and institutional arrangements are sufficient to manage long-term environmental responsibilities. A documented problem can remain difficult even when it is not secret.

The sovereignty question becomes more important over time. Greenland is not an empty technical canvas. Decisions concerning environmental contamination, military history, future land or ice use, and remediation involve authorities and communities with legitimate interests. The fact that the U.S. military built the base under Cold War agreements cannot substitute for present accountability. Nor can an outside writer speak for Greenlanders without documented accounts of their views. The history requires that those interests be included in a serious final edition.

A climate scientist may see Camp Century as a valuable measurement site. A military historian may see it as an unusual experiment in Arctic operations. An environmental manager may see it as a legacy contamination issue. A Greenlandic political authority may see obligations arising from activities carried out on its territory. The radar image can appear identical to all these people while the questions they ask differ. The physical object has acquired several histories because its consequences have lasted longer than its original purpose.

In one sense, the buried base has become an accidental archive. Its trenches, abandoned infrastructure and waste preserve a record of engineering decisions. The ice core preserves an environmental record vastly older than the Army. Radar can make some of the structures visible again without excavation. No one planned for one site to connect Cold War nuclear logistics, subglacial vegetation history and modern environmental accountability. The connections emerged because the same ice sheet was involved in all three.

This is what makes Camp Century more remarkable than a secret-city rumor. It was a real nuclear-powered base, part of an ambitious military proposal that was not fully built, and the source of material later used to understand Greenland's climate history. Its legacy sits inside a changing physical environment and a still-active political conversation about who is responsible for what remains.

The city under the ice was built by people. The older landscape beneath the ice was not. The documents and measurements distinguish them, and the resulting history has more depth than a story about a lost military tunnel system.

The project's military and environmental histories also raise questions of sovereignty. Greenland was and is a place with political institutions, people and legal relationships, not an empty field on which a foreign military could pursue any experiment it liked. Denmark's historical role and the arrangements governing American activity are part of the record. An account that describes the ice simply as unused territory misses the political context of a foreign military facility and the later responsibilities associated with it.

The phrase abandoned city can imply that its human population vanished suddenly, leaving the entire project buried in place. The actual installation was shut down and personnel left through an organized process, with equipment removed or left depending on its nature and the decisions taken. Some waste and infrastructure remained. The difference matters because it shifts the story from a mystery about lost inhabitants to a history of military engineering and long-term disposal obligations.

There is a beautiful irony in the timeline. The Army built a facility in ice partly because it seemed suitable for protecting and concealing equipment. Later observers began to ask whether the ice itself would remain sufficiently stable to keep the legacy material isolated. A substance chosen as shelter becomes a changing environmental condition. The design assumption outlived the Cold War strategy that produced it.

Climate research has also transformed the meaning of Greenland to readers outside the region. The ice sheet is no longer merely an apparently permanent surface beneath polar aircraft routes and military installations. It is part of a changing global system. Models of ice-sheet dynamics and melt conditions involve uncertainty, but the need to account for change is well established. A historical waste site can acquire new relevance because the environment surrounding it is not fixed.

This is not a justification for describing Camp Century as a ticking nuclear bomb. The reactor was removed after operation, and the specific nature and location of residual waste require evidence. The GAO report addresses long-term management questions, not an announcement that an active reactor remains buried and is about to detonate. The truthful version is stranger and more bureaucratically difficult: a Cold War facility generated materials whose future risk depends on environmental changes and responsible monitoring.

The installation is a case study in the lifespan of engineering decisions. Governments can fund a temporary research project, learn something about a difficult technology, cancel a more ambitious plan and move on. The physical environment may keep part of the project long after institutional attention has shifted. The resulting maintenance and contamination questions can be inherited by people who never participated in the original decision.

I would put Camp Century beside the Guano Islands Act in an exhibit about geography turned into strategy. In one case a resource-rich island became an object of territorial law. In the other a thick ice sheet became a possible shelter for military infrastructure. Both involved governments looking at landscapes through the needs of a particular historical moment. Both left consequences that later generations had to interpret under different circumstances.

The United States really built and operated a nuclear-powered installation beneath the Greenland ice sheet. A larger missile-basing scheme was seriously explored but not completed. The underground system was real, as were the people who worked there. The later questions about what remains are real as well.

The idea sounds like science fiction until the government's audit arrives. The audit, of course, is less cinematic and more important.
