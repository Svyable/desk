# Two Spiders Off the Planet

In 1973, two spiders named Arabella and Anita went into orbit aboard the American space station Skylab. Their job was to construct webs without the normal cue of gravity. The experiment had been proposed by a high-school student, Judith Miles, who wanted to know what would happen when an animal performed an elaborate, familiar behavior in an unfamiliar physical environment. It was approved, flown, photographed and analyzed. The [Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum](https://www.si.edu/object/nasm_A19740484001) holds the preserved body of Arabella. There is an accompanying specimen for Anita. The animals are museum objects because they participated in a real space experiment.

The question is much better than it first sounds. A spider's web looks like geometry. It has radial lines, spiral elements and distances that can be measured. But a web is not produced by a ruler, a pencil and a human intention to make something beautiful. It emerges through a sequence of actions performed by an animal that responds to its body, surroundings and the structure it has already built. Gravity may provide orientation and influence the placement of threads. If gravity is removed, which parts of the behavior change?

A high-school student could ask that without needing a sophisticated theory of orbital mechanics. The experiment required a system that would carry living spiders, let them build webs, record the results and provide ground comparisons. NASA did the engineering and astronaut support. Miles provided the question. This arrangement is one of the best examples of an institution making room for genuine curiosity rather than merely inviting students to admire achievements already decided upon by professionals.

The [NASA history of Skylab 3](https://www.nasa.gov/history/skylab-3-return-to-skylab/) confirms that the mission launched on July 28, 1973, with astronauts Alan Bean, Owen Garriott and Jack Lousma. It also carried a small collection of living organisms for research: fish, eggs, fruit flies, mice and the two spiders. The word passenger is appealing, but it hides the roles involved. The animals were experimental subjects, not crew members making decisions about their own voyage. Their presence added a biological dimension to a mission focused on long-duration human spaceflight, solar observation and engineering.

The mission's actual student-experiment paperwork is far better than the sentence about spiders adapting to space. NASA assigned the experiment the designation ED-52 and treated it as one of a selection of proposals from high-school students. The [Skylab Student Project Report](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19740025164/downloads/19740025164.pdf?attachment=true) documents the selection, hardware, observations and difficulties encountered in orbit. It is a record of a small experiment conducted inside a much larger space mission rather than a public-relations story that ends the moment a nice web appears.

The program emerged from a collaboration between NASA and science educators. High-school students were invited to submit research questions that might be addressed in orbit. Some ideas required custom apparatus; others could use equipment already available on Skylab. Judith Miles's spider experiment was among the selected proposals. The educational value was obvious, but the selection was also an actual engineering commitment. NASA personnel had to make a student's question physically testable in an environment where every object, food source and procedure imposed constraints.

A spider constructs a web in the presence of several cues. Gravity may assist orientation and influence the tension of silk, but the animal also responds to a surface, its own movement and the developing structure. The student wanted to know whether the absence of the usual gravity cue would change the pattern. This was a hypothesis that could be investigated through photographs and comparisons. It did not require the spiders to learn mathematics or astronauts to provide a miniature drawing lesson.

The [Smithsonian's educational history](https://www.smithsonianeducation.org/students/secrets_of_the_smithsonian/spiders_in_space.html) describes the special cage, lighting and imaging equipment built for the experiment. The point of the container was not to make a comfortable home according to human aesthetics. It was to give the animal enough room and supporting surfaces to construct a web that could be observed and measured. If the cage forced the spider into an abnormal pattern, the experiment might be measuring the cage's design rather than gravity's effect.

The NASA report preserves a sequence that shows why one photographs the same process repeatedly. Arabella was released into the web-forming frame in early August. Initial structures were irregular and sometimes incomplete. She later produced more organized webs. On August 13, astronaut Owen Garriott removed part of an existing web, after which the spider consumed the remainder and did not immediately rebuild. Water was provided, and a new web followed. On August 21 the existing web was removed again, and a web discovered the next day was judged one of the best in the mission. These dates turn the slogan spiders learn to weave in space into a particular record of behavior under intervention and changing conditions.

The sequence also makes a causal inference difficult. When a web changes after water is provided, was the relevant cause improved hydration, experience in weightlessness, a response to losing the previous web, or a combination? The experiment does not permit every factor to be isolated. An astronaut could observe the effects but not always separate them. A biological study in orbit can generate unique evidence and also expose its subjects to conditions that make interpretation more complicated than a neat laboratory comparison.

On August 26 Arabella was returned to her vial and Anita was placed in the web-forming frame, according to the NASA report. Anita's initial behavior and later web construction could be compared with Arabella's sequence. The two spiders were not identical copies of a mechanism, however. Individual differences, their condition and the timing of release matter. The ability to compare two animals strengthens observation while remaining an extremely small sample for broad claims about spider behavior.

The [NASA technical account](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19740025164/downloads/19740025164.pdf?attachment=true) records that Anita was found dead on September 16 and that Arabella was also dead by the time the returned specimens were examined. The report described visible signs of dehydration. The distinction between observed condition and proven cause should be kept clear. A note about signs of dehydration is not a comprehensive postmortem explanation of every physiological factor. It does mean that the animals' access to water and general condition were genuine complications in interpreting the experiment.

The death of both subjects prevents an uncomplicated triumphal conclusion. The spiders spun webs in weightlessness, demonstrating some flexibility of the behavior. The support system did not sustain the animals to the end of the program in the way one might have wished. An experiment can produce valuable measurements and still teach its operators that the organism's welfare and basic requirements were inadequately handled. The negative lesson is part of the result.

The [1977 evaluation of the records](https://www.drpeterwitt.com/project/spider-web-building-in-outer-space-evaluation-of-records-from-the-skylab-spider-experiment-the-journal-of-arachnology-2/) adds another essential layer. Peter Witt and colleagues analyzed photographs and web samples, including preflight comparisons and the work completed after the space mission. The original scientists' interpretation was not simply that the webs looked normal. They examined geometric properties and the thickness of silk under conditions where gravity, stress and water status could all matter.

The study describes ground tests preceding the flight in which spiders were exposed to vibration and atmospheric conditions intended to approximate particular aspects of the launch environment. Comparing webs before and after those exposures helped researchers investigate whether observed changes might arise from non-gravitational stresses. The tests did not recreate all the conditions of space, but they recognized a basic experimental problem: an animal reaching orbit has experienced launch, confinement and altered care as well as weightlessness.

The researchers measured quantities including web geometry and silk properties. A web that looks broadly similar to an orb on Earth can differ in thread thickness, spacing or symmetry. An observer's impression of a normal web is not an adequate measurement if the scientific question concerns which cues organize construction. A photograph is useful because it can be studied repeatedly and converted into quantitative data, but it must be taken at an angle and resolution suitable for the particular measurements.

The [NASA photographs of Anita's web](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/nasa-photographs-volume-3/21/) describe the parameters Miles was examining: spirals, radial threads, angles, separation between elements and the area of the web. That list might look trivial to someone who expects space science to concern planets and rockets. Each parameter is a way to test whether a familiar behavioral pattern changed under an unusual physical condition. The experiment becomes science when the pleasing photograph is compared with explicit measurements.

The silk itself produced a result worth attention. The flight webs included threads finer than those observed in preflight construction. The [NASA report](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19740025164/downloads/19740025164.pdf?attachment=true) describes the difference and discusses its possible relation to weight sensing and thread production. The finding is interesting even if the ultimate mechanism remains open to interpretation. Gravity might affect how an animal manages silk tension or material allocation. But hydration and physiological condition complicate the picture. One measured difference should not be elevated into a universal explanation of web construction.

The scientific result therefore includes a modest but important separation. The spiders could create orb-like webs in orbit. The webs were not perfectly identical in every measured feature. The animals' condition and the procedures made it difficult to attribute every difference to weightlessness alone. That is a stronger account than saying they adapted completely in three days. It is also a better argument for why further experiments could be informative.

The experiment's educational form matters. A young person posed a well-defined biological question, an agency built equipment, astronauts performed observations, and later researchers analyzed the results. The chain connects public curiosity with specialized technology without asking the student to pretend to be a senior scientist or the astronauts to pretend their primary mission was arachnology. The work was collaborative, bounded and dependent on institutions with very different capacities.

The Smithsonian's [specimen records](https://www.si.edu/object/nasm_A19740484001) also clarify what survives. Arabella and Anita are preserved as biological specimens from a particular flight. Their presence in the collection connects the behavior recorded on camera with actual animals. It does not mean the spiders were interchangeable tokens of an abstract theory. They were living individuals whose responses and welfare affected the outcome. That is an unusually tangible reminder of what experimental subjects are.

The exhibit would need to give visitors both the successful web and the dates of the animals' deaths. The first is evidence of behavioral capability. The second prevents an easy story in which the environment presented no serious biological challenges. Both belong to the same NASA record. An honest experiment can be fascinating precisely because its successes and shortcomings are inseparable.

There was no need to call the spiders astronauts in order to make them interesting. They went into orbit, built webs and died during or at the end of a documented experiment. A teenager's question generated a record that researchers could study. The outcome was not a slogan about adaptation. It was a set of observations that made the question more precise.

The initial results were awkward. The spiders did not immediately build perfect familiar webs in weightlessness. NASA's [official 1973 chronology](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/1973.pdf) notes that their early webs were irregular and often built in corners. As the experiment progressed, the structures became more orderly. Astronaut Garriott observed and reported the changes. The gradual adjustment was significant because it suggested that web-building was not wholly dependent on an earthly gravitational cue. The spider could perform much of the behavior in a new environment, though the details changed.

The story is often compressed into a phrase about spiders learning to weave in space. That is evocative, but the scientific question is harder. Did the animals learn a new method during the mission, acclimate to unfamiliar conditions, or express an existing flexible behavior under changed physical cues? Those possibilities are not identical. The webs demonstrate performance. Establishing the mental process behind the performance requires more than noticing that the later webs looked better.

A [1977 analysis of the Skylab spider records](https://www.drpeterwitt.com/wp-content/uploads/1977-SpiderWebBuilding-small.pdf) compared the structures with webs constructed on Earth. The researchers found differences in thread characteristics and geometry. The absence of gravity could account for some of the deviations, but other factors such as stress and nutritional condition also mattered. The analysis is a useful correction to the casual claim that the spiders produced completely normal webs after a brief period of training. They produced informative webs. Informative and identical are not the same conclusion.

There is also a disturbing detail in the mission's biological record. Anita was found dead during the flight, in September. Arabella died before or by the time of return. NASA's experiment summary suggests dehydration as a likely contributor. The [Smithsonian's preserved specimens](https://www.si.edu/object/spider-anita-skylab-3%3Anasm_A19740484000) make the deaths materially present. A visitor may find the story charming because it involves tiny creatures in an enormous space program. The animals did not come home alive. Their welfare and the practical difficulties of supporting life in orbit belong in the account.

The mission took place when NASA was still learning the logistics of extended occupancy beyond the Earth. Equipment failed or needed adjustment, crews had limited time, and each scientific activity competed with others for attention and resources. The spider experiment was small in scale. It still required a cage, imaging procedures, feeding and water, protocols for releasing and retrieving the animals, and a plan for obtaining useful observations. The scientific result cannot be separated from those operational conditions.

A particularly revealing detail appears in [NASA's archived student experiment report](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19740025164/downloads/19740025164.pdf?attachment=true), which describes astronauts tending the spiders, handling webs and attempting to provide water and food. These were not machines that could be switched on and left alone. Their biological requirements affected the experiment and could themselves influence the results. When the animals' nutritional and hydration state changed, their web-building behavior might change for reasons unrelated to gravity. This is the difficulty of biological research in a new environment: many variables become entangled at once.

The experiment also provided a public opportunity to see science in progress. The question was accessible. Anyone who had seen a spider web could understand why the absence of gravity might matter. The observations were visual and relatively easy to compare. When the first webs were messy, the result was immediately legible. When the later structures became more regular, the change was equally clear. The public did not have to master a complex instrument to appreciate why the observations were interesting.

This accessibility can also make the science vulnerable to exaggeration. A photograph of an orbital web does not automatically reveal the spider's neural mechanism. A repeated pattern does not prove that all spiders can build equally well in every gravitational environment. A pair of animals is not a representative sample of all arachnids. The observed behavior is real. Its generalization to a much larger biological claim requires additional work.

The strongest version of the finding is therefore narrower than the popular slogan. In the Skylab experiment, two common cross spiders were able to produce orb-like webs in weightlessness after an initial period of abnormal construction. Their webs differed from Earth controls in measurable ways, and the animals' condition and mission procedures limited interpretation. NASA and later researchers learned something about the flexibility of web construction and the challenges of observing living organisms in orbit.

Why does that matter beyond the charm of two named spiders? Because many terrestrial behaviors rely on cues that are so constant we do not notice them. Gravity tells animals which way is down, affects movement and sensory input, and shapes how structures are built. Removing it is a way of asking which part of an observed behavior is hardwired, which part is flexible and which part depends on an external condition. An experiment in space can make the ordinary organization of life on Earth visible by changing one aspect of the environment.

The same difficulty applies when people ask what animals can do in other environments. An organism evolved under one set of physical and ecological constraints may remain capable under another, but its performance can change. A fish, insect or spider may show resilience without proving that it has suddenly evolved. The experiment does not require science-fiction claims about animals learning cosmic secrets. It requires careful observation of the limits of a familiar behavior.

I especially like that Judith Miles's question was specific. It did not ask whether animals could live indefinitely in space or whether the absence of gravity would transform consciousness. It asked whether spiders could spin webs. The experiment had a measurable output and a comparison method. A bold question can be narrow enough to answer. In fact, a narrow question is often the only way to turn curiosity into evidence.

The high-school origin also carries a danger of false inspiration. It is tempting to write that one teenager changed the history of space exploration by asking a simple question. That would exaggerate the scientific significance and flatten the efforts of researchers, engineers and astronauts. NASA's own [history of Skylab experiments](https://sma.nasa.gov/SignificantIncidents/assets/living-and-working-in-space.pdf) treats the educational value of student involvement as a major achievement while acknowledging the limited scientific advances from some projects. The experiment was important in part because students could see their questions taken seriously by a national research institution.

An experiment can be genuinely useful without being revolutionary. It can produce modest results, reveal procedural weaknesses and inspire better future questions. The scientific institution gains experience in working with small biological systems. The student gains a concrete understanding that a test may produce messy observations rather than a beautiful yes or no. The public gets an unusual demonstration of how familiar living behavior can be investigated.

A body in a vial at the Smithsonian is a different sort of artifact from Skinner's pigeon nose cone. The pigeon apparatus was built to see whether animal behavior could guide weapons. Arabella and Anita were carried to space to investigate their own behavior under unusual physics. The animals were still experimental subjects, and their welfare matters, but the purposes were scientifically and ethically distinct.

I would put the preserved spider beside a photograph of a web built in orbit, with the dated experimental notes nearby. The exhibit would need little narration. The object would show that a living animal traveled to space. The web would show what it did there. The notes would tell us what researchers could reasonably conclude.

Two spiders went into orbit and built webs. One student asked the question. The animals died, but the records came home. They are strange enough without any invented spider astronaut dialogue.
