# The Baby Who Went by Post

In January 1913, a boy in Batavia, Ohio, traveled about a mile to his grandmother's house with the mail. His parents paid fifteen cents and insured him for fifty dollars. The boy was entrusted to Vernon Little, a rural letter carrier, and delivered to the grandmother. This is the earliest case of a child being transported under the newly established American parcel post service described by the Smithsonian's [National Postal Museum](https://www.si.edu/collections/snapshot/special-delivery). The child's parents were Mr. and Mrs. Jesse Beauge of Glen Este. They did not place their son in a sack, glue stamps to his forehead, and send him through the sorting machinery. They arranged for a trusted person who regularly traveled that route to take him.

I find the truth considerably more disturbing than the joke. Somebody evidently saw a new federal service, read its possibilities, and worked out that a small child could be carried for fifteen cents. A child has to get to his grandmother. A mail carrier has a route. The arrangement seems to have met the needs of the parties who made it, if one puts aside the moment when a human being appears as an insured parcel. The government had built a service so new that the boundary between sending an object and transporting a person could briefly be confused by people who actually used it.

The confusion is easy to mock after more than a century of regulation. It is harder to mock before one remembers what parcel post represented. For Americans living far from large towns, receiving an item through the post could be more convenient than traveling to buy it. The new service began on January 1, 1913, after years in which private express companies and the postal service had divided the transportation of letters and parcels in particular ways. Its purpose was to move packages economically through the existing postal network, especially to communities that were otherwise expensive to serve.

There was a genuine institutional innovation underneath the child. A rural carrier was already traveling a repeated route and was already a trusted figure in the community. If the carrier could carry goods, perhaps he could carry something that was not ordinarily classified as goods. The invention made one category of transaction newly convenient. People found the category's edges before administrators had fully drawn them.

A government rarely knows in advance every use people will find for its services. This is not unique to postal delivery. A public road is built for traffic and becomes a parade route, an informal market, a place to exercise, or an obstacle to people crossing it. A telephone system is built to connect callers and becomes a platform for recorded messages, sales campaigns, emergencies, and scams. The public post office acquires a new transportation capability and immediately someone asks whether it can carry a child. That last question is peculiar enough to preserve as a museum anecdote. The administrative problem is ordinary.

The famous photographs do not help us understand it. One popular image shows a small child standing in a mailbag, a carrier looming nearby. Another presents the child's face and stamps in an arrangement clearly intended to delight or alarm. The Smithsonian explicitly identifies such pictures as staged photographs made for amusement. The real reported deliveries involved children traveling with known carriers. The photographs often circulate as proof precisely because they make the claim look less plausible, not more. They are visual jokes attached to a historical fact.

This is why the first question in the book is not whether something sounds ridiculous. It is what kind of evidence we are looking at. A photograph may be an authentic historical photograph and depict an imaginary arrangement. A newspaper report may be contemporary and describe a genuine event with imperfect detail. A later museum account may be carefully researched but depend on earlier institutional files. The words happened, photographed, recorded and reenacted cannot be substituted for one another merely because they all make a useful caption.

The 1913 boy was followed by other cases. The Smithsonian's account notes another child transported in February 1914, after which a question about the legality of mailing children prompted the Postmaster General to direct postmasters that human beings were barred from the mails. Even this did not instantly end the practice. More children were sent in the following period; the accounts cease in late 1915. The prohibition sounds absurd to modern ears because we assume the category was obvious. It became explicit because somebody had tested it.

There is a particular kind of bureaucratic prose that I admire. A rule may state that a human being cannot be mailed. It is a sentence nobody should need to write, and yet writing it may be the only way to end a specific practice. Most rules of the same type never make the news. They define whether a package can contain live animals, plants, potentially dangerous chemicals, perishable material, fragile goods, or things that cannot be insured in the ordinary way. A complex system draws thousands of boundaries that seem self-evident once they are established. The interesting moments occur where people find a space between them.

What did the parents believe they were doing? The historical record in the museum summary tells us that the trip occurred and describes the payment. It does not give us license to reconstruct an argument in the family kitchen, or to announce that the parents were unusually reckless or affectionate. They may have trusted the carrier. They may have thought the payment an ingenious arrangement. They may have regarded the whole transaction as a joke between people who knew one another. Those are possibilities, not findings. A novelist could select one and write a scene. This book cannot.

The absence of their testimony is a real constraint on the story. It limits how much we can know about motive, meaning and family relations. It does not make the event unreal. Some of the most fascinating records in public history preserve transactions without the inner lives of the people who made them. The parties saw a practical problem. The institution recorded a transaction. A century later the surviving paperwork and reports let us see the absurd shape of the solution without pretending we can read the participants' minds.

There was also an economic logic. Fifteen cents was a fee for a service with a regular route and some government support. It was unlikely to represent a comprehensive insurance premium for the risks of transporting a child. The fifty-dollar insurance figure belongs to a historical transaction, not a statement that an infant's life was worth fifty dollars to the family. To tell the anecdote as proof that Americans could once buy a child's safety for fifty dollars would be a grotesque misreading of a postal category. The record shows what the parents paid and how the parcel-post convention was used. It does not give a market valuation of a person.

I wonder how a modern service would encounter the same problem. A company offers local deliveries through a network of people with vehicles and travel routes. Users discover that a feature intended to move packages can be repurposed to transport vulnerable people. The platform's software may permit a booking before its legal and safety teams have considered the consequences. A driver may be trusted by a family but not trained or authorized to transport children. The booking system is delighted: an origin, a destination, and a paying customer. The real world is less easily reduced to fields.

The analogy is worth making carefully. A child traveling with a familiar rural carrier in 1913 is not the same as a child entering a stranger's app-based delivery vehicle in 2026. The technology, law, social relationships and operational controls differ. The common problem is category ambiguity. A service intended to make one kind of movement easy can make adjacent movements tempting before its responsibilities have been defined.

The Post Office's answer was not to create a child-delivery department with special rates. It was to prohibit the use. That decision may have disappointed particular families who found the arrangement harmless and convenient. It acknowledged that a parcel service was not structured to provide what transporting children properly requires. The best solution to a user-discovered feature is sometimes a line in the rules.

There is an important counterargument to the affectionate retelling of children in the mail. We like the story because it presents a world of charming small-town relationships, when everyone knew the carrier and a child could be entrusted to the postal route. This can become a nostalgia machine. It risks using the apparent trust of one family to claim that an earlier era was generally safer, kinder or simpler. Nothing in the incident proves any of that. It proves a narrow event occurred under conditions that the postal system later disallowed.

The historian's temptation runs the other way. The story can become an illustration of how foolish earlier Americans were compared with their enlightened descendants. That conclusion also demands more evidence. People constantly find uses for new institutions that legislators did not anticipate. When those uses are desirable, we celebrate creativity. When they are unsafe or socially unacceptable, we call them loopholes. The boundary is often discovered by trying the thing.

An archive preserves the uncomfortable interval between invention and prohibition. The boy in Batavia did reach his grandmother. The fee was paid. The government issued a later ruling about human beings in the mails. These facts are enough to show a real administrative experiment. They are also enough to refuse several ridiculous embellishments.

If I were exhibiting the episode, I would place the staged photograph beside the museum account, with the differences printed at equal size. Visitors would see the picture that made the story famous, then read the less theatrical details that make it historically defensible. They would have to decide which version they would tell at dinner. I suspect the photograph would win. It is too efficient an image.

That is a problem for anybody trying to write a strange but truthful book. The embellished story is usually less trouble to repeat. It comes preassembled with an ending, a visual and a reason for everybody to laugh. The documented version requires an extra sentence about how the evidence works. Once that sentence has been omitted often enough, even a real story begins to behave like a legend.

I would rather keep the extra sentence. I am suspicious of any miracle, scandal or grotesque administrative invention that becomes less interesting when the primary source is consulted. This one becomes more interesting. It tells us what a newly capable national institution looked like before its users and administrators agreed on what counted as a package. It also tells us how little a photograph can establish when everyone already knows what they want the picture to mean.

The surprising part is not that somebody thought a baby could travel with the mail. It is that, for a short period, the post office found itself obliged to say that a human being could not.
