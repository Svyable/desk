# The Fly Factory

The United States has used factories to produce enormous numbers of flies for the purpose of making a fly disappear. The insects are New World screwworms, a species whose larvae feed on living tissue in warm-blooded animals. They are a serious threat to livestock and wildlife and can infest people. The control program breeds flies, sterilizes them, and releases them into areas where wild populations remain. A wild female that mates with a sterile male produces no viable offspring. Repeated at sufficient scale under appropriate conditions, the reproductive population can be driven down.

This is one of the rare sentences in which releasing more of a pest can help eradicate it. The idea is not a metaphor. The [U.S. Department of Agriculture](https://www.aphis.usda.gov/animals/animal-health/livestock-and-poultry-disease/stop-screwworm/using-sterile-insect-technique) describes the sterile insect technique as a cornerstone of the screwworm control and eradication system. It reports that the method helped eliminate New World screwworm from the United States in 1966 and was used to respond to a small outbreak in the Florida Keys in 2017. Those are claims about a real public-health and agricultural intervention, not a speculative invention.

The biology is ugly enough to explain why governments would invest in such an unusual measure. Screwworm larvae infest living tissue rather than limiting themselves to decaying material. Animals with wounds can become hosts. A severe infestation can cause serious damage and death. The harm creates costs for livestock producers, animal welfare and veterinary systems. The agricultural consequences make the pest a problem for public agencies, not merely for individual farmers whose animals are affected.

Conventional control might focus on killing pests at the point where they are found. A farmer treats an infested animal. A surveillance program searches for outbreaks. Chemical methods may reduce local populations. Those approaches can be necessary but do not necessarily eliminate a widely distributed reproductive population. The sterile insect strategy tries to interrupt reproduction itself, using the species' own mating system as the place where control becomes possible.

The method depends on an inconvenient biological property: the female's mating behavior must allow sterilized males to compete successfully with fertile males. If females mate frequently with many partners under the conditions relevant to the species, the results can differ from a system in which a mating effectively determines the outcome for that female's offspring. Sterilized insects also have to survive handling, transport and release and remain capable of attracting mates. A perfectly sterile fly that cannot mate is a poor control instrument.

This makes the technique less like spraying a poison and more like deploying a manufactured biological population. The factory must maintain insect colonies, breed enormous numbers under controlled conditions, apply the sterilization process, check quality, and ship insects to release locations. The program needs information about where wild populations are present and how they change. It must coordinate with veterinary surveillance and animal movement controls. The apparent simplicity of the mechanism conceals an extensive operating system.

It is worth distinguishing sterilization from the popular image of genetic alteration. The traditional sterile insect method for screwworms uses irradiation to make released insects reproductively ineffective. It does not require creating a hereditary self-spreading genetic change in the wild population. The released insects are expected to fail to produce viable descendants. That is part of the strategy's appeal: its effect depends on repeated managed releases rather than on a modified trait spreading indefinitely through the environment.

The method also avoids a common misunderstanding. Sterile males are not released because they physically attack or poison the wild flies. Their function is reproductive. They occupy mating opportunities that would otherwise generate the next generation. In a successful program, the effect emerges over population cycles. A person might see a large number of flies released and wrongly infer that the government is making the infestation worse. The biological mechanism works through what does not happen afterward.

The [USDA's 2026 account](https://www.aphis.usda.gov/animals/animal-health/livestock-and-poultry-disease/stop-screwworm/using-sterile-insect-technique) discusses efforts to improve production efficiency, including a male-only strain. The distinction matters because males are the insects needed for sterile mating. Producing and shipping females that cannot contribute to the desired reproductive interference can use resources without delivering the same benefit. The program's refinement is an engineering problem: how can the same public-health effect be obtained with fewer production and transport requirements?

None of this means sterilized releases work automatically. A region may contain an unusually dense wild population. New insects may arrive across borders or through the movement of infected animals. Weather can affect survival and distribution. A control program may lose ground if releases are insufficient or if surveillance misses an outbreak. The economics are ongoing and regional. It is easier to count insects produced than to prove the durable absence of a pest across a broad landscape.

Eradication is a stronger claim than suppression. A program that reduces cases for a season has accomplished something useful but may not have removed the reproductive population. A program that eliminates a pest from one country can still need defenses against reintroduction from neighboring regions. The 1966 U.S. success therefore did not mean the organism vanished from the planet. The history includes continued cooperative efforts in other countries and continuing vigilance. The target is a moving biological population, not a single machine that can be shut off.

I find the institutional engineering as interesting as the entomology. A factory can be staffed and managed like an industrial operation. Its output is live insects whose quality depends on both manufacturing controls and biological vitality. A batch can be uniform in number but variable in its ability to compete in the wild. Quality control has to evaluate sterility, survival, flight and mating performance, not just whether a box contains the expected count.

The industrial scale can be measured in flies per week, which is one of the least appealing units in a government capital budget. As of October 21, 2026, the [USDA's production-facilities report](https://www.aphis.usda.gov/animals/animal-health/livestock-and-poultry-disease/stop-screwworm/sterile-fly-production-dispersal) described a plant in Pacora, Panama, operating through a binational commission called COPEG. The facility produced and dispersed approximately one hundred million sterile screwworm flies every week. This was an existing operational program, not a proposed factory awaiting a groundbreaking.

An institution capable of manufacturing a hundred million insects each week looks very different from the laboratories in which the sterile insect concept was originally established. It requires regular supply of material for rearing larvae, environmental controls, personnel, quality assurance, sterilization capacity, distribution arrangements and a program for monitoring results outside the factory. A breakdown at any one stage can reduce the quality or quantity of insects released. The production line is biological, so it cannot be evaluated entirely through the industrial measures used for bolts or bottles.

The Panama facility's location also explains why a U.S. animal-health problem cannot be solved by American farmers acting alone. Screwworm populations do not respect national borders. Moving infected animals can introduce larvae into new areas, and wild populations can expand when environmental conditions permit. A regional program needs surveillance, veterinary cooperation, movement controls and production capacity in places connected by the animals' ecology rather than by the location of a single country's agricultural headquarters.

COPEG's binational structure matters. The [USDA account](https://www.aphis.usda.gov/animals/animal-health/livestock-and-poultry-disease/stop-screwworm/sterile-fly-production-dispersal) identifies joint management and funding by the United States and Panama. This is an unusual kind of infrastructure: a facility owned and operated through international cooperation to produce insects that will be released into landscapes where they can interrupt a pest's reproduction. The immediate material output of the factory does not enter a retail market. The social value is the protection of livestock, wildlife and people from future infestations.

The arrangement raises questions of accountability. A country contributing to the facility wants evidence that production and release reduce risk. Another country hosting or supporting the program wants assurance that its own animal-health needs are respected. Producers require guidance about detecting and reporting cases. Veterinarians need diagnostics and treatment options. The quality of a single fly batch cannot reveal whether the regional system is succeeding. The relevant outcomes are patterns of confirmed infestations, spread, animal health and the durability of suppression.

The same October 2026 report describes a renovated production facility at Metapa, Mexico. The department stated that renovation was completed in late June 2026 and that operations were being increased toward an intended capacity of one hundred million sterile screwworm flies a week. The distinction between operational status and full design capacity matters. A new facility can begin producing insects before it consistently reaches its planned output. A press release celebrating construction is not an audited production record.

There is also a planned domestic facility at Moore Air Base in Texas, a location selected for a substantial new sterile-fly production operation. The USDA stated that construction was under way, in partnership with the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, and gave an intended capacity of three hundred million flies a week. That number describes a projected capability, not a current flow of insects. The department's own status categories let a careful reader separate a working plant, a plant increasing production and a facility still being built.

The figures are startling because the operation is meant to reduce a population. Producing more sterile flies is useful only if the insects are released in conditions where they can compete with wild males and disrupt reproduction. A program could build more capacity than it can use effectively in a particular region. It could also face an outbreak requiring rapid expansion beyond ordinary production. Public authorities have to decide how much spare capacity to maintain and whether additional factories are justified by the risk of a larger spread.

The distribution stage is often less visible than manufacturing. Sterile insects must reach the areas where wild populations remain. If released too far away, they may not encounter the females whose reproduction the program hopes to interrupt. If released at the wrong time, a local population can continue growing. If the insects are damaged during handling, an apparently successful production count can translate into poor mating competition. The intended effect occurs through millions of encounters in a landscape rather than through the direct action of a machine that can be observed on demand.

This makes surveillance a central part of the system. Authorities need to know where infestations are occurring, which animals are at risk and how the population changes after interventions. A low rate of reported cases could indicate effective control. It could also mean that local detection or reporting is weak. An honest evaluation has to distinguish a decline in the organism from a decline in the observations being made. The lesson is familiar from public health, where a disease can become less visible when testing drops even if transmission has not.

There is a further operational issue concerning insect quality. A fly can be sterile but ineffective as a mate if the handling process damages its ability to survive, disperse or compete. It can be physically healthy but insufficiently sterile for the program's goal. A production facility therefore needs biological performance measures that look beyond how many individuals emerge from a rearing system. The quality of the intervention lives in the insect's behavior after release, much of which cannot be followed directly for every individual.

The goal of male-only production, described by [USDA's sterile insect technique program](https://www.aphis.usda.gov/animals/animal-health/livestock-and-poultry-disease/stop-screwworm/using-sterile-insect-technique), addresses the efficiency of the release. Male insects are the necessary participants in the sterile mating mechanism. Producing females that will not contribute the same reproductive interference can require resources and complicate release operations. A strain or processing system that increases the proportion of usable males could improve the relationship between factory output and field effect. The benefit has to be measured in actual mating performance and costs, not assumed from a cleaner-looking production statistic.

The broader biological history explains why mass releases are not equivalent to introducing a new self-reproducing species. Traditional sterile-insect programs aim to prevent viable offspring. Each effective generation of the released insects is an intervention supplied by the factory. If releases cease while wild populations remain or are reintroduced, the control effect may weaken. The program therefore creates an ongoing public service whose future costs must be weighed against the damage it prevents. Eradication in a region can permit production to be concentrated elsewhere, but the threat of reintroduction may require a long-term barrier.

This makes the program almost the opposite of a conventional pesticide sale. A pesticide manufacturer may profit from selling more product to users who respond to visible infestations. A public sterile-fly operation measures success partly by reducing the number of future cases and, in some settings, the long-run need for intensive intervention. The factory's output and the social problem it addresses move in opposite directions when the system works. A budget officer who rewards the greatest number of insects produced without assessing the resulting animal-health benefits could favor the wrong outcome.

The technology is also not a magical exemption from ecological risk. A program must understand the target species, the possible effects of release activity and the characteristics of the receiving environment. It should not assume every insect can be controlled by the same reproductive trick. For species with different mating patterns, dispersal, longevity or population dynamics, the economics can be entirely different. The success of screwworm control is evidence for a particular, deeply studied biological application, not a promise that every pest can be eradicated by building a larger insect factory.

The most extraordinary thing about the facility may be that it has become ordinary enough to appear on an agency website beside construction schedules and procurement plans. The government does not need to hide an impossible biological invention. It needs to describe which facilities are operating, which are being expanded and how production capacity will contribute to controlling a real pest. The bizarre premise survives contact with the budget because the mechanism has been used and measured in the field.

There is a social achievement beneath the technical one. An agricultural sector can benefit from a coordinated public service whose outputs are difficult for any single farmer to purchase or manage alone. One producer releasing a few sterile males into a large wild population may accomplish little; regional coverage can produce a much larger effect when it is organized across borders and sustained through time. The collective organization is part of the technology, as essential as the insects themselves.

A hundred million flies per week is a number that sounds like a threat in an ordinary conversation. At the Panama facility it describes a real protective intervention. The flies are produced so that future generations of a parasite will not be. That strange economic and biological inversion is what makes the factory worth including in a book of documented impossibilities.

The distribution system is equally peculiar. An ordinary factory tries to put its product into the hands of consumers. A sterile-insect factory tries to distribute its product into ecosystems so that its biological effect is felt through encounters no worker directly observes. The useful result is fewer future insects. A transport network, release schedule and surveillance program together determine whether those invisible encounters are frequent enough to alter the population.

This is why the headline government breeds flies to eliminate flies is accurate but incomplete. The method would not work merely because a government built a very large insect breeding facility. The strategy requires a population model, knowledge of the species, reliable sterilization and continuous measurement. If insect quality is poor or release coverage inadequate, the output can be enormous without producing the intended result. Counting boxes of insects leaving a factory would be a dangerously inadequate measure of success.

The ethical considerations are different from those of Skinner's pigeons or the bat bomb. Sterile-insect programs are designed to reduce the suffering caused by a parasitic pest. The insects themselves are nonetheless bred and manipulated for human ends. A thoughtful account should acknowledge the animals involved while also recognizing the serious harms the wild population causes to hosts. There is no easy universal principle that decides every case of human intervention in ecology. Here the public-health and animal-welfare benefits can be substantial, but the operation still requires environmental judgment.

There are wider ecological questions. Eliminating one species from a region can alter relationships with other organisms. The relevant assessment should consider actual consequences, not assume that every fly performs an irreplaceable ecological service or that no effect is possible. Screwworms are a particular parasitic problem with a documented control history. The fact that sterile insect releases have been effective against them does not prove the approach will work equally well for every other insect, whose mating and dispersal biology may be different.

The record demonstrates something almost counterintuitive about technology. Sometimes the most effective way to control a biological system is to understand its behavior well enough to make an intervention inside that behavior. The release of sterile insects is not a brute-force attempt to kill all visible pests. It is an engineered failure of reproduction, repeated across a landscape.

The original innovation required people to accept a peculiar tactical move: make more flies, then release them. That is the kind of plan one expects an audience to reject when proposed in a fictional committee meeting. The documented results forced agricultural authorities to consider it seriously and eventually operate it at scale.

Unlike the pigeon-guided missile, the fly program did not remain a promising prototype. Unlike the CIA cat, it achieved the behavior its designers needed from the animals under relevant conditions. Unlike the bat bomb, its intended social effect was protective rather than destructive. The comparison is not meant to make the insects into heroes. It shows why the function of animal behavior cannot be understood merely from how odd it looks to an observer.

A factory can produce thousands or millions of insects and have its greatest success in the absence of insects later. It sounds like a bureaucratic paradox. It is a real pest-management technology, recorded by the agency that operates it.
