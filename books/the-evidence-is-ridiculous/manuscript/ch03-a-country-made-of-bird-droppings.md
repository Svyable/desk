# A Country Made of Bird Droppings

On August 18, 1856, President Franklin Pierce signed a law under which a citizen of the United States could discover an island with valuable deposits of bird droppings, occupy it under stated conditions, and ask the president to treat the place as belonging in some sense to the United States. The legal word was *appertaining*. It was a wonderfully elastic term for a moment in history when fertilizer could help pull a piece of land into a country's territorial orbit. The law was the Guano Islands Act. It remains one of the most remarkable examples of a national government writing its appetite for a specific natural resource into a mechanism of territorial acquisition.

The [House of Representatives' own history](https://history.house.gov/HouseRecord/Detail/15032450277) gives the essentials. Guano, the accumulated droppings of seabirds, was rich in nutrients farmers wanted to put into depleted soil. In the nineteenth century, demand for fertilizer became a commercial and political problem. Access to major sources was constrained by overseas trade and control. American farmers and entrepreneurs were looking for ways to obtain the material, and legislators responded with an arrangement that treated small, otherwise neglected islands as strategic sources of agricultural wealth.

The easiest version of the story says that the United States went around annexing islands made of bird poop. It is nearly irresistible. It is also incomplete in ways that matter. A deposit of guano does not make the ground itself a pile of waste. The island may contain geological structures, habitats and ecological relationships beyond the commodity. The law did not grant anyone an automatic private right to declare sovereignty over a foreign government's territory. Its language required that an island or rock not be within the lawful jurisdiction of another government and not be occupied by the citizens of one. The president retained discretion over whether to consider the island as appertaining to the country.

The statutory conditions created plenty of room for dispute. Who knew whether a remote place was within another state's lawful jurisdiction? What would count as occupation? Could someone who had arrived recently make a valid discovery claim for an island already known to sailors, fishermen or nearby communities? Would an affidavit made in the United States settle matters on the ground? It was a legal tool designed to speed a resource acquisition process, and in the process it forced the government to confront questions of territory and authority.

The language can still be read in the [Interior Department's account of island acquisitions](https://www.doi.gov/oia/islands/acquisitionprocess). A citizen who found a deposit could take possession under the conditions of the law and report the facts. Officials could investigate, and the president could decide whether to accept the claim. The department's list includes places such as Baker Island, Howland Island, Jarvis Island, Johnston Atoll, Kingman Reef and Navassa Island. Those names are a reminder that the mechanism had actual geographical consequences. The Act was not a satirical bill introduced to make the public laugh.

At a time when modern readers may think of islands as vacation destinations or military outposts, the economic interest in accumulated seabird excrement seems comically disproportionate to the legislative effort. The perspective changes when one takes agriculture seriously. Farmers remove nutrients from fields when they harvest crops. Those nutrients have to be replaced if the soil is to remain productive. Before the modern nitrogen fertilizer industry, access to concentrated naturally occurring fertilizer was capable of affecting yields and the economics of cultivation. Guano was not a novelty ingredient. It was a valuable commodity in a world searching for dependable supplies of soil fertility.

The material was valuable enough to support difficult extraction, shipping and storage operations. A remote island could be commercially important despite its distance from a large population. The costs of transporting fertilizer across the sea would be weighed against its value in American agricultural districts. A country that depended on imports could view control over some sources as a matter of practical economic strategy. Those considerations do not make territorial acquisition ethically uncomplicated. They explain why legislators who would never dream of building a nation around a joke found guano worthy of federal law.

An early House committee had investigated the trade. The [1854 report on the guano trade](https://history.house.gov/HouseRecord/Detail/15032450277) preceded the Act by two years. The House's historical account connects the law with import difficulties and trade constraints involving other countries. That chronology is important. The United States did not spontaneously invent a bizarre new method of expansion because somebody thought an island smelled profitable. The law emerged from a recognizable problem in the agricultural economy and an existing set of international commercial relations.

Yet the resulting solution was remarkable even by the standards of economic legislation. One might expect a government to subsidize trade, negotiate contracts or support domestic fertilizer production. Here the proposal opened a path by which private resource seekers could become agents of a country's territorial ambitions. A citizen's discovery and occupation could initiate a process culminating in a national claim. The ordinary distinction between commercial prospecting and foreign policy began to blur.

The most consequential demonstration of the Act's reach arrived not in a fertilizer shipment but in a homicide prosecution. The case was *Jones v. United States*, decided by the Supreme Court in 1890. The island in question was Navassa, a small place in the Caribbean. An American company mined phosphate there. Laborers worked in conditions that produced serious conflict. In September 1889, an uprising on the island led to deaths. The federal government prosecuted Henry Jones for murder. A dispute over guano had become a dispute about whether American criminal law could punish a person for acts committed on a remote island.

This was no academic puzzle about a dot on a map. The [Supreme Court opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/137/202) records the workforce and the methods of extraction. On the day described in the record, the Navassa Phosphate Company employed 137 Black laborers and eleven white officers or supervisors on the island. The work included digging, blasting, moving material by a manually propelled railway and loading it aboard ships. The document preserves the structure of an operation whose products were valuable far away, but whose workers were living and laboring in extraordinary isolation.

A modern reader has to sit with that detail. The opening description of guano islands makes the statute sound like a comic territorial scavenger hunt. The litigation reveals a system of labor, physical danger, racial inequality and corporate authority. A law ostensibly written to provide fertilizer to farmers had helped establish a regime in which people traveled to a remote island to dig valuable material under conditions that became violently contested. The joke about bird droppings cannot carry the ethical weight of that history.

The opinion does not, by itself, tell us all the grievances that led to the uprising. It contains testimony and allegations produced during criminal litigation. Defendants, witnesses, prosecutors and company officials had different interests in how the events were described. A serious history of the workers' lives would have to consult those records and other contemporary evidence, rather than reducing a labor conflict to the single legal question that eventually reached the Supreme Court. The judgment can establish what the federal courts decided about jurisdiction; it does not automatically establish a balanced social history of Navassa.

The jurisdiction problem was unusual. Navassa was not one of the states. It had been treated by the executive branch as appertaining to the United States under the Guano Islands Act. Congress had provided that certain federal criminal laws could apply to offenses committed on qualifying islands. Jones challenged the government's authority in circumstances where traditional territorial categories did not map neatly onto the place where the alleged crime occurred.

The [Court's decision](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/137/202/) upheld the constitutionality of the Act and the application of the federal criminal provisions. The justices treated the determination of territorial status by the political branches as a question the courts were generally bound to recognize. The case therefore did far more than decide whether guano was useful. It helped articulate the relationship among presidential recognition, congressional power and federal judicial jurisdiction over places obtained under an unusual statute.

The opinion describes how a private entrepreneur, Edward K. Cooper, had received recognition concerning Navassa in the nineteenth century and how the claim passed into a commercial operation. The government presented documents establishing that the island had been treated as connected to the United States under the 1856 Act. This was the legal chain from a commercial resource discovery to a criminal prosecution decades later. It is difficult to invent a better example of how a temporary economic premise can create enduring political obligations.

One might suppose that a company can operate abroad simply by contracting with workers and obeying whatever local law applies. Navassa forced the government to identify which public authority could exercise jurisdiction over events on the island. A company could not provide all the legitimacy required for criminal punishment. The state had to say on what basis its courts could try the accused. The resource claim and the executive recognition that once facilitated extraction thus became central to a murder case.

The opinion also contains a striking account of the distinction between possession and permanent annexation. A nation may exercise jurisdiction over territory acquired for a particular use even without bringing it into the same constitutional and administrative arrangement as its mainland. The justices drew on international-law concepts and the language of the Act. It is possible to disagree with aspects of the historical framework while recognizing the decision's importance. The island's legal status could not be decided merely by asking how long its fertilizer deposits would last.

The labor dispute also puts an uncomfortable limit on the idea that the law was simply entrepreneurial. The phrase citizen discovers deposit conjures an individual explorer who finds an unoccupied resource and offers it to the national economy. By 1889 the operation involved a corporation, a substantial workforce, managers and an elaborate movement of material from mine to ship. Private discovery and national jurisdiction had become the background for commercial activity involving coercive pressures and human consequences far beyond the original explorer's interests.

Consider the railway mentioned in the Supreme Court record. It was not the impressive iron route of a continental railroad advertisement. It was a means of moving extracted material around the island, propelled by human labor. The presence of a railway signals capital investment in making mineral extraction more efficient. The fact that humans supplied its motive power reveals something about how the burden of that efficiency was distributed. A resource may be valuable enough to draw a company across the sea while the people who produce it still perform exceptionally hard manual work.

The record is also a warning against using the word unoccupied too casually. A place can lack a resident population or recognized government claim under the language of a particular statute and still have ecological, navigational or cultural significance. More importantly, an island may become inhabited by people brought there to work, whose lives and rights then require more than a resource-access rule. The statute could begin by defining how a government claimed a commodity and end by testing how it governed people who had to live beside it.

The Supreme Court's recognition of territorial jurisdiction solved one legal problem: federal criminal law could reach acts on the island under the relevant provisions. It did not resolve every problem of worker protection, contract fairness or political representation. A government may possess authority to prosecute a crime and still face questions about how effectively it protected the people whose labor generated the wealth. Jurisdiction is necessary for many forms of accountability, but it is not identical to justice.

The Navassa story therefore offers a powerful counterweight to an amusing map covered in tiny guano claims. The government made a mechanism for accessing agricultural fertilizer. That mechanism supported a commercial occupation. Years later, a criminal dispute required a decision about the reach of the nation itself. The Supreme Court's opinion is part of the documentation. It gives this odd economic law a constitutional and human history.

There is another consequence for the image of America acquiring a kingdom of bird droppings. The Act did not automatically turn every claimed island into a permanently governed place with a fixed status. The executive branch exercised discretion, commercial interests changed, and later legal arrangements differed across places. The fate of a particular island must be investigated separately. A list of island names cannot substitute for a history of each claim's recognition, operation and subsequent status.

Navassa's geographic position added diplomatic complexity. It lay in a region where other states and populations had their own histories and interests. A claim of lawful possession by one government did not magically erase competing understandings. The 1890 Court treated the political branches' recognition as binding for the federal courts in the case before it. That was an answer to a question of American judicial authority, not a historical proof that every other party on Earth accepted the acquisition.

The phrase laws of nations in the opinion reflects a nineteenth-century legal vocabulary in which European and American powers often claimed territory using doctrines that deserve modern scrutiny. The fact that judges applied those doctrines is itself historical evidence. It should not be confused with an endorsement of every political assumption they carried. The historian's task is to explain what the court decided and the world in which it did so without pretending the decision settles all ethical questions for a later reader.

The court record also illustrates how easily a legal precedent can be misused by modern storytellers. The case is sometimes cited as though it proved that a citizen may still conquer any uninhabited rock containing droppings and thereby gain a private country. That is not the law's structure. The original statute relied on presidential discretion, legal conditions, and a public rather than private claim. International relations and later legal developments are not suspended by a colorful nineteenth-century phrase.

The case provides a useful ending to the episode's expansion. The material extracted from an island could improve soil elsewhere. The contracts and labor that produced it could become a source of conflict. The government that encouraged discovery had to reckon with its claim of authority over the place. A Supreme Court case about murder became an interpretation of a fertilizer statute.

The distance between a bird's nest and a federal courthouse was much shorter than anyone admiring the original economic idea might have expected.

The Act also contained an exit clause. As recorded in a [USGS historical bulletin](https://pubs.usgs.gov/bul/0817/report.pdf), the government was not obliged to retain possession after the guano had been removed. There is an efficiency to the wording that makes the imperial dimension harder to ignore. The place was valuable because it contained a resource. Once the material had been carried away, the government reserved the right to regard the island as disposable from its own standpoint. That is an astonishing proposition for land that may have had ecological meaning long before an American entrepreneur arrived.

The wording reveals something about the category being created. The island was neither an ordinary newly admitted state nor necessarily a permanent possession with the full protections and obligations that status might imply. It was a place the government could regard as connected to it for specified practical reasons. The phrase appertaining to the United States was useful because it did not settle every constitutional and diplomatic question at the moment the resource was claimed. Ambiguity was not a clerical error. It was part of what made the law politically workable.

This same ambiguity caused trouble. Historical studies note that more than seventy island claims were advanced under the Act, with confusion over names, positions and competing descriptions. The [USGS account](https://pubs.usgs.gov/bul/0817/report.pdf) describes erroneous geographical identifications and some islands assigned several names. The file was a geography problem as well as a foreign-policy problem. A remote rock could be claimed under one designation and later rediscovered in the papers under another. Maps were imperfect, journeys difficult and economic incentives strong.

This is the moment when the story stops being just a joke about bird droppings. A government was creating legal claims to real places based on information supplied by people with commercial incentives. Some of those claims were disputed or involved questions about existing connections to other states and peoples. The history belongs to the wider story of territorial expansion, maritime power and the ability of resource-rich countries to turn diplomatic and military capacity toward securing supplies. The material happened to be guano. The mechanism could serve many other commodities.

It is tempting to see the 1856 law as an eccentric extinct artifact. But the underlying problem has hardly disappeared. Modern governments and firms compete over minerals used in batteries, semiconductor production, energy systems and defense equipment. A resource found in a remote location can become strategically important long before most citizens learn its name. The law may be different, and the methods of acquisition constrained in other ways, but the appetite for control over a concentrated resource remains familiar. Nobody should equate a modern mineral supply agreement with an 1856 territorial claim. The analogy lies in the way an apparently obscure material can shape national policy.

There is a separate ecological consequence worth keeping in view. Seabird colonies occupy islands and produce guano through ongoing biological activity. Intensive extraction can alter habitats and disturb breeding grounds. The nineteenth-century legal framework was not a comprehensive system for evaluating the long-term effect of removing the deposits. It was designed to secure an economically valuable material. The interests of seabirds and the wider ecology appeared mainly as circumstances affecting resource availability. Modern conservation concerns make it easier to see what the original formulation excluded.

Imagine, as an exercise in legal reading rather than a claim about an actual meeting, a congressman explaining the Act to a voter who has never seen an ocean. The congressman would not need to describe exotic bird colonies. He could speak about fertilizer costs, farm productivity and securing supplies outside established monopolies. The listener might approve the policy without learning that the law gave private citizens a route toward territorial claims. A familiar economic objective can carry an extraordinary legal instrument inside it.

The original legislators' motives should not be rewritten by a modern author for comic effect. There were commercial interests and a documented policy debate. The Act's surviving text and the House's historical record show what was proposed and enacted. They do not tell us that every supporter was cynically pursuing empire, or that every opponent was concerned about ecology or foreign sovereignty. Specific votes and contemporary statements would be needed for those claims. The written mechanism is remarkable enough.

This book has begun with the postal service discovering the boundary of a parcel and a missile proving the limits of spectacular transport. The Guano Islands Act takes the same habit of stretching a familiar category into much more consequential territory. A farmer needs fertilizer. A private entrepreneur finds an island. A president may claim it for a country. The steps are linked by law, commerce and the strange reach of bureaucratic definitions.

There are now maps on which these islands have names and legal histories. Some have other strategic or conservation roles. Their present statuses cannot be inferred simply from a nineteenth-century fertilizer statute. That is part of the story's lengthening shadow: an economic device adopted for one era can create a political and legal inheritance in another.

If a novel introduced the premise that an empire expanded by inviting citizens to seize isolated islands covered with seabird droppings, an editor might ask for more believable motives. The actual Congress supplied the motives in a committee report. The law supplied the procedure. The maps supplied the places.

The joke belonged to the reader. The bill was serious.
