# The Evidence Is Ridiculous

*Twenty Things That Really Happened*

**Sven Hardy Benson**

## A Necessary Warning About the World

The nice thing about an invented story is that someone can be held responsible for its plausibility. A novelist who introduces a submarine that fires a missile full of mail can expect a reasonable objection: no government would do that. Why would it? The machine is expensive, the payload small, and the postmaster seems to have wandered into the wrong department.

On June 8, 1959, the United States Navy did precisely that. Its Regulus I missile carried three thousand letters from the *USS Barbero* to a naval station in Florida. The [Smithsonian National Postal Museum](https://postalmuseum.si.edu/collections/object-spotlight/regulus-missile-mail-letter) has the envelopes and the containers. There is a difference between saying the Navy proposed missile mail and saying the Navy fired the mail. In this instance it fired the mail.

It is possible to make a whole life out of being sensible and still end up living in a world that routinely overrules your instincts about what sensible institutions will do. I have gathered some of the more spectacular corrections in this book. Most arrived with paperwork. A few came with a surviving piece of equipment. Others are recorded in laboratory measurements or a film somebody had the unfortunate judgment to keep rolling.

I want the reader to be able to check every consequential assertion. The footnotes should not have to rescue the story; the evidence should create it. I also want the distinction between a thing that was proposed, a thing that was tested, a thing that worked, and a thing that merely acquired a good rumor. The British did discuss using chickens to warm an atomic land mine. That is sufficiently strange without announcing that a flock was placed in a live nuclear weapon. The United States flew an operating reactor aboard a bomber. It never flew that bomber using nuclear propulsion. A cat was surgically fitted with listening equipment by the CIA. The most popular endings to the story remain less reliable than the actual agency account.

Some chapters are funny. Others are not. A lake in Cameroon released enough carbon dioxide to kill at least seventeen hundred people in 1986. It does not become amusing because a lake that exhales sounds like a trick from a fairy tale. The same requirement holds in both directions: scientific restraint should not flatten a remarkable event, and an appetite for the strange should not turn someone else's disaster into a punch line.

Nothing in the following twenty chapters requires a visitor from another world, a hidden civilization, or a supernatural exemption to the ordinary laws of nature. The laws of nature are strange enough. So are governments, postal regulations, animal behavior and a surprising number of engineers.

The reader is permitted disbelief. I only ask that the disbelief be pointed at the right thing.
