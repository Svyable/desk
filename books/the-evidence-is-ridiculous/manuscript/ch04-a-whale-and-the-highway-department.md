# A Whale and the Highway Department

On November 12, 1970, the Oregon State Highway Division tried to dispose of a dead whale with dynamite. The whale had washed onto a beach near Florence. The highway division was responsible for the problem, and somebody judged that a controlled explosion would help remove the carcass. The result was not controlled in the way officials had hoped. Large pieces of whale were thrown across the surrounding area. One damaged a car. A television crew had the good fortune, from a professional perspective, to be there with a camera.

This story is famous because it exists on film. The reporter was Paul Linnman of KATU television in Portland. The cameraman was Doug Brazil. The original news report, preserved and remastered in cooperation with the Oregon Historical Society, can be viewed through the [archive of the footage](https://www.theexplodingwhale.com/evidence/the-video). A separate [annotated transcript](https://www.theexplodingwhale.com/evidence/resources/transcript) reproduces Linnman's report. The transcript matters because a later retelling can turn an already excellent mishap into something more spectacular than the film records.

The whale was approximately forty-five feet long and weighed an estimated eight tons, according to the contemporary report. The highway division believed the carcass could not simply be buried because beach conditions might later expose it again. The alternative chosen was to use explosives to break the animal into manageable pieces. The aim was not to celebrate dynamite or stage an entertainment. It was to solve a disagreeable disposal problem that had arrived without warning.

This is an important distinction. A government agency can make an absurd decision while addressing a real public need. A dead animal of that size is a considerable sanitation and logistical problem. Leaving it in place was not obviously satisfactory. Moving the entire carcass may have been difficult with equipment then available and the state of the beach. Burial could prove unreliable. The officials had reasons to consider other methods, even if their chosen method became one of the best examples of unintended consequences in the history of local news.

The footage shows officials preparing the explosive charge and spectators gathering at a distance. The blast sends material flying, accompanied by an enormous cloud. The actual film and the reporter's narration are sufficient to convey the scale of the mistake. No invented conversation in a state office is necessary. Nobody needs to claim that an engineer laughed maniacally or that officials had consulted a mysterious expert. The camera records what happened when the chosen plan met a very large physical object.

What does a controlled demolition require? The engineer needs to know the properties of the object, where force will go, what fragments will be produced and how far those fragments might travel. The object here was not a concrete structure whose behavior could be predicted with standard demolition assumptions. It was a large animal carcass, with a difficult mix of tissues, water and internal structure. The effect of an explosive charge could not be understood by the simple intuition that more energy would create smaller pieces. Too much energy can accelerate large fragments far beyond the intended work area.

I want to resist the easy modern pleasure of declaring everyone involved incompetent. The available evidence shows a decision that ended badly. To turn it into an indictment of the entire highway division would require broader evidence about its personnel, procedures and other work. Even an experienced organization can confront a rare problem for which established procedures are absent. The fact that the incident is so memorable partly reflects how unusual the combination was: a stranded whale, a government disposal responsibility, a proposed explosion, a film crew and a large, clearly visible consequence.

There is also a difference between looking foolish and acting maliciously. The highway workers did not set out to injure spectators or damage property. A poorly assessed hazard can harm people regardless of intent. Good intentions do not move the fragments back into the blast area. But understanding why an agency chose a method is essential to designing safer procedures for the next unusual event.

The annotated news transcript includes a remark from the reporter about a car that was damaged by falling debris. It is this physical consequence, not the spectacular blast alone, that should end the public-relations portion of the story. A piece of whale large enough to harm a car illustrates the energy transferred to material that was never meant to leave the immediate area. The incident could have caused injury. That prospect makes it a safety and risk-management event as well as a comic video.

A modern agency presented with a stranded marine mammal would face a different procedural world. Wildlife specialists, environmental authorities, coastal managers and contractors may have defined responsibilities. Options can include monitoring natural decomposition, towing, burial where appropriate, or other controlled methods depending on species, location and conditions. Marine mammals can also fall under particular legal protections and disease considerations. There is no universal recipe, and a large carcass can still be a problem. The lesson is not that every whale should be buried rather than exploded. It is that removal strategies require site-specific expertise and a serious account of hazards to people and the surrounding environment.

The 1970 event belongs to an era in which television was becoming especially capable of preserving local decisions in a form that could be replayed far beyond their original audience. A newspaper article might have described the experiment and its difficulties. The moving image made a legend durable. The film could be copied, shown to friends and rediscovered when the internet provided a new distribution system for archival footage. The government action did not become more dramatic in hindsight. The means of transmitting it became extraordinarily effective.

There is something comic about a public works accident that outlives the public works agency's original files. People who have never visited the Oregon coast can recognize the phrase exploding whale. The event becomes a story about how institutions make decisions, though its specific facts are often reduced to a single visual. A viewer remembers the blast. The viewer may not remember what problem the division was trying to solve, how large the whale was, what the reporters actually said, or which consequences were recorded.

The temptation to add details is strong. Online retellings can assign exact quantities of explosives with unwarranted confidence, invent elaborate meetings or turn a damaged automobile into a series of destroyed vehicles. Some versions treat the event as if officials fired the whale high enough to be seen from distant cities. The contemporaneous film can check many of these claims. Where the record does not settle a quantity, a responsible narrator should say estimated or omit the number. The core absurdity requires no enhancement.

The video's survival is a useful lesson about evidence for this entire book. A primary recording is powerful because it can discipline the imagination. It is not omniscient. A camera frames what the operator can see, records an interval, and may miss planning conversations or consequences outside the field of view. The reporter's narration adds contemporary interpretation, which can contain mistakes. The archival version may involve later transfers and annotations. A careful history uses the film as evidence of what it shows and looks elsewhere for questions it cannot answer.

That limitation is especially important when the story is funny. Audiences forgive comic material a degree of simplification that they would never accept in an account of a catastrophe. The explosion becomes evidence that everyone in government is an idiot, or that explosives have no place in land management, or that old public workers were somehow more reckless than modern ones. None of those sweeping claims follows from a single incident. They are the kind of comfortable generalization that a filmed mistake encourages.

The real failure may have been in the chain between a plausible disposal objective and an inadequately considered physical method. If the goal is to move or break apart a heavy object, the selected technique must be judged by where the material will end up. The energy is not useful simply because it is impressive. A public project is not finished when a blast occurs. It is finished when the unwanted material has been handled safely and the surrounding site no longer presents the original problem or a new one.

A particularly unfortunate aspect of the filmed outcome is that not all the carcass had been cleared. The original report suggests the explosion did not eliminate the problem as intended. The visible failure therefore operated on two levels: debris traveled beyond the safe area, and the primary task remained incompletely solved. If a method increases risk while failing to accomplish the original purpose, it deserves serious reassessment rather than a more energetic repetition.

I would like to know more about what the officials did after the cameras stopped. That question would require additional records rather than a confident reconstruction. Did the division adopt different procedures? Who decided what happened to the remaining whale? How were costs assessed? What did personnel write about the failed operation? Contemporary footage is an excellent starting point, but the institutional learning that followed is a separate subject.

There is a reason I have placed the whale after the guano islands. Both stories involve a public agency confronting a problem that seems laughable until the economic or administrative context is restored. Congress cared about bird droppings because farms required nutrients. The Oregon highway division cared about a dead whale because somebody had to manage a decaying carcass on a public beach. In one case the solution became a territorial law. In the other it became a lesson about explosive energy and public safety.

The distinction between them is not their capacity to make a reader laugh. It is that one changed the legal map while the other left a famous piece of film. Their absurdity begins from practical problems and becomes visible only when the chosen instruments are examined.

The footage is still worth watching. It is an unusually exact antidote to a kind of historical cynicism. A narrator could invent a fantastically foolish public works operation, fill it with dialogue and call the outcome inevitable. The surviving camera does something more useful. It shows a real plan, a real physical result and the limits of what can be known from an image. It leaves the author with no need to embellish the blast.
