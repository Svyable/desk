# The Evidence Is Ridiculous — editorial brief

**Title:** The Evidence Is Ridiculous
**Subtitle:** Twenty Things That Really Happened
**Byline:** Sven Hardy Benson
**Form:** narrative documentary nonfiction; twenty linked true episodes, essays rather than entries.

## Central question

How can the actual world repeatedly exceed invented absurdity—and what happens when the record is even stranger, more precise, or less flattering than the story people repeat?

**Reader:** someone drawn to weird history, anomalous science and unorthodox engineering who has grown tired of unsourced listicles, polished myths and the reflex to declare every unexplained fact paranormal.

**Contribution:** The previous Desk title *Ideas From The Future & Other Odd Things In My Head* poses hypothetical inventions and disciplines them with current evidence. This book starts from things that happened or were documented at the time and works backward through the evidence: specimens, war records, film, legislation, equipment, and field research. It must not become a sequel of imagined receipts, fake dialogue or future scenes. Nor should it reproduce the esoteric thesis structure of *Wild Speculation* or *The World Beneath the World*.

**Strongest competing view:** There is no serious book here, only an assortment of internet curiosities that a reader could browse in ten minutes. The response must be detailed causal history and scientific method: show institutional incentives, experiment design, human costs, uncertainty and the difference between the verified fact and the headline-sized myth. A chapter that offers only the oddity and an obvious moral has failed.

## Sequence

The first four episodes reveal how normal administrative problems produce surreal transactions. Five through ten explore military and intelligence projects, especially the boundary between actual prototypes and theatrical claims. Eleven through sixteen move into experiment and biological systems without pretending that organisms are little people. Seventeen through twenty return to technological ambition, environmental consequence and finally a naturally occurring nuclear reactor that was not engineered by humans. The ending should alter the meaning of the opening: not everything absurd is false, and not everything documented was sensible.

## Evidence discipline

- Never invent a scene or thought inside a participant's head. Describe documented acts, dates, designs and decisions.
- Keep words such as proposed, selected, tested, launched, observed, reconstructed and confirmed distinct.
- Be careful with the 1913 baby mail images; the famous photos are staged. The real deliveries involved trusted carriers, not children stuffed into sacks.
- Blue Peacock chicken heaters were a documented suggestion, not a verified operational deployment. The airplane reactor did not propel the aircraft. The CIA's competing tales about the cat's fatal ending cannot be promoted as fact.
- For animal behavior, distinguish the demonstrated experimental response from claims of humanlike intention.
- For Lake Nyos and Japanese balloon fatalities, keep the people and consequences central; no comic punch lines.
- Avoid tempting but unsupported numbers and quotations; prefer traceable records and state uncertainty.
- At each chapter's end, ask whether the evidence made the reader understand the particular incident differently. Do not end every chapter with an aphorism about humanity.

## Research tasks

The research ledger gives one to three real primary or institutional sources per case, while flagging myths and missing contemporary records. Keep the ledger distinct from narrative; a link is not a substitute for reading the actual report. Conduct the full-book literary/fact/rights/metadata check before any claim of publication readiness.
