## Author's Note

Competition is one of the first words business teaches us to see.

Competitors have market share. Competitors have features. Competitors have prices. Competitors appear in slides with checkmarks arranged into neat columns, as if the fate of a company can be read from whichever row has the most boxes filled in.

Sometimes that is exactly the right way to look.

Often it is not.

The businesses that become unusually hard to displace tend to accumulate advantages that do not fit comfortably into a comparison table. The product may be only part of the reason customers stay. The rest can live in habit, training, integrations, supplier relationships, distribution, credibility, data, institutional memory, accumulated process knowledge, or the simple fact that replacing the product would require replacing a portion of the customer's own organization.

None of these advantages is mystical. None is permanent. Some deserve admiration; others deserve scrutiny.

This book is about learning to see them before they become obvious.

“Win without an opponent” does not mean destroy competition. It does not mean seek legal protection from rivalry, coordinate with competitors, lock customers in against their interests, or confuse monopoly rent with excellence.

It means build something for which the obvious alternative is no longer equivalent.

That is a different game.
