# Chapter briefs — Autonomy Half-Life

## Ch 1 — The Permission That Outlived the World
Open with a plainly labeled composite agent scenario: every credential remains technically valid while the purpose and facts underneath it change. Establish the difference between valid permission and live justification. Introduce the book’s governing question: what gives an actor the right to continue?

## Ch 2 — Authority Has a Birth Certificate
Show that every delegation rests on conditions even when the conditions are implicit: purpose, facts, scope, risk, identity, resources, accountability, expected change. Connect to principal-agent theory and mission command. Argue that later evaluation is impossible if the grant’s rationale was never recorded.

## Ch 3 — The Clock Is the Wrong Meter
Use OAuth expiration, distributed-system leases, and legal sunsets to show why time bounds are powerful. Then expose their limitation: age is a proxy for staleness. Contrast stable long-running authority with rapidly invalidated authority.

## Ch 4 — The World Moves Sideways
Borrow carefully from concept drift. Models become obsolete when relationships or distributions change; authority can become obsolete when relevant conditions change. Stress that this is an analogy, not an assertion that social legitimacy is a machine-learning loss function.

## Ch 5 — The Mandate Gap
Separate capability, access, trust, instruction, and authority. A system may be able to act, hold a valid token, and even be historically reliable while lacking a live mandate for the present action.

## Ch 6 — Permission Debt
Describe stale authority as a stock of permissions whose original reasons are no longer visible. Show how organizations accumulate exceptions, admin rights, standing approvals, and inherited scopes. Debt grows quietly because revocation requires attention while retention is automatic.

## Ch 7 — The Emergency Never Ends
Examine emergency powers and sunset clauses. Show both the value of automatic expiry and the failure mode of ritual reauthorization. Derive a principle: renewal must reopen the factual premise of the grant, not merely extend the date.

## Ch 8 — The Delegate’s Delegate
Explore recursive delegation. Every hop can preserve formal scope while losing context. Connect to 2026 agent-delegation proposals that constrain scope and TTL down a mandate chain. Add the book’s claim: context should also become harder to inherit as delegation depth grows.

## Ch 9 — The Quiet Coup of Convenience
Study temporary exceptions that become permanent through inertia. The “coup” is structural, not conspiratorial: convenience rewrites authority because continued use is cheaper than reconsideration. Show how defaults determine institutional memory.

## Ch 10 — Read-Only Before Off
Reject the false choice between full authority and sudden revocation. Develop graduated modes: observe, recommend, prepare, simulate, draft, execute reversible actions, execute irreversible actions. Decay should often reduce consequence before it reduces awareness.

## Ch 11 — The Cost of Asking Again
Make the strongest case against the thesis. Renewal consumes time, attention, and managerial bandwidth; it can centralize power and suppress initiative. Use mission-command logic to show why rigid reapproval can be dangerous. Define the design goal as selective friction.

## Ch 12 — Trust Is Not Authority
Separate competence and reliability from legitimacy. A trustworthy actor may still lack permission for a new purpose; an authorized actor may be poorly trusted. Discuss Progressive Trust as adjacent work while preserving the distinction between trust decay and authority reduction.

## Ch 13 — Machines Do Not Wait for Monday
Explain why autonomous agents make the issue more acute: they can operate asynchronously, retry, delegate, and act at machine speed. Use current Internet-Draft work to show the emerging authorization gap. Focus on the delay between human intention and external effect.

## Ch 14 — The Price of Reversibility
Argue that the shorter half-life should attach to actions with greater irreversibility, externality, or propagation. Sending money, publishing, deleting, signing, or changing production deserves fresher authority than reading, drafting, simulating, or staging.

## Ch 15 — The Distance to the Grant
Present the book’s core design vocabulary without a fake universal formula. Compare current action with original purpose, facts, counterparties, risk, scope, delegation depth, resources, and reversibility. Treat differences as a structured review, not a magic score.

## Ch 16 — Designing a Decay Budget
Translate the thesis into architecture. For each authority class, decide which changes should force narrowing, escalation, or renewal. Use leases, policy engines, scoped tokens, approval thresholds, and human escalation as examples rather than one mandatory implementation.

## Ch 17 — Renewal Is Evidence
Define meaningful renewal as evidence production. Require the renewer to establish what remains true, what changed, and why the mandate still fits. Warn against checkbox renewal and “evergreen” approvals that preserve the ceremony while removing review.

## Ch 18 — The Principal Can Drift Too
Complicate the principal-agent model. The authorizer can change incentives, lose expertise, be captured, leave office, or cease to represent affected people. Sometimes reauthorization must come from a different or broader principal.

## Ch 19 — Institutions That Remember Why
Argue for provenance of authority: not only who approved and when, but why, under which assumptions, and for which expected range of variation. Institutional memory makes future decay legible and future renewal cheaper.

## Ch 20 — The Right to Continue
Return to the normative claim. Autonomy should be generous at the moment of action but modest about permanence. The right to continue should be continually supported by fit between mandate and world. End on the idea that durable autonomy requires a mechanism for admitting that the world has changed.
