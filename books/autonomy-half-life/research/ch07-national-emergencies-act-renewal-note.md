# Chapter 7 research note — annual termination, continuation, and emergency persistence

## Why this belongs in the chapter

Chapter 7 argues that sunsets are useful because they reverse the default: continuation requires an affirmative act. It also argues that a formal renewal can become weaker than it looks if the institution merely renews the date without re-examining the factual predicate that justified exceptional authority.

The U.S. National Emergencies Act provides a unusually clean primary-source example because it does both things at once. Section 202(d), codified at 50 U.S.C. § 1622(d), provides for automatic termination of a national emergency on its anniversary unless the President publishes a continuation notice in the Federal Register and transmits it to Congress within the statutory window. The mechanism is therefore genuinely expiration-by-default, not merely advisory review.

Primary source: U.S. Code, 50 U.S.C. Chapter 34, National Emergencies, including § 1622(d): https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&path=/prelim@title50/chapter34

## The empirical stress test

The statutory backstop does not imply that emergencies will be short.

On November 5, 2025, the President continued for another year the national emergency with respect to Iran first declared by Executive Order 12170 on November 14, 1979. The 2025 notice states that relations with Iran had not normalized and that implementation of the January 19, 1981 agreements remained ongoing; on that basis it continued the emergency beyond November 14, 2025.

Primary source: Federal Register public-inspection notice, Continuation of the National Emergency With Respect to Iran, filed November 2025: https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2025-19829.pdf

This is useful to the chapter because it prevents an over-neat lesson. An annual sunset can force an explicit act of continuation for decades without forcing the underlying legal category to disappear. The mechanism creates a recurring decision point; it does not guarantee that the decision point will produce contraction.

A separate March 2, 2026 continuation notice for the national emergency declared in 1995 with respect to Iran makes an additional distinction that the chapter should preserve: one country or policy area can sit under multiple legally distinct emergencies with different predicates and histories. The 2026 notice explicitly says the 1995 emergency is separate from the 1979 emergency and that its renewal is distinct from the November 2025 renewal.

Primary source: Federal Register, Continuation of the National Emergency With Respect to Iran, March 2, 2026: https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/full_text/html/2026/03/05/2026-04450.html

## What this sharpens

The strongest version of the chapter's argument is not that sunset clauses fail because governments simply ignore them. Under the National Emergencies Act, the annual continuation requirement is real and publicly observable. The stronger point is that **procedural recurrence and substantive reconsideration are different things**.

The statute makes the holder of authority return to the question. But a continuation notice can still operate at a broad level: the President states that the relevant circumstances continue and renews the emergency for another year. That may be entirely justified. It also means the chapter should not treat the existence of a renewal vote or notice as evidence that each component of the authority bundle has been independently revalidated against current conditions.

This strengthens the manuscript's later proposal to map predicates to powers rather than treat “the emergency” as one boolean state. A renewal can be legally proper while still leaving open the design question: which present condition supports which present power?

## Strongest counterargument / disconfirming evidence

Long duration is not evidence that a national emergency is illegitimate, obsolete, or maintained in bad faith. Some threats, foreign-policy disputes, sanctions regimes, or unresolved legal obligations can persist for many years. The 2025 Iran notice gives an affirmative current rationale for continuation; the 2026 notice concerning the separate 1995 Iran emergency similarly identifies ongoing conduct that the President determined continued to pose an unusual and extraordinary threat.

The National Emergencies Act should therefore not be used as a morality tale in which longevity proves abuse. The better counterfactual is procedural: without the annual termination rule, continuation would require no affirmative public act at all. The statute can improve accountability even when the outcome is repeated renewal.

A second boundary matters. The continuation notice renews the national emergency as a legal state, but the exact authorities exercised under that emergency can change over time through subsequent executive orders, statutes, regulations, sanctions changes, litigation, and administrative practice. The manuscript should avoid implying that a 1979 power bundle simply survived unchanged for more than four decades.

## Editorial use

A later prose pass could replace some of Chapter 7's abstract discussion of “renewal theater” with this concrete contrast:

- the National Emergencies Act really does make annual continuation affirmative rather than automatic;
- the 1979 Iran emergency was nevertheless renewed again in November 2025;
- that does not prove the renewal was wrong;
- it shows why a recurring expiration date and a recurring examination of the original factual predicate are not the same institutional design.

Keep the example short. Its value is the surprise of the chronology and the precision of the boundary, not a detour into sanctions law or presidential-power doctrine.
