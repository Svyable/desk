# Chapter 7 research audit — the review Congress was told to hold

## The factual correction

Chapter 7 currently describes sunset-style renewal as though the legislature predictably reaches the renewal question: “The renewal happens on schedule. The legislature votes. The formal requirement is satisfied.” That is a useful model, but it is not a safe description of the U.S. National Emergencies Act.

Section 202(b) of the National Emergencies Act, 50 U.S.C. § 1622(b), says that no later than six months after a national emergency is declared, and every six months thereafter while it continues, each House of Congress **shall meet to consider a vote** on a joint resolution to determine whether the emergency should be terminated.

Primary statutory text: https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title50-section1622

The Congressional Research Service’s 2025 report on the Act’s expedited procedures says the practice has been different. Congress does not routinely meet and consider termination legislation every six months, and CRS says it does not appear that Congress has consistently met to review national emergencies on that statutory schedule.

Authoritative source: Congressional Research Service, *National Emergencies Act: Expedited Procedures in the House and Senate*, updated 2025: https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R46567/R46567.8.pdf

This distinction matters because the chapter’s argument is stronger when it does not imagine that procedural recurrence automatically occurs merely because the statute commands it.

## A cleaner concrete example

The September 14, 2001 national emergency declared after the September 11 attacks remains in force. A September 2025 continuation notice states that the terrorist threat continues and extends that emergency for another year beyond September 14, 2025.

Primary source: Federal Register public-inspection notice, *Continuation of the National Emergency With Respect to Certain Terrorist Attacks*, September 2025: https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2025-16941.pdf

That chronology is useful, but it should not be turned into a cheap “twenty-four-year emergency” punchline. The original attacks are over; the asserted predicate in the continuation notice is the continuing terrorist threat. The legal state persisted while the factual description supporting it became more general than the event that triggered it.

That is precisely the chapter’s interesting problem: what must remain the same for an emergency authorization to count as a continuation rather than a new grant?

## Strongest counterargument

Long duration does not establish abuse. A threat can genuinely persist or mutate, and the National Emergencies Act’s annual continuation rule under § 1622(d) forces the President to make an affirmative, public continuation decision rather than allowing an emergency to survive through pure inertia. The 2025 notice supplies a current rationale: the terrorist threat continues.

The chapter should therefore avoid implying that a long-lived emergency is self-evidently illegitimate. The better criticism is institutional and narrower. A broad predicate such as “the terrorist threat continues” can remain true while telling us little about whether every authority activated, delegated, or normalized under the emergency still needs the same scope.

There is also a constitutional complication. Congress’s six-month review mechanism is not simply an automatic veto over presidential action. After *INS v. Chadha* invalidated the one-house legislative veto, termination under the Act operates through a joint resolution subject to the ordinary lawmaking process. That makes congressional review politically and procedurally more demanding than the original 1976 architecture suggested.

Authoritative source: CRS, *National Emergencies Act: Expedited Procedures in the House and Senate*, discussing the post-*Chadha* amendments and joint-resolution process: https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R46567/R46567.8.pdf

## Editorial implication

A high-value prose pass should replace the generic claim that “the legislature votes” with the stranger real mechanism:

Congress wrote a six-month review command into the National Emergencies Act. In practice, CRS says Congress has not consistently performed that review. Meanwhile the executive branch must make a separate annual continuation decision, and the emergency declared after September 11 was renewed again in September 2025.

That produces three distinct layers the manuscript should keep separate:

1. **A statutory review obligation can exist without being reliably performed.**
2. **An annual continuation act can be real without being granular.**
3. **A continuing threat can justify some continuing authority without automatically revalidating the whole authority bundle.**

The strongest version of the chapter is therefore not “sunsets fail.” It is that expiry dates, review duties, continuation notices, and substantive revalidation are different institutional objects. A system can possess the first three and still do the fourth badly—or not at all.

## Suggested narrative move

Keep the example compact. The surprising sentence is not that governments like emergency power. It is that Congress enacted a law telling itself to revisit ongoing national emergencies every six months, and the Congressional Research Service now describes that review as something Congress does not routinely do.

That is more vivid than another abstract paragraph about renewal theater, and it carries a useful warning for agent governance: a scheduled review is not a review. It is only an appointment on a calendar until somebody is required to produce evidence, answer the predicate, and accept a consequence for failing to do so.
