# Back Matter

*Autonomy Half-Life* began with a deliberately sharp proposition: every grant of autonomy should decay unless renewed.

The manuscript ends in a more demanding place.

Some authority should decay quickly. Some should remain durable. Some should narrow only at irreversible boundaries. Some should survive changing principal preferences precisely because insulation and continuity are part of the reason the authority exists.

The distinction is not age.

It is the relationship between the present action and the conditions that made the grant legitimate.

That is the book’s claim.

The practical implication is equally simple to state and harder to build: consequential autonomy should carry enough memory of its purpose, boundaries, principal, expected variation, and renewal logic to know when continued execution deserves fresh evidence.

The book calls this autonomy half-life, but it does not claim a universal mathematical decay law. No quantitative constant is proposed for legitimacy, authority, trust, or organizational discretion. The half-life language is a design metaphor and a burden-of-proof rule. Where measurable signals are useful, systems may use them. Where law, management, professional judgment, or politics are involved, qualitative judgment remains unavoidable.

The research trail for this draft is maintained in `research/` rather than being disguised inside the prose as false certainty. The source ledger includes work on zero-trust architecture, OAuth and expiring credentials, distributed-system leases, concept drift, principal-agent theory, mission command, emergency powers and sunset clauses, and emerging 2026 proposals for autonomous-agent authorization, delegation, progressive trust, and runtime legitimacy.

Those sources do not all make the argument made here. Their differences are part of the point.

A security token is not a constitutional mandate.

A military subordinate is not an API client.

A legislature is not a policy engine.

A machine-learning distribution shift is not political legitimacy.

The book uses these domains comparatively because each has already confronted some version of the same structural problem: a decision made under one state of the world must continue operating while the world changes.

The adjacent work also matters for intellectual provenance.

The exact phrase *Autonomy Half-Life* did not surface in the novelty search used to prepare this manuscript, but neighboring 2026 work uses terms including *Authority Half-Life*, *Authority Decay*, trust half-lives, time-bounded agent mandates, and authority contraction under changed conditions. Those ideas are acknowledged in the research brief. The contribution claimed here is therefore deliberately narrower: the most useful half-life for autonomy is driven primarily by **environmental distance from the conditions of grant**, with elapsed time serving as one signal and backstop rather than the governing principle.

The thesis should be treated as falsifiable in practice.

If context-sensitive renewal consistently produces more failure than it prevents; if organizations cannot identify material grant conditions without making work unusable; if decay mechanisms predictably centralize authority in less informed principals; if dynamic authorization becomes too opaque to preserve rights and durable commitments; or if the cost of monitoring drift exceeds the value of catching stale mandates, then the theory needs narrowing.

Several counterarguments were intentionally preserved rather than resolved away.

Fixed expirations are sometimes better because they are legible and difficult to rationalize around.

Stable mandates can protect institutions and professionals from arbitrary principal interference.

The agent may know more than the principal.

Renewal can become theater.

Context signals can be wrong or manipulated.

Some functions must continue when the principal is unreachable.

And not every right should be turned into a context-sensitive permission whose holder lives at the mercy of a real-time policy system.

Any implementation that forgets those objections would miss the argument of the book.

The intended direction is not toward universal supervision.

It is toward selective friction and stronger memory.

Let delegates absorb ordinary variation.

Let low-consequence preparation continue under weaker mandates when rights permit it.

Require fresher evidence as actions become less reversible, more externally binding, more deeply delegated, or more distant from the purpose that created them.

Record enough provenance that renewal can focus on what changed instead of replaying the entire approval ceremony.

And when authority is meant to be durable, record that reason too.

The question left for the reader is not whether autonomy is good.

Modern institutions cannot function without delegation. Autonomous software will make that fact more visible, not less.

The question is what kind of autonomy can remain legitimate after the world stops resembling the moment in which permission was granted.

A system that answers only “the credential is still valid” has not answered enough.

Neither has a system that answers only “the human clicked approve again.”

The harder answer lives in continuing fit between mandate and world.

That fit will never be perfect.

The goal is not perfect control.

It is authority with a memory, a boundary, and a way to admit that its reasons have changed.

— Sven Hardy Benson
