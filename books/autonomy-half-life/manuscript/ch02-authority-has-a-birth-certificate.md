# Authority Has a Birth Certificate

Most permissions arrive without a birth certificate. They have a timestamp, an approver, perhaps a scope, amount, role, ticket number, expiration date, or line in a policy. What they often lack is a durable record of the conditions that made the grant sensible.

That omission is easy to miss at the beginning because the conditions are obvious to everyone involved. A manager tells a team to hire contractors because a product launch is six weeks away. A board lets management draw on a credit facility because a transaction is expected to close. A regulator relaxes a requirement during a crisis. A user tells an agent to sell a block of stock because she wants to reduce concentration. A commander gives a subordinate discretion to secure a route because the larger purpose and risks are understood by both.

At the moment of delegation, the because lives in the room. Later, it does not. People leave. Messages disappear beneath new messages. Circumstances become familiar enough that nobody remembers they were once exceptional. A permission migrates from one system to another. A role is copied. A policy exception is inherited by a new team. A software agent receives a mandate from another agent that never saw the original human conversation.

The yes survives better than the because, and that creates a design problem. A grant of authority is not just a statement about the delegate. It describes a relationship among a delegate, an objective, a set of boundaries, and a world. Preserve only the delegate and the allowed action and much of the information needed to know when the grant should stop making sense is gone.

This is what I mean when I say authority has a birth certificate. I do not mean a literal document. I mean that every meaningful grant has origins. Someone wanted something. Certain facts appeared true. Certain risks seemed tolerable. Certain counterparties were known. Certain resources were available. The principal believed the delegate could act inside a particular range without returning for permission. Those origins define the mandate even when nobody writes them down.

Principal-agent theory begins from a related problem. A principal delegates because the principal cannot or does not want to perform every act personally. The arrangement creates benefits along with information and incentive problems. The agent may know more about local conditions. The principal may have different objectives. Monitoring is costly, while detailed control can defeat the point of delegation. That literature is not a theory of autonomy half-life, but it exposes the structure beneath it: authority is relational. Delegation is never merely the transfer of a verb.

Spend, negotiate, approve, deploy, respond: none of those verbs means much without purpose and boundary. A chief operating officer authorized to spend $5 million does not possess a free-floating right to spend $5 million on anything. A physician's authority to treat a patient is not a general license to perform every procedure on any person. A military order is interpreted through mission, commander's intent, law, and situation. A software token with a payment scope may technically permit a transaction that the human principal never meant to authorize under changed facts.

The bare permission is a compressed representation of a richer agreement. Compression is necessary; nobody can reconstruct the entire conversation behind every authorized act. The danger begins when the compressed representation becomes the only representation.

Consider how organizations document money. A budget is usually more durable than the assumptions behind it. The number is entered into a system. The forecast that justified it may live in a spreadsheet, the competitive assumption in a strategy deck, the key dependency in a conversation nobody recorded. Months later a team can point to the number with great precision while the reasoning that produced it has become archaeological. Authority behaves the same way: the delegated limit remains legible after the environment that produced it has faded.

A birth certificate restores some of that lost relationship. A future reviewer should be able to recover the purpose the delegate was expected to advance, the actions inside scope, the facts or assumptions important enough that a change would have altered the original decision, the variation the principal expected, the risks accepted, who remained accountable, and the kinds of change meant to trigger another look. Not every grant needs every field, and not every domain can encode them the same way.

Software authorization can make purpose identifiers, recipient restrictions, amount limits, expiration, environment, tool scopes, delegation depth, and approval provenance machine-readable. A board resolution cannot encode every market contingency and should not try. A military commander's intent uses human language precisely because judgment has to survive situations that cannot be enumerated. A legislature may define the factual basis and limits of an emergency power without pretending to describe every future case. The common requirement is smaller: preserve enough of the conditions that later drift can be recognized.

Without those conditions, renewal becomes shallow. Suppose an employee's elevated database access comes up for annual review. The reviewer sees that the employee still works in engineering and clicks Approve. That renews identity, not necessarily purpose; the access may have been granted for a migration project that ended nine months ago. Or imagine an autonomous agent with a long-running mandate to optimize advertising spend. A quarterly check confirms that it remains reliable and within budget, while the company has changed its objective from growth at any cost to cash preservation. The delegate did not drift first. The principal's world did.

Purpose belongs on the birth certificate because authority without purpose is difficult to kill: almost any action can be defended by pointing to scope. Purpose also protects autonomy. Military mission-command doctrine has spent generations confronting the fact that detailed orders become stale in uncertain environments. Subordinates need enough understanding of commander's intent to act when the plan no longer fits. The structure neither demands robotic obedience to an obsolete method nor grants unlimited freedom. Initiative remains connected to a larger purpose and lawful boundary.

Autonomy half-life depends on the same distinction. A route may become obsolete while the mission remains. A supplier may become unavailable while the procurement purpose remains. A planned sequence of API calls may change while the user's desired outcome remains stable. Bind authority too tightly to method and the system becomes brittle; bind it only to broad capability and it invites overreach. The useful grant leaves room to adapt methods while making changes in purpose, affected parties, accepted risk, or consequence visible.

Negotiation makes the distinction concrete. A team may be authorized to settle a commercial dispute within a financial range, while the principal also cares about confidentiality, timing, precedent, and the continuing business relationship. An offer can remain inside the dollar ceiling and still become unacceptable because it creates a public admission, grants rights affecting future cases, or changes the structure of the relationship. The number alone has compressed away what mattered.

The birth certificate is not a demand for perfect foresight. It records which kinds of surprise deserve another look. Good contracts do something similar: they define obligations, representations, covenants, thresholds, and events that alter rights without pretending to predict the world. A principal can say, in effect, that ordinary variation in price, timing, and implementation is the delegate's job, while a changed counterparty, a move beyond a stated range, an irreversible step, a legal change, or the disappearance of the purpose requires another decision.

That can create more autonomy, not less. The delegate no longer has to treat every small change as suspicious. A weak mandate says what the delegate may do. A stronger one also says what would make that permission stop fitting.

Our systems are generally better at entry than exit. Access-control software answers whether a request matches policy now. Corporate approval systems collect signatures. Laws confer authority in legally valid language. Management processes assign owners. Future invalidation conditions are often left to external revocation: somebody notices, calls, and stops the task.

That assumption weakens as autonomy grows. A person working down the hall may notice a material change and ask. An autonomous system operating overnight may not. A multinational organization may not propagate a changed strategic assumption into every standing delegation. A government may not revisit an exceptional power when the facts that justified it fade gradually rather than ending on a clear date. The faster a delegate can act relative to the principal's ability to notice, the more invalidation logic has to travel with the grant.

This is not only an AI problem. It is a scale problem. Large organizations create distance between authorization and action because distance is what delegation is for. A senior executive approves a policy thousands of employees will apply to cases the executive never sees. A legislature delegates to an agency because the agency has expertise the legislature lacks. A board empowers management because the board cannot operate the company. The actor closest to the facts may therefore be the first to know that the mandate no longer fits.

The principal owns the authority, but the agent may own the freshest information. Requiring the principal to detect every change will fail. Letting the agent decide unilaterally that its own mandate remains valid can fail too. Observation and self-expansion have to be separated. The agent can be allowed, even required, to detect facts that might weaken authority without receiving the power to reinterpret those facts in whichever direction preserves its autonomy.

Safety systems already use versions of this separation. Sensors can trigger a shutdown without redefining the safety threshold. A trading system can trip a limit without raising its own limit. A subordinate can report changed conditions without rewriting the law governing the mission. Autonomy half-life works best when the conditions of decay are defined by a layer other than the actor whose authority is decaying. That layer can be machine-enforced; it need not always be human.

The birth certificate therefore serves both the delegate and the future reviewer. It tells the delegate what the permission means and where adaptation is expected, while giving a later human or machine a reference point against which drift can be judged. That second use becomes more important with every delegation hop.

Suppose a principal authorizes Agent A to arrange travel within a budget. Agent A asks Agent B to book a hotel. Agent B asks a payment service to execute the transaction. By the third hop, each actor may possess only a narrow operational instruction. That is efficient, and it is also how purpose evaporates. If the hotel dates no longer match the trip, can Agent B tell that the payment mandate has become stale? If the payment service sees a changed merchant, does it know whether that is ordinary substitution or a material change?

Provenance helps. The chain should carry enough of the mandate's origin to prevent downstream actors from inheriting broader authority than the original purpose can support. Scope attenuation is essential, but it solves a different problem. Containment prevents expansion. It cannot stop a perfectly narrow permission from becoming wrong after its purpose disappears.

Security thinking naturally emphasizes least privilege: verify identity, narrow scope, limit duration, reduce blast radius. Those are good principles. Yet *needs* is a present-tense word. A permission that was least-privilege yesterday can be unnecessary today. A role can be narrow relative to a job that no longer exists. A transaction limit can be conservative relative to an objective that has been abandoned. Least privilege at issuance is not enough if the system cannot remember what justified the privilege.

That memory changes the language of authority. Instead of saying, "I have approval," a person can say, "I have approval for this purpose under these conditions." The second sentence is less politically useful as a shield. It is also more accurate.

People and institutions tend to defend powers they have been given. Teams organize around them. Systems build dependencies on them. Temporary processes acquire permanent staff. A role that began as an exception becomes evidence that the exception is necessary because people now rely on the role. When the original reason is explicit, later review has something firmer than habit to examine: why did this exist, which facts remain true, and what would we grant today if we were starting from zero?

Organizations rarely start from zero. They inherit permissions, structures, budgets, jurisdictions, and precedents, which gives the status quo a procedural advantage: it already exists. Removal requires action. Autonomy half-life reverses that advantage for selected powers by requiring continued authority to remain connected to the conditions that produced it.

The connection need not be constantly proved. Stable environments can justify long intervals. Low-risk and reversible actions can tolerate more drift. Experienced delegates can receive broader regions of expected variation. The point is not permanent supervision but traceable origin.

Traceable origin also makes renewal cheaper. If a reviewer must reconstruct everything from scratch, asking again becomes bureaucracy. If purpose, assumptions, and triggers remain visible, the renewed decision can be narrow: the supplier changed, but the objective, amount, risk classification, and delivery need remain the same. Approve the substitution. That is a smaller judgment than reapproving the whole procurement.

Cheap, precise renewal is what keeps autonomy half-life from becoming a machine for centralization. We do not want to drag every decision back to its origin. We want the origin to remain readable when the decision has traveled too far.

Authority has a birth certificate whether we record it or not. The practical choice is whether anyone can still read it when the world changes.
