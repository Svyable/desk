# The Principal Can Drift Too

The simplest picture of delegation has two characters.

A principal wants something done.

An agent acts on the principal’s behalf.

The principal supplies purpose and authority. The agent supplies effort, expertise, or execution. Governance then appears to be the problem of keeping the agent aligned with the principal as conditions change.

That picture is useful.

It is also dangerously incomplete.

The principal can drift too.

The person who granted authority can lose information, change incentives, leave office, become conflicted, become captured, forget the original purpose, or cease to represent the people whose interests made the authority legitimate in the first place.

An autonomy-half-life system that treats the principal as a permanent source of truth simply moves the stale-authority problem one level upward.

This matters because most control systems are built with an asymmetry.

The agent is monitored.

The principal is assumed.

The employee’s access is reviewed by the manager. The manager’s right to decide is usually inherited from the org chart. The autonomous agent’s tool access is reviewed against a user instruction. The user’s right to issue the instruction may receive less attention. An agency’s exercise of delegated power is reviewed, while the political principal’s incentives in changing the mandate can be treated as background.

That asymmetry is sometimes correct. Authority has to bottom out somewhere operationally.

It should not bottom out intellectually.

The first way a principal drifts is informational.

Delegation exists partly because the agent knows things the principal does not.

A board cannot know the operating detail management knows. A general cannot see what every unit sees. A legislature delegates because agencies develop technical expertise. A user asks an agent to compare hundreds of options because the user does not want to inspect them all personally.

The more successful the delegation, the larger the information gap can become.

This produces a paradox when renewal is required.

The principal holds the authority to renew.

The agent may hold the evidence needed to decide wisely.

If the renewal system reduces the process to “ask the principal again,” it can force the decision toward the less informed actor.

A manager can veto a technical change she barely understands. A user can approve a financial transaction after skimming an agent’s summary. A political principal can override an expert institution for reasons unrelated to the factual basis of the mandate.

Formal authority does not guarantee current competence.

This is one reason Chapter 17 treated renewal as evidence rather than obedience.

The agent should bring the delta.

The principal should not be forced to reconstruct the world from a button.

But information can shift even more dramatically.

The original principal can disappear.

An employee who initiated a long-running task leaves the company. A manager changes roles. A founder steps down. A customer dies. A public official leaves office. A contractor’s sponsor departs.

The authority chain has to decide what survives.

Some mandates are personal.

If I authorize an agent to purchase a gift for me, my withdrawal should usually end the task.

Some mandates are institutional.

If a departing employee initiated a payroll process on behalf of the company, the company’s purpose can survive the employee.

The mistake is identifying the person who clicked Approve with the true principal in every case.

The true principal may be an organization, a client, a patient, a shareholder body, the public under law, or a combination of actors and rules.

This matters for agent design because software likes stable identifiers.

User 817 created the task.

User 817 is no longer active.

Cancel task?

The correct answer depends on what authority User 817 was exercising when the task began.

If the user acted personally, perhaps yes.

If the user acted as an officeholder for an organization, the mandate may be reassignable to the successor role.

If the user lacked the right to authorize the task in the first place, the task should not survive merely because the identity was authenticated.

Principal identity is therefore another part of the mandate’s birth certificate.

Not only who approved.

In what capacity?

For whose interests?

Under what source of authority?

Those questions are familiar in law because agency relationships have long distinguished personal acts from acts in representative capacity.

Software systems often flatten the distinction into account ownership.

Autonomy makes that flattening more dangerous because tasks can continue after accounts change.

The second way a principal drifts is incentive.

A principal’s interests at renewal may differ from the interests that justified the original grant.

A manager who delegated broad authority during a crisis may later want to preserve the power because it made the team successful. A political principal may want to revoke an independent institution’s mandate because the institution produced an inconvenient result. A shareholder may prefer short-term cash extraction from a company whose governance was designed around longer-term commitments. A platform may use renewal conditions to force dependent developers into new terms.

The principal is not always a neutral guardian of the original purpose.

This is why durable institutions exist.

Contracts bind future preferences.

Corporate charters allocate authority in ways individual managers cannot casually rewrite.

Judicial independence protects adjudication from immediate political command.

Professional duties can require an agent to resist the person who hired them. Trustees, lawyers, directors, doctors, and public officials can owe duties that are not reducible to the latest instruction from whoever appears highest in a hierarchy.

A stable mandate can protect against principal drift.

That sentence should make any simple theory of decaying authority uncomfortable.

If every grant weakens whenever the current principal wants another decision, autonomy becomes conditional loyalty.

The principal can keep the agent perpetually dependent by shortening the renewal interval.

The language of safety can disguise domination.

“Conditions changed” becomes a universal justification for pulling power back to the center.

A good half-life theory therefore asks not only when the agent’s mandate should decay.

It asks which aspects of the principal’s authority are themselves stable and which are conditional.

Suppose a board grants a chief executive authority to run the company inside a strategy and budget. The board retains oversight and can replace the chief executive under defined governance rules. That does not mean an individual director can interrupt ordinary operations whenever she dislikes a decision.

The chief executive’s autonomy is partly protected by the institutional form of the grant.

The principal is the board acting as a board, not whichever director calls first.

The same structure matters in software systems that serve organizations.

A manager may be able to request a workflow. A compliance policy may limit what the manager can authorize. A contract with the customer may define rights the manager cannot waive. The organization itself may require two roles for certain decisions.

The apparent principal is one layer inside a larger authority graph.

Autonomous agents should not collapse the graph into “user asked.”

This becomes especially important when the action affects third parties.

A user can ask an agent to analyze her own notes.

She may not have the right to disclose another person’s confidential information contained in them.

A manager can ask an agent to rank employees.

Employment law, company policy, collective agreements, or fairness requirements may constrain what the manager may legitimately delegate.

A public official can ask for an action.

The office may not possess that power under law.

The principal’s intent is necessary evidence.

It is not sufficient authority.

The third way a principal drifts is representational.

Who does the principal represent, and does that relationship remain legitimate?

This question appears most obviously in politics, but organizations encounter versions of it too.

A board represents a corporation under a legal structure. An executive acts for a company. A fund manager acts for investors under a mandate. A guardian acts for a child or incapacitated person under law. A government official exercises public power under an office whose authority comes from a constitutional and electoral system.

The individual principal is a node in a larger chain.

When that chain changes, the mandate can change even if the individual’s preferences remain stable.

A government loses an election.

A board is replaced.

A trustee is removed.

A manager’s role is narrowed.

A client terminates representation.

The authority to renew does not persist merely because the person remembers the password.

This sounds like ordinary identity lifecycle management in software.

It is more than that.

A role change can alter the source of legitimacy for outstanding tasks.

The system should be able to distinguish a task owned by the institution from a task owned by the officeholder personally, and it should know which successor, if any, can inherit renewal power.

Without that distinction, organizations either kill useful work when people move or let departed principals continue governing through zombie mandates.

Both are forms of stale authority.

The fourth way a principal drifts is temporal preference.

Humans discount the future. Leaders face deadlines. Politicians face elections. Executives face quarters. Managers face performance cycles. Founders face financing rounds.

A principal at time two may prefer something different not because new evidence changed the underlying objective, but because the principal’s horizon changed.

Institutions often respond by making some mandates harder to change.

Debt covenants constrain future managers.

Pension obligations constrain future budgets.

Constitutions constrain temporary majorities.

Long-term contracts constrain opportunistic renegotiation.

These arrangements can be frustrating precisely because they resist contextual reauthorization.

That resistance can be valuable.

Autonomy half-life therefore needs a distinction between factual drift and preference drift.

If the conditions supporting the grant changed, reconsideration may be appropriate.

If only the principal’s appetite for control changed, the case is weaker where the original mandate was designed to be durable.

This is not to say principals should never change their minds.

Of course they should.

The question is what procedure and evidence the original relationship requires for a change of mind to become authoritative.

A user can usually cancel her personal shopping task instantly.

A chief executive cannot cancel employee pension rights by changing her mind.

A legislature may amend a statute through lawmaking procedures.

An executive official cannot necessarily suspend it because conditions feel inconvenient.

A company may terminate a contract according to its terms.

It cannot pretend the contract’s authority simply decayed because strategy changed.

Durable commitments are a deliberate exception to flexible autonomy.

Their value comes from limiting future discretion.

A theory that places all authority on a half-life curve would destroy the possibility of commitment.

The book’s claim is narrower.

Delegated operational autonomy should weaken when the material conditions that justified the delegation drift, unless stability against that class of drift was itself part of the grant.

That final clause protects commitment.

It also makes the birth certificate more important.

Why was the authority made durable?

Was the point to give a delegate room to adapt inside a mission?

To protect professional judgment from political pressure?

To create credible long-term commitments?

To separate ownership from day-to-day management?

To ensure a person’s rights do not depend on continuous permission from a stronger actor?

If durability serves the purpose, then some forms of environmental or principal change should not shorten the mandate.

The half-life can be intentionally long.

This does not create immunity.

Even durable institutions have amendment procedures, removal standards, judicial review, elections, contractual termination rights, or other mechanisms of renewal and change.

The important feature is that change occurs through the right authority.

A principal’s drift does not automatically rewrite the rules.

The fifth way a principal drifts is moral or legal authority.

A principal can ask for something wrong.

The phrase “agent alignment” often imagines the goal as fidelity to the principal’s preferences. Human institutions have learned the hard way that fidelity is not always enough.

Employees can be ordered to discriminate.

Lawyers can be asked to conceal evidence.

Public officials can be instructed to exceed lawful powers.

Companies can ask contractors to violate regulations.

Users can ask software to invade another person’s privacy.

An agent’s duty can include refusing the principal.

That is not a breakdown of delegation.

It can be the correct execution of a higher-order mandate.

This means autonomy systems need more than a principal-agent edge.

They need an authority hierarchy capable of representing constraints that neither the principal nor the agent may override alone.

In software, some of those constraints can be policy.

In institutions, they can be law, fiduciary duty, professional ethics, contractual obligation, constitutional limitation, or rights held by third parties.

The mandate is the intersection of these authorities.

When one layer changes, the action may need fresh review even if the immediate principal says continue.

The principal can drift outside the lawful region too.

This complicates renewal enormously because there may be disagreement about which layer controls.

That disagreement cannot always be automated.

A system should not pretend that a policy engine can settle constitutional interpretation or fiduciary conflict by assigning weights.

Where the authority conflict is genuinely legal, political, or moral, the correct degraded mode may be to preserve evidence, suspend irreversible action, and route the dispute to the institution designed to resolve it.

Autonomy half-life does not abolish courts, boards, compliance functions, elections, or professional judgment.

It gives them a cleaner trigger.

The current mandate no longer has an uncontested source.

The sixth way a principal drifts is through succession.

A new principal inherits an old mandate and can misunderstand why it exists.

A new manager sees a team with unusual discretion and assumes it is cultural. A new government sees an agency power and assumes it is ideological. A new executive sees a contractual restriction and assumes it is legacy clutter. A new account owner sees an agent permission and assumes the previous owner intended permanent access.

Succession breaks memory.

This is where institutions that remember why become essential, the subject of the next chapter.

A successor should not have to guess whether an old authority is stale or intentionally durable.

The record should explain the purpose, source, limits, and renewal logic.

That record protects both directions.

It makes stale power easier to challenge because the old justification can be compared with the present world.

It makes legitimate durable power harder to casually dismantle because the reason for stability remains visible.

The same provenance that enables decay can protect continuity.

This is one of the more surprising consequences of the theory.

Good memory is not inherently anti-authority.

It is anti-amnesia.

Amnesia favors whoever currently controls the interpretation of the grant.

If no one remembers why a power exists, the holder can say it was meant to be permanent.

The principal can say it was always revocable.

The successor can project present preferences backward.

A birth certificate constrains everyone with history.

That is healthy.

The principal’s drift also changes how we should think about AI systems that learn user preferences.

Suppose an assistant observes a user for years. It becomes good at predicting what the user likes. It learns travel preferences, spending habits, writing style, scheduling choices, and risk tolerance.

The model of the principal becomes richer.

It can also become stale.

People change.

A user marries, has a child, changes jobs, becomes wealthier, becomes poorer, develops new obligations, changes health priorities, or simply changes taste.

The agent’s confidence in historical preference can rise at the same time the relevance of the history falls.

This is principal drift in its most intimate form.

A useful assistant should therefore distinguish preference learning from present mandate.

“You usually choose the cheapest nonrefundable ticket” is evidence.

It is not permanent authorization to do so when the trip is different.

The system should know which preferences can be applied automatically and which deserve confirmation after meaningful life changes or high-consequence decisions.

The user herself may not think to update the model.

The agent should not treat silence as a lifetime contract.

This is where autonomy half-life becomes a design principle for personalization.

Preferences can have half-lives too, but again the useful variable is not time alone. A preference for aisle seats may remain stable for twenty years. A spending preference may change overnight after job loss. A medical preference may change after diagnosis. A communication style may change by audience.

The agent should be sensitive to context rather than assuming historical frequency equals current authority.

The principal moves through the world too.

The book began by asking when a delegate still has the right to continue.

By this chapter, the question has become more difficult.

Which principal still has the right to renew?

In what capacity?

For whose interests?

Under which higher-order rules?

With what information?

Those questions prevent autonomy half-life from becoming a simple technology of recall.

The point is not that the principal gets another veto whenever the environment changes.

The point is that delegated power remains connected to a legitimate source as the entire relationship changes.

Sometimes that means asking the original principal again.

Sometimes it means asking a successor.

Sometimes it means a board, court, regulator, customer, patient, second officer, or other authority must participate.

Sometimes it means the delegate is specifically protected from current principal preference.

Sometimes it means the immediate principal lacks the right to renew at all.

The theory becomes more cumbersome at this point.

Reality was cumbersome first.

Every system that pretends one authenticated user is the sovereign source of all authority is buying simplicity by throwing away relationships that reappear later as failures.

The principal can drift.

The architecture should remember that before it builds a permanent button labeled Ask Again.
