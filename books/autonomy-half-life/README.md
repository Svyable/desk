Why authority should decay as the world changes.

Every grant of autonomy is made inside a particular world: a purpose, a set of facts, a risk level, a counterpart, a budget, a model of what might happen, and an understanding of who remains accountable. We usually preserve the grant until somebody explicitly revokes it. This book argues for the opposite default. Authority should weaken as the conditions that justified it drift away, and it should recover only through fresh evidence or deliberate renewal.

The book calls this **autonomy half-life**. The half-life is not merely a duration on a clock. A mandate can remain sound for months in a stable environment and lose most of its legitimacy in minutes when the objective, facts, risk, delegation chain, or reversibility changes. The practical question is therefore not simply “Has the permission expired?” but “How far is this action from the world in which permission was granted?”

The argument applies to AI agents, corporate delegation, emergency powers, military mission command, access control, institutions, and everyday managerial authority. It also takes the strongest objection seriously: constant reauthorization can paralyze organizations, centralize power, and destroy the local initiative autonomy was meant to create. The aim is not to make everyone ask permission forever. It is to design authority that knows when its own justification has become stale.
