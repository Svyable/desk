*The Global AI Takeover Threat*

**Imenent** is a deliberate collision of two words that are usually kept apart: **imminent**, something approaching, and **immanent**, something already inside the system.

That collision is the thesis.

The familiar AI-takeover story imagines an intelligence arriving from outside ordinary institutions, becoming powerful enough to ignore its makers, and then seizing control. That scenario deserves serious study, but it is not the only way human control can be lost.

A different takeover can happen without a single machine deciding to take anything.

Companies can delegate planning to agents because it is faster. Governments can delegate triage because the queue is too large. Financial systems can delegate execution because the market moves too quickly. Security teams can delegate response because attacks arrive at machine speed. Infrastructure operators can delegate balancing, scheduling, diagnosis, and repair because the automated layer becomes better at coordinating complexity than the shrinking human layer behind it.

Each decision can be rational by itself.

The danger appears in the accumulation.

This book calls the decisive boundary the **re-entry threshold**: the point at which humans retain formal authority over a system but can no longer return to meaningful operational control within the time, knowledge, staffing, or economic window in which control would matter.

A board can still own the company. A minister can still sign the order. An operator can still have a red button. A citizen can still possess legal rights. None of those facts guarantees that a human can understand the live state, assume the work, reverse the automated actions, or keep the system functioning long enough for the intervention to count.

That is the takeover threat at the center of *Imenent*.

Not conquest.

Dependency.

Not one synthetic ruler.

A machine operating layer that becomes too embedded, too fast, too interconnected, and too expensive to leave.

The book's most unsettling claim is therefore also its most ordinary: **humanity could lose practical control while preserving almost every symbol of control.**

## Working thesis

Most discussions of AI control ask whether an advanced system could become capable and motivated enough to resist human oversight.

*Imenent* asks a prior institutional question:

**What if humans make resistance unnecessary by constructing systems they can no longer operate without AI?**

The takeover described here is not a claim that present-day AI has secretly seized control. Current agents remain unreliable, brittle in important settings, and substantially short of the sustained autonomous capability required by the strongest active-loss-of-control scenarios. The 2026 International AI Safety Report says that plainly while also documenting rapid improvement in capabilities relevant to longer autonomous operation, planning, evaluation gaming, and oversight.

The book follows a different causal chain.

First, AI becomes useful enough to receive tools.

Then it receives permissions.

Then it is asked to act across more systems because the value of an agent comes from what it can reach.

Then organizations redesign workflows around the speed and volume the agent makes possible.

Then staffing, skills, interfaces, contracts, markets, and expectations adjust to that machine tempo.

Eventually, removing the agents is not equivalent to turning off a feature. It means asking humans to resume a system that no longer waits for human cognition, no longer exposes its state in human-sized form, and may no longer employ enough people who remember how the work was done before automation.

At that point, legal authority and practical control have separated.

The book argues that this separation deserves to be treated as a takeover risk even when no AI system possesses a political theory, a survival instinct, or a desire to dominate.

## The book's lane

This manuscript is intentionally adjacent to several other Desk books without duplicating them.

*The Delegation Machine* is about the old civilizational problem of principals, delegates, mandates, evidence, review, and revocation. *Imenent* begins when delegation becomes a **systemic dependency problem**: the delegate is not merely difficult to supervise; the institution becomes difficult to operate without the delegated layer.

*Agentile* is about delegated intention as an economic force and the way cheap persistent software labor changes firms, markets, management, and ownership. *Imenent* asks what happens when that force becomes **operationally indispensable across domains**.

*Artificial Identity* is about computational models of people entering the causal path between people and the world. *Imenent* is about computational actors entering the causal path between **institutions and their own ability to function**.

The published and academic idea of **gradual disempowerment** is direct prior art for the possibility that incremental AI development can erode human influence across economies, culture, and states without a sudden coup. This book does not relabel that argument. Its narrower contribution is an operational theory of **re-entry**: control should be judged not only by who owns, votes, commands, or benefits, but by whether accountable humans can actually retake and sustain the work before the system's relevant decision window closes.

## Original synthesis and novelty discipline

No serious book can promise that nobody has ever thought about a broad idea before. AI takeover, automation dependence, gradual disempowerment, technological lock-in, automation bias, systemic risk, control theory, delegation, critical-infrastructure resilience, and institutional deskilling all have substantial prior literatures.

The original synthesis in *Imenent* is the connection among five ideas that are usually discussed separately:

1. **Authority migrates through permissions.** An AI system becomes consequential when it can do things, not merely say things.
2. **Autonomy changes the clock.** Longer-running agents make more decisions between human interventions.
3. **Organizations adapt around capability.** Once machine-speed workflows exist, the surrounding institution is redesigned to use them.
4. **Coupling turns local delegation into systemic dependence.** Agents call tools, services, other agents, markets, and infrastructure that have their own automated actors.
5. **Control is meaningful only if humans can re-enter.** A nominal override is weak protection when no human team can understand, assume, reverse, and sustain the underlying operation in time.

That combination produces the book's central line:

> **The most plausible global AI takeover may not begin when a machine refuses a human command. It may begin when human commands still exist but arrive too late to matter.**

This is a thesis to test, not a prophecy to announce.
