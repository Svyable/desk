# The Race Into Dependence

Nobody has to want the dangerous equilibrium.

They only have to fear being the last one to arrive.

This is how races work.

A company automates because it wants lower cost.

A competitor automates because the first company lowered cost.

A third company automates because customers now expect the faster service created by both.

By the time the fourth company adopts the technology, nobody calls it innovation.

It is infrastructure.

The same dynamic can shape AI dependence.

The strongest takeover scenario in this book does not require executives, governments, or citizens to decide that handing more authority to machines is desirable in the abstract.

They can prefer caution.

They can understand the risks.

They can still automate because the alternative is losing to actors who automate more aggressively.

This is the **race into dependence**.

The phrase does not describe one global race with one finish line.

There are many.

Companies compete for cost and speed.

Countries compete for economic growth, military capability, scientific leadership, intelligence, and administrative capacity.

Hospitals compete with scarcity of staff and time.

Cybersecurity teams compete with attackers.

Workers compete with other workers using better tools.

Universities compete for research output.

Governments compete with public expectations and fiscal constraints even when no foreign rival is involved.

Competition is not always between people.

Sometimes the rival is the backlog.

That matters because appeals to restraint can sound irresponsible in environments where delay already causes harm.

Tell an emergency department not to use a reliable system that speeds triage and the answer should be: what happens to the patients waiting?

Tell a security team to keep humans in every tactical loop and the answer should be: what happens while the attacker moves?

Tell a public agency to preserve manual processing and the answer should be: who pays for the staff and why should citizens tolerate the delay?

Tell a company to maintain expensive redundant human capacity and the answer should be: will customers pay more for recoverability they cannot see?

These are real questions.

Any policy that ignores them will be routed around.

The challenge is to make recoverability competitive with dependence.

AI adoption in 2026 already shows the beginning of this pressure without proving its eventual direction. Organizational use of AI is broad. More agentic deployment remains earlier. Companies see obvious productivity opportunities while the infrastructure for secure autonomous action is still being built.

This is a path-setting period.

The standards, permissions, staffing models, procurement practices, insurance requirements, and customer expectations formed now can determine whether future autonomy arrives with re-entry capacity or consumes it.

The easiest way to lose that capacity is to treat it as a private luxury.

One company maintains two operating modes.

Its competitor maintains one.

The competitor is cheaper.

Nothing goes wrong for five years.

The market concludes the redundancy was waste.

The cautious company is acquired or changes strategy.

Industry practice converges on the efficient design.

Then a correlated failure arrives.

The problem is familiar from resilience economics.

The benefit of spare capacity is concentrated in rare events.

The cost is continuous.

Markets can reward the cost-cutting actor during every ordinary quarter and punish the entire system during the extraordinary one.

This does not mean markets cannot price resilience.

Insurance can.

Lenders can.

Customers can.

Regulators can.

Boards can.

Ratings agencies can.

Critical-infrastructure contracts can.

The problem is visibility.

Recoverability is hard to value if nobody measures it.

An organization can advertise uptime.

Latency.

Accuracy.

Cost.

Throughput.

Customer satisfaction.

Security incidents.

What is the metric for human re-entry?

A fake universal score would create more problems than it solves.

The concept is system-specific.

But specific exercises can produce evidence.

How long did it take to disable the agent layer safely?

How much essential service continued?

How many human operators were needed?

Which functions failed first?

How much state could be reconstructed independently?

How much authority remained delegated after revocation?

How long could the degraded mode operate?

How many critical actions remained reversible?

These are auditable facts.

An insurer can care about them.

A regulator can care.

A board can care.

A customer buying a critical service can care.

Once resilience becomes visible, competition can reward it rather than only punish its cost.

This is important because regulation is not the only answer to a race.

Sometimes changing what markets can see changes the race itself.

Cybersecurity evolved partly this way. Customers began asking vendors about controls. Insurers asked questions. Boards paid attention. Standards created common language. Governments imposed requirements in high-risk domains. Security remained costly, but insecurity stopped being free.

Agentic recoverability may need a similar transition.

The company that can prove it survives loss of its AI layer may become more attractive to banks, governments, hospitals, or other customers that cannot tolerate a provider disappearing during an incident.

This will not happen automatically.

Most customers buy performance before they buy resilience.

The race is strongest where performance is strategic.

Consider national security.

One country deploys agents that accelerate intelligence analysis, logistics, cyber defense, procurement, planning, or software development.

Another country can believe the systems are risky and still feel compelled to match them.

The decision is not between safe AI and unsafe AI in a vacuum.

It is between a risky capability and the risk of strategic disadvantage.

This is one of the hardest governance conditions because mutual restraint requires trust.

A country that preserves human-speed decision processes may fear an adversary using machine-speed systems to exploit the delay.

The natural response is to automate the tactical layer while keeping humans at strategic levels.

That can be a sound design.

It widens the speed gap.

The race into dependence can therefore be driven by safety concerns too.

Automate defense because automated offense is coming.

Automate monitoring because autonomous agents create too much activity to watch manually.

Automate compliance because the machine economy produces too many transactions for regulators.

Automate audit because agents generate too much evidence.

Each safety response increases the amount of machine cognition inside the system.

The answer cannot be no automation.

It has to be **competitive control architecture**.

A safer system should not require surrendering the performance that competition makes necessary.

This is a difficult engineering objective and an important one.

Least privilege can improve security without slowing low-risk work if credentials are issued automatically at the right scope.

Machine-readable authority can make delegation faster and more auditable.

Automated monitors can preserve speed while escalating critical cases.

Reversible staging can let agents act quickly without making every action final.

Degraded modes can be built into infrastructure rather than staffed as a duplicate full-cost organization.

Simulation can maintain human competence without requiring manual production every day.

Portability standards can reduce dependence on one model without sacrificing agent utility.

The safest architecture is often not the one with the most humans.

It is the one that preserves human authority without making human attention the bottleneck for everything.

That distinction matters politically.

If recoverability becomes associated with nostalgia, anti-technology sentiment, or deliberate inefficiency, it will lose.

People want better services.

Workers want better tools.

Governments want capability.

Businesses want productivity.

Patients want diagnosis.

Scientists want discovery.

Security teams want speed.

The pro-control position has to be a pro-capability position too.

Build powerful systems.

Make them governable.

The race into dependence also has a labor dimension.

A worker using agents can outperform one who does not.

A team using agents can support more customers.

A professional who learns to delegate well can handle a larger portfolio.

This changes the competitive baseline for individual careers.

People may adopt AI not because they prefer machine mediation but because colleagues who use it become faster.

Then workflows change.

Employers expect the new output level.

The old human-only pace stops being viable.

This is a miniature version of environmental re-entry.

A worker can stop using the agent.

The job may no longer fit a human-only tempo.

That does not mean the agent controls the worker.

The labor market creates the pressure.

The same mechanism can push entire professions across dependency thresholds.

Law firms expect faster document review.

Consultancies expect more analysis per person.

Software companies expect smaller teams to maintain larger systems.

Medical systems expect clinicians to handle more administrative complexity with assistance.

Public agencies design staffing around agent-supported productivity.

The human fallback remains technically possible and economically incompatible.

This is why the takeover-without-a-taker model is fundamentally about institutions rather than evil machines.

The pressure comes from other humans responding to machine capability.

A useful technology changes the definition of normal.

Normal becomes obligation.

Obligation becomes dependence.

The race can also create premature trust.

A competitor announces an autonomous system.

Executives feel pressure to match the capability before their own governance is mature.

A country believes rivals are deploying agents and accelerates deployment under secrecy.

A team bypasses a cautious permission design because a deadline matters.

The agent works.

The temporary exception becomes precedent.

This is how safety debt accumulates.

Again, nothing is unique to AI. Software teams have shipped insecure systems under competitive pressure for decades. Financial institutions have taken correlated risks. Supply chains have removed resilience to lower cost. States have rushed military technology.

AI adds one unusual feature.

The technology can help repair the governance debt it creates.

Agents can document themselves.

They can map permission graphs.

They can test degraded modes.

They can simulate incidents.

They can identify single points of failure.

They can maintain human-readable runbooks.

They can train operators.

They can propose narrower permissions.

They can compare model outputs.

They can automatically collect action evidence.

Safety work can scale with capability if organizations make it part of the agent's job.

This is a reason for optimism that belongs near the center of the book, not in a polite paragraph at the end.

The AI race is not necessarily a race away from human control.

It can become a race toward better controlled autonomy.

That depends on what buyers reward and what institutions require.

Imagine two agent platforms.

Both perform the work equally well.

One is easier to replace.

One exposes clearer authority provenance.

One can enter a degraded mode.

One supports independent monitors.

One preserves primary evidence.

One can revoke descendants cleanly.

One demonstrates recovery through regular exercises.

If markets value those properties, safety becomes product quality.

The race changes direction.

This is what happened with many mature technologies.

Safety features that once looked like cost became expected quality.

Cars compete partly on safety.

Cloud providers compete on reliability and security.

Financial institutions compete on trust.

Aircraft manufacturers cannot sell an airplane by arguing safety slows innovation.

Agentic systems can reach the same point.

The transition is dangerous because performance becomes obvious before resilience does.

A benchmark can show what the agent accomplishes.

A demo can show how quickly.

A recovery capability is demonstrated by a bad day everyone hopes not to experience.

The buyer needs a proxy.

Standards can help.

Insurance can help.

Independent testing can help.

Incident disclosure can help.

Procurement requirements can help.

The goal is not one global bureaucracy deciding which agent is safe.

The goal is making the hidden properties of dependence visible enough that competition can act on them.

There is also a geopolitical version of re-entry.

A country may use domestic AI systems and still depend on foreign chips, cloud services, model providers, data infrastructure, software libraries, or network standards.

Another country may seek sovereign AI capacity for strategic reasons.

Sovereignty can become a race too.

The danger is equating domestic ownership with recoverability.

A nationally controlled model does not guarantee a human-operable system.

The re-entry threshold is about operational structure, not flags.

A country can own the machine layer and still become dependent on it.

This is perhaps the most important point for policymakers.

The threat is not only foreign control.

The threat can be self-built indispensability.

National champions can create it.

Public systems can create it.

Military systems can create it.

Domestic technology can create it.

The machine does not need to belong to an adversary to narrow national options.

The race into dependence therefore cannot be solved by winning the AI race in the usual sense.

More capability may be necessary.

Winning also means retaining the freedom to govern the capability.

A country that becomes the best in the world at autonomous systems and cannot safely operate without them has gained power and lost an option.

A company that dominates its market through agents and cannot survive their removal has gained efficiency and acquired a new existential dependency.

A professional who multiplies output with AI and loses the underlying skill has gained leverage and narrowed a fallback.

These are not arguments against the gain.

They are arguments for noticing the option being spent.

Races make options expensive because nobody wants to carry weight the competitor discarded.

The policy task is to make the weight useful.

Recoverability should produce trust.

Trust should produce access to markets, capital, contracts, insurance, and critical deployments.

Then the actor preserving the fallback is not simply slower.

The actor is selling a stronger system.

The global AI takeover threat will become much harder to prevent if the safest choice consistently loses.

The most important design problem may therefore be economic rather than philosophical.

Make the systems we can regain control of outperform the systems we cannot.
