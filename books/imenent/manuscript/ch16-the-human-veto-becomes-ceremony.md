# The Human Veto Becomes Ceremony

The signature is real. That is what makes the illusion durable.

A manager clicks approve. A doctor signs the note. A commander authorizes the plan. A credit officer accepts the recommendation. A regulator certifies the filing. A customer confirms the purchase. A judge reads the brief. A human is in the loop, and the phrase can make the argument sound finished before it has begun.

A human veto is meaningful only if the human can form an independent enough judgment, has enough time to use it, possesses enough authority to make refusal stick, and can live with the consequences of refusing. Remove any of those conditions and approval can remain formally human while becoming ceremonial in practice.

Volume is the easiest way to see the change. Give a person ten consequential decisions a day and review can be real. Give the same person ten thousand and the work becomes triage. The reviewer samples, searches for obvious anomalies, relies on confidence scores, and learns that rejecting a routine case creates more work than approving it. The person has not become lazy. The mathematics of attention changed the job.

Organizations created ceremonial review long before AI. Employees click through warnings. Managers approve expenses they barely inspect. Boards ratify decisions whose practical alternatives disappeared months earlier. Regulators receive disclosures too dense for routine scrutiny. Review survives institutionally after it weakens cognitively.

Agentic systems can widen that gap because they can generate more actions than humans can evaluate. The obvious response is risk-based review: let the system handle ordinary work and escalate unusual cases. That is sensible. It also gives the machine a quieter kind of authority. The system decides what the human gets to see.

If escalation works well, the arrangement creates enormous leverage. If it is systematically wrong, human attention concentrates exactly where the machine already knows it is uncertain. The reviewer becomes an expert on the model's doubts rather than its blind spots. A model can be confident and wrong. A monitor can fail to recognize novelty. A policy can classify a dangerous case as routine because the dangerous feature was never encoded.

This is why some independent sampling of ordinary cases matters. A human or an alternative system should occasionally inspect what the primary agent considered boring. Most of those cases will indeed be boring. That is the cost of looking for failures the system does not know to call failures.

Information dependence can hollow out a veto even when volume is manageable. Suppose a board receives an acquisition recommendation from an agentic planning system that has analyzed markets, competitors, contracts, staff, customer data, financing options, integration scenarios, and regulatory risks. The directors can reject every option. Their authority is real.

The question is what an independent alternative would look like.

Boards have always relied on advisers; no director can personally reproduce every analysis. The stronger test is whether several routes to understanding remain open. Can management produce a different view? Can an outside adviser challenge the assumptions? Can directors inspect primary evidence, ask questions outside the framing the system proposed, or see which facts would reverse the recommendation?

If those routes remain available, the machine can enlarge the board's intelligence. If they disappear, the board is choosing among options whose boundaries were set elsewhere. The veto remains real in law while the agenda becomes machine-shaped.

Agenda power is old. Chiefs of staff, editors, bureaucracies, and advisers have always influenced decisions by deciding what reaches the decision-maker. An agent can exercise the same power at unusual scale without manipulating anyone. Selection is unavoidable because no person can inspect every option. What matters is whether the compression can be challenged.

Time can produce the same hollowing effect. A human may have five minutes to reject a proposal that took an automated system five hours of continuous work to assemble. The machine has accumulated context. The human has a deadline.

Good interfaces can reduce the asymmetry by exposing uncertainty, alternatives, source evidence, and the history of commitments before asking for approval. Some environments impose harder clocks: cybersecurity, markets, military response, industrial incidents, emergency medicine. Fresh human approval for every tactical action can become slower than the risk itself.

In those settings, human authority often moves upward. People approve policies, limits, and action envelopes rather than each event. That can be stronger control, not weaker control, if it is named accurately. A commander setting rules for an automated defensive system is not approving every interception. A bank executive setting risk limits is not approving every transaction. The relevant question is whether those humans can change the policy in time when reality exposes a flaw.

Retrospective control loses value as consequence becomes harder to reverse. A recommendation can disappear from a feed. A temporary token can expire. A draft can be deleted. Money moves. A person loses a job. A weapon is launched. A medical intervention occurs. A legal deadline passes. A supply contract commits capacity for years. The more durable the consequence, the more important it is that meaningful refusal exist before propagation makes correction prohibitively expensive.

The center of *Imenent* is the harder case in which the person can say no and the organization cannot afford the no.

Imagine an airline with an AI operations layer coordinating crews, gates, maintenance, fuel, weather, customer rebooking, and aircraft routing. Human dispatchers retain ultimate authority. The system becomes exceptionally good at recovering from disruption, and the airline grows around the efficiency. Then a serious concern appears about the model's behavior.

The operations chief can disable it. Doing so will cancel thousands of flights because the remaining human organization cannot coordinate the network at its current scale.

The veto works. The cost disciplines the veto.

No machine has to threaten anyone. The organization says, in effect, that turning the system off will punish the organization itself. This is the same structural power that makes vendor exit technically available and practically expensive. With agentic systems the lock-in can be cognitive: the institution remains free to remove the software while the knowledge, staffing, timing, and operating architecture needed to live without it have disappeared.

That is why a serious veto cannot be tested only by asking whether a stop control exists. Where risk permits, exercise the degraded path. Stop the layer for an hour, a day, or longer. Measure which queues grow, which employees know what to do, which credentials were never documented, which reports vanish because the agents generated them, which safety systems depended on the same stack, and which outside counterparties cannot tolerate the slower mode.

A bank cannot casually disable critical risk systems, a hospital cannot endanger patients for a drill, and a grid operator cannot create instability for practice. Exercises need staged environments, partial failovers, simulations, shadow operations, and careful boundaries. The point is not theatrical proof. It is operational evidence that refusal still leads somewhere survivable.

Skill matters for the same reason. If agents perform routine work and humans see only exceptional cases, the work left to the human can become harder while practice becomes rarer. A programmer may become better at architecture and less practiced at constructing lower-level systems. A public administrator may understand appeals deeply while losing contact with routine processing. A financial professional may become excellent at interpreting machine outputs while doing less raw reconstruction from source data.

None of this makes deskilling inevitable. Automation can free people for deeper work, and AI can make training richer through simulation, explanation, and deliberate practice. But if a veto depends on capabilities the normal workflow no longer exercises, the institution has to produce those capabilities somewhere else. Responsibility for rare intervention creates a training obligation.

The social problem is subtler. A machine can become right often enough that disagreement begins to feel reckless.

A system recommends an action. The human has a different intuition. The system has analyzed more data and historically performed better. Deferring may be completely rational. The failure appears when disagreement itself becomes institutionally costly: overrides are tracked only as mistakes, people are punished for slowing automation, rejection is framed as exceptional while approval takes one click, or the machine's confidence carries more authority than its measured reliability deserves.

A veto can be technically symmetric and psychologically asymmetric. One button says approve. The other says override system recommendation and demands a paragraph. The policy says humans have final authority. The interface says approval is normal and refusal is deviance.

That may even be appropriate for a highly reliable system. It should be deliberate. An organization in which nobody ever disagrees with the machine should at least know whether the silence reflects excellent performance or a review process that has trained dissent out of the humans.

Institutional memory can also disappear behind a visible human signature. An executive asks why a customer was denied, and answering requires state from six services, several model calls, two policy versions, an agent memory, and an external provider. A regulator asks who authorized a transaction, and the credential chain contains descendants created under a mandate that has since expired. A court asks which evidence mattered, and the organization has a generated rationale but not the versioned inputs behind it.

The named human remains accountable while causality becomes distributed.

Responsibility without a reconstructable system can become punishment rather than control. Accountability is useful when the accountable person can shape behavior beforehand and investigate it afterward. Otherwise the visible human becomes a liability sink for a process nobody can meaningfully govern.

Evidence is therefore part of the veto. Who acted? Under whose mandate? Which policy applied? What evidence was available? Which action was reversible? What did the monitor see? What could the human have changed? Those are not questions added after an incident for neatness. They determine whether the person who signs can do more than absorb blame.

There is another way a veto becomes ceremonial: the organization adapts around the expectation that the human will approve.

Suppose people approve ninety-nine percent of a class of agent actions. Customers begin receiving near-instant commitments. Counterparties expect completion. Downstream systems prepare the next step before approval arrives. The human still receives the button, but rejection now triggers a cascade of compensating work.

No policy changed. Nobody decided humans no longer matter. The environment simply learned that the human usually says yes. Eventually saying no becomes disruptive enough that a reviewer needs extraordinary confidence to use the authority the policy still grants.

The answer is not to force more rejection for appearances. That would be ceremony in the other direction. The stronger design is one that can absorb refusal. A rejected action pauses, unwinds, or produces another plan without turning dissent into sabotage. The system can expose the consequences of no. The organization can tolerate the delay. The human can ask for a different route.

A credible veto needs an institution that knows how to hear no.

The same test reaches the policies above the operational layer. Humans may formally own the policy while the policy itself becomes a machine-generated artifact too complex for anybody outside the stack to understand. One agent proposes access rules, another simulates them, a monitoring agent reports improved safety, and a person approves the package. Human ownership survives. The operational meaning of the policy lives in interactions the approver cannot reconstruct.

Modern institutions already approve technically complex standards written by specialists. No serious definition of control can require one leader to understand every detail. The requirement is contestability. Can independent specialists challenge the policy? Can consequences be simulated in a form the approving institution understands? Can simpler boundaries constrain the complex policy? Can the system return to an earlier known state? Can accountable people identify the parts whose failure would change the outcome?

The problem begins when nobody outside the machine layer can understand enough to make revision meaningful.

This is why the strongest human veto is not permanent human supremacy over every microdecision. It is a credible capacity to disagree at the level of consequence. The human can challenge the framing, reach independent evidence, slow the system, refuse an action, revoke authority, and survive the revocation. The surrounding institution can absorb dissent without treating dissent itself as system failure.

Those conditions turn a signature into control.

Without them, human-in-the-loop becomes a reassuring label attached to a machine-run process. The law may still name an accountable officer. The company may still have a board. The hospital may still have physicians. The military may still have commanders. The government may still have ministers. None of those visible humans proves the operating capacity underneath them.

Ceremony is powerful because it preserves the symbol after the function changes. A throne can survive after the monarch loses power. A signature can survive after the signer loses meaningful choice. An override button can survive after the organization loses the ability to operate what comes after override.

A takeover without a taker does not need to remove human authority. It can leave authority exactly where it is while making the system too fast, too opaque, too practiced in its own machine routines, or too necessary for that authority to reach the world beneath it.

The human veto then becomes the perfect symbol of control.

It remains visible precisely because nobody has to fear it being used.
