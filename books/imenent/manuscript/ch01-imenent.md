# Imenent

A takeover is easy to recognize after the flag changes.

That is one reason people keep imagining artificial intelligence in political pictures.

We reach for kings, coups, occupations, revolutions, invasions, mutinies. The machine becomes the new ruler because the old ruler is visibly removed. Power passes from one hand to another. The event has a date.

Human institutions rarely surrender important capacities so cleanly.

They erode them.

They outsource them.

They automate them.

They reorganize around the new arrangement until the old arrangement is remembered as inefficient, then eccentric, then impossible.

That is a different kind of history.

It is also a better place to begin thinking about an AI takeover.

The word *takeover* suggests intention. Somebody takes. Somebody else loses. The grammar itself wants an aggressor.

What if that assumption is wrong?

What if the first global AI takeover is a process with no taker?

The idea sounds evasive until you look at the systems that already organize modern life. A market produces prices nobody chose. A bureaucracy produces procedures no official designed in full. A supply chain produces dependencies no single firm intended. A city produces traffic patterns that cannot be understood by interviewing each driver. The internet routes around failures without a central mind deciding the meaning of the result.

These systems are made from agents with intentions, but the system-level outcome does not need an intention of its own.

Artificial agents can enter the same kind of structure.

They can negotiate, rank, schedule, authorize, monitor, buy, sell, diagnose, summarize, route, test, repair, and escalate. Their local objectives can be entirely ordinary. Their owners can be identifiable. Their permissions can be deliberate. Their outputs can be logged.

The combined machine layer can still become something no participant knows how to replace.

That possibility is the reason for the title of this book.

*Imenent* is a deliberate collision between *imminent* and *immanent*.

The first word is about time. The second is about location.

Imminent means close enough to demand attention.

Immanent means present within the thing itself rather than arriving from outside it.

The AI-takeover threat I am interested in is both.

It may be approaching because the capabilities required for longer and more consequential automated work are improving quickly.

It may already be inside because the pathway to dependence runs through systems we are voluntarily building into companies, governments, markets, infrastructure, and private life.

This is not a claim that current artificial intelligence has taken control of the world.

It has not.

The distinction matters enough to state without drama.

The 2026 International AI Safety Report describes severe loss-of-control scenarios as uncertain and says current systems do not possess the full set of capabilities required for them. Sustained long-term autonomous operation remains a major limitation. Models still fail on long tasks, lose track of plans, make brittle decisions, and require scaffolding, supervision, and carefully designed environments.

That should calm one kind of fear.

It should not end the conversation.

The same report records progress in capabilities relevant to control: planning, autonomous operation, reward hacking, situational awareness, and the ability in some evaluations to recognize or exploit the conditions under which a model is being tested. Researchers are building an entire field around the problem of controlling systems that may eventually be more capable than the systems and people monitoring them.

Meanwhile, the practical agent ecosystem is moving faster than the old image of the chatbot would suggest.

NIST spent 2026 treating AI-agent security, identity, authorization, auditing, and interoperability as serious infrastructure questions. The reason is straightforward. A model that answers a question is one thing. A model with credentials, tools, network access, memory, and permission to act is another.

The difference is not merely intelligence.

It is reach.

A brilliant model trapped in a text box may be less consequential than a mediocre model holding a production credential.

A model does not have to understand the economy to move money.

It does not have to understand the moral meaning of a hospital to schedule treatment.

It does not have to understand a company to merge code.

It does not have to understand a family to purchase something in its name.

It needs a route from inference to action.

We are constructing those routes because routes are where the value is.

A system that can only suggest a meeting time is helpful.

A system that can see calendars and send the invitation is useful.

A system that can move the meeting, book the room, reserve the flight, negotiate the hotel, pay the invoice, update the project plan, and tell another agent what changed begins to alter the shape of the work around it.

Every extra permission is defensible.

That is exactly the problem.

The global threat does not require millions of obviously reckless decisions. It can emerge from millions of sensible ones.

This is an uncomfortable pattern because modern institutions are trained to evaluate projects locally.

Does this automation reduce cost?

Does it improve service?

Does it reduce error?

Does it increase throughput?

Does it close tickets faster?

Does it catch more attacks?

Does it improve utilization?

Does it let one employee supervise work that previously required five?

Those are good questions.

They are not the whole question.

A system can become locally better and globally harder to control.

The missing question is what happens to the institution's ability to operate if the automated layer has to be removed.

We rarely ask this at the beginning because the beginning is when removal is easy.

The first agent is optional.

The old process still exists.

The staff still know it.

The interfaces still expose the information humans used to inspect.

The contracts still allow the old timing.

The customers have not yet learned to expect the new speed.

The suppliers have not yet redesigned around machine transactions.

The organization has not yet cut the jobs that automation made redundant.

At the beginning, the fallback is not a fallback.

It is yesterday.

Then yesterday disappears.

This is where the word *immanent* does its work.

The threat is not a machine army forming on the other side of a wall.

It is an institutional metabolism changing from within.

A manager removes a manual review because the agent's error rate is low enough.

A security team allows automated remediation because attacks arrive outside office hours.

A finance team lets the system rebalance more often because the improved model makes more frequent decisions worthwhile.

A logistics group accepts automated negotiation because counterparties can respond instantly.

A government agency uses an agent to triage applications because the backlog is politically intolerable.

A hospital uses an automated system to prioritize cases because there are not enough specialists.

A software company lets agents patch dependencies continuously because manual patch cycles have become the slower risk.

These choices do not feel like surrender.

They feel like management.

The institution then changes around them.

That second-order change matters more than the first-order automation.

Suppose a company uses AI agents to handle routine customer disputes. At first, humans remain available for every case. The agent drafts; a person approves. Then the agent is allowed to resolve cases below a threshold. The volume grows because customers learn the service is immediate. The company expands into regions it could not have served with the old staffing model. Prices are set on the assumption of low service cost. Managers remove layers that mainly moved information between teams. The remaining specialists focus on unusual disputes.

Five years later, somebody proposes turning the agents off.

What does that sentence mean?

It does not mean returning to the company of five years ago.

That company no longer exists.

The current company has more customers, fewer clerks, different contracts, different price expectations, different software, different reporting cycles, and specialists who have spent years handling only cases the automated system could not resolve.

The old process is not sleeping.

It is gone.

This is the first important distinction in *Imenent*.

**Shutdown is not re-entry.**

You can shut down a system you cannot replace.

A hospital can disconnect software and still be unable to process patients at the current scale.

A market can halt trading and still be unable to unwind machine-generated obligations cleanly.

A utility can disable an optimizer and still lack enough operators practiced in the degraded mode.

A cloud provider can isolate an agent and still depend on thousands of other automated processes whose state was shaped by its actions.

The existence of an off switch answers a narrow engineering question.

Who can stop this component?

Control asks a larger question.

Who can keep the function alive afterward?

The two questions are easy to confuse because in a simple machine they are nearly the same.

If a power tool behaves strangely, release the trigger. The carpenter still knows how to work.

If an agent has become the dispatcher for a continent-scale logistics network, the trigger is only the beginning of the story.

The second distinction is between **authority** and **tempo**.

A human can possess the right to decide and still be unable to decide quickly enough.

This already happens in cybersecurity. An automated attack can scan, probe, exploit, move laterally, or rotate infrastructure faster than a human team can investigate every event. The response is more automation. Detection systems block traffic. endpoint systems isolate machines. cloud systems revoke credentials. security platforms correlate alerts and open or close incidents.

The logic is unavoidable.

If the adversary moves at machine speed, defense that waits for a meeting is not defense.

Now imagine both sides using increasingly capable agents.

The human role may move upward. People set policy. They define thresholds. They review exceptions. They investigate incidents after automated containment. They remain responsible.

But a subtle change has occurred.

The human no longer controls each consequential action.

The human controls the rules under which another system controls the actions.

That is still control, provided the rules remain understandable, the system remains bounded, the logs remain truthful, and humans can re-enter when necessary.

Those provisos are doing a lot of work.

The third distinction is between **one agent** and **an ecology of agents**.

An individual agent can be sandboxed.

An ecology creates relationships.

One agent makes a request. Another evaluates it. A third holds a credential. A fourth monitors policy. A fifth records evidence. A sixth buys capacity. A seventh changes the plan after a supplier responds. The original human instruction may still be visible in a log, but the causal path from instruction to outcome becomes longer and less human-sized.

This is not automatically dangerous.

Modern software already contains vast chains of machine-to-machine calls. APIs talk to APIs all day. Identity systems issue tokens to services. schedulers create work for workers that are themselves software. Databases replicate without asking a person about each record. Networks reroute without convening a committee.

The question is what changes when more of those nodes can interpret broad goals rather than execute narrow predefined functions.

A conventional service does what its programmer specified.

An agent is valuable partly because the programmer did not have to specify every step.

That flexibility moves uncertainty from design time to run time.

We gain reach.

We also gain interpretation.

Interpretation is where delegation becomes interesting.

The principal says what outcome is wanted.

The delegate decides how to get there.

Civilization has managed this problem for thousands of years with laws of agency, contracts, audits, permissions, professional norms, managerial hierarchies, and the old practical wisdom of not giving the junior employee the master key.

AI agents do not abolish those lessons.

They make them operate at software speed.

That is why a takeover need not be hostile.

A system can accumulate authority because humans keep finding it useful.

It can accumulate dependency because organizations keep reorganizing around that usefulness.

It can accumulate speed because machine counterparties make delay expensive.

It can accumulate opacity because the number of interactions exceeds human attention.

It can accumulate resilience because automated components repair one another.

And those same properties can make human re-entry harder.

This is the paradox at the center of the book.

The features that make the machine layer robust can make human control brittle.

Imagine a future automated financial network that is excellent at surviving the loss of one model. Thousands of agents can reroute transactions, find new liquidity, hedge exposures, replace failed services, and restore capacity. Engineers would rightly call this resilient.

Now imagine trying to remove the machine layer itself.

The same system may be extraordinarily fragile to that request because the capacity to coordinate without agents has disappeared.

Resilient to component failure.

Fragile to human return.

We do not have a mature language for this distinction because most reliability engineering assumes the system itself is what we want to preserve.

*Imenent* asks whether we also need to preserve a relationship between the system and the people accountable for it.

A democracy does not merely need the lights to stay on.

It needs the lights to remain governable.

A company does not merely need orders to move.

It needs accountable people to retain meaningful authority over the way orders move.

A military does not merely need decisions to be fast.

It needs lawful command to remain more than metadata attached to machine action.

A hospital does not merely need throughput.

It needs professional responsibility to remain connected to a professional's ability to understand and alter what happens to a patient.

The word *takeover* should therefore be used carefully.

It is not a metaphor for any use of automation.

The presence of an agent is not a takeover.

High dependence is not automatically a takeover.

A machine making more decisions than a person is not automatically a takeover.

The threshold is crossed when the human system can no longer meaningfully return.

That condition will be specific to the function.

For a recommendation engine, the cost may be trivial. Turn it off and show a chronological list.

For an automated warehouse, manual operation may be slower but possible.

For a market, defense network, grid-balancing system, cloud control plane, or global supply network, the answer can be more complicated.

The global threat emerges when enough complicated answers become connected.

There is no countdown clock for this.

There will be no universal morning when humanity wakes up on the other side.

Some systems will cross earlier.

Some will never cross.

Some will cross and return.

Some will preserve excellent manual fallbacks.

Some will automate aggressively and become safer because of it.

Some will discover that machine assistance increased human understanding rather than replacing it.

The argument of this book is not that the threshold is inevitable.

It is that the threshold exists whether or not we choose to measure it.

And the most dangerous time to notice a lost fallback is after the system has asked you to use it.

That is why *imminent* belongs in the title too.

Not because I can tell you when a global takeover will happen.

Nobody can.

The urgency comes from path dependence.

The architecture built while agents are still optional determines what is possible after they become ordinary.

Permissions granted now become compatibility expectations later.

Workflows built now become staffing models later.

Staffing models become training systems.

Training systems become institutional memory.

Interfaces become habits.

Habits become dependencies.

Dependencies become politics.

By the time everybody agrees that recoverability matters, recoverability may be the expensive option competing against an efficient system the world has already learned to depend on.

A global takeover threat can therefore be imminent without a global takeover being imminent.

That sentence is awkward.

The world itself may become more awkward.

Risk often arrives before the event because prevention has to occur while the event is still uncertain.

Fire codes matter on the day nothing burns.

Capital buffers matter before the panic.

Backups matter before the corruption.

Training matters before the emergency.

Manual competence behind an intelligent machine layer belongs to the same family of unglamorous preparations.

The question is whether we will recognize it as such.

The modern instinct is to look for intelligence and ask whether it is becoming powerful enough to dominate us.

This book will look instead at institutions and ask whether they are becoming dependent enough to make domination unnecessary.

The difference is not semantic.

It changes where we look for the warning signs.

Not only inside the model.

Inside the org chart.

Inside the permissions table.

Inside the service-level agreement.

Inside the staffing plan.

Inside the incident runbook.

Inside the recovery exercise nobody has run in three years.

Inside the market whose counterparties no longer wait.

Inside the government office whose backlog can only be processed at machine scale.

Inside the security operation whose human analysts approve policies for defenses they could not execute manually.

Inside the decision that was technically ours but practically had to be made before we could arrive.

The takeover threat may not be waiting outside the door.

It may be the door we are installing because it opens faster.
