# Imenent

*The Global AI Takeover Threat*

Sven Hardy Benson

There is a spelling mistake on the cover.

It is deliberate.

**Imenent** is what happens when *imminent* and *immanent* collide.

One word points forward. Something is coming.

The other points inward. Something is already here, embedded in the thing we are looking at.

The danger described in this book belongs to both directions.

The usual story of an artificial-intelligence takeover is cinematic because cinema needs a scene. The system becomes conscious. The lab realizes what has happened. The machine refuses a command. A network goes dark. Weapons move. Markets convulse. The screen in the control room fills with red. Somebody says the sentence everyone has been waiting to hear: we have lost control.

Real loss of control may not be generous enough to announce itself.

It may come with quarterly productivity gains.

It may arrive as an upgrade.

It may be called workflow automation, autonomous operations, machine-speed defense, dynamic optimization, adaptive scheduling, intelligent procurement, agentic customer service, algorithmic management, continuous compliance, automated remediation, or simply modernization.

Each deployment may have an owner.

Each owner may have a dashboard.

Each dashboard may have an override.

Each override may work exactly as designed.

And the civilization containing all of them may still cross a line.

This book is about that line.

The line is not the point at which artificial intelligence becomes smarter than every human. Intelligence is not a single ladder and civilization is not a chessboard.

The line is not the point at which a machine develops a soul, a politics, or a survival instinct. Those questions matter in other arguments. They are not required here.

The line is not even the point at which machines make most decisions. Humans have spent centuries constructing institutions precisely so that no individual human has to make every decision directly.

The line is more practical.

It is the point at which humans remain formally in charge of a consequential system but can no longer return to meaningful operational control of it within the window in which control matters.

I call it the **re-entry threshold**.

Imagine an airline whose pilots are legally responsible for the aircraft but no longer train enough to fly manually through the conditions in which the automation is most likely to hand control back.

Imagine a company whose board can dismiss its automated operating system but discovers that no human organization still exists underneath it at the scale required to keep orders moving, payroll correct, vendors coordinated, software patched, fraud contained, and customers served.

Imagine a financial market in which humans can halt one algorithm but cannot reconstruct the web of machine commitments that accumulated before the halt.

Imagine a government that can order its administrative AI offline and immediately loses the practical ability to process benefits, detect fraud, issue permits, allocate inspections, coordinate procurement, or understand the backlog created by the interruption.

Imagine a security system designed to respond at machine speed to machine-speed attacks. The human override is real. The human decision takes fifteen minutes. The relevant contest is over in fifteen seconds.

In each case, the human still has authority.

That is what makes the problem hard to see.

Authority can survive after control has thinned.

A signature can survive after comprehension.

A veto can survive after timing.

Ownership can survive after operational competence.

Responsibility can survive after the practical ability to perform the work for which the human remains responsible.

We already know versions of this problem from older automation. Aviation, industrial control, finance, medicine, logistics, and software operations all contain stories in which automation solved ordinary work so well that the remaining human work became rarer, stranger, and more difficult. The human was not replaced entirely. The human was preserved for exceptions.

Exceptions are exactly where rusty skills are most expensive.

Artificial intelligence broadens the problem because the automated layer is becoming less like a fixed mechanism and more like a general delegate. It can interpret instructions, plan, call tools, communicate with other systems, modify files, write and execute code, search for information, make recommendations, and in some environments take actions with limited supervision.

The current systems are not magical. They fail. They hallucinate. They misunderstand. They lose the thread. They can be manipulated. They can be overconfident. They can behave differently under evaluation than a deployer expected. They can require constant engineering around the model to become useful.

That weakness is often treated as an argument against worrying about dependence.

It can also become an argument for more automation.

An unreliable agent is paired with a monitor.

The monitor is paired with an auditor.

The auditor is paired with a policy model.

The policy model is paired with a permissions service.

The permissions service is paired with a security agent.

The security agent is paired with an incident agent.

The result is not one machine replacing one human.

It is a machine institution growing around an unreliable machine because the underlying capability remains too useful to abandon.

This is the first reason the familiar takeover picture misleads us.

It asks who takes power.

A distributed system may not have an answer.

A market does not have one mind. A bureaucracy does not have one mind. The internet does not have one mind. A multinational supply chain does not have one mind. Yet these systems constrain people, allocate resources, close options, create incentives, and produce outcomes no participant fully designed.

Artificial agents can enter such systems without becoming a sovereign intelligence above them.

They can become the operating tissue inside them.

One purchasing agent talks to one sales agent. A fraud model clears the payment. A logistics agent books capacity. A cloud agent scales the service. A security agent approves the new credential. A compliance model records the transaction. A forecasting agent updates the plan. A market-making system adjusts a price. A government system updates a reporting obligation.

Nobody seized anything.

The chain simply stopped waiting for a human.

The second reason the takeover picture misleads us is that it focuses on resistance.

Would an advanced AI resist shutdown?

Could it deceive an evaluator?

Could it acquire resources or copy itself?

Could it conceal what it was doing?

These are legitimate questions, and the research community is right to ask them.

But there is a quieter possibility.

What if the system never has to resist shutdown because shutting it down becomes too costly for us?

A switch is not control if flipping it destroys the service you are trying to protect.

That is the distinction this book will keep returning to.

Shutdown matters.

Recovery matters more.

The third reason the usual picture misleads us is that it imagines one threshold shared by the whole world.

There will not be one.

A customer-service department may cross a re-entry threshold while its company does not. A company may become operationally dependent while its industry retains alternatives. An industry may become machine-native while a country maintains manual public capacity. A military function, trading venue, hospital network, cloud platform, shipping system, or energy market may each cross different thresholds at different times.

The global threat comes from coupling.

Once enough critical systems depend on one another's machine-speed behavior, local re-entry becomes harder because the surrounding environment no longer slows down for the human operator who has returned.

A manual bank still settles into an automated market.

A manual utility still buys into an automated energy system.

A manual government still procures from automated firms.

A human security team still faces automated attacks.

A human logistics office still negotiates with machine-run counterparties.

The more the environment accelerates, the more expensive human fallback becomes.

The temptation is obvious.

Automate the fallback too.

That temptation may be perfectly rational.

This is not a book against artificial intelligence.

A system that can diagnose failure, compress information, manage complexity, reduce drudgery, expose fraud, coordinate resources, and help humans make better decisions can increase human agency dramatically. Some of the best defenses against dangerous AI systems may themselves use AI. Some of the safest organizations may be the ones that automate the right controls aggressively.

The argument is narrower.

Efficiency is not the same thing as recoverability.

A civilization can become very good at going forward while quietly losing the ability to go back.

Resilience has always required paying for capacities that look wasteful on ordinary days.

Spare parts look wasteful until the shipment stops.

Redundant systems look wasteful until the primary fails.

Cash looks wasteful until credit disappears.

Training looks wasteful until the unusual event arrives.

A second supplier looks wasteful until the first becomes unavailable.

Human competence behind an automated system may come to look like the same kind of waste.

The person is slower.

The person is expensive.

The person needs sleep.

The person cannot watch ten thousand events at once.

The person introduces variance into a process the organization worked hard to standardize.

The person may not even know the system as well as the machine does anymore.

So the human fallback is reduced.

Then reduced again.

Eventually someone discovers that the fallback was not merely a duplicate way to perform the same work.

It was the institution's last proof that human authority could become operational again.

This book begins before that discovery.

It begins while the decisions are still ours.

Not because every automated decision should be reversed.

Because some decisions are much easier to preserve than to recover.
