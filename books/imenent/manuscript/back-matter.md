# Back Matter

*Imenent* is a thesis-driven book about a possible route to loss of human control over consequential systems. It is not a claim that present-day AI has already seized power, that every form of automation is a takeover, or that a global loss-of-control event is inevitable.

The book's central term, **re-entry threshold**, is an original analytic device used here to describe the point at which formal human authority survives but accountable humans can no longer retake, understand, operate, reverse, and sustain the relevant function within the time window in which intervention would matter. It is not a technical standard, legal definition, or validated universal metric.

The coined title **Imenent** deliberately combines *imminent* and *immanent*. The first points toward capabilities and deployment choices that may matter sooner than institutions expect. The second points inward: the threat described in this book can emerge from ordinary, legitimate decisions to connect, authorize, automate, optimize, and depend. The spelling is a title, not a claim that a new scientific term has been discovered.

The closest prior conceptual work includes research on gradual disempowerment, passive and active loss of control, automation dependence and out-of-the-loop performance, principal-agent problems, technological lock-in, systemic risk, AI control, model evaluation, and agent security and authorization. The book's contribution is the synthesis among those fields around one operational question: **can accountable humans still return to the work?**

The research trail for this manuscript is maintained separately from the narrative chapters so the prose can remain readable without disguising uncertainty. The book's `research/README.md` records the evidentiary boundary, prior-art distinctions, language guardrails, counterevidence, and falsification tests. `research/chapter-map.md` ties each chapter to the evidence it is allowed to rely on. `research/source-ledger.csv` records the underlying sources and their role in the argument.

Among the important source families are the 2026 International AI Safety Report; current NIST work on AI-agent security, identity, authorization, auditing, and standards; METR's measurements of frontier-agent task-completion horizons; Stanford's 2026 AI Index; research and telemetry on real-world agent autonomy; work on AI-control protocols, monitoring, deferral, legibility, and adaptive attacks; established delegation and authorization standards; and the academic literature on gradual disempowerment and correlated delegation failure.

Those sources do not prove the book's thesis. They establish pieces of the environment in which the thesis could become relevant: longer autonomous task horizons, increasingly capable tool use, organizational adoption, live authorization problems, machine-assisted monitoring, uncertainty about control protocols, competitive deployment pressure, and the possibility that many locally reasonable decisions can combine into systemic effects.

The manuscript deliberately preserves evidence against its own strongest reading.

Current systems remain substantially short of the sustained autonomous capabilities required by the most severe active loss-of-control scenarios. Agent benchmarks are not the same thing as reliable performance in open-ended institutions. Human operators can be slower, more biased, more corrupt, more tired, and less consistent than well-designed automation. Manual fallback can create risk rather than reduce it. Machine monitoring can improve oversight. AI can make complex systems more legible to people. Bounded permissions can make software safer than a human employee holding broad standing access. Competitive markets can reward resilience. Standards can make authority chains clearer. Diverse automated systems can provide redundancy rather than common-mode failure.

If those countervailing forces dominate, the re-entry threshold may remain distant even in a world saturated with AI.

That would be a good outcome.

The book's falsification tests are therefore important. Its thesis should weaken if long-running autonomy plateaus; if high-impact permissions remain tightly bounded; if critical institutions routinely preserve and exercise human-operable degraded modes; if consequential execution continues at human decision speeds; if inter-agent coupling stays shallow; if oversight and interpretability improve faster than autonomy; if buyers and regulators consistently reward recoverability; or if diverse machine systems reliably check one another without correlated failure.

The argument should also narrow when a domain does not deserve the language of takeover. A recommendation engine that becomes inconvenient to live without is not equivalent to an electrical grid, a financial settlement system, a defense network, or public administration. Criticality matters. So do access, authority, duration, capability, and consequence.

The design agenda developed in the final chapters is likewise not offered as a settled standard. Least privilege, explicit authority chains, subdelegation limits, independent action evidence, reversible staging, degraded modes, retained human skill, portability, diverse monitoring, dependency mapping, and regular re-entry exercises are directions to test against particular systems. Every safeguard has costs. Some increase latency. Some introduce new attack surfaces. Some create paperwork that may become ceremonial. Some forms of manual redundancy are less safe than automation. Good engineering requires choosing controls against real failure modes rather than accumulating rituals.

The central distinction should remain intact through those tradeoffs.

**Shutdown is not re-entry.**

A system can possess an effective emergency stop and still leave its operators unable to preserve the function that mattered after the stop. Human control therefore cannot be inferred from the presence of a switch, an approval box, a legal owner, a board, a minister, or a nominal human-in-the-loop. It has to be demonstrated through the ability to inspect, constrain, interrupt, recover, replace, and redesign the operating system of the institution.

That claim is intentionally stronger than a call for human supervision and weaker than a prediction of inevitable machine sovereignty.

It is a test for whether human accountability remains operational.

The broader warning can be stated without any forecast about machine consciousness.

A society can surrender options without surrendering titles.

It can keep human owners while making companies impossible to run without autonomous systems. It can keep elected governments while making administrative functions impossible to deliver without machine cognition. It can keep commanders while pushing tactical decisions beyond human time. It can keep rights while allowing the non-automated path for exercising them to disappear. It can keep off switches while making the economic and political cost of pressing them unbearable.

That is the form of power migration *Imenent* asks readers to notice.

The desired future is not one in which machines remain weak so humans can remain important.

It is one in which increasingly capable machine systems expand the range of human action without silently consuming the capacity to choose otherwise.

If the systems become more powerful and human decisions continue to matter, the threat described in this book has been answered in the best possible way.
