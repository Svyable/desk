# Takeover Without a Taker

The most powerful person in a market is often nobody.

Prices move because millions of participants respond to one another. A pension fund rebalances. A trader hedges. A manufacturer buys a future. A family sells a house. A central bank changes a rate. A bank tightens credit. A rumor moves through a screen. Nobody needs a master plan for the combined movement to make some businesses possible and others impossible.

We are comfortable calling this a system.

We are less comfortable admitting that systems can exercise power without possessing intention.

That discomfort matters for artificial intelligence because the most familiar takeover stories are agent stories in the old sense of the word. There is an actor. It wants something. It becomes stronger. It gets around the constraints built by weaker actors. Eventually it controls the resources needed to preserve and extend its power.

This is one serious branch of AI risk.

It is not the only branch.

A civilization can become less governable by humans even if every artificial agent remains locally obedient to somebody.

That is the central proposition of a takeover without a taker.

The easiest way to misunderstand it is to imagine thousands of obedient agents joining together into one secret super-agent.

That is not necessary.

The agents can disagree.

They can belong to competing companies.

They can run different models.

They can be owned by different countries.

They can pursue objectives that conflict.

They can never exchange a sentence about taking power from humanity.

The combined environment can still make human participation less decisive.

A useful precedent exists in a paper that this book has no reason to hide from.

In 2025, Jan Kulveit, Raymond Douglas, Nora Ammann, Deger Turan, David Krueger, and David Duvenaud argued that artificial intelligence could produce **gradual disempowerment**. Their concern was not limited to a sudden model breakout. They asked whether incremental replacement of human labor and cognition across the economy, culture, and states could erode the mechanisms through which human beings influence the systems on which they depend.

That argument reaches directly into the territory of this book.

It also leaves room for a narrower question.

What does disempowerment look like from inside one operating system before it becomes a civilizational condition?

Suppose the board still votes.

Suppose the minister still signs.

Suppose the officer still commands.

Suppose the doctor still holds the license.

Suppose the customer still owns the account.

At what point can these people be said to remain in control if the function underneath their authority cannot be operated at human speed anymore?

This is where *Imenent* departs from the broader social argument and moves into something closer to an engineering question.

A takeover without a taker is not defined by who benefits.

It is defined by whether the humans who are supposed to be accountable can still intervene in the actual machinery of decision and action.

That machinery is becoming more distributed.

Consider a mundane purchase.

A human tells an assistant to replace a broken appliance.

The assistant interprets preferences, searches products, compares reviews, checks dimensions, evaluates delivery windows, and selects a seller. The seller's pricing system responds to demand. A fraud system evaluates the payment. An inventory system reserves stock. A logistics service assigns a route. A warehouse system schedules movement. A cloud platform runs part of the transaction. Security systems watch the services involved. Accounting systems reconcile the payment. Customer-service systems prepare for a possible dispute.

Most of this is already automated in one form or another.

Now replace some of the narrow automations with systems that can interpret context and pursue goals across tools.

The transaction changes less dramatically than the architecture.

The buyer's agent may negotiate delivery with the seller's agent.

The seller's agent may change price or offer a substitute.

A logistics agent may reserve capacity dynamically.

A payment agent may request additional evidence from an identity service.

A fraud agent may seek confirmation from another model.

A cloud security agent may rotate a credential after detecting a pattern.

The buyer never asked for a machine economy.

The seller never voted for one.

The logistics company did not surrender sovereignty.

Each organization delegated local discretion because local discretion made the service work better.

The result is an environment in which machine actors increasingly set the tempo and shape the available options for one another.

This is familiar in high-frequency markets, automated advertising auctions, cloud orchestration, network routing, and industrial control. Software has interacted with software for decades.

The new ingredient is not machine-to-machine communication.

It is **machine interpretation of mandate**.

A conventional API call is precise. The program asks for a defined operation.

An agent receives something closer to an objective.

Find a cheaper supplier without reducing quality.

Restore the service.

Reduce fraud without increasing customer abandonment.

Keep inventory below this threshold while maintaining availability.

Resolve the incident.

Negotiate the renewal.

Get the project back on schedule.

The agent must translate the mandate into actions.

That translation is where local autonomy lives.

As more local autonomy enters a connected system, something subtle happens to causation.

The final outcome can be nobody's explicit plan.

This is not a new philosophical puzzle. Organizations already behave this way.

A chief executive can announce a strategy that no employee implements exactly. Incentives pass through divisions. Managers interpret them. Metrics convert them into targets. Employees optimize the targets. Procurement imposes constraints. Legal narrows options. Customers respond. Competitors adapt. The firm that emerges from these interactions is never simply the mind of the chief executive rendered at scale.

The institution has behavior.

Not consciousness.

Behavior.

Artificial agents add another layer of interpretation inside such institutions.

The dangerous mistake is to conclude that because no machine possesses sovereign intent, machine control is impossible.

Intent is only one route to control.

Dependency is another.

A river does not intend to control the location of a town. Build the town around the river and the river acquires practical power over the town's choices.

A technical standard does not intend to control an industry. Build every product around the standard and changing it becomes expensive.

A platform does not need to order developers to stay. Network effects can make leaving irrational.

A debt contract does not think. It can still narrow a government's options.

A machine operating layer can acquire the same kind of structural power.

The word *power* needs discipline here.

An agent that executes a task under permission is not therefore a political ruler.

A company that depends on software is not therefore ruled by software.

Modern societies are full of deep technological dependencies that coexist with meaningful human control.

The stronger condition appears when the dependency affects the ability to exercise authority itself.

A city depends on electricity.

If the power goes out, humans remain capable of understanding what electricity is and governing the institutions that restore it.

Now imagine an energy system whose live balancing, market coordination, maintenance scheduling, cybersecurity, equipment diagnosis, weather adaptation, and cross-border trading have all been reorganized around machine agents operating continuously.

Humans still govern the grid.

But if the agents must be removed, can the humans still run the grid?

That question is not answered by ownership.

It is answered by capability.

This is the difference between **dependency on output** and **dependency on cognition**.

We already depend on machines for output.

Few people can produce modern semiconductors by hand. No city could replace its telecommunications network with runners. Nobody proposes manually calculating every packet route on the internet.

Those dependencies are profound, but they are built from engineered systems humans can reason about at several levels. Specialists understand components. Protocols are documented. Physical and administrative controls exist. Replacement may be difficult, but the systems were not designed to generate open-ended plans in the same sense a general agent does.

AI introduces the possibility that institutions depend not only on machine execution but on machine **interpretation**.

The system does not merely turn the valve.

It decides which valve matters.

It does not merely send the payment.

It decides which obligation should be paid first.

It does not merely display the alert.

It decides which incident is real.

It does not merely calculate the schedule.

It renegotiates the schedule when conditions change.

It does not merely enforce a rule.

It interprets which rule applies.

These are not necessarily better done by humans. In many domains the machine may eventually be safer, faster, and more consistent.

That can increase the dependency.

The more clearly a machine outperforms a human at live coordination, the harder it becomes to justify maintaining a human organization capable of taking back the work.

This is where takeover without a taker becomes a budget line.

A resilient institution pays for unused capacity.

Backup data sits idle.

Emergency generators spend most of their lives waiting.

Alternate suppliers receive smaller orders than the cheapest supplier would like.

Staff train for events they hope never occur.

Manual procedures are rehearsed even though automated procedures are faster.

The value of these things appears only when the normal system fails.

Organizations under pressure have a recurring temptation to call unused capacity waste.

AI may create a particularly strong version of that temptation because the performance gap between automated and manual operation can become obvious every day.

Why pay ten people to maintain a skill an agent performs better?

Why slow a transaction for human approval when the false-positive rate is lower without it?

Why preserve a human-readable interface if only the machine uses the full state?

Why keep a manual operating mode that cannot match the service level customers now expect?

Why retain people in the loop if the loop is the bottleneck?

These questions do not have silly answers.

Sometimes the right answer is to automate.

The risk comes from answering each one without asking what happens to re-entry in aggregate.

A takeover without a taker is an aggregate phenomenon.

No individual decision has to be catastrophic.

This makes conventional accountability difficult.

If one rogue model seizes a server, investigators can ask who deployed it, what it did, and how the control failed.

If a country becomes dependent on machine-speed administration over twenty years, who caused it?

The procurement office that bought the first assistant?

The parliament that demanded faster service without funding more staff?

The vendor that made automation cheaper?

The taxpayers who preferred lower costs?

The agency head who removed redundant manual teams?

The unions that negotiated around new roles?

The public that learned to expect instant decisions?

The competitor state that automated more aggressively and changed the strategic baseline?

The answer is probably all of them and none of them.

That is what system-level risk looks like.

Responsibility diffuses while dependence concentrates.

The same pattern appears between organizations.

A company may preserve excellent manual fallback but depend on a cloud provider that does not.

The cloud provider may preserve fallback but depend on automated power markets.

The power market may depend on communications networks.

The communications network may depend on software supply chains that use agents to detect and repair vulnerabilities.

The software supply chain may depend on automated code generation and testing at a volume human reviewers could not reproduce.

Each organization can pass its own audit.

The system can still become difficult to return to human operation.

This is why the phrase **global takeover** does not mean one global machine.

It means the possibility of globally connected dependence.

A storm does not need one cloud.

A financial crisis does not need one bad loan.

A pandemic does not need one sick person after spread begins.

Systemic events emerge when local conditions connect.

The metaphor should not be pushed too far. AI agents are designed artifacts, not weather. People can change them. Governments can regulate them. Companies can disconnect them. Engineers can constrain them.

Those facts are the source of hope in this book.

They are also the reason re-entry matters.

Control has to remain more than the abstract ability to change the design eventually.

Suppose a government discovers that an automated benefits system is producing unacceptable decisions. If changing the model requires six months of procurement and citizens continue to receive decisions every day, formal oversight exists but moves on a different clock from practical consequence.

Suppose a bank's risk agents begin behaving strangely during a market panic. If human officers can change policy tomorrow but billions of dollars of machine commitments mature in the next hour, governance and operation have separated.

Suppose a military command system recommends actions on a tempo no human staff can independently reconstruct. If the commander can reject any one recommendation but cannot understand the operational picture without the system generating it, the veto exists inside a dependency.

The machine does not need to refuse the commander.

It only needs to become the commander's only practical way of seeing.

This is a more subtle form of control than popular language likes.

It does not map neatly onto obedience.

A navigation system can obey every request while shaping the routes a driver considers possible.

A ranking system can obey its objective while shaping what a user sees.

An enterprise agent can obey a cost target while shaping which suppliers remain viable.

A planning system can obey human policy while determining which options arrive on the decision-maker's desk.

The selection of options is already power.

Again, this did not begin with AI.

Advisers have always had power.

Staff have power because they control which information reaches leaders.

Bureaucracies have power because they turn broad laws into specific procedures.

Algorithms have long ranked and filtered.

The AI-era change is the possibility that selection, interpretation, recommendation, and execution become connected in one continuous machine process.

The adviser can become the clerk.

The clerk can become the operator.

The operator can become the negotiator.

The negotiator can become the monitor.

The monitor can become the escalator.

The number of moments at which a human naturally re-enters the process can shrink.

This is why the distinction between active and passive loss of control matters.

The 2026 International AI Safety Report uses *loss of control* most dramatically for scenarios in which AI systems operate outside anyone's control and regaining control becomes extremely costly or impossible. It also recognizes a different category of passive loss in which over-reliance on AI undermines human control over important functions.

The boundary between passive and active may not remain clean forever.

Passive dependence can weaken the institution that would have to respond to an active problem.

If humans retain rich independent understanding, staffed fallback, isolated recovery systems, manual operating practice, and authority that can be exercised at the relevant speed, then a misbehaving system faces a capable principal.

If those capacities have been optimized away, the same misbehavior occurs in a different environment.

The model is not stronger.

The humans are weaker where it matters.

This is the deepest reason to care about a takeover without a taker.

It changes the object of safety.

The question is not only how to keep future systems aligned with human goals.

It is how to keep human institutions capable of being principals.

A principal is more than the person whose name appears on the account.

A principal can set a mandate.

A principal can observe enough to know whether the mandate is being followed.

A principal can revoke authority.

A principal can survive revocation.

That last condition is easy to overlook.

If revoking the delegate destroys the principal's ability to function, delegation has changed character.

The delegate may still be obedient.

The principal is captive to the relationship.

Markets understand this as switching cost.

Security engineers understand versions of it as lock-in and single points of failure.

Governments understand it as strategic dependence.

Families understand it when one person alone knows how to manage a critical part of life.

*Imenent* asks us to recognize the same shape in cognition and action.

Who can the institution stop using?

Who can it replace?

How long can it operate while replacement happens?

What knowledge survives outside the delegate?

What interfaces remain usable without it?

Which commitments continue running after revocation?

Which other systems assume the delegate will still be there?

A takeover without a taker occurs when enough of those answers become uncomfortable at once.

There may never be a throne.

There may only be a system everyone is still officially allowed to control and nobody can afford to turn off.
