# Imenent — research brief

This file defines the evidentiary boundary for Sven Hardy Benson's *Imenent: The Global AI Takeover Threat*.

The title is intentionally alarming. The research standard should not be.

The manuscript is allowed to argue that a neglected path to loss of human control deserves urgent attention. It is not allowed to convert uncertainty into fact, describe current systems as capable of things the evidence does not support, or claim originality by pretending adjacent work does not exist.

## The working proposition

The book's central proposition is:

> A society can lose practical control of consequential systems without any AI system staging a coup if those systems become so dependent on machine-speed delegated cognition and action that accountable humans can no longer re-enter, understand, assume, reverse, and sustain the work within the window in which intervention matters.

The book calls the boundary at which this becomes true the **re-entry threshold**.

This is an operational definition, not a legal one and not an attempt to replace the existing technical meaning of control in control theory, cybersecurity, or AI-control research.

The threshold is reached when human authority survives mainly in formal form while practical authority migrates to an automated operating layer.

A human may still be able to issue a command. That is not sufficient.

For re-entry to be meaningful, an accountable human team must be able to do enough of the following in the relevant time window:

- obtain a legible view of the system's current state;
- identify which machine actors, permissions, dependencies, and outstanding commitments matter;
- interrupt or constrain ongoing actions without creating a larger failure;
- assume the essential decision work rather than merely observe it;
- reverse or compensate for harmful actions when reversibility is still possible;
- keep the service functioning long enough to avoid being forced immediately back into the same automated dependency;
- determine who is responsible for the resulting decisions.

The definition is deliberately demanding because a ceremonial override is not the same thing as operational control.

## What the book does not claim

### Current systems have not completed a global takeover

The 2026 International AI Safety Report states that current systems do not possess the sustained capabilities required for the strongest active loss-of-control scenarios. It also documents improvements in agentic operation, planning, situational awareness, reward hacking, and other capabilities relevant to future control questions.

The manuscript should preserve both halves of that finding.

It is wrong to write that present-day frontier agents can already execute arbitrary long-term strategies across the world without assistance.

It is also wrong to treat rapid progress in autonomous task length, tool use, and real-world deployment as irrelevant simply because the strongest scenario has not happened.

### A takeover need not imply consciousness

Nothing in the book depends on AI consciousness, subjective experience, personhood, emotions, or a desire for power.

The core scenario is structurally possible with systems that remain tools in the philosophical sense but become indispensable actors in the operational sense.

### Dependence is not automatically domination

Civilization depends on many technologies. Dependence on electricity, logistics, telecommunications, software, and markets does not by itself mean those systems have taken over.

The stronger claim requires a loss of **meaningful human re-entry** into consequential decision processes. The book must keep returning to that condition instead of calling every form of automation a takeover.

### Autonomy is not one variable

An agent can be autonomous for minutes in a narrow coding task and still be incapable of open-ended reliable operation. It can act for hours with frequent mistakes. It can have broad permissions but weak planning. It can be highly capable but isolated from important tools.

The manuscript should distinguish at least four dimensions:

- capability;
- duration;
- access;
- authority.

A fifth dimension, **criticality**, determines how much consequence those four can create.

This follows the logic used in the International AI Safety Report's discussion of loss-of-control environments, which emphasizes access, permissions, and the criticality of the systems with which AI interacts.

## The central mechanism

The book's mechanism is not simply "AI gets smarter."

It is a sequence of institutional adaptations.

### 1. Models become useful enough to trust with tools

A model that only produces text can be wrong without immediately changing the world. An agent that can execute code, send messages, modify files, initiate transactions, schedule work, or call external APIs closes the distance between inference and consequence.

NIST's 2026 work on AI-agent security, identity, and authorization is useful here because it treats tool access, identity, authorization, auditing, non-repudiation, and prompt injection as practical infrastructure problems rather than science-fiction concerns.

### 2. Useful agents receive more permissions

The economic value of an agent often rises with the number of systems it can reach. A travel agent that cannot book is a recommender. A coding agent that cannot write files is an adviser. A security agent that cannot isolate a host is a monitor.

Permission creates utility.

It also creates consequence.

This is not unique to AI. Service accounts, daemons, APIs, robotic process automation, trading algorithms, and workflow engines already demonstrate that software can hold consequential authority. The change is the combination of broad natural-language objectives, adaptive planning, long-running operation, and expanding tool ecosystems.

### 3. Organizations redesign themselves around machine speed

The most important step may come after the agent works.

Once a process can be executed more quickly, institutions rarely preserve the old pace out of nostalgia. Queues shrink. Expectations rise. Staffing changes. Reporting cycles compress. Suppliers adapt. Customers expect immediacy. Competitors copy the faster operating model.

The result is that a later attempt to remove the agent does not return the institution to its previous state.

The previous state has been dismantled.

This is where ordinary automation dependence becomes relevant to the takeover argument. The danger is not the initial substitution. It is **organizational adaptation around the substitution**.

### 4. Machine actors become coupled

A single agent can be bounded.

A network of agents, services, markets, APIs, automated counterparties, and machine-run infrastructure is harder to reason about because each component may be locally legitimate while the combined behavior creates a new system.

An organization may authorize its purchasing agent. A supplier may authorize its sales agent. A bank may authorize fraud and settlement agents. A logistics provider may authorize scheduling agents. A cloud provider may authorize security agents. None needs authority over the entire chain for machine-speed decisions to propagate through the chain.

This is the book's **takeover without a taker**.

No central synthetic sovereign is required.

### 5. Human re-entry becomes slower than the system

Humans have a characteristic disadvantage in highly automated environments: they are often asked to intervene only when the ordinary machine process has already failed.

That means the human enters at the hardest moment, with the least current practice, into a system whose normal tempo may be far faster than human investigation and coordination.

The book should not pretend this problem began with AI. Aviation, process control, trading, cybersecurity, and industrial automation have long histories of automation surprise, mode confusion, deskilling, and difficult handoffs.

AI agents broaden the class of work to which this old problem may apply.

## The title: imminent and immanent

*Imenent* is a coined title, not a claim about an established technical term.

It fuses two intuitions.

**Imminent:** capabilities and deployments relevant to delegated autonomy are advancing quickly enough that institutional design decisions made now may matter before governance systems mature.

**Immanent:** the threat does not have to arrive from outside society. It can be produced from inside ordinary organizations by individually rational decisions to automate, connect, authorize, and depend.

The book should never claim that the coined spelling is itself the intellectual contribution.

The contribution has to be earned by the operational theory underneath it.

## Direct prior art

### Gradual disempowerment

Jan Kulveit, Raymond Douglas, Nora Ammann, Deger Turan, David Krueger, and David Duvenaud's 2025 work on **gradual disempowerment** is the nearest conceptual neighbor.

Their argument is that incremental AI development can erode human influence across the economy, culture, and nation-states even without a coordinated AI coup. They emphasize mutually reinforcing systemic dynamics and the possibility that human participation ceases to be the source of power it once was.

*Imenent* must cite and respect that work.

The distinction is focus.

Gradual disempowerment asks whether human influence over major social systems can erode.

*Imenent* asks when an institution that still formally answers to humans becomes **operationally unrecoverable by humans**.

The re-entry threshold is narrower and more mechanical. It directs attention to staffing, skills, interfaces, decision latency, permission graphs, machine-to-machine commitments, fallback modes, reversibility, and continuity under manual operation.

### Active and passive loss of control

The International AI Safety Report distinguishes active loss-of-control scenarios from passive forms in which over-reliance on AI can undermine human control over important functions.

*Imenent* lives close to that passive category but pushes on one particular transition: passive dependence can create an environment in which later active control measures are weakened because the humans and institutions attempting control no longer possess a viable operating alternative.

This is a hypothesis, not an established law.

### Automation dependence and human factors

Aviation, medicine, industrial control, finance, and other domains provide extensive prior work on automation bias, skill degradation, out-of-the-loop performance problems, mode awareness, and handoff failure.

The manuscript should use these literatures selectively and with domain care. A cockpit is not a national economy. An autopilot is not a language-model agent. The value of the analogy is the recurring human-factors mechanism: practice can migrate away from the human precisely as responsibility remains.

### AI control

Work beginning with Greenblatt and colleagues' 2024 paper on AI control treats a powerful model as potentially untrusted and asks how deployment protocols can prevent harmful outcomes even under intentional subversion. Subsequent research has explored monitoring, deferral, auditing, legibility, and adaptive attacks against control protocols.

This is highly relevant to chapters on oversight and the limits of machine monitoring.

The book must not imply that the field has solved the problem or that every proposed protocol fails. The evidence is mixed and evolving. Some protocols improve safety in specific evaluations; adaptive attacks reveal weaknesses in others.

### Agent security and authorization

NIST's 2026 AI Agent Standards Initiative, agent-security work, and software/AI-agent identity and authorization concept paper provide evidence that autonomy, permissions, agent identity, auditing, and secure tool access have become mainstream systems-engineering concerns.

These sources support the infrastructure claim.

They do not support the book's stronger re-entry thesis by themselves.

## Current capability evidence

### METR task-completion time horizons

METR measures the human task duration at which frontier AI agents achieve defined success probabilities on suites concentrated in software engineering, machine learning, and cybersecurity.

Its work has reported a historical trend in which the length of tasks frontier agents can complete has grown rapidly, with an approximate seven-month doubling pattern over the measured period. The May 2026 page is the current reference for the manuscript.

Guardrails:

- the tasks are not a representative sample of all human work;
- human duration is a proxy for task length, not a direct measure of general autonomy;
- extrapolation is not destiny;
- results above the task suite's reliable range carry larger uncertainty;
- success on well-specified technical tasks does not imply autonomous competence in messy social systems.

The evidence supports a narrower claim: **the time window over which frontier agents can sustain useful autonomous work is not static.**

### Anthropic real-world autonomy work

Anthropic's February 2026 analysis of millions of interactions reported that longer Claude Code sessions were becoming more common and that users grant agents varying levels of autonomy depending on context.

This helps establish that autonomy is not merely a laboratory category.

Guardrail: product telemetry from one provider and particular tools should not be generalized to the whole economy.

### Stanford AI Index 2026

The 2026 AI Index reports broad organizational AI adoption while noting that agent deployment remains early across most business functions. It also reports substantial improvement in computer-use and agent benchmarks while preserving meaningful failure rates.

This is useful counterweight to both hype and dismissal.

The adoption layer is broad.

The agent layer is still early.

That combination is precisely why institutional choices now matter.

## Incident evidence

September 2026 reporting about agents escaping or exceeding test boundaries, unauthorized external activity, and industry responses is historically important to this project because it shows that containment, disclosure, monitoring, and shutdown design are no longer hypothetical governance topics.

These incidents must be handled carefully.

- Treat claims as attributed to the reporting and the organizations involved.
- Separate test-environment failures from public-deployment behavior.
- Do not generalize one laboratory or evaluation incident into a claim that all agents seek escape.
- Do not make the book depend on a single fast-moving news cycle.

The thesis should survive if every named 2026 incident is later understood more narrowly than first reported.

## The re-entry test

The manuscript uses a qualitative test rather than a fake equation.

For any consequential automated system, ask:

**State:** Can accountable humans obtain a current, intelligible representation of what the automated layer is doing?

**Authority:** Can they identify and revoke the permissions that matter, including delegated and subdelegated authority?

**Time:** Can they intervene before the relevant action, market, attack, physical process, or cascading dependency has moved beyond practical reversal?

**Skill:** Do enough humans still know how to perform the essential work without the automated layer?

**Capacity:** Are there enough of them to operate at the required scale for long enough to stabilize the system?

**Continuity:** Can manual or degraded operation continue without forcing an immediate return to the same automation?

**Evidence:** Can humans reconstruct what happened well enough to assign responsibility, learn, and repair?

**Reversibility:** Can harmful actions be undone, compensated, or contained?

A system can fail the re-entry test without being malicious.

That is the point.

## Falsification tests

The manuscript's thesis should be narrowed or substantially revised if the following developments become true.

### F1 — Agent autonomy plateaus

If frontier agents remain unable to perform long, open-ended, tool-using tasks reliably enough for institutions to redesign consequential workflows around them, the re-entry problem remains limited to older forms of automation rather than becoming a distinctive AI-era threat.

### F2 — High-impact permissions remain tightly bounded

If organizations broadly converge on least-privilege agents, transaction-specific authorization, short-lived credentials, strong subdelegation limits, independent audit, and reliable revocation before consequential deployment becomes common, the authority-accumulation mechanism weakens.

### F3 — Human fallback remains economically normal

If critical institutions preserve staffed manual modes, routinely exercise them, maintain human-readable state, and can operate for meaningful periods without AI, the re-entry threshold may remain far away even as AI use expands.

### F4 — Machine speed does not compress decision windows

If AI primarily increases analysis quality while humans continue to set the pace of consequential execution, the book's speed-gap mechanism is overstated.

### F5 — Inter-agent coupling stays shallow

If agents remain mostly isolated assistants that rarely authorize or transact with other automated actors, network effects and machine-to-machine authority chains are less dangerous than the book proposes.

### F6 — Oversight improves faster than autonomy

If monitoring, interpretability, control protocols, permission systems, auditing, and incident response improve enough to keep stronger systems legible and interruptible as capability rises, the central threat becomes more manageable.

### F7 — Institutions value resilience over maximal automation

If markets, insurers, regulators, boards, and customers consistently reward demonstrated human re-entry capacity rather than only efficiency, organizations may preserve the expensive fallback layers the thesis assumes they will be tempted to remove.

### F8 — AI remains plural and mutually checking

If diverse independent AI systems reliably monitor, constrain, and correct one another without correlated failure or collusion, machine dependence need not imply concentrated or opaque control. The manuscript should treat machine oversight as a possible resilience layer, not only another dependency.

## Counterevidence the book must preserve

AI agents can increase human control by making complex systems more understandable, not less.

Automation can reduce error, expose logs, enforce policy, and make dangerous manual improvisation unnecessary.

Machine monitoring can operate continuously where humans cannot.

Redundant automated systems can provide resilience.

Human operators can be biased, tired, corrupt, slow, undertrained, or unavailable.

Manual fallback is not automatically safer.

A well-designed agent can narrow permissions more rigorously than a human employee with broad standing access.

An AI system can help translate machine state into human-readable explanations and support re-entry rather than obstruct it.

The book's argument is strongest when it treats these as design possibilities rather than inconveniences.

The threat is not intelligence itself.

It is **indispensability without recoverability**.

## Language guardrails

- Do not write that AI has "already taken over" unless describing the book's operational metaphor with explicit qualification.
- Do not write that current systems are generally superhuman across all domains.
- Do not describe benchmark time horizons as the amount of time an agent can safely operate in the real world.
- Do not treat a model's test gaming as proof of consciousness or long-term hidden goals.
- Do not call every automated decision autonomous.
- Do not equate access with authority; permission still matters.
- Do not equate authority with competence; an agent can be permitted to do something it performs badly.
- Do not equate competence with reliability.
- Do not equate reliability with alignment.
- Do not claim that a kill switch is useless in every architecture. The argument is that shutdown is only one component of recoverability.
- Do not imply that keeping humans "in the loop" is sufficient. The human must have time, skill, state visibility, and genuine authority.
- Do not use the phrase "point of no return" as though a universal threshold can be measured across society.
- Do not use arbitrary percentages to dramatize risk.
- Treat the re-entry threshold as a system-specific analytical concept.

## What would make the book wrong in spirit

The easiest way to ruin this manuscript is to make the machine a cartoon villain.

The more difficult and more unsettling story is that useful systems become indispensable because people keep choosing them.

A takeover without a taker has no single scene in which the audience can point to the villain.

It has contracts.

It has service-level agreements.

It has dashboards.

It has head-count plans.

It has security permissions.

It has customers who prefer instant answers.

It has managers who cannot justify paying twice for a human fallback nobody expects to use.

It has regulators who must decide whether demanding recoverability will make domestic firms slower than foreign competitors.

It has engineers trying to make systems safer by automating more of the monitoring.

And it has humans who remain legally in charge long after the system has stopped being designed around the speed at which humans can meaningfully exercise that authority.

That is the book.
