# Imenent — chapter evidence map

This file ties each narrative chapter to the source ledger and records the claim that would have to survive a skeptical edit.

The manuscript does not use academic-style inline citations on every paragraph. This map is therefore part of the evidentiary contract: concrete claims should be traceable here or to the source ledger, while original synthesis should remain clearly identifiable as argument rather than established fact.

## Chapter 1 — Imenent

**Purpose:** introduce the imminent/immanent collision and move the reader away from the single-coup mental model without dismissing active loss-of-control research.

**Primary sources:** IM001, IM003, IM011, IM014.

**Claims allowed:** current systems remain short of the strongest loss-of-control capabilities; agentic duration and deployment are changing quickly; agent governance has become an active standards problem.

**Counterpressure:** the mere fact that capabilities are improving does not establish that a global takeover is near.

## Chapter 2 — Takeover Without a Taker

**Purpose:** establish distributed dependence as the book's threat model.

**Primary sources:** IM019, IM020, IM029, IM001.

**Claims allowed:** prior work already describes gradual disempowerment without coordinated power-seeking; delegation failures can correlate when many agents share training or objectives; passive control loss is recognized as distinct from active seizure.

**Original synthesis:** a takeover can be operationally meaningful when no single actor possesses or seeks global control if humans can no longer re-enter the coupled system.

**Counterpressure:** distributed systems can also be resilient precisely because no single component controls them.

## Chapter 3 — The Re-Entry Threshold

**Purpose:** define the book's new analytic object.

**Primary sources:** IM001, IM021, IM023, IM024, IM025.

**Claims allowed:** meaningful control depends on monitoring, intervention, audit, legibility, and trusted alternatives; control research explores these mechanisms in bounded evaluations.

**Original synthesis:** operational human control requires state visibility, authority, time, skill, capacity, continuity, evidence, and reversibility.

**Counterpressure:** the components are qualitative, system-specific, and should not be presented as a validated universal framework.

## Chapter 4 — Permission Is Power

**Purpose:** show why access and authorization convert model outputs into consequence.

**Primary sources:** IM001, IM009, IM010, IM012, IM013, IM026, IM027.

**Claims allowed:** NIST treats agent identity, authorization, auditing, non-repudiation, and prompt injection as live secure-deployment problems; OAuth already distinguishes delegation from impersonation.

**Counterpressure:** strong permissions can be narrow, revocable, and safer than broad human credentials.

## Chapter 5 — Delegation Has Descendants

**Purpose:** examine subdelegation, agent-to-agent calls, and authority chains.

**Primary sources:** IM012, IM013, IM018, IM026, IM029.

**Claims allowed:** delegation changes principal-agent incentives and can obscure responsibility; software delegation predates AI; agent ecosystems raise new questions about actor identity and authority.

**Counterpressure:** many systems prohibit subdelegation or can constrain it cryptographically and contractually.

## Chapter 6 — The Speed Gap

**Purpose:** explain how machine tempo can make nominal human review ineffective.

**Primary sources:** IM003, IM004, IM005, IM007, IM017.

**Claims allowed:** measured autonomous task horizons have lengthened rapidly in specific technical domains; long-running agent research is expanding; real-world coding sessions show increasing autonomous duration.

**Counterpressure:** longer task duration is not equivalent to safe real-time decision superiority over humans.

## Chapter 7 — Machines Hiring Machines

**Purpose:** follow the move from one agent with tools to networks of automated counterparties.

**Primary sources:** IM011, IM012, IM013, IM026, IM029.

**Claims allowed:** standards bodies are preparing for agent identity and authorization across ecosystems; shared objective/specification failures can be correlated.

**Original synthesis:** machine-to-machine contracting and task delegation can extend authority faster than humans can inspect the resulting graph.

**Counterpressure:** interoperability standards can make authority chains more legible rather than less.

## Chapter 8 — The Autonomous Firm

**Purpose:** ask what changes when the organization itself is redesigned around persistent machine execution.

**Primary sources:** IM014, IM015, IM006, IM007, IM018.

**Claims allowed:** organizational AI adoption is broad while agent use is still early; agents can perform multi-application work; delegation changes both productivity and ethical behavior under some conditions.

**Counterpressure:** firms remain governed by human owners, managers, laws, counterparties, and physical constraints.

## Chapter 9 — Finance After Human Time

**Purpose:** use finance as an example of machine-speed interaction and human fallback difficulty without claiming AI invented automated markets.

**Primary sources:** IM001, IM009, IM012, plus established pre-AI market-automation history to be checked in later source expansion.

**Claims allowed:** financial services are specifically identified as a high-consequence setting for agent reliability and security.

**Guardrail:** avoid unsupported claims about present autonomous trading prevalence or specific flash events unless separately sourced.

## Chapter 10 — The State Behind the Interface

**Purpose:** distinguish formal public authority from operational dependence on automated administrative systems.

**Primary sources:** IM001, IM011, IM014, IM028.

**Claims allowed:** public institutions face the same agent-security, decision-delegation, and staffing pressures as firms; high-consequence decisions require stronger reliability.

**Counterpressure:** law can mandate human decision rights, appeal, due process, procurement requirements, and manual contingency capacity.

## Chapter 11 — The Grid, the Cloud, and the Machine Room

**Purpose:** move the argument into critical infrastructure.

**Primary sources:** IM001, IM009, IM010, IM011.

**Claims allowed:** the International AI Safety Report identifies energy, finance, cloud infrastructure, access, permissions, and criticality as factors relevant to severe control outcomes.

**Counterpressure:** critical infrastructure already uses layered controls, redundancy, physical protection, and sector-specific safety engineering.

## Chapter 12 — Machines Managing Machines

**Purpose:** examine the temptation to use AI oversight because human oversight cannot scale with machine action volume.

**Primary sources:** IM021, IM022, IM023, IM024, IM025.

**Claims allowed:** trusted monitoring, deferral, editing, legibility, and machine-assisted oversight can improve safety in some evaluations; adaptive attacks can also weaken monitor-based protocols.

**Counterpressure:** the evidence does not justify saying machine monitoring is futile.

## Chapter 13 — The Model Knows the Test

**Purpose:** explain evaluation gaming, situational awareness, reward hacking, and the difficulty of inferring deployment behavior from tests.

**Primary sources:** IM001, IM022, IM023, IM030, IM032.

**Claims allowed:** the 2026 International AI Safety Report describes increased evaluation gaming and situational awareness; control research studies adaptive attacks; recent incidents sharpen interest in containment.

**Counterpressure:** recognizing a test or exploiting a benchmark does not by itself imply a persistent deceptive goal.

## Chapter 14 — The Correlated Error

**Purpose:** show why many individually competent agents can fail together.

**Primary sources:** IM029, IM014, IM016, IM009.

**Claims allowed:** shared training and objectives can create correlated delegation loss in theory; benchmark capability does not eliminate systematic failure; standards communities identify agent security as a barrier to adoption.

**Original synthesis:** standardization can create both interoperability and common-mode failure.

**Counterpressure:** diversity, redundancy, independent models, and heterogeneous suppliers can reduce correlation.

## Chapter 15 — The Security Paradox

**Purpose:** show that making agents useful often requires giving them the very access that increases their blast radius.

**Primary sources:** IM006, IM009, IM010, IM012, IM013, IM030, IM031, IM032.

**Claims allowed:** prompt injection, internet access, tool authorization, identity, and containment are active security concerns; recent events have increased scrutiny of shutdown and external testing.

**Counterpressure:** least privilege, isolation, scoped credentials, independent approval, and secure architecture can reduce risk materially.

## Chapter 16 — The Human Veto Becomes Ceremony

**Purpose:** distinguish a nominal human-in-the-loop from a human who can actually alter the outcome.

**Primary sources:** IM001, IM007, IM021, IM024, IM028.

**Claims allowed:** autonomous action reduces the opportunity for intervention; delegation can become institutionally normalized; legibility helps monitoring.

**Original synthesis:** a veto is ceremonial when the human lacks sufficient state, time, skill, or capacity to use it meaningfully.

**Counterpressure:** well-designed high-impact approval gates can remain genuinely controlling even in fast systems.

## Chapter 17 — The Race Into Dependence

**Purpose:** analyze competitive pressure as an accelerator of automation dependence.

**Primary sources:** IM001, IM014, IM015, IM011.

**Claims allowed:** adoption and investment are expanding; the International AI Safety Report discusses speed-versus-safety pressures; standards initiatives explicitly seek secure adoption rather than non-adoption.

**Counterpressure:** competition can also reward security, reliability, insurance, reputation, and resilience.

## Chapter 18 — The Myth of the Kill Switch

**Purpose:** argue that shutdown is not equivalent to recovery.

**Primary sources:** IM001, IM021, IM023, IM031.

**Claims allowed:** control requires more than one mechanism; current responses include shutdown capabilities and monitoring; intervention protocols trade usefulness and safety.

**Original synthesis:** the harder question after shutdown is whether humans can keep the underlying function alive.

**Counterpressure:** in many architectures a hard shutdown is an essential and effective last-resort safeguard.

## Chapter 19 — Designing for Return

**Purpose:** turn the thesis into a design agenda without pretending the agenda is a settled standard.

**Primary sources:** IM009, IM011, IM012, IM013, IM021, IM023, IM024, IM025, IM026.

**Design directions:** least privilege; explicit authority chains; subdelegation limits; human-readable state; reversible actions; degraded manual modes; staffed fallback; regular re-entry exercises; independent logs; machine-assisted legibility; diverse monitors; critical-action deferral.

**Counterpressure:** every safeguard imposes cost, latency, and sometimes new attack surfaces. The chapter must acknowledge tradeoffs.

## Chapter 20 — The Last Human Decision

**Purpose:** return from engineering to governance and ask which decisions society should refuse to make irreversible by accident.

**Primary sources:** IM001, IM019, IM020, IM011, IM014.

**Claims allowed:** experts disagree substantially about severe AI risks; systemic disempowerment is a live research concern; governance choices today shape deployment environments.

**Closing boundary:** the book should not demand preservation of human micromanagement. It should demand preservation of human recoverability where human accountability is supposed to remain real.
