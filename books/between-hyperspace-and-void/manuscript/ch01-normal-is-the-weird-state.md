# Chapter 1 — Normal Is the Weird State

A molecule can be ordinary at one concentration and cosmological at another.

That sentence is less mystical than it sounds. Carbon dioxide is ordinary until it is not. Glutamate is indispensable until too much of it becomes destructive. Cortisol gets blamed for everything because wellness culture requires villains, but without it mornings would be considerably harder. Biology does not sort chemicals into sacred and profane. It uses gradients, timing, location, receptor populations, enzymes, transporters, feedback loops. Dose is not a footnote to function. Dose is often the function.

Which makes N,N-dimethyltryptamine an unusually irritating molecule.

Give a human enough exogenous DMT, rapidly enough, and the visual world can be replaced rather than merely decorated. Geometry arrives with authority. Space acquires machinery. Agency can appear where the room used to be. People report environments, presences, impossible architecture, narrative density, astonishment. Calling this “hyperspace” is not neuroscience, but it is a useful piece of phenomenological shorthand because the word preserves something clinical language tends to bleach out: the experience often feels crowded with structure.

5-methoxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine can be just as radical while doing something that, in many reports, sounds almost opposite. Less carnival. Less architecture. Less furniture in reality. At sufficiently strong doses, reports often converge on ego dissolution, unity, absence, whiteness, stillness, annihilation, boundlessness—the vocabulary varies because language is being asked to describe a condition in which the describer may have temporarily disappeared.

The temptation is immediate.

One compound gives too much world. The other removes the borders that make a world divisible. Both are close chemical relatives. Both act in serotonergic systems. Both occur in nature. DMT is endogenous in mammals. 5-MeO-DMT has long been discussed as potentially endogenous in humans, although that proposition is much less secure than popular accounts imply.

So here is the dangerous question.

What if the spectacular states are pharmacological caricatures of quieter jobs?

Not secret psychedelic reservoirs waiting to flood the brain at death. Not a pineal portal. Not proof that consciousness is transmitted from somewhere else. Something simultaneously less romantic and more interesting: endogenous chemistry participating, at concentrations far below a psychedelic dose, in the continuous regulation of how much the brain connects and how much it separates.

Association and dissociation.

Binding and cutting.

A mind has to do both.

Every second of ordinary consciousness is an editing problem. The nervous system must decide which sensations belong to the same object, which memories belong to the present problem, which internal signals deserve awareness, which predictions should be updated, which background processes should remain background. Too little connection and experience fragments. Too much indiscriminate connection and noise becomes significance. A stable mind is not maximally integrated. Nor is it maximally segregated. It is dynamically selective.

The hypothesis of this book is that endogenous tryptamines deserve investigation inside that control problem.

That is a hypothesis, not a finding.

The distinction matters especially here because psychedelic literature has a bad habit of allowing chemical existence to inherit the meaning of a drug experience. Finding DMT in an organism does not mean the organism is microdosing itself. Finding a biosynthetic enzyme in brain tissue does not establish the molecule’s behavioral job. Showing that a milligram-scale exogenous dose changes consciousness does not tell us what picomolar or nanomolar endogenous concentrations do locally, transiently, or intracellularly. And finding two molecules whose large-dose experiences look poetically complementary does not establish a biochemical opposition between them.

Those are four different bridges. Each needs its own load test.

The first bridge is stronger than it used to be. In 2019, Jon Dean and colleagues reported INMT transcripts in rat and human brain tissue and measured extracellular DMT in the visual cortex of freely moving rats. Pineal removal did not eliminate cortical DMT. In those rat microdialysates, measured baseline DMT concentrations were in the same broad nanomolar neighborhood as canonical monoamines measured with the same approach. The study did not prove a human neurotransmitter function. It did something more modest and more useful: it made the sentence “the mammalian brain may make and release DMT” experimentally harder to dismiss.

The second bridge is shakier. The literature around endogenous 5-MeO-DMT contains old detections, nondetections, precursor chemistry, methodological disputes, and a persistent tendency in secondary retellings to upgrade “reported in a body fluid” into “produced by the human brain.” Modern reviews are more cautious. Human endogenous production remains plausible enough to investigate and insufficiently established to use as a premise.

That asymmetry is not an inconvenience to the book. It is part of the book.

If the proposed balancing system requires two endogenous neuromodulators and one of them turns out not to be meaningfully present in the human brain, the elegant theory loses. Elegance is cheap. Measurements get veto power.

The third bridge concerns pharmacology. DMT is usually discussed through 5-HT2A, though its binding is broader. 5-MeO-DMT is especially interesting because its receptor profile is not simply “DMT but stronger.” Modern pharmacology gives 5-MeO-DMT particularly high affinity at 5-HT1A as well as activity at 5-HT2A and other serotonergic targets. That difference gives the balancing hypothesis something more substantial than opposite trip reports. Different receptor weighting could, in principle, push circuits differently.

Could.

That word is doing work.

The fourth bridge is the strangest: network dynamics. Human psychedelic imaging increasingly describes altered consciousness not only in terms of named brain regions but in changes to hierarchy, integration, segregation, signal diversity, and communication among networks. Exogenous DMT has been associated with increased global functional connectivity and altered network organization. The subjective state is not well summarized as simply “more connected,” because local and global measures can move in different directions, ordinary hierarchies can flatten, and connectivity without constraint is not the same thing as useful association.

Likewise, calling 5-MeO-DMT a chemical of “disconnection” would be premature. Ego dissolution may involve the collapse of some boundaries while increasing another kind of global unity. A person reporting no distinction between self and universe is not obviously less integrated. They may be less differentiated. Those are not synonyms.

This is where the original seesaw becomes interesting enough to break.

Perhaps the useful axis is not connection versus disconnection.

Perhaps it is differentiation versus unification.

Perhaps it is model proliferation versus model collapse.

Perhaps it is precision allocated outward versus precision released from the self-model.

Perhaps DMT and 5-MeO-DMT are not opponents at all. Perhaps their spectacular contrast at high doses is produced by receptor occupancy patterns that never occur endogenously. Perhaps endogenous DMT does something boring in peripheral tissue and endogenous 5-MeO-DMT, if it exists in humans at all, is metabolically incidental.

Good. Those possibilities belong in the room.

A theory about consciousness should not become more persuasive because it is beautiful. Beauty is a reason to design the experiment.

The experiment we do not yet have is conceptually simple and technically vicious. Measure both compounds, with enough temporal and anatomical resolution to matter, in living human physiology. Measure their precursors and metabolites. Measure serotonin. Measure receptor-relevant concentrations rather than merely proving chemical presence. Track sleep, waking, stress, sensory deprivation, meditation, anesthesia, dreaming, seizures, near-death physiology where ethically possible, and ordinary cognitive transitions that require rapid binding or separation of information. Simultaneously measure network dynamics. Then ask whether endogenous fluctuations predict anything after the usual neuromodulatory machinery is accounted for.

If they do not, the chemical seesaw goes away.

If they do, normal consciousness becomes stranger.

Not because everyone is secretly tripping.

Because the compounds capable of dismantling ordinary reality at pharmacological doses may turn out to have quieter physiological relatives of those effects at endogenous ones. The spectacular state would then be less like an alien country than an amplifier test: turn a control far beyond its operating range and discover, grotesquely enlarged, what the control normally helps regulate.

This possibility is not established. It is barely properly formulated.

That is why it deserves a book.
