# Two Compasses in One Animal

Navigation problems are usually narrated as if the animal has a destination and needs a direction.

That skips the harder question. A direction is only useful if you know where you are relative to where you want to go.

A compass says which way is north, or poleward, or along some stable axis. A map says something about position. Human navigation separates them cleanly because our tools do: a magnetic compass provides heading; a chart provides spatial relationships; GPS provides coordinates. In an animal, the systems can be entangled behaviorally, but they need not share a receptor.

The distinction has haunted magnetoreception for decades.

Migratory birds can maintain seasonally appropriate headings under controlled magnetic conditions. That is compass behavior. Other experiments physically or virtually displace animals and ask whether they compensate toward a breeding or wintering region. That requires positional information in addition to a compass. Sea turtles respond to magnetic fields characteristic of different geographic regions. Salmon return to natal areas correlated with geomagnetic signatures. Lobsters exposed to fields simulating locations north or south of capture can orient in ways consistent with a magnetic map. The Earth’s field contains gradients that make such behavior physically possible.

But a magnetic map does not need to work like a magnetic compass.

The 2025 loggerhead study made this experimentally difficult to ignore.

Kayla Goforth and colleagues raised juvenile loggerheads and trained them to associate feeding with magnetic fields reproducing the signatures of particular locations. The turtles developed a distinctive anticipatory response in the rewarded field—the press-friendly version became the “turtle dance,” though the underlying result is conditioned discrimination. They could learn that one invisible magnetic condition predicted food while another did not.

The researchers then applied oscillating radiofrequency fields.

The turtles’ magnetic compass orientation was disrupted. Their learned map response was not.

One animal carried two magnetic behaviors separated by a physical perturbation.

This is stronger than merely observing that map and compass behavior differ. It is a dissociation. If the RF field reaches the animal and measurably changes one magnetic function while leaving another magnetic function intact, a single upstream mechanism becomes harder to defend.

The authors interpreted the result as evidence that the magnetic map and magnetic compass rely on distinct mechanisms. The compass behavior fit a radical-pair-sensitive route; the map behavior appeared to rely on something else.

What is the something else?

The paper did not identify it. Particle-based magnetoreception is an obvious candidate because magnetite-based mechanisms need not show the same RF vulnerability as radical-pair spin chemistry. But “not disrupted by this RF treatment” does not equal “therefore magnetite.” Other mechanisms are possible, and even a particle-based hypothesis needs a receptor organ and neural pathway.

This is where good uncertainty becomes generative.

The result narrows the mechanism without pretending to finish it. It suggests a two-system architecture and supplies a tool for probing each system independently.

A 2026 paper in the *Journal of Experimental Biology* added an anatomical clue. Researchers attached weak magnets to different regions of juvenile loggerheads trained in a magnetic map assay. A magnet on the head reduced responses to the food-associated magnetic field, whereas comparable placement elsewhere on the body did not produce the same disruption. The authors described this as evidence for cephalic magnetic map receptors. Again, the experiment localizes without identifying the receptor cell.

Put the 2025 and 2026 results together carefully.

The map system is learnable.

The tested RF field did not disrupt the learned map response.

A local magnetic disturbance near the head interfered with that response more than disturbances elsewhere.

Those facts are compatible with a head-located particle-based map receptor. They do not prove one.

This is the sort of layered inference magnetoreception demands.

Bird work suggests a similar division. The European robin’s magnetic compass survives trigeminal nerve section under conditions in which cluster N lesions abolish it. Eurasian reed warblers need trigeminal input to compensate after virtual magnetic displacement. A visual-linked compass and trigeminal-linked map can coexist without contradiction.

The phrase “two compasses” in this chapter title is therefore deliberately wrong in the ordinary sense. An animal may carry a compass and a map, plus calibration systems that make each usable. What feels like one navigational faculty from the outside can be a committee of sensors.

That committee has to solve several distinct estimation problems.

Heading: what direction am I moving relative to a stable reference?

Position: what region of the magnetic landscape am I in?

Calibration: how should magnetic direction be related to celestial or landmark cues today?

Context: is the current magnetic pattern trustworthy, or is it being distorted by local geology, storms, infrastructure or experimental coils?

Memory: have I experienced this field signature before, and what happened there?

Goal selection: which destination matters at this stage of migration, life history or foraging?

Motor control: how do I convert a desired course into actual movement under wind or current?

No single receptor solves all of that.

This matters because mechanism disputes often occur at the wrong level. One group shows retinal dependence and argues for cryptochrome. Another shows trigeminal dependence and argues for magnetite. If the experiments ask different navigational questions, both can be right.

The 2025 turtle study does not settle the avian debate, but it establishes the biological plausibility of multiple magnetic mechanisms within one vertebrate navigation system.

There is an analogy with the human vestibular system. We casually call it “balance,” but it contains semicircular canals sensitive to angular acceleration and otolith organs sensitive to linear acceleration and gravity. Vision and proprioception contribute too. A person can lose one component and show selective deficits. The subjective unity of balance hides multiple transducers.

Animal navigation may be even more distributed.

The magnetic field itself encourages such division because different physical features carry different information. Direction can be extracted from field orientation. Position can be estimated from intensity, inclination and regional gradients. Temporal change can help distinguish movement through a spatial gradient from field variation caused by geomagnetic activity. A chemical radical-pair sensor may be excellent for direction but poor for absolute intensity. A magnetic-particle receptor may be better suited to intensity or gradients. Evolution could therefore assign different magnetic variables to different physical mechanisms.

This leads to a more precise interpretation of the title of this book.

Radio did not break “the magnetic sense.”

In the strongest experiments, RF fields disrupted a magnetic compass behavior. A separate magnetic map behavior can survive. The title earns its drama only if the body text keeps that distinction intact.

This also changes the environmental question. Suppose anthropogenic RF noise degrades a radical-pair compass in a city. An animal with a separate map system still knows something about location. It may compensate using stars, landmarks or a particle-based magnetic cue. The ecological consequence depends on redundancy and context. Conversely, a local ferromagnetic structure could distort a particle-based map cue while leaving the radical-pair compass operational. Different infrastructure could perturb different layers of navigation.

A city is therefore not just noisy. It is magnetically complicated.

Steel, electrical currents, radio transmitters, underground cables, vehicles and buildings create static anomalies, low-frequency fields and radiofrequency noise. An animal moving through that environment may encounter a set of perturbations unlike any single laboratory treatment. To understand behavior, researchers need to know which sensor each perturbation reaches.

This is where the two-system model becomes experimentally productive.

Expose turtles or birds to a static magnetic anomaly without RF noise and measure map versus compass behavior.

Expose them to broadband RF with the static field carefully preserved.

Alter inclination while holding total intensity constant.

Alter intensity while holding direction constant.

Temporarily interfere with trigeminal input and ask which responses disappear.

Manipulate candidate cryptochrome photochemistry and ask whether only compass behavior changes.

The factorial design grows large quickly, but the predictions become sharper.

A unified “magnetic sense” theory has to explain all outcomes with one transducer. A multiple-system theory is more flexible, which creates a danger: it can explain anything after the fact by inventing another sensor. The cure is preregistration and cross-task perturbation. Specify which mechanism should affect which behavior before testing it.

For example, a strong version of the dual-system hypothesis might predict that radical-pair-disrupting RF fields impair inclination-compass orientation but preserve learned magnetic-map discrimination; localized static magnetic perturbations near a particle receptor impair map discrimination but preserve compass orientation; trigeminal disruption selectively impairs map tasks; retinal cryptochrome disruption selectively impairs compass tasks. Each dissociation should replicate across appropriate species, and crossovers should not be explained away ad hoc.

That is falsifiable enough to be useful.

The deeper conceptual lesson is that evolution does not owe us sensory categories.

Humans divide the world into light, sound, chemicals, temperature, touch and a few internal senses because those are the channels our nervous system makes obvious to us. A turtle navigating across an ocean experiences a different partition. Geomagnetic inclination can be directional information in one circuit and place information in another. The same environmental field can enter the animal through separate physical mechanisms and converge only later in the brain.

Once that possibility is admitted, several old contradictions begin to look like category mistakes.

The field spent years asking which mechanism wins.

The animal may have been using both.
