# A Protein From a Robin

By the time cryptochrome 4 became the star candidate for the avian magnetic compass, the field had already learned one reason to be suspicious of star candidates.

A receptor hypothesis can become persuasive through accumulation. The molecule is in the right tissue. Its expression pattern fits the behavior. Its photochemistry fits a physical mechanism. Its sequence contains the residues a model requires. It interacts with plausible signaling partners. Every new result narrows the gap until the remaining unknowns begin to feel administrative.

Then someone asks whether the molecule itself responds to the field.

The 2021 *Nature* paper “[Magnetic sensitivity of cryptochrome 4 from a migratory songbird](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-021-03618-9)” mattered because it addressed that question directly. Jingjing Xu and a large interdisciplinary team purified CRY4 proteins from the European robin, chicken and pigeon and measured their light-induced radical chemistry using several spectroscopic methods. Under their in vitro conditions, robin CRY4 showed magnetic field effects and did so more strongly than the homologous proteins from the two non-migratory species. Site-directed mutations along a chain of tryptophans changed the electron-transfer chemistry in ways consistent with the proposed radical-pair mechanism.

A protein from an animal famous for magnetic orientation was magnetically sensitive in a test tube.

That sentence sounds like the end of a search. It is closer to the middle.

The road to CRY4 had been built from several kinds of circumstantial evidence. Avian retinas express multiple cryptochromes, complicating the assignment. Some have obvious circadian roles. A useful magnetoreceptor candidate should plausibly be present when the animal needs a magnetic compass, not only at a narrow phase of the circadian cycle. In 2018, Atticus Pinzon-Rodriguez, Staffan Bensch and Rachel Muheim reported that retinal *Cry4* expression in zebra finches remained comparatively constant across the circadian day while *Cry1* and *Cry2* showed daily variation. Their paper, “[Expression patterns of cryptochrome genes in avian retina suggest involvement of Cry4 in light-dependent magnetoreception](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsif.2018.0058),” made CRY4 attractive by exclusion as much as by positive evidence.

The same year, Anja Günther and colleagues studied European robin CRY4 more directly. In “[Double-Cone Localization and Seasonal Expression Pattern Suggest a Role in Magnetoreception for European Robin Cryptochrome 4](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2017.12.003),” they reported CRY4 localization associated with double cones and long-wavelength single cones and higher retinal expression during migratory seasons in robins, an effect not mirrored in domestic chickens in the same way. The title used the right verb: *suggest*.

Localization matters because a directional chemical compass cannot be a bag of freely tumbling molecules. To encode field direction, magnetically sensitive reaction centers need some reproducible orientation relative to the animal. Photoreceptor structures offer ordered membranes and proteins, though exactly how CRY4 might be immobilized and aligned at the relevant scale remains unresolved. The double-cone result therefore supplied anatomy to a theory that badly needed it.

In 2020, another layer appeared. Researchers described at least two European robin CRY4 splice variants, CRY4a and CRY4b. CRY4a showed the more stable expression pattern and remained the stronger magnetoreceptor candidate, while CRY4b displayed diurnal variation. The result was a reminder that even the name “CRY4” can hide molecular diversity. A gene does not produce a single timeless object. Alternative splicing, post-translational modification, binding partners, redox state and subcellular environment can all change what a protein does.

Another 2020 study examined potential interaction partners. Haijia Wu and colleagues reported that avian CRY4 could interact with cone-specific proteins, including long-wavelength opsin/iodopsin-associated machinery, suggesting routes by which a magnetic chemical event might couple into retinal signaling. A parallel *Science Advances* study on pigeon CRY4 reported photoreduction and long-lived spin-correlated radical pairs and found CRY4 broadly expressed in the retina, enriched at synaptic regions, with candidate interacting proteins involved in signaling. None of this proved magnetic transduction in vivo. It made the missing bridge less abstract.

Then came the 2021 spectroscopy.

The study attacked the protein from several angles because any single technique could mislead. The team measured photoreduction kinetics, transient absorption, cavity-enhanced spectroscopy and electron paramagnetic resonance. They used molecular dynamics and quantum chemistry to model structure and spin interactions. The key electron-transfer route involved a tetrad of tryptophan residues connecting the flavin cofactor to the protein surface. Mutating these residues changed radical formation and magnetic effects.

The phrase “tryptophan tetrad” is worth lingering over because it shows how far the mechanism had moved from metaphor.

A popular story says light hits cryptochrome, an electron jumps, quantum weirdness senses north. The actual protein is a three-dimensional environment that constrains a sequence of electron-transfer steps. Each radical pair has a lifetime, separation, exchange interaction, hyperfine landscape and possible recombination path. Which pair dominates the biologically relevant magnetic response is not automatically obvious. A chain can generate successive radical states, some better suited to magnetic sensitivity and some better suited to signaling stability.

The Xu paper argued that the architecture could separate sensing and signaling functions: early radical pairs could be magnetically responsive, later states could persist long enough to influence protein conformation or binding. That is a compelling solution to an engineering problem. It is still a model of what the protein *could* do in the retina.

The authors and their institutions said this plainly. Oxford’s summary of the paper quoted Henrik Mouritsen emphasizing that it still needed to be shown that the process occurs in birds’ eyes. The distinction is easy to lose because molecular evidence feels more concrete than behavior. Purified protein comes with spectra, rate constants and mutations. A bird scratching in a funnel looks messier. Yet the behavioral fact is closer to the biological function, while the protein fact is closer to the physical mechanism. A complete explanation must make them meet.

Several unresolved questions stand between them.

First, what is the physiological redox cycle? A cryptochrome in a purified cuvette is prepared under chosen illumination and chemical conditions. A photoreceptor outer segment is a crowded, dynamic membrane environment with oxygen, reductants, signaling proteins and continuous light adaptation. The radical states populated in vitro may not have the same yields or lifetimes in vivo.

Second, how are CRY4 molecules oriented? Directional magnetic sensitivity depends on anisotropy. Random orientation averages the directional pattern away. The retinal localization suggests plausible structural order, but direct measurement of CRY4 orientation at the relevant molecular scale is still needed.

Third, how is the signal amplified? Earth-strength field effects on chemical yields can be small. Sensory systems routinely amplify small molecular events—one photon can matter in vision under extreme conditions—but the amplification architecture must exist. Candidate protein interactions are clues, not a completed circuit.

Fourth, how does the neural system separate magnetically modulated chemistry from ordinary photoreception? If CRY4 sits in cone-associated structures, magnetic information could be entangled with color, brightness and adaptation. Behavioral wavelength effects show that illumination changes compass performance, but the detailed relationship is complex and has produced results that do not fit one simple spectral curve.

Fifth, is CRY4 necessary?

This is the experiment that hangs over every candidate molecule. In a genetically tractable organism one might knock out the gene, preserve retinal health, test magnetic behavior under double-blind conditions, rescue the phenotype with wild-type protein, and then rescue or fail to rescue it with mutations predicted to abolish magnetic sensitivity while preserving other functions. Migratory songbirds are not fruit flies. The behavioral assay is seasonal and demanding. Gene manipulation in the relevant retinal cells is nontrivial. A clean causal knockout has therefore lagged the chemistry.

The absence of that decisive experiment does not reduce the existing evidence to speculation. It changes its class.

The magnetic sensitivity of purified robin CRY4 is documented. The retinal expression and localization are documented. The tryptophan-chain dependence of the in vitro photochemistry is documented. The seasonal expression pattern is documented. The behavioral radiofrequency effects are documented in multiple experiments.

The proposition “CRY4 is the primary avian magnetic compass receptor” remains a highly credible but incomplete synthesis.

The difference is important because candidate molecules have a history of being promoted too quickly. In magnetoreception, the upper-beak iron story became anatomically specific before the tissue identity was secure. CRY4 is supported by far richer mechanistic evidence, but the lesson still applies: a receptor is not a molecule with the right résumé. It is a causal component of a sensory pathway.

There is also counterevidence worth keeping visible. In 2020, a *Scientific Reports* study reported that oscillating magnetic fields applied locally to the eyes of garden warblers did not disrupt their magnetic compass, contrary to one straightforward version of the retinal radical-pair prediction. The authors argued that their result challenged the most ambitious form of the model while not necessarily excluding radical-pair chemistry altogether. Whether local field geometry, exposure, receptor location or other factors reconcile the finding remains debated. A mature case for CRY4 should include such results instead of treating every failed perturbation as irrelevant.

A 2026 review in *Physiological Reviews* likewise emphasizes unresolved inconsistencies: wavelength-dependent behaviors that do not map neatly onto fully oxidized FAD absorption, magnetosensory results in other organisms that complicate canonical cryptochrome requirements, and the remaining uncertainty over whether cryptochrome is the primary receptor or one component of a larger transduction complex.

This is exactly the sort of evidence landscape in which a book can become dishonest through tone alone.

Write “scientists discovered the molecule birds use to see Earth’s magnetic field,” and the case sounds solved.

Write “scientists have no idea how birds navigate,” and decades of constrained mechanism disappear.

The accurate sentence is awkward: CRY4 is the strongest current molecular candidate for a light-dependent avian magnetic compass, supported by retinal localization, expression patterns, photochemistry, magnetic sensitivity in vitro and behavior consistent with radical-pair physics, but direct in vivo causal proof connecting CRY4 magnetic chemistry to neural compass signals remains incomplete.

Awkwardness is not a defect when the world is at that stage.

The protein from the robin is valuable precisely because it turned a broad mystery into a finite list of experiments. Where is it? Known approximately. Does it bind flavin? Evidence says yes. Can it generate radical pairs? Yes. Are those radicals magnetically sensitive? In vitro, yes. Do specific residues matter? Yes. Does the molecule sit in plausible retinal structures? Yes. Does disrupting the molecule in a living migratory bird selectively abolish compass behavior? Not yet demonstrated to the standard the field wants.

That last blank is not an invitation to mysticism.

It is a shopping list for biology.
