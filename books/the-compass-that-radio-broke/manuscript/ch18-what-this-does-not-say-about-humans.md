# What This Does Not Say About Humans

Humans are difficult witnesses to their own missing senses.

We know what color looks like because vision reaches consciousness. We know what balance feels like mostly when the vestibular system fails. We regulate blood pressure, oxygen, carbon dioxide, osmolarity and immune state through sensory pathways that rarely become explicit experience. A neural signal can alter behavior or physiology without producing a reportable sensation.

This makes human magnetoreception an unusually treacherous subject.

Ask people to point north under controlled conditions and the historical record is inconsistent. Put a magnetic field around the head and ask what they feel, and conscious reports do not reveal an obvious magnetic sense. Yet absence of conscious perception does not prove absence of transduction.

That distinction is the only reason the modern human case remains interesting.

In 2019, Connie Wang, Isaac Hilburn, Daw-An Wu, Yuki Mizuhara, Sam Bernstein, Joseph Kirschvink and colleagues published [“Transduction of the Geomagnetic Field as Evidenced from alpha-Band Activity in the Human Brain”](https://doi.org/10.1523/ENEURO.0483-18.2019) in *eNeuro*.

The experiment was not a north-pointing game.

Thirty-six adult participants sat in a shielded chamber inside a three-axis coil system while Earth-strength magnetic fields were rotated around them. The researchers recorded 64-channel EEG and looked for changes in alpha-band power, roughly 8–13 Hz. Under particular magnetic rotations they reported alpha event-related desynchronization: alpha power fell after the field changed.

Alpha-ERD is not a magnetic-specific signature. It appears in many sensory and cognitive tasks. Its relevance here came from stimulus selectivity.

The strongest response occurred for particular counterclockwise horizontal rotations when the static vertical component of the magnetic field pointed downward, matching Northern Hemisphere geomagnetic geometry. Comparable rotations with the vertical component reversed upward did not produce the same response. In another set of manipulations, inclination changes also produced alpha suppression.

The asymmetry is the point.

If the EEG effect were merely current induced in electrodes by a changing magnetic field, reversing a static component while keeping the dynamic sweep the same should not selectively erase the response. The authors also ran saline and cantaloupe EEG phantoms and reported the short induction artifact during the magnetic transition without the delayed alpha-ERD pattern seen in responsive people.

The study therefore did more than place humans in a coil and notice that EEG changed.

It tried to make several artifact explanations lose.

The authors also used polarity as a mechanistic discriminator. A classic radical-pair compass is expected to respond to magnetic-field axis rather than north-versus-south polarity. The reported human EEG response changed when polarity-relevant field geometry changed. The authors therefore argued that their result fit a ferromagnetic transduction mechanism better than the avian-style radical-pair mechanism discussed elsewhere in this book.

That is a mechanistic inference.

It is not an identified receptor.

No magnetic organ was found. No nerve carrying the signal was mapped. No receptor cell was silenced. No magnetite structure was shown to drive the EEG response.

The result stops earlier.

Some human brains in this experiment appeared to respond selectively to controlled Earth-strength magnetic-field rotations.

Even that sentence needs its denominator.

Participants varied substantially. The group effect did not mean every person produced a large response. The authors identified four strongly responding participants and retested them weeks or months later. Those four reproduced similar response tuning, timing and scalp topography within the same research program.

That is useful internal repeatability.

It is not independent replication.

A separate sham declination experiment included eighteen participants and did not show a significant magnetosensory response under the sham conditions. The paper also released raw EEG data and analysis code, which is exactly what a field with a long history of hard-to-reproduce behavioral claims should do.

The study earned serious attention.

It did not earn closure.

That status has not quietly changed in the years since publication.

A broad 2026 *Physiological Reviews* synthesis calls the Wang/Kirschvink work the most rigorous human geomagnetic EEG evidence to date and describes the alpha-ERD findings as the first substantial neurophysiological evidence for a possible subconscious human magnetic response. The same review immediately keeps the brake on: **independent replication is essential before firm conclusions can be established**.

That sentence belongs near every popular claim that the human magnetic sense has now been “proved.”

Repeatability has levels.

A participant responding twice is stronger than a participant responding once.

Several participants responding under the same apparatus are stronger than one responder.

A blinded sham in the same laboratory is stronger than no sham.

Open data are stronger than inaccessible data.

But a second laboratory rebuilding the field-control system, reproducing the stimulus geometry, preregistering the analysis and obtaining the same directional alpha-ERD is a different evidentiary event.

The human case still needs that event.

This matters because the signal is strange in a specific way. The reported horizontal response was strongly directional: counterclockwise rotations under one geomagnetic geometry produced the clearest effect while clockwise rotations did not. That asymmetry might reflect receptor orientation, neural adaptation, hemispheric population history, or some feature of the experimental design we do not yet understand.

A good replication should not merely ask whether “alpha goes down under magnets.”

It should freeze the original response surface.

Downward vertical component versus upward.

Counterclockwise versus clockwise.

Inclination sweeps.

Active versus antiparallel-current sham.

Rotation speed.

Field intensity.

Participant heading.

Pre-stimulus alpha power.

If the same asymmetries appear independently, the peculiar shape becomes an asset. A complicated result is harder to reproduce accidentally than a generic change in EEG power.

If the effect moves when the apparatus moves, the apparatus becomes the suspect.

This is why the correct evidence class remains credible but incomplete.

The paper is especially useful in this book because it prevents an easy evolutionary story. One might assume that if vertebrate ancestors possessed magnetoreception, humans simply lost it. A replicated neural response could mean some transduction survives without conscious use.

Evolution leaves fragments.

But “fragment” cannot become an all-purpose explanation for any odd EEG result. A vestigial sense should still possess stable stimulus tuning, anatomy and causal perturbations.

Magnetite in human tissue does not supply those missing links. Magnetic particles have been reported in brain tissue, and environmental magnetite pollution can also enter the body. The pigeon-beak lesson applies with full force.

Material is not receptor.

Iron is not a sense organ.

Nor does the robin RF literature imply that everyday wireless exposure is disrupting human cognition.

The radical-pair compass is a specialized candidate sensory mechanism. The human EEG result, notably, was interpreted by its own authors as inconsistent with a radical-pair compass because the response was polarity-sensitive. Different candidate mechanism, different predictions.

This is where weak-field discourse often becomes careless.

A paper about migratory bird orientation is cited as evidence of generalized electromagnetic hypersensitivity in humans. The argument runs: birds are affected by weak RF fields; humans are biological; therefore human symptoms attributed to Wi-Fi may be caused by weak RF fields through an unknown shared sensitivity.

The conclusion does not follow.

Biology is full of specialized sensitivities. Pit vipers detect infrared through specialized organs. Electric fish detect electric fields. Bees see ultraviolet. Bats hear ultrasonic frequencies. The existence of a receptor in one lineage does not establish the receptor in another.

Even if humans retain magnetic transduction, its mechanism and functional significance must be measured directly.

Claims of electromagnetic hypersensitivity deserve seriousness toward the people reporting symptoms and equal seriousness about causal testing. Blinded provocation studies ask whether symptoms track actual exposure better than sham exposure. Human magnetoreception cannot be used as a shortcut around those experiments.

There is also a reverse shortcut.

Because broad claims about human EMF effects are often weak, some skeptics treat weak-field biological sensitivity itself as implausible. The bird compass makes that dismissal too broad. Specialized biological systems can amplify tiny environmental signals.

Specificity protects both conclusions.

Birds can possess an RF-sensitive magnetic compass without ordinary Wi-Fi producing a general human illness syndrome.

Humans can show a candidate neural response to Earth-strength field rotation without possessing a useful conscious compass.

A polarity-sensitive human response, if real, can point away from avian cryptochrome rather than toward it.

The mechanism has to be earned species by species and function by function.

The human question becomes more interesting when separated from paranormal claims.

People have long claimed an innate sense of direction. Some individuals navigate cities or wilderness unusually well. Those abilities are readily explained through learned spatial cognition, visual landmarks, path integration, memory, sun cues and experience unless controlled magnetic manipulation shows otherwise. Calling someone a “human compass” is not evidence of magnetoreception.

A rigorous behavioral bridge would have to connect magnetic manipulation to performance prospectively.

First, independently replicate the alpha-ERD response with equipment built by another laboratory.

Second, preregister the original stimulus asymmetries rather than searching a large field-parameter space for whichever contrast works.

Third, map individual response curves across polarity, inclination, intensity and rotation speed.

Fourth, test whether participants with strong neural responses perform differently on a nonverbal orientation or forced-choice task when the magnetic field is manipulated double-blind.

Fifth, localize the neural sources and identify a peripheral pathway.

Sixth, perturb the candidate pathway in a mechanism-specific way.

The order matters.

Without the first step, later mechanism stories can grow around an effect whose portability is unknown.

If the neural response replicates but never predicts behavior, it may represent transduction with no navigational role.

That would still be interesting.

The nervous system contains many signals that never become useful conscious variables. Sensory pathways can be weak, vestigial, redundant or functionally isolated.

Alternatively, carefully designed behavior might reveal a small unconscious magnetic bias. That would not mean humans navigate like pigeons. A few degrees of heading bias under stripped-down conditions could be the entire effect.

The scale of the claim must follow the data.

The same humility applies to subjective imagery.

Popular illustrations often show birds seeing colorful magnetic arcs or patterns across the visual field. That may be a useful representation of one radical-pair model. It is not a report from a robin.

Likewise, an EEG deflection in a human is not a hidden feeling waiting to be rediscovered through meditation.

No evidence reviewed here establishes that contemplative practice, altered states, psychedelics, grounding, or “reconnecting with the Earth” restores a human magnetic sense. Those ideas may be culturally attractive because they turn a physiological uncertainty into a story of lost natural ability. They remain unearned.

The human result is already strange enough without adding anything to it.

Thirty-six people sat inside a controlled magnetic-field system. They reported no awareness of the field rotations. Under some rotations, the brain signal changed. Four strong responders showed similar patterns when brought back. The artifact controls were unusually thoughtful. Seven years later, a major field review still says independent replication is required.

That is the whole tension.

Not proof.

Not nothing.

A result waiting to discover whether it can travel.

Humans are bad witnesses to senses that may not enter consciousness.

Science has a remedy for that problem.

Make another laboratory see it.
