# Magnetic Maps Are Not Latitude and Longitude

The Earth has no obligation to provide animals with a tidy coordinate system.

Its magnetic field is generated mostly by convection in the liquid outer core, distorted by crustal magnetization, influenced by currents in the ionosphere and magnetosphere, and slowly changing through secular variation. The geometry is global enough to navigate by and messy enough to punish anyone who mistakes a field model for graph paper.

This matters because “magnetic map” is one of those phrases that becomes less accurate as it becomes more vivid.

A human map is an external representation. It can assign coordinates to every point in a defined reference frame. A biological magnetic map may be only an internal relationship between measured field features, memory and action. It does not need to represent empty territory. It does not need to be globally unique. It does not need to tell an animal where it is in a form that could be translated into degrees of latitude and longitude.

It needs to improve decisions.

Suppose an animal can measure total magnetic intensity and inclination with finite precision. Across a large ocean basin, those variables form broad gradients. At some locations their contours intersect at informative angles. At others they run nearly parallel, reducing positional resolution. The same pair of values can recur in multiple places. Secular variation moves the contours over time.

A nervous system can still use them.

Navigation is an inference problem under uncertainty. An animal begins with prior information: which ocean basin it inhabits, what route stage it is in, what direction it has been traveling, what currents it recently experienced, perhaps what odors or celestial cues are available. A magnetic measurement updates that prior. The correct mathematical metaphor is closer to Bayesian localization than to reading coordinates off a display, though there is no reason to assume the brain implements Bayesian equations explicitly.

This perspective helps reconcile several classes of experiment.

Juvenile sea turtles exposed to fields characteristic of different geographic regions can change swimming direction in ways that make ecological sense for their migratory route. They do not need to know a global coordinate. An inherited rule can be conditional: when the local combination of intensity and inclination resembles region A, swim approximately southwest; when it resembles region B, choose another direction. The field acts as a trigger inside a route program.

Experienced migratory birds can compensate for virtual displacement because the local magnetic signature is inconsistent with where their memory says they should be. Again, no explicit coordinate is required. A mismatch between expected and measured magnetic conditions can be enough to choose a corrective heading.

Turtles can learn that a particular signature predicts food. That is even more local. A field pattern becomes a landmark.

Salmon offer a population-scale version. If young salmon imprint on the magnetic field associated with the mouth of their natal river and later use the remembered signature during return migration, secular variation should shift the oceanic location at which that signature occurs. The observed route correlations around Vancouver Island are consistent with such a process. The fish need not encode “49.1 degrees north.” They need to recognize a magnetic neighborhood associated with the route home.

The field’s imperfection becomes an experimental tool.

Secular variation generates natural changes in the relationship between geography and magnetic signature. Researchers can ask whether animal distributions, nesting sites or route choices move in predicted ways. Local anomalies create places where magnetic and geographic cues disagree. Geomagnetic storms produce transient disturbances. Each event can test how heavily animals weight the field relative to alternatives.

The temptation is to tell a GPS story because GPS is the navigation technology readers know.

The comparison is useful only if we preserve the differences.

GPS depends on an engineered constellation whose satellite positions and clock signals are known. A receiver solves for position by measuring signal travel times. The system is designed to make coordinates globally comparable. The geomagnetic field is not a transmitter network. Its gradients arise from planetary physics. Animals cannot ask the core to send a correction packet.

A magnetic map is therefore robust in some ways and fragile in others.

It is passive. There is no signal to jam in the conventional radio sense. The field penetrates water and darkness. It operates across enormous areas. But it is low-information compared with a modern GNSS signal, spatially ambiguous in places, temporally variable, and vulnerable to local magnetic distortion.

Those properties make it attractive to military and engineering researchers interested in navigation without satellites. Humans already use magnetic anomaly maps for geomagnetic navigation concepts. Aircraft, submarines and other platforms can compare measured magnetic fields against high-resolution maps built through surveying. That engineered approach differs fundamentally from an animal’s internal map but shares the same planetary substrate.

Biomimicry should not blur the distinction.

A turtle does not need to carry a crustal magnetic anomaly database. A submarine can.

The animal’s advantage is not raw precision. It is that evolution has integrated weak, drifting cues into a multisensory control system with little power, no external transmission, and graceful degradation when one channel fails. That architecture may be more instructive than the receptor itself.

Consider a migrating bird crossing Europe on an overcast night. Stars are obscured. The magnetic compass is available unless RF noise or unusual conditions disrupt it. The map signal may be coarse. Wind creates displacement. The bird’s route memory and endogenous migratory program supply priors. If the clouds clear at twilight, celestial cues can recalibrate direction. Near a familiar stopover, odors and landmarks may take over.

This resembles robust navigation under sensor uncertainty more than a single biological instrument.

The field itself supplies multiple variables that change on different scales.

Intensity varies smoothly over broad distances but also contains crustal anomalies.

Inclination varies from near horizontal at the magnetic equator toward vertical near magnetic poles.

Declination—the angle between geographic and magnetic north—varies by location and time.

Short-term disturbances alter these components during geomagnetic activity.

Gradients encode spatial structure.

Animals may not measure all of them. Different receptor mechanisms may be suited to different components.

A radical-pair compass is naturally oriented toward direction because reaction yields can depend on molecular orientation relative to the field axis. A particle-based receptor might encode intensity or gradient through force or torque. A map could combine measurements. This division would make physical sense of the biological dissociations.

But the map literature has its own evidentiary hazards.

When a field corresponding to a distant location elicits a behavior appropriate to that location, researchers infer that the animal treats the magnetic condition as positional information. This is a strong inference when the experimental manipulation isolates the field. Yet the geographic label is supplied by the investigator. The animal may not represent “Florida” or “north of the capture site.” It responds to the stimulus according to inherited or learned rules.

The difference becomes important when extrapolating.

A response to two simulated locations does not prove a continuous map across an entire basin. A conditioned discrimination between two signatures does not show that the turtle can interpolate arbitrary coordinates. A displacement response in experienced birds does not establish how the map works in juveniles.

Good studies expand the stimulus space.

Test multiple locations.

Create hybrid fields combining intensity from one place and inclination from another.

Shift one parameter independently.

Use fields that do not correspond to any natural point and see whether the animal interpolates or becomes confused.

Repeat after learning.

Repeat after trigeminal disruption or RF exposure.

The 2025 turtle study used mismatched combinations to probe what was learned, strengthening the case that turtles can use more than one magnetic component in place discrimination.

A future decisive experiment could treat the animal’s map as a psychophysical surface. Instead of two fields, present a dense grid of intensity-inclination combinations around a learned target. Measure conditioned response strength. The resulting contour would reveal the animal’s generalization function: how much a signature can change before the place stops feeling like the same place.

Then repeat that surface after months of exposure to a slowly drifting field. Does the target representation update? Does it track the field or remain anchored to some other cue?

That experiment would address a deep problem raised by secular variation.

If magnetic maps are learned, they must either tolerate drift or be recalibrated. Long-lived animals such as sea turtles can return to regions after years. The Earth’s field changes during that interval. A rigid signature memory would gradually mislocalize the target. Learning provides a solution: update the association when other cues confirm arrival.

Inheritance provides another solution over evolutionary time. If magnetic contours move predictably enough across generations, selection can tune response thresholds. But rapid secular change relative to generation time would challenge purely inherited maps.

The likely answer is again plural.

Some route responses can be inherited.

Some place signatures can be learned.

Some components can be recalibrated.

Some environments provide enough redundant cues that the magnetic map can be wrong without catastrophe.

This is why I resist the romance of a secret biological GPS. GPS is powerful because it is exact and standardized. Animal magnetic navigation may be powerful because it is neither.

A coarse cue can be extraordinarily useful inside the right control system.

The Earth does not print latitude and longitude in the sky.

It supplies a slowly moving, imperfect physical landscape.

The animal learns what parts of that landscape are worth remembering.
