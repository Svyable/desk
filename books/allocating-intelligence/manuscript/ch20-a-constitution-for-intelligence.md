# A Constitution for Intelligence

A constitution is an allocation document.

It allocates powers, limits, procedures, rights, offices, and routes of appeal. It does not try to predict every future decision. It creates a structure inside which decisions can be made without having to renegotiate the legitimacy of the system each morning.

Artificial intelligence needs constitutions at many scales.

A personal agent needs rules about privacy, spending, identity, memory, and when to ask. A company needs rules about models, data, permissions, review, incident response, and nondelegable decisions. A profession needs standards for competence and responsibility. A society needs rights and boundaries around consequential automation.

These constitutions should not be copies of political constitutions. The analogy is functional. When capability becomes abundant and action becomes cheap, we need durable rules for who may do what with whose intelligence.

The mistake would be to answer that need with a thicker binder.

A constitutional system is not strong because it has many rules. It is strong when the rules reach the places where power actually changes hands. A company can publish thirty pages of AI principles and still give an agent permission to spend money, contact customers, alter production data, and approve its own work. A school can promise human-centered use while grading students through a system nobody can challenge. A government can require transparency in principle while the citizen still cannot discover which record produced the decision that matters.

The practical question is simpler: where does intelligence become consequence?

That boundary deserves most of the design effort.

The first discipline is to allocate according to consequence rather than prestige. Strong models, senior experts, committees, and elaborate verification all carry cost. The right place to spend them is where another increment of care changes the outcome. Routine, reversible work should usually flow through cheaper paths. Ambiguous, irreversible work deserves more depth. A trivial task does not become important because a frontier model performed it. A consequential task does not become safe because a senior person glanced at the output.

This sounds obvious until organizations are given a new capability. New technology attracts ceremony. Executives want access to the strongest system. Teams route ordinary work upward because doing so signals seriousness. People add human approval to low-risk actions because approval sounds responsible. At the same time, high-volume routing, ranking, scheduling, and filtering systems receive less scrutiny because they look administrative.

But administration is where allocation hides.

The cheap classifier that decides which case reaches the expensive model may matter more than the expensive model. The queue that determines which patient is seen first may matter more than the quality of advice available ten steps later. The alert threshold that decides whether a human is interrupted may matter more than the eloquence of the explanation once the interruption occurs.

So consequence has to be traced through the whole path, not assigned to the glamorous component.

That path has a clock.

Some failures become worse by the minute. Others become worse when people move too quickly. A constitution for intelligence therefore needs a view of time that is more precise than “fast is good” or “high stakes require humans.” Emergency care, industrial control, cybersecurity, credit, hiring, education, and public administration run on different clocks. Even within one system, one event may need immediate containment and slow adjudication afterward.

The important design choice is often to separate the two.

A suspicious transaction can be paused quickly and reviewed more slowly. A machine can stop itself when a sensor crosses a threshold without deciding the entire cause of the anomaly. A public system can preserve a filing deadline while delaying a final adverse decision. Reversibility buys time. Precommitment buys time. Staged authority buys time.

Latency is part of correctness, but so is the cost of acting before uncertainty has earned action.

This is why capability and authority must remain separate concepts even as models become better. A system may be capable of drafting a contract without being authorized to sign it, capable of identifying a likely fraud without being authorized to punish a customer, capable of diagnosing a pattern without being authorized to choose treatment, capable of writing code without being authorized to deploy it to production.

The distinction is not an insult to intelligence. Human institutions make it constantly. A brilliant lawyer does not acquire the client’s signature authority by becoming more brilliant. A physician’s expertise does not erase consent. An analyst can recommend a trade without owning the portfolio. Knowledge and permission overlap, but they are not the same resource.

Machine systems make the difference easier to forget because capability arrives through an interface that can also contain tools. The same box can advise, click, spend, send, modify, and remember. Product design can turn a recommendation into an action with one permission toggle.

A constitution should resist that visual compression. Advice and action should look different. Pending and completed work should look different. Reversible and irreversible acts should look different. The system should make visible what it has observed, what it believes, what it proposes, what it has already done, what authority it used, and what can still be undone.

Conversation alone is a poor control room.

A fluent agent can describe itself beautifully while leaving the operator unsure which files changed, which messages were sent, which credentials were used, or what remains queued. Operational state needs its own surface. Logs, receipts, diffs, permissions, checkpoints, and explicit pending actions are not bureaucratic decoration. They are how a person or another system knows where the world now differs from the plan.

The same is true of memory.

Memory increases the usefulness of intelligence because context accumulates. It also increases power. A system that remembers previous decisions, preferences, mistakes, relationships, and exceptions can act with far more continuity than one starting fresh. That continuity is valuable until memory loses provenance, crosses a boundary the person did not expect, or hardens an old fact into a permanent identity.

Useful memory therefore needs two opposite capacities: retention and forgetting.

Institutions should preserve what serves safety, continuity, accountability, and learning. They should also know what expires. A temporary failure should not become an immortal label merely because storage is cheap. A correction should travel with the original record. A summary should point back toward evidence. A decision record should preserve enough context to show what was known at the time rather than rewriting history after the outcome.

Memory without source becomes folklore. Total memory becomes surveillance. The interesting design lives between them.

Verification belongs there too.

Cheap generation increases the temptation to check everything because another review is easy to request. It also increases the temptation to check nothing because the output looks polished and the queue is long. Neither scales well.

Verification should be proportional to consequence and as independent as the consequence requires.

Sometimes the best check is deterministic: a balance must reconcile, a schema must validate, a limit must not be exceeded, a test must pass. Sometimes the check requires another method, another model family, another dataset, or another person. Sometimes the right check is delayed and empirical: did the decision actually improve the outcome? Asking the same system to admire its answer twice may catch a typo. It is weaker evidence against a shared blind spot.

The important word is not “human.” It is “independent.”

Humans can be correlated with the system they supervise. They can inherit the same assumptions, read the same summary, accept the same framing, and rubber-stamp the same recommendation. Machines can sometimes provide genuinely independent checks when they use different evidence or methods. The architecture matters more than the species label.

Independence also matters because the system that recommends an action should not automatically authorize it, execute it, verify success, investigate failure, and judge the appeal. Combining all those functions is efficient. It is also how one mistaken frame becomes the whole institution.

Separation of powers can be technical rather than ceremonial. One service can enforce limits neither model may waive. Another can record actions in a log the acting system cannot rewrite. A different model or team can review incidents. A consequential appeal can leave the product team’s chain of command. The purpose is not to surround every agent with committees. It is to prevent convenience from creating common-mode authority.

That becomes especially important when people are affected by the system rather than merely using it.

Every classifier creates edge cases. Every summary leaves things out. Every model works from a frame. People outside that frame sometimes know the missing fact.

A rejected applicant may know that the record is wrong. A worker may know the schedule cannot be performed safely. A patient may know that a copied medical history is incomplete. A citizen may know that the address in the database belongs to a building that no longer exists. A community may know that a category that looks neat from headquarters cuts across reality on the ground.

An appeal route is therefore more than a courtesy. It is a sensor.

Giving affected people standing to challenge a consequential decision does not mean every objection wins. It means the institution has a channel through which evidence can enter from outside the model’s current representation. The quality of the appeal system should be judged partly by whether it changes the system’s understanding, not merely whether it closes tickets quickly.

The burden of proof should also follow consequence. A low-stakes recommendation can be easy to accept. A severe deprivation of rights should demand stronger justification. Emergency action may invert the normal burden temporarily, but temporary authority should leave a record and face review when the emergency passes.

Uncertainty is never eliminated. A constitution decides who has to live with it.

That is one reason public uses of artificial intelligence cannot be reduced to product design. When the state scores, ranks, investigates, allocates, or denies, a citizen should not have to accept “the model said so” as the final reason. The technical explanation may matter, but public power also needs public justification. The rule has to connect the estimate to a legitimate authority and a contestable procedure.

A classifier can estimate. A constitution has to explain why the estimate is allowed to matter.

The same constitutional logic appears inside private organizations, though the rights and remedies differ. A worker may not have democratic standing inside a company, but the company still has to decide which decisions can be delegated, which actions require explanation, which monitoring is legitimate, how mistakes are corrected, and whether efficiency gains increase the worker’s agency or merely increase the rate at which work is demanded.

That last question is easy to lose in productivity debates.

AI can save time. Saved time does not arrive with instructions.

An organization can convert it into more output, lower prices, stronger verification, shorter queues, shorter hours, deeper service, more experimentation, or reserve capacity. If no choice is made, the usual result is that the saved minute is consumed by the next request. The system becomes faster without anybody becoming less rushed.

The allocation of the productivity dividend is therefore part of the constitution whether leaders acknowledge it or not. A call center can use assistance to help agents solve harder cases or to compress handle times until every pause looks suspicious. A hospital can use administrative automation to return time to care or to raise the expected patient load. A software team can use agents to deepen testing or simply increase the number of concurrent projects.

Throughput is one possible use of abundant cognition. It is not the definition of value.

The same caution applies to centralization.

Strong general models make central coordination attractive. A central system can see across many cases, standardize decisions, identify patterns, and distribute expertise. Those are real advantages. They can reduce arbitrary local variation and make scarce capabilities widely available.

But local knowledge does not disappear because the center became smarter.

People at the edge see different things. They know which workaround is carrying the process, which customer cannot use the official channel, which machine sounds wrong before a sensor crosses a threshold, which rule is producing absurd outcomes, which language a community actually uses, which exception has become common enough to stop being exceptional.

A good allocation system lets abnormalities travel upward without requiring the center to micromanage every ordinary act. It also lets authority travel downward where the action is reversible and the local information is better.

This is why the dispatcher remains a useful image. The dispatcher does not become valuable by knowing everything. The value comes from maintaining routes between partial views.

Those routes need reserve.

Automation tends to consume practice. A task that a machine performs every day is a task a person no longer performs every day. That can be a gift. It can also leave the system without practiced human competence when conditions fall outside the machine’s envelope.

The answer is not to force humans to repeat routine work for nostalgia. Reserve has to be designed deliberately. Critical skills can be maintained through simulation, rotation, drills, supervised exceptions, and periods of direct operation. Some domains may decide that manual reserve is not worth the cost because automation remains safer even in degraded modes. Others will discover that the cost of preserving a human fallback is small compared with the cost of losing it.

The question is empirical: what capability must remain available when the normal system fails, and what practice is required to keep that capability real?

Correlated failure makes the question harder. A thousand agents can look like a thousand workers while sharing one model, one update, one data source, one toolchain, and one mistaken assumption. Scale creates diversity of instances without necessarily creating diversity of judgment.

Where common-mode failure matters, institutions need some heterogeneity: independent methods, staged rollouts, fallback paths, model diversity, human reserve, or physical limits that stop a software error from reaching the whole system at once. Standardization can improve reliability and simplify repair; diversity can increase complexity. The constitution has to decide where correlation is the larger risk.

It also has to decide what happens when all the exceptions arrive together.

Fallback systems are often designed around ordinary rates of failure. They work beautifully until a shared dependency fails and every case becomes an exception at once. Then the human review team, secondary model, call center, spare server, or manual process collapses under the very event it was supposed to absorb.

Graceful degradation requires priorities chosen before the surge. Which services continue? Which pause? Which users receive scarce human attention? Which actions become temporarily unavailable? Who can declare emergency mode, what authority expands, and when does that authority expire?

A system that invents its emergency constitution during the emergency is allocating power at the moment fear is highest.

Scarcity also survives outside the cognitive system.

An answer without a bed, teacher, engineer, permit, treatment, network connection, or legal authority may not change reality. This is the most stubborn limit on the fantasy of intelligence abundance. Cognition can be plentiful while implementation remains painfully physical.

A city can have excellent models of housing demand and still lack permission to build. A school can have a tutor for every student and still lack safe classrooms, stable broadband, or adults who can notice distress. A clinic can improve diagnostic support and remain constrained by staffing, medicine, transport, or specialist appointments. A small firm can gain world-class analysis and remain unable to finance inventory.

The constitution should follow those complements. Otherwise the organization optimizes the part of the system that became cheap and congratulates itself while the real bottleneck sits downstream.

This matters for public intelligence in particular.

Some cognitive capabilities have public-good characteristics or produce social value that private markets may underprovide: translation, navigation through government, emergency information, scientific tools, educational support, legal orientation, accessibility, public records, and basic factual explanation. Making these capabilities broadly available can increase agency far beyond the cost of another premium service.

But “public AI” should not mean one official model through which citizens must speak to the state. Public provision needs plural routes, privacy, accessibility, appeal, and a way back to accountable institutions. The useful public system is not a chatbot wearing a seal. It is cognitive infrastructure that helps people understand and exercise rights without becoming the only doorway through which those rights can be reached.

The same principle applies to identity and delegation between private actors. As agents act for people and organizations, counterparties need to know whom the agent represents and what authority it carries. A purchasing agent authorized to compare prices is different from one authorized to sign a contract. An assistant drafting an email is different from an agent permitted to negotiate on the sender’s behalf. Identity, signatures, scoped credentials, and receipts will become ordinary parts of cognition because delegated intelligence is also delegated agency.

The constitution should be legible enough that these differences can be inspected.

Legibility is not the same as total transparency. No serious institution exposes every internal deliberation, every security control, every personal record, or every trade secret. The useful requirement is narrower: people should be able to understand the rules that govern consequential action, the authority being exercised, the evidence relevant to their case where rights permit, and the route for correction.

Policy that exists only in a legal memo is too far from the machine. Policy that exists only in code is too far from managerial and democratic understanding.

The two have to meet.

That meeting is often uncomfortable because institutions already live under multiple constitutions. The handbook says one thing. The software allows another. Managers expect a third. Workers learn a fourth through experience. AI makes the contradiction faster. Automated systems execute the technical constitution repeatedly, at scale, long after everybody has forgotten which meeting produced the permission.

So material changes in authority should be visible as material changes. New data categories, expanded permissions, altered appeal rights, new affected populations, substantially different decision consequences, or a new model placed inside an old workflow should trigger more attention than a cosmetic interface update.

Not every change deserves a committee. Governance that treats every adjustment as constitutional will drown in trivia. But an institution should know what counts as a meaningful shift in power.

It should also know how to amend the rules.

A verification requirement that was prudent for a weak system may become pointless friction after evidence improves. An autonomy limit may become too restrictive once monitoring and reversibility are stronger. A memory policy may become too permissive after a new use creates unexpected harm. A public service may need a different interface when people begin arriving through personal agents rather than websites.

Good rules need an amendment path because technology changes, organizations learn, and failures reveal assumptions that nobody knew were assumptions.

The amendment process should leave memory. What was the old rule? What evidence justified the change? Who approved it? What outcomes should cause reconsideration? Can the change be rolled back?

Without that record, institutions drift. Permissions expand quietly through product updates, exceptions become defaults, and a system that once asked begins to act. The opposite failure is fossilization: controls remain because changing them is politically harder than measuring whether they still work.

The cure is not constant reform. It is visible revision.

The constitutional questions become political at the point where they touch power that cannot be justified by convenience alone.

Which public decisions may be delegated? Which forms of surveillance are acceptable? Which memories can follow a person across institutions? Which rights require a human hearing? Which services should remain available outside an automated channel? Which powers should be impossible for a vendor, agency, employer, or model to accumulate simply because integration made accumulation easy?

There is no benchmark for those answers.

The machine can help analyze trade-offs, summarize law, simulate outcomes, surface precedents, and reveal inconsistencies. It cannot make legitimacy disappear. A technically superior allocation can still be politically unacceptable. A slower process can be worth preserving because it creates standing, explanation, or dignity. A locally inefficient option can be part of the price of pluralism.

Intelligence can tell us that one route is shorter, one patient is at higher risk, one applicant resembles previous defaults, one tax return looks anomalous, one policy may produce more growth. None of those statements, by themselves, tells us what an institution is entitled to do to a person.

That bridge is institutional.

The coming decades will produce models more capable than the ones that motivated this book. Some technical examples here will age. Specific benchmarks will disappear. Interfaces will change. Costs will fall. New failure modes will arrive.

The allocation questions are more durable.

What deserves attention? Which errors matter? Who may act? What must remain reversible? Which memory should persist? Where should expertise live? Who bears the burden of verification? Which capabilities should be public? How is dissent heard? Which powers cannot be delegated merely because delegation is convenient?

These are not twenty commandments for an AI age. They are different views of the same problem.

A civilization that can manufacture cognition still has to decide how cognition becomes action.

It can chase capability alone, adding more minds to every problem and trusting abundance to organize itself. That path will produce impressive local results and strange global failures: attention floods, verification bottlenecks, brittle centralization, dormant human skill, automated inequality, and systems too fast for their own controls.

Or allocation can become a first-class design problem.

That path is less theatrical. It requires queues, permissions, interfaces, rights, memory policy, training, procurement, public infrastructure, and the occasional deliberate pause. It looks like administration because administration is the technology through which collective intelligence becomes action.

The dispatcher from the first chapter sits at the end of the book for this reason.

The dispatcher is surrounded by partial knowledge. The caller sees one part of the event. The crew knows another. The institution has maps, procedures, jurisdiction, and resources. Time passes while nobody possesses the whole picture. The work is not to become the smartest person in every relevant profession. The work is to direct the next useful capability toward the place where it can still matter, preserve a route for new information, and change course when the first classification stops fitting the world.

Civilization has always done this with minds.

Families decide who handles a crisis. Firms route questions through hierarchies. Professions certify expertise. Markets bid for labor. States build schools and research agencies. Libraries preserve memory. Courts allocate hearing. Networks allocate bandwidth. Every institution is partly a system for deciding whose cognition counts, where, and when.

Artificial intelligence enlarges the pool and changes its composition.

That does not make allocation less important.

It makes allocation visible.

The age of abundant intelligence will need better administration of mind, not because administration is more noble than invention, but because invention becomes consequence only after somebody decides who may use it, what it may touch, how fast it may move, who checks it, what survives in memory, and what happens when a person says the system is wrong.

Intelligence can answer questions.

A constitution decides how answers become power.
