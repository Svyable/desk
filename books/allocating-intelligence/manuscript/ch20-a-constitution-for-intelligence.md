# A Constitution for Intelligence

A constitution is an allocation document. It assigns powers, limits, procedures, rights, offices, and routes of appeal. It does not predict every future decision. It creates a structure inside which decisions can be made without renegotiating the legitimacy of the system each morning.

Artificial intelligence needs constitutions at several scales. A personal agent needs rules about privacy, spending, identity, memory, and when to ask. A company needs rules about models, data, permissions, review, incident response, and nondelegable decisions. Professions need standards for competence and responsibility. Societies need rights and boundaries around consequential automation. These should not be copies of political constitutions. The analogy is functional: when capability becomes abundant and action becomes cheap, durable rules have to govern who may do what with whose intelligence.

The mistake would be to answer that need with a thicker binder. A constitutional system is strong when its rules reach the places where power actually changes hands. A company can publish thirty pages of AI principles and still give an agent permission to spend money, contact customers, alter production data, and approve its own work. A school can promise human-centered use while grading students through a system nobody can challenge. A government can require transparency while a citizen still cannot discover which record produced the decision that matters. The practical question is where intelligence becomes consequence.

That boundary deserves most of the design effort. Strong models, senior experts, committees, and elaborate verification all carry cost, so they should be spent where another increment of care changes the outcome. Routine, reversible work can usually flow through cheaper paths; ambiguous or irreversible work deserves more depth. Prestige can distort this allocation. Organizations add human approval to low-risk actions because approval sounds responsible while high-volume routing, ranking, scheduling, and filtering receive less scrutiny because they look administrative.

Administration is where allocation hides. The cheap classifier that decides which case reaches the expensive model may matter more than the expensive model. The queue that determines which patient is seen first may matter more than the advice available ten steps later. An alert threshold may decide whether a human is interrupted at all. Consequence has to be traced through the whole path rather than assigned to the glamorous component, and that path has a clock. A suspicious transaction can be paused quickly and reviewed more slowly. A machine can stop itself when a sensor crosses a threshold without deciding the entire cause of the anomaly. Reversibility, precommitment, and staged authority buy time when the world does not permit one uniform pace.

Capability and authority have to remain separate even as models improve. A system may be capable of drafting a contract without being authorized to sign it, identifying likely fraud without being authorized to punish a customer, diagnosing a pattern without choosing treatment, or writing code without deploying it. A brilliant lawyer does not acquire a client’s signature authority by becoming more brilliant. A physician’s expertise does not erase consent. Knowledge and permission overlap, but they are not the same resource.

Machine systems make the distinction easy to lose because the same interface can advise, click, spend, send, modify, and remember. Advice and action, pending and completed work, reversible and irreversible acts should remain visibly different. The operator should be able to see what the system observed, what it proposes, what it has done, what authority it used, and what can still be undone. Conversation alone is a poor control room. Logs, receipts, diffs, permissions, checkpoints, and explicit pending actions show where the world now differs from the plan.

Memory raises the same constitutional problem. Context makes intelligence more useful, but accumulated context is also power. A system that remembers previous decisions, preferences, mistakes, relationships, and exceptions can act with far more continuity than one starting fresh. That continuity becomes dangerous when memory loses provenance, crosses a boundary the person did not expect, or hardens an old fact into a permanent identity. Corrections should travel with original records; summaries should point back toward evidence; decision records should preserve enough context to show what was known at the time. Memory without source becomes folklore. Total memory becomes surveillance.

Verification has to be allocated too. Sometimes the best check is deterministic: a balance reconciles, a schema validates, a limit holds, a test passes. Sometimes it requires another method, model family, dataset, or person. Sometimes the useful check is delayed and empirical: did the decision improve the outcome? Asking the same system to admire its answer twice may catch a typo, but it is weak evidence against a shared blind spot.

Independence matters more than whether the checker is human or machine. Humans can inherit the same assumptions and rubber-stamp the same recommendation; machines can provide genuinely different evidence or methods. The architecture should keep one convenient path from becoming common-mode authority. The system that recommends an action should not automatically authorize it, execute it, verify success, investigate failure, and judge the appeal. A separate service can enforce limits neither model may waive, a log can remain outside the acting system’s control, and a consequential appeal can leave the product team’s chain of command.

Appeal matters because people affected by a system sometimes possess evidence the system does not. A rejected applicant may know the record is wrong. A worker may know the schedule cannot be performed safely. A patient may know a copied medical history is incomplete. An appeal route gives that evidence somewhere to enter. The burden of proof should then follow consequence: a low-stakes recommendation can be easy to accept, while a severe deprivation of rights should demand stronger justification. Emergency action may invert the normal burden temporarily, but temporary authority should leave a record and face review when the emergency passes.

Public uses of artificial intelligence make this especially visible. When the state scores, ranks, investigates, allocates, or denies, a citizen should not have to accept “the model said so” as the final reason. Technical explanation may matter, but public power also needs public justification. A classifier can estimate; an institution still has to explain why the estimate is allowed to matter.

Private organizations face the same structural question in different legal and political terms. AI can save time, but saved time does not arrive with instructions. A call center can use assistance to help agents solve harder cases or compress handle times until every pause looks suspicious. A hospital can return administrative time to care or raise the expected patient load. A software team can deepen testing or increase the number of concurrent projects. Throughput is one possible use of abundant cognition, not the definition of value.

Strong general models also make central coordination attractive. A central system can see across cases, standardize decisions, identify patterns, and distribute expertise. Those advantages are real, but local knowledge does not disappear because the center became smarter. People at the edge know which workaround is carrying the process, which customer cannot use the official channel, and which exception has become common enough to stop being exceptional. A good allocation system lets abnormalities travel upward without requiring the center to micromanage every ordinary act, while allowing authority to travel downward where action is reversible and local information is better.

The dispatcher remains useful here. The dispatcher does not become valuable by knowing everything. The value comes from maintaining routes between partial views, including a route for the case that no longer fits its first classification.

Those routes need reserve. Automation tends to consume practice because a task a machine performs every day is a task a person no longer performs every day. Critical skills can be maintained through simulation, rotation, drills, supervised exceptions, and direct operation where the cost is justified. The point is not nostalgia for routine work. It is to know which capability must remain available when the normal system fails and what practice keeps that capability real.

Correlated failure makes reserve harder to judge. A thousand agents can look like a thousand workers while sharing one model, update, data source, toolchain, and mistaken assumption. Where that common mode matters, institutions may need genuinely different methods, staged rollouts, fallback paths, human reserve, or physical limits that stop a software error from reaching the whole system at once. The fallback also has to survive the moment everyone needs it. A human review team or secondary model that works at ordinary exception rates can collapse when a shared dependency fails and every case becomes exceptional together. Graceful degradation therefore needs priorities chosen before the surge, not while the queue is already burning.

Scarcity survives outside the cognitive system too. An answer without a bed, teacher, engineer, permit, treatment, network connection, or legal authority may not change reality. A city can model housing demand and still lack permission to build. A school can have a tutor for every student and still lack stable broadband or adults who notice distress. A clinic can improve diagnostic support and remain constrained by staffing, medicine, transport, or specialist appointments. The constitution has to follow those complements rather than optimizing the part that became cheap while the real bottleneck sits downstream.

Some cognitive capabilities also have public-good characteristics or produce social value private markets may underprovide: translation, navigation through government, emergency information, scientific tools, educational support, legal orientation, accessibility, and public records. But “public AI” should not mean one official model through which citizens must speak to the state. Public provision needs plural routes, privacy, accessibility, appeal, and a way back to accountable institutions. The useful public system is not a chatbot wearing a seal. It is cognitive infrastructure that helps people understand and exercise rights without becoming the only doorway through which those rights can be reached.

As agents act for people and organizations, counterparties need to know whom an agent represents and what authority it carries. A purchasing agent authorized to compare prices is different from one authorized to sign a contract; an assistant drafting an email is different from an agent permitted to negotiate on the sender’s behalf. Identity, scoped credentials, signatures, and receipts make delegated intelligence legible without requiring total transparency. People need to understand the rules governing consequential action, the authority being exercised, the evidence relevant to their case where rights permit, and the route for correction. Policy that exists only in a legal memo is too far from the machine; policy that exists only in code is too far from managerial and democratic understanding.

Institutions already live under several constitutions at once. The handbook says one thing, the software allows another, managers expect a third, and workers learn a fourth through experience. AI makes the contradiction faster because automated systems execute the technical constitution repeatedly, at scale, long after everybody has forgotten which meeting produced the permission. Material changes in authority should therefore be visible as material changes: new data categories, expanded permissions, altered appeal rights, new affected populations, or a new model placed inside an old workflow.

The rules also need a way to change without drifting. A verification requirement prudent for a weak system may become pointless friction after evidence improves. An autonomy limit may become too restrictive once monitoring and reversibility are stronger. A memory policy may become too permissive after a new use creates unexpected harm. The record should show the old rule, the evidence for changing it, who approved the change, and what would cause reconsideration. Otherwise permissions expand quietly through product updates, or old safeguards fossilize after their reason has disappeared.

The constitutional questions become political where they touch power that cannot be justified by convenience alone. Which public decisions may be delegated? Which forms of surveillance are acceptable? Which memories can follow a person across institutions? Which rights require a human hearing? Which services should remain available outside an automated channel? A machine can analyze trade-offs, summarize law, simulate outcomes, surface precedents, and reveal inconsistencies. It cannot make legitimacy disappear. A technically superior allocation can still be politically unacceptable, and a slower process can be worth preserving because it creates standing, explanation, or dignity.

The models that motivated this book will age. Benchmarks will disappear, interfaces will change, costs will fall, and new failure modes will arrive. The allocation questions are more durable: what deserves attention, which errors matter, who may act, what must remain reversible, which memory should persist, where expertise should live, how dissent is heard, and which powers cannot be delegated merely because delegation is convenient. A civilization that can manufacture cognition still has to decide how cognition becomes action.

The dispatcher from the first chapter belongs at the end for this reason. The caller sees one part of an event. The crew knows another. The institution has maps, procedures, jurisdiction, and resources. Time passes while nobody possesses the whole picture. The dispatcher directs the next useful capability toward the place where it can still matter, preserves a route for new information, and changes course when the first classification stops fitting the world.

Civilization has always done versions of this through families, firms, professions, markets, schools, libraries, courts, and networks. Artificial intelligence enlarges the pool and changes its composition. The age of abundant intelligence will need better administration of mind because invention becomes consequence only after somebody decides who may use it, what it may touch, how fast it may move, who checks it, what survives in memory, and what happens when a person says the system is wrong.

Intelligence can answer questions. A constitution decides how answers become power.
