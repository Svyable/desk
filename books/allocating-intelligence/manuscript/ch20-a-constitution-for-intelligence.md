# A Constitution for Intelligence

A constitution is an allocation document. It allocates powers, limits, procedures, rights, offices, and routes of appeal. It does not try to predict every future decision. It creates a structure inside which decisions can be made without renegotiating the legitimacy of the system each morning.

Artificial intelligence needs constitutions at many scales. A personal agent needs rules about privacy, spending, identity, memory, and when to ask. A company needs rules about models, data, permissions, review, incident response, and nondelegable decisions. A profession needs standards for competence and responsibility. A society needs rights and boundaries around consequential automation. These constitutions should not be copies of political constitutions. The analogy is functional: when capability becomes abundant and action becomes cheap, we need durable rules for who may do what with whose intelligence.

The mistake would be to answer that need with a thicker binder. A constitutional system is not strong because it has many rules. It is strong when the rules reach the places where power actually changes hands. A company can publish thirty pages of AI principles and still give an agent permission to spend money, contact customers, alter production data, and approve its own work. A school can promise human-centered use while grading students through a system nobody can challenge. A government can require transparency in principle while the citizen still cannot discover which record produced the decision that matters. The practical question is where intelligence becomes consequence. That boundary deserves most of the design effort.

The first discipline is to allocate according to consequence rather than prestige. Strong models, senior experts, committees, and elaborate verification all carry cost. The right place to spend them is where another increment of care changes the outcome. Routine, reversible work should usually flow through cheaper paths; ambiguous, irreversible work deserves more depth. This sounds obvious until organizations are given a new capability. New technology attracts ceremony. Executives want access to the strongest system. Teams route ordinary work upward because doing so signals seriousness. People add human approval to low-risk actions because approval sounds responsible, while high-volume routing, ranking, scheduling, and filtering systems receive less scrutiny because they look administrative.

Administration is where allocation hides. The cheap classifier that decides which case reaches the expensive model may matter more than the expensive model. The queue that determines which patient is seen first may matter more than the quality of advice available ten steps later. The alert threshold that decides whether a human is interrupted may matter more than the eloquence of the explanation once the interruption occurs. Consequence has to be traced through the whole path, not assigned to the glamorous component.

That path has a clock. Some failures become worse by the minute; others become worse when people move too quickly. Emergency care, industrial control, cybersecurity, credit, hiring, education, and public administration run on different clocks. Even within one system, an event may need immediate containment and slow adjudication afterward. A suspicious transaction can be paused quickly and reviewed more slowly. A machine can stop itself when a sensor crosses a threshold without deciding the entire cause of the anomaly. A public system can preserve a filing deadline while delaying a final adverse decision. Reversibility, precommitment, and staged authority buy time. Latency is part of correctness, but so is the cost of acting before uncertainty has earned action.

Capability and authority therefore have to remain separate even as models improve. A system may be capable of drafting a contract without being authorized to sign it, identifying likely fraud without being authorized to punish a customer, diagnosing a pattern without being authorized to choose treatment, or writing code without being authorized to deploy it. Human institutions make the distinction constantly. A brilliant lawyer does not acquire the client’s signature authority by becoming more brilliant. A physician’s expertise does not erase consent. An analyst can recommend a trade without owning the portfolio. Knowledge and permission overlap, but they are not the same resource.

Machine systems make this easier to forget because capability arrives through an interface that can also contain tools. The same box can advise, click, spend, send, modify, and remember; product design can turn a recommendation into an action with one permission toggle. A constitution should resist that visual compression. Advice and action, pending and completed work, reversible and irreversible acts should look different. The system should make visible what it observed, what it believes, what it proposes, what it has done, what authority it used, and what can still be undone.

Conversation alone is a poor control room. A fluent agent can describe itself beautifully while leaving the operator unsure which files changed, which messages were sent, which credentials were used, or what remains queued. Operational state needs its own surface. Logs, receipts, diffs, permissions, checkpoints, and explicit pending actions are not bureaucratic decoration. They show where the world now differs from the plan.

Memory increases the usefulness of intelligence because context accumulates. It also increases power. A system that remembers previous decisions, preferences, mistakes, relationships, and exceptions can act with far more continuity than one starting fresh. That continuity is valuable until memory loses provenance, crosses a boundary the person did not expect, or hardens an old fact into a permanent identity. Useful memory needs both retention and forgetting. Institutions should preserve what serves safety, continuity, accountability, and learning, while knowing what expires. A temporary failure should not become an immortal label merely because storage is cheap. A correction should travel with the original record. A summary should point back toward evidence. A decision record should preserve enough context to show what was known at the time rather than rewriting history after the outcome. Memory without source becomes folklore; total memory becomes surveillance. The interesting design lives between them.

Verification has a similar allocation problem. Cheap generation makes another review easy to request, yet polished output and long queues tempt people to check nothing. Neither extreme scales. Sometimes the best check is deterministic: a balance reconciles, a schema validates, a limit holds, a test passes. Sometimes the check needs another method, model family, dataset, or person. Sometimes the right check is delayed and empirical: did the decision improve the outcome? Asking the same system to admire its answer twice may catch a typo, but it is weaker evidence against a shared blind spot.

The important property is independence, not species. Humans can inherit the same assumptions, read the same summary, accept the same framing, and rubber-stamp the same recommendation. Machines can sometimes provide genuinely independent checks when they use different evidence or methods. The architecture matters. The system that recommends an action should not automatically authorize it, execute it, verify success, investigate failure, and judge the appeal. One service can enforce limits neither model may waive; another can record actions in a log the acting system cannot rewrite; a different model or team can review incidents; a consequential appeal can leave the product team’s chain of command. The point is not to surround every agent with committees. It is to prevent convenience from becoming common-mode authority.

That matters especially when people are affected by a system rather than merely using it. Every classifier creates edge cases, every summary leaves things out, and people outside the frame sometimes know the missing fact. A rejected applicant may know the record is wrong. A worker may know the schedule cannot be performed safely. A patient may know a copied medical history is incomplete. A citizen may know the address in the database belongs to a building that no longer exists. An appeal route is therefore more than a courtesy; it is a sensor. Giving affected people standing to challenge a consequential decision does not mean every objection wins. It gives the institution a channel through which evidence can enter from outside the model’s representation.

The burden of proof should follow consequence. A low-stakes recommendation can be easy to accept. A severe deprivation of rights should demand stronger justification. Emergency action may invert the normal burden temporarily, but temporary authority should leave a record and face review when the emergency passes. Uncertainty is never eliminated. A constitution decides who has to live with it.

That is why public uses of artificial intelligence cannot be reduced to product design. When the state scores, ranks, investigates, allocates, or denies, a citizen should not have to accept “the model said so” as the final reason. Technical explanation may matter, but public power also needs public justification. A classifier can estimate; an institution still has to explain why the estimate is allowed to matter.

Private organizations face a related question in different legal and political terms. They have to decide which decisions can be delegated, which actions require explanation, which monitoring is legitimate, how mistakes are corrected, and whether efficiency gains increase a worker’s agency or merely increase the rate at which work is demanded. AI can save time, but saved time does not arrive with instructions. An organization can convert it into more output, lower prices, stronger verification, shorter queues, shorter hours, deeper service, more experimentation, or reserve capacity. If no choice is made, the saved minute is usually consumed by the next request. The system becomes faster without anybody becoming less rushed.

The productivity dividend is therefore an allocation decision whether leaders acknowledge it or not. A call center can use assistance to help agents solve harder cases or compress handle times until every pause looks suspicious. A hospital can return administrative time to care or raise the expected patient load. A software team can deepen testing or simply increase the number of concurrent projects. Throughput is one possible use of abundant cognition. It is not the definition of value.

Strong general models also make central coordination attractive. A central system can see across cases, standardize decisions, identify patterns, and distribute expertise. Those advantages are real. But local knowledge does not disappear because the center became smarter. People at the edge know which workaround is carrying the process, which customer cannot use the official channel, which rule is producing absurd outcomes, and which exception has become common enough to stop being exceptional. A good allocation system lets abnormalities travel upward without requiring the center to micromanage every ordinary act, while letting authority travel downward where action is reversible and local information is better.

This is why the dispatcher remains useful. The dispatcher does not become valuable by knowing everything. The value comes from maintaining routes between partial views, and those routes need reserve.

Automation tends to consume practice. A task a machine performs every day is a task a person no longer performs every day. That can be a gift, but it can also leave the system without practiced human competence when conditions fall outside the machine’s envelope. The answer is not to force humans to repeat routine work for nostalgia. Critical skills can be maintained through simulation, rotation, drills, supervised exceptions, and periods of direct operation. Some domains may find manual reserve is not worth the cost because automation remains safer even in degraded modes. Others will find the fallback cheap compared with losing it. The empirical question is what capability must remain available when the normal system fails, and what practice keeps it real.

Correlated failure complicates the answer. A thousand agents can look like a thousand workers while sharing one model, update, data source, toolchain, and mistaken assumption. Where common-mode failure matters, institutions need some heterogeneity: independent methods, staged rollouts, fallback paths, model diversity, human reserve, or physical limits that stop a software error from reaching the whole system at once. Standardization can improve reliability and simplify repair; diversity can increase complexity. The larger risk depends on the system.

Fallbacks also fail under load. A human review team, secondary model, call center, spare server, or manual process may work beautifully at ordinary exception rates and collapse when a shared dependency fails and every case becomes exceptional at once. Graceful degradation requires priorities chosen before the surge: which services continue, which pause, which users receive scarce human attention, which actions become temporarily unavailable, who can declare emergency mode, and when expanded authority expires. Inventing the emergency constitution during the emergency means allocating power when fear is highest.

Scarcity survives outside the cognitive system too. An answer without a bed, teacher, engineer, permit, treatment, network connection, or legal authority may not change reality. A city can model housing demand and still lack permission to build. A school can have a tutor for every student and still lack safe classrooms, stable broadband, or adults who notice distress. A clinic can improve diagnostic support and remain constrained by staffing, medicine, transport, or specialist appointments. A small firm can gain world-class analysis and remain unable to finance inventory. The constitution should follow those complements rather than optimizing the part that became cheap while the real bottleneck sits downstream.

Some cognitive capabilities also have public-good characteristics or produce social value private markets may underprovide: translation, navigation through government, emergency information, scientific tools, educational support, legal orientation, accessibility, public records, and basic factual explanation. Broad access can increase agency far beyond the value of another premium service. But “public AI” should not mean one official model through which citizens must speak to the state. Public provision needs plural routes, privacy, accessibility, appeal, and a way back to accountable institutions. The useful public system is not a chatbot wearing a seal. It is cognitive infrastructure that helps people understand and exercise rights without becoming the only doorway through which those rights can be reached.

As agents act for people and organizations, counterparties also need to know whom the agent represents and what authority it carries. A purchasing agent authorized to compare prices is different from one authorized to sign a contract. An assistant drafting an email is different from an agent permitted to negotiate on the sender’s behalf. Identity, signatures, scoped credentials, and receipts become ordinary parts of cognition because delegated intelligence is also delegated agency.

These differences need to be inspectable, but legibility is not total transparency. No serious institution exposes every internal deliberation, security control, personal record, or trade secret. People do need to understand the rules governing consequential action, the authority being exercised, the evidence relevant to their case where rights permit, and the route for correction. Policy that exists only in a legal memo is too far from the machine; policy that exists only in code is too far from managerial and democratic understanding.

Institutions already live under multiple constitutions. The handbook says one thing, the software allows another, managers expect a third, and workers learn a fourth through experience. AI makes the contradiction faster because automated systems execute the technical constitution repeatedly, at scale, long after everybody has forgotten which meeting produced the permission. Material changes in authority should therefore be visible as material changes. New data categories, expanded permissions, altered appeal rights, new affected populations, substantially different decision consequences, or a new model placed inside an old workflow deserve more attention than a cosmetic interface update. Not every change deserves a committee, but an institution should know what counts as a meaningful shift in power.

It should also know how to amend the rules. A verification requirement prudent for a weak system may become pointless friction after evidence improves. An autonomy limit may become too restrictive once monitoring and reversibility are stronger. A memory policy may become too permissive after a new use creates unexpected harm. Good rules need an amendment path because technology changes, organizations learn, and failures reveal assumptions nobody knew were assumptions. The record should show the old rule, the evidence for changing it, who approved the change, what outcomes should cause reconsideration, and whether the change can be rolled back. Without that memory, permissions expand quietly through product updates and exceptions become defaults. The opposite failure is fossilization. The useful discipline is visible revision.

The constitutional questions become political where they touch power that cannot be justified by convenience alone. Which public decisions may be delegated? Which forms of surveillance are acceptable? Which memories can follow a person across institutions? Which rights require a human hearing? Which services should remain available outside an automated channel? Which powers should be impossible for a vendor, agency, employer, or model to accumulate simply because integration made accumulation easy?

There is no benchmark for those answers. A machine can analyze trade-offs, summarize law, simulate outcomes, surface precedents, and reveal inconsistencies. It cannot make legitimacy disappear. A technically superior allocation can still be politically unacceptable. A slower process can be worth preserving because it creates standing, explanation, or dignity. A locally inefficient option can be part of the price of pluralism. Intelligence can tell us that one route is shorter, one patient is at higher risk, one applicant resembles previous defaults, one tax return looks anomalous, or one policy may produce more growth. None of those statements, by themselves, tells us what an institution is entitled to do to a person. That bridge is institutional.

The coming decades will produce models more capable than the ones that motivated this book. Technical examples here will age. Benchmarks will disappear. Interfaces will change. Costs will fall. New failure modes will arrive. The allocation questions are more durable: what deserves attention, which errors matter, who may act, what must remain reversible, which memory should persist, where expertise should live, who bears verification, which capabilities should be public, how dissent is heard, and which powers cannot be delegated merely because delegation is convenient. These are different views of the same problem. A civilization that can manufacture cognition still has to decide how cognition becomes action.

Chasing capability alone will produce impressive local results and strange global failures: attention floods, verification bottlenecks, brittle centralization, dormant human skill, automated inequality, and systems too fast for their own controls. Treating allocation as a first-class design problem is less theatrical. It requires queues, permissions, interfaces, rights, memory policy, training, procurement, public infrastructure, and the occasional deliberate pause. It looks like administration because administration is the technology through which collective intelligence becomes action.

The dispatcher from the first chapter belongs at the end for this reason. The caller sees one part of an event. The crew knows another. The institution has maps, procedures, jurisdiction, and resources. Time passes while nobody possesses the whole picture. The dispatcher’s work is not to become the smartest person in every relevant profession. It is to direct the next useful capability toward the place where it can still matter, preserve a route for new information, and change course when the first classification stops fitting the world.

Civilization has always done this with minds. Families decide who handles a crisis. Firms route questions through hierarchies. Professions certify expertise. Markets bid for labor. States build schools and research agencies. Libraries preserve memory. Courts allocate hearing. Networks allocate bandwidth. Every institution is partly a system for deciding whose cognition counts, where, and when.

Artificial intelligence enlarges the pool and changes its composition. That does not make allocation less important. It makes allocation visible.

The age of abundant intelligence will need better administration of mind, not because administration is more noble than invention, but because invention becomes consequence only after somebody decides who may use it, what it may touch, how fast it may move, who checks it, what survives in memory, and what happens when a person says the system is wrong.

Intelligence can answer questions. A constitution decides how answers become power.
