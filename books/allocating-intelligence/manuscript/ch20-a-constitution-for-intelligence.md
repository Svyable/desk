# A Constitution for Intelligence

A constitution is an allocation document. It assigns powers, limits, procedures, rights, offices, and routes of appeal. It does not predict every future decision; it creates a structure inside which decisions can be made without renegotiating the legitimacy of the system each morning.

Artificial intelligence needs constitutions at several scales. A personal agent needs rules about privacy, spending, identity, memory, and when to ask. A company needs rules about models, data, permissions, review, incident response, and nondelegable decisions. Professions need standards for competence and responsibility. Societies need rights and boundaries around consequential automation. These should not be copies of political constitutions. The analogy is functional: when capability becomes abundant and action becomes cheap, durable rules have to govern who may do what with whose intelligence.

The mistake would be to answer that need with a thicker binder. A company can publish thirty pages of AI principles while giving an agent permission to spend money, contact customers, alter production data, and approve its own work. A school can promise human-centered use while grading students through a system nobody can challenge. A government can require transparency while a citizen still cannot discover which record produced the decision that matters. A constitutional system is strong when its rules reach the places where intelligence becomes consequence.

That is where care should be spent. Strong models, senior experts, committees, and elaborate verification all carry cost, so routine, reversible work can usually take cheaper paths while ambiguous or irreversible work receives more depth. Prestige can distort the allocation: organizations add human approval to low-risk actions because approval sounds responsible, while high-volume routing, ranking, scheduling, and filtering receive less scrutiny because they look administrative. Administration is where allocation hides. The cheap classifier that decides which case reaches the expensive model may matter more than the expensive model, and a queue or alert threshold may determine whether scarce human attention arrives at all. Consequence has to be traced through the whole path, including its clock. A suspicious transaction can be paused quickly and reviewed more slowly; a machine can stop on a dangerous sensor reading without settling the cause. Reversibility, precommitment, and staged authority buy time when the world does not permit one uniform pace.

Capability and authority have to remain separate even as models improve. A system may be capable of drafting a contract without being authorized to sign it, identifying likely fraud without being authorized to punish a customer, diagnosing a pattern without choosing treatment, or writing code without deploying it. A brilliant lawyer does not acquire a client’s signature authority by becoming more brilliant, and a physician’s expertise does not erase consent. Machine interfaces make this distinction easy to lose because the same surface can advise, click, spend, send, modify, and remember. Advice and action, pending and completed work, reversible and irreversible acts should remain visibly different. Conversation alone is a poor control room; logs, receipts, diffs, permissions, checkpoints, and explicit pending actions show what the system observed, what it proposed, what it changed, and what can still be undone.

Memory raises a related problem. Context makes intelligence more useful, but accumulated context is also power. A system that remembers previous decisions, preferences, mistakes, relationships, and exceptions can act with far more continuity than one starting fresh. That continuity becomes dangerous when memory loses provenance, crosses a boundary the person did not expect, or hardens an old fact into a permanent identity. Corrections should travel with original records, summaries should point back toward evidence, and decision records should preserve enough context to show what was known at the time. Memory without source becomes folklore; total memory becomes surveillance.

Verification also has to follow consequence rather than prestige. Sometimes the best check is deterministic: a balance reconciles, a schema validates, a limit holds, a test passes. Sometimes it requires another method, model family, dataset, or person. Sometimes the useful check is delayed and empirical: did the decision improve the outcome? Asking the same system to admire its answer twice may catch a typo, but it is weak evidence against a shared blind spot. Independence matters more than whether the checker is human or machine. The system that recommends an action should not automatically authorize it, execute it, verify success, investigate failure, and judge the appeal. A separate service can enforce limits neither model may waive, a log can remain outside the acting system’s control, and a consequential appeal can leave the product team’s chain of command.

Appeal gives affected people a way to introduce evidence the system does not possess. A rejected applicant may know the record is wrong; a worker may know the schedule cannot be performed safely; a patient may know a copied medical history is incomplete. The burden of proof should rise with consequence, and emergency powers that temporarily invert the normal burden should leave a record and face review afterward. This becomes especially important in public systems. When the state scores, ranks, investigates, allocates, or denies, “the model said so” cannot be the final reason. A classifier can estimate; an institution still has to explain why the estimate is allowed to matter.

Private institutions face a parallel question in less constitutional language. AI can save time, but savings arrive without instructions. A call center can use assistance to solve harder cases or compress handle times until every pause looks suspicious. A hospital can return administrative time to care or raise the expected patient load. A software team can deepen testing or increase the number of concurrent projects. Throughput is one possible use of abundant cognition, not the definition of value.

Strong general models also make central coordination attractive. A center can see across cases, identify patterns, and distribute expertise, but local knowledge does not disappear because the center became smarter. People at the edge know which workaround is carrying the process, which customer cannot use the official channel, and which exception has become common enough to stop being exceptional. The useful architecture lets abnormalities travel upward without requiring the center to micromanage ordinary work and lets authority travel downward where action is reversible and local information is better. The dispatcher remains useful precisely because the job is not to know everything. It is to maintain routes between partial views, including a route for the case that no longer fits its first classification.

Those routes need reserve. Automation consumes practice when machines absorb the work people once did every day, so critical skills have to be maintained deliberately through simulation, rotation, drills, supervised exceptions, or direct operation where the cost is justified. Correlated failure makes the problem harder: a thousand agents can look like a thousand workers while sharing one model, update, data source, toolchain, and mistaken assumption. Where that common mode matters, reserve must be genuinely different enough to fail differently, and it must survive the moment everyone needs it. A human review team or secondary model that works at ordinary exception rates can collapse when a shared dependency fails and every case becomes exceptional together. Graceful degradation therefore needs priorities chosen before the queue is burning.

Scarcity also survives outside the cognitive system. An answer without a bed, teacher, engineer, permit, treatment, network connection, or legal authority may not change reality. A city can model housing demand and still lack permission to build; a school can have a tutor for every student and still lack stable broadband or adults who notice distress; a clinic can improve diagnostic support and remain constrained by staffing, transport, medicine, or specialist appointments. Allocation has to follow those complements instead of optimizing the part that became cheap while the real bottleneck sits downstream.

Some cognitive capabilities have public-good characteristics or produce value private markets may underprovide. Translation, government navigation, emergency information, scientific tools, educational support, legal orientation, accessibility, and public records can widen practical agency. But “public AI” should not mean one official model through which citizens must speak to the state. The useful public system is cognitive infrastructure with plural routes, privacy, accessibility, appeal, and a way back to accountable institutions, not a chatbot wearing a seal.

As agents act for people and organizations, counterparties also need to know whom an agent represents and what authority it carries. A purchasing agent authorized to compare prices is different from one authorized to sign a contract; an assistant drafting an email is different from an agent permitted to negotiate on the sender’s behalf. Identity, scoped credentials, signatures, and receipts make delegated intelligence legible without requiring total transparency. Policy has to be close enough to the machine to constrain action and legible enough to people that its authority can be understood and contested.

Institutions already live under several constitutions at once: the handbook says one thing, the software allows another, managers expect a third, and workers learn a fourth through experience. AI makes those contradictions faster because automated systems execute the technical constitution repeatedly and at scale. A new data category, expanded permission, altered appeal right, new affected population, or new model inside an old workflow can materially change authority even when the interface barely moves. Rules also need a disciplined way to change. A verification requirement can become pointless friction after evidence improves; an autonomy limit may become too restrictive once monitoring and reversibility are stronger; a memory policy may prove too permissive after a harmful new use. The record should show what changed, why, who approved it, and what evidence would reopen the decision. Otherwise permissions expand quietly through product updates or old safeguards fossilize after their reason disappears.

The constitutional questions become political where power cannot be justified by convenience alone. Which public decisions may be delegated? Which forms of surveillance are acceptable? Which memories can follow a person across institutions? Which rights require a human hearing? Which services should remain available outside an automated channel? A machine can analyze trade-offs, summarize law, simulate outcomes, surface precedents, and reveal inconsistencies. It cannot make legitimacy disappear. A technically superior allocation can still be politically unacceptable, and a slower process can be worth preserving because it creates standing, explanation, or dignity.

The models that motivated this book will age. Benchmarks will disappear, interfaces will change, costs will fall, and new failure modes will arrive. The dispatcher from the first chapter is more durable. The caller sees one part of an event; the crew knows another; the institution has maps, procedures, jurisdiction, and resources. Time passes while nobody possesses the whole picture. The dispatcher directs the next useful capability toward the place where it can still matter, preserves a route for new information, and changes course when the first classification stops fitting the world.

Civilization has always done versions of this through families, firms, professions, markets, schools, libraries, courts, and networks. Artificial intelligence enlarges the pool and changes its composition. The difficult work begins when invention becomes consequence: deciding who may use it, what it may touch, how fast it may move, who checks it, what survives in memory, and what happens when a person says the system is wrong.

Intelligence can answer questions. A constitution decides how answers become power.
