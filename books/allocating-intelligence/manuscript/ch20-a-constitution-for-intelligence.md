# A Constitution for Intelligence

A constitution is an allocation document. It assigns powers, limits, procedures, rights, offices, and routes of appeal. It does not predict every future decision. It creates a structure inside which decisions can be made without renegotiating the legitimacy of the system each morning.

Artificial intelligence needs constitutions at several scales. A personal agent needs rules about privacy, spending, identity, memory, and when to ask. A company needs rules about models, data, permissions, review, incident response, and nondelegable decisions. Professions need standards for competence and responsibility. Societies need rights and boundaries around consequential automation. These should not be copies of political constitutions. The analogy is functional: when capability becomes abundant and action becomes cheap, durable rules have to govern who may do what with whose intelligence.

The mistake would be to answer that need with a thicker binder. A constitutional system is not strong because it has many rules. It is strong when the rules reach the places where power actually changes hands. A company can publish thirty pages of AI principles and still give an agent permission to spend money, contact customers, alter production data, and approve its own work. A school can promise human-centered use while grading students through a system nobody can challenge. A government can require transparency in principle while a citizen still cannot discover which record produced the decision that matters. The practical question is where intelligence becomes consequence.

That boundary deserves most of the design effort. Strong models, senior experts, committees, and elaborate verification all carry cost, so they should be spent where another increment of care changes the outcome. Routine, reversible work can usually flow through cheaper paths; ambiguous or irreversible work deserves more depth. New technology makes this harder than it sounds because prestige attracts attention. Executives want the strongest system. Teams route ordinary work upward because doing so signals seriousness. Organizations add human approval to low-risk actions because approval sounds responsible, while high-volume routing, ranking, scheduling, and filtering receive less scrutiny because they look administrative.

Administration is where allocation hides. The cheap classifier that decides which case reaches the expensive model may matter more than the expensive model. The queue that determines which patient is seen first may matter more than the advice available ten steps later. An alert threshold may determine whether a human is interrupted at all. Consequence has to be traced through the whole path rather than assigned to the glamorous component.

That path also has a clock. Emergency care, industrial control, cybersecurity, credit, hiring, education, and public administration do not run at the same speed. Even one event may need immediate containment and slow adjudication afterward. A suspicious transaction can be paused quickly and reviewed more slowly. A machine can stop itself when a sensor crosses a threshold without deciding the entire cause of the anomaly. A public system can preserve a filing deadline while delaying a final adverse decision. Reversibility, precommitment, and staged authority buy time. Latency is part of correctness, but so is the cost of acting before uncertainty has earned action.

Capability and authority therefore have to remain separate even as models improve. A system may be capable of drafting a contract without being authorized to sign it, identifying likely fraud without being authorized to punish a customer, diagnosing a pattern without choosing treatment, or writing code without deploying it. Human institutions make this distinction constantly. A brilliant lawyer does not acquire a client’s signature authority by becoming more brilliant. A physician’s expertise does not erase consent. An analyst can recommend a trade without owning the portfolio. Knowledge and permission overlap, but they are not the same resource.

Machine systems make this easy to forget because the same interface can advise, click, spend, send, modify, and remember. Product design can turn a recommendation into an action with one permission toggle. A constitutional design should resist that compression. Advice and action, pending and completed work, reversible and irreversible acts should remain visibly different. The operator should be able to see what the system observed, what it believes, what it proposes, what it has done, what authority it used, and what can still be undone.

Conversation alone is a poor control room. A fluent agent can describe itself beautifully while leaving the operator unsure which files changed, which messages were sent, which credentials were used, or what remains queued. Logs, receipts, diffs, permissions, checkpoints, and explicit pending actions are not bureaucratic decoration. They show where the world now differs from the plan.

Memory raises the same constitutional problem. Context makes intelligence more useful, but accumulated context is also power. A system that remembers previous decisions, preferences, mistakes, relationships, and exceptions can act with far more continuity than one starting fresh. That continuity becomes dangerous when memory loses provenance, crosses a boundary the person did not expect, or hardens an old fact into a permanent identity. A temporary failure should not become an immortal label merely because storage is cheap. Corrections should travel with original records; summaries should point back toward evidence; decision records should preserve enough context to show what was known at the time. Memory without source becomes folklore. Total memory becomes surveillance. Useful institutional memory lives between them.

Verification has to be allocated too. Cheap generation makes another review easy to request, while polished output and long queues tempt people to check nothing. Sometimes the best check is deterministic: a balance reconciles, a schema validates, a limit holds, a test passes. Sometimes it requires another method, model family, dataset, or person. Sometimes the useful check is delayed and empirical: did the decision improve the outcome? Asking the same system to admire its answer twice may catch a typo, but it is weak evidence against a shared blind spot.

Independence matters more than whether the checker is human or machine. Humans can inherit the same assumptions, read the same summary, accept the same framing, and rubber-stamp the same recommendation. Machines can sometimes provide genuinely independent checks when they use different evidence or methods. What matters is architecture. The system that recommends an action should not automatically authorize it, execute it, verify success, investigate failure, and judge the appeal. One service can enforce limits neither model may waive; another can record actions in a log the acting system cannot rewrite; a different model or team can review incidents; a consequential appeal can leave the product team’s chain of command. Not every agent needs a committee. Consequential systems do need protection against convenience becoming common-mode authority.

Appeal matters because people affected by a system sometimes possess evidence the system does not. A rejected applicant may know the record is wrong. A worker may know the schedule cannot be performed safely. A patient may know a copied medical history is incomplete. A citizen may know the address in the database belongs to a building that no longer exists. An appeal route is therefore more than a courtesy. It is a sensor through which evidence can enter from outside the model’s representation. Giving people standing to challenge a consequential decision does not mean every objection wins.

The burden of proof should follow consequence. A low-stakes recommendation can be easy to accept. A severe deprivation of rights should demand stronger justification. Emergency action may invert the normal burden temporarily, but temporary authority should leave a record and face review when the emergency passes. Uncertainty is never eliminated. A constitution decides who has to live with it.

That is why public uses of artificial intelligence cannot be reduced to product design. When the state scores, ranks, investigates, allocates, or denies, a citizen should not have to accept “the model said so” as the final reason. Technical explanation may matter, but public power also needs public justification. A classifier can estimate; an institution still has to explain why the estimate is allowed to matter.

Private organizations face a related problem in different legal and political terms. They have to decide which actions can be delegated, which require explanation, how mistakes are corrected, and whether efficiency gains increase a worker’s agency or merely increase the rate at which work is demanded. AI can save time, but saved time does not arrive with instructions. A call center can use assistance to help agents solve harder cases or compress handle times until every pause looks suspicious. A hospital can return administrative time to care or raise the expected patient load. A software team can deepen testing or increase the number of concurrent projects. Throughput is one possible use of abundant cognition. It is not the definition of value.

Strong general models also make central coordination attractive. A central system can see across cases, standardize decisions, identify patterns, and distribute expertise. Those advantages are real, but local knowledge does not disappear because the center became smarter. People at the edge know which workaround is carrying the process, which customer cannot use the official channel, which rule is producing absurd outcomes, and which exception has become common enough to stop being exceptional. A good allocation system lets abnormalities travel upward without requiring the center to micromanage every ordinary act, while allowing authority to travel downward where action is reversible and local information is better.

The dispatcher remains useful here. The dispatcher does not become valuable by knowing everything. The value comes from maintaining routes between partial views, and those routes need reserve.

Automation tends to consume practice. A task a machine performs every day is a task a person no longer performs every day. That can be a gift, but it can also leave a system without practiced human competence when conditions fall outside the machine’s envelope. The answer is not to make humans repeat routine work for nostalgia. Critical skills can be maintained through simulation, rotation, drills, supervised exceptions, and periods of direct operation where the cost is justified. Some domains may find manual reserve is not worth maintaining because automation remains safer even in degraded modes. Others will find the fallback cheap compared with losing it. The empirical question is what capability must remain available when the normal system fails, and what practice keeps it real.

Correlated failure makes the answer harder. A thousand agents can look like a thousand workers while sharing one model, update, data source, toolchain, and mistaken assumption. Where common-mode failure matters, institutions may need independent methods, staged rollouts, fallback paths, model diversity, human reserve, or physical limits that stop a software error from reaching the whole system at once. Standardization can improve reliability and simplify repair; diversity can increase complexity. The larger risk depends on the system.

Fallbacks themselves fail under load. A human review team, secondary model, call center, spare server, or manual process may work at ordinary exception rates and collapse when a shared dependency fails and every case becomes exceptional at once. Graceful degradation therefore requires priorities chosen before the surge: which services continue, which pause, which users receive scarce human attention, which actions become temporarily unavailable, who can declare emergency mode, and when expanded authority expires. Inventing those rules during the emergency means allocating power when fear is highest.

Scarcity also survives outside the cognitive system. An answer without a bed, teacher, engineer, permit, treatment, network connection, or legal authority may not change reality. A city can model housing demand and still lack permission to build. A school can have a tutor for every student and still lack safe classrooms, stable broadband, or adults who notice distress. A clinic can improve diagnostic support and remain constrained by staffing, medicine, transport, or specialist appointments. A small firm can gain world-class analysis and remain unable to finance inventory. The constitution has to follow those complements rather than optimizing the part that became cheap while the real bottleneck sits downstream.

Some cognitive capabilities have public-good characteristics or produce social value private markets may underprovide: translation, navigation through government, emergency information, scientific tools, educational support, legal orientation, accessibility, public records, and basic factual explanation. Broad access can increase agency far beyond the value of another premium service. But “public AI” should not mean one official model through which citizens must speak to the state. Public provision needs plural routes, privacy, accessibility, appeal, and a way back to accountable institutions. The useful public system is not a chatbot wearing a seal. It is cognitive infrastructure that helps people understand and exercise rights without becoming the only doorway through which those rights can be reached.

As agents act for people and organizations, counterparties also need to know whom an agent represents and what authority it carries. A purchasing agent authorized to compare prices is different from one authorized to sign a contract. An assistant drafting an email is different from an agent permitted to negotiate on the sender’s behalf. Identity, signatures, scoped credentials, and receipts become ordinary parts of cognition because delegated intelligence is also delegated agency.

Those differences need to be inspectable, but legibility is not total transparency. No serious institution exposes every internal deliberation, security control, personal record, or trade secret. People do need to understand the rules governing consequential action, the authority being exercised, the evidence relevant to their case where rights permit, and the route for correction. Policy that exists only in a legal memo is too far from the machine; policy that exists only in code is too far from managerial and democratic understanding.

Institutions already live under multiple constitutions. The handbook says one thing, the software allows another, managers expect a third, and workers learn a fourth through experience. AI makes the contradiction faster because automated systems execute the technical constitution repeatedly, at scale, long after everybody has forgotten which meeting produced the permission. Material changes in authority should therefore be visible as material changes: new data categories, expanded permissions, altered appeal rights, new affected populations, substantially different decision consequences, or a new model placed inside an old workflow. Not every change deserves a committee, but an institution should know what counts as a meaningful shift in power.

It should also know how to amend the rules. A verification requirement prudent for a weak system may become pointless friction after evidence improves. An autonomy limit may become too restrictive once monitoring and reversibility are stronger. A memory policy may become too permissive after a new use creates unexpected harm. The record should show the old rule, the evidence for changing it, who approved the change, what outcomes should cause reconsideration, and whether the change can be rolled back. Without that memory, permissions expand quietly through product updates and exceptions become defaults. The opposite failure is fossilization. The useful discipline is visible revision.

The constitutional questions become political where they touch power that cannot be justified by convenience alone. Which public decisions may be delegated? Which forms of surveillance are acceptable? Which memories can follow a person across institutions? Which rights require a human hearing? Which services should remain available outside an automated channel? Which powers should be impossible for a vendor, agency, employer, or model to accumulate simply because integration made accumulation easy?

There is no benchmark for those answers. A machine can analyze trade-offs, summarize law, simulate outcomes, surface precedents, and reveal inconsistencies. It cannot make legitimacy disappear. A technically superior allocation can still be politically unacceptable. A slower process can be worth preserving because it creates standing, explanation, or dignity. A locally inefficient option can be part of the price of pluralism. Intelligence can tell us that one route is shorter, one patient is at higher risk, one applicant resembles previous defaults, one tax return looks anomalous, or one policy may produce more growth. None of those statements by itself tells us what an institution is entitled to do to a person. That bridge is institutional.

The models that motivated this book will age. Benchmarks will disappear, interfaces will change, costs will fall, and new failure modes will arrive. The allocation questions are more durable: what deserves attention, which errors matter, who may act, what must remain reversible, which memory should persist, where expertise should live, who bears verification, how dissent is heard, and which powers cannot be delegated merely because delegation is convenient. A civilization that can manufacture cognition still has to decide how cognition becomes action.

The dispatcher from the first chapter belongs at the end for this reason. The caller sees one part of an event. The crew knows another. The institution has maps, procedures, jurisdiction, and resources. Time passes while nobody possesses the whole picture. The dispatcher’s work is to direct the next useful capability toward the place where it can still matter, preserve a route for new information, and change course when the first classification stops fitting the world.

Civilization has always done versions of this. Families decide who handles a crisis. Firms route questions through hierarchies. Professions certify expertise. Markets bid for labor. States build schools and research agencies. Libraries preserve memory. Courts allocate hearing. Networks allocate bandwidth. Artificial intelligence enlarges the pool and changes its composition, which makes the allocation problem easier to see and harder to avoid.

The age of abundant intelligence will need better administration of mind because invention becomes consequence only after somebody decides who may use it, what it may touch, how fast it may move, who checks it, what survives in memory, and what happens when a person says the system is wrong.

Intelligence can answer questions. A constitution decides how answers become power.
