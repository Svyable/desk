# A Constitution for Intelligence

A constitution is an allocation document.

It allocates powers, limits, procedures, rights, offices, and routes of appeal. It does not try to predict every future decision. It creates a structure inside which decisions can be made without having to renegotiate the legitimacy of the system each morning.

Artificial intelligence needs constitutions at many scales.

A personal agent needs rules about privacy, spending, identity, memory, and when to ask. A company needs rules about models, data, permissions, review, incident response, and nondelegable decisions. A profession needs standards for competence and responsibility. A society needs rights and boundaries around consequential automation.

These constitutions should not be copies of political constitutions. The analogy is functional. When capability becomes abundant and action becomes cheap, we need durable rules for who may do what with whose intelligence.

The preceding chapters can be condensed into a set of constitutional habits. They are not universal laws. They are tests to apply when designing systems where human and machine cognition mix.

First, allocate to consequence, not prestige.

Use the strongest, most expensive, or most senior intelligence where it changes outcomes, not where it looks reassuring. Routine tasks often belong to cheap systems. High-consequence ambiguity deserves depth. Expertise should be preserved for cases where expertise matters.

Second, price attention.

Every alert, report, recommendation, and generated artifact makes a claim on somebody’s finite life. Interruption should have thresholds. Low-value information should batch. Systems should measure whether alerts change action. Saved attention should have an owner rather than disappearing into more demand.

Third, route before solving.

The first intelligent act is often classification: what kind of problem is this, what clock governs it, what evidence is missing, and who should see it next? Cheap routing can create more value than expensive universal reasoning.

Fourth, match the clock.

Some decisions need speed. Some need patience. Some benefit from precommitment. Some need reversible containment that buys time. Latency is part of correctness.

Fifth, separate capability from authority.

A system’s ability to perform an action does not entitle it to perform the action. Authority should be scoped, revocable, and connected to consequence. High social or irreversible consequence deserves smaller autonomy budgets or stronger checks.

Sixth, make state visible.

Conversation is not enough for operational control. Users need to know what has happened, what is pending, which permissions are in use, and what can be undone. Advice and action should look different.

Seventh, verify proportionally and independently.

Do not ask for perfect proof where error is cheap and reversible. Do not rely on plausibility where error is catastrophic. Prefer narrow checks, deterministic constraints, independent methods, and outcome loops. A second copy of the same blind spot is not redundancy.

Eighth, preserve provenance.

Memory without source becomes folklore. Generated summaries should link to evidence. Decision records should preserve what was known at the time. Institutions should distinguish observation, interpretation, decision, and outcome.

Ninth, protect forgetting.

Total memory is not wisdom. Retain what serves safety, continuity, rights, and learning. Expire what would turn temporary mistakes into permanent identity. Give people control over personal cognitive histories where possible.

Tenth, keep routes for appeal and override.

Every classifier creates edge cases. Every summary hides something. Every model can be wrong. Affected people need routes to challenge consequential outcomes. Operators need credible authority to stop systems. Appeals are sensors, not annoyances.

Eleventh, maintain human reserve.

Automation removes practice. Reserve capability must be trained deliberately. Critical systems need humans who can understand, intervene, and operate in degraded modes. Apprenticeship must be redesigned rather than accidentally deleted.

Twelfth, preserve diversity against common-mode failure.

Shared models create economies of scale and correlated risk. Critical verification and infrastructure should include independent methods, staged updates, fallback, and heterogeneity where failure correlation matters.

Thirteenth, allocate public intelligence as infrastructure where markets underprovide it.

Translation, public information, education, science, emergency support, and government navigation may deserve civic provision. Public systems should be accountable, pluralistic, privacy-preserving, and connected to real institutions rather than offered as generic chatbots with official logos.

Fourteenth, follow the complements.

An answer without a bed, teacher, engineer, permit, treatment, network, or authority may not change reality. Do not optimize cognitive supply while ignoring the physical and institutional bottleneck downstream.

Fifteenth, preserve local knowledge.

Central models see widely. People at the edge see differently. Use AI to empower local judgment, not merely to make the center more omniscient. Allow abnormalities to travel upward and authority to travel downward where safe.

Sixteenth, allocate the productivity dividend explicitly.

AI can save time. Decide whether the gain becomes more output, lower cost, stronger verification, shorter hours, deeper service, or reserve. The default is that every saved minute will be consumed by the next demand.

Seventeenth, measure outcomes, not theater.

Prompt counts, agent adoption, generated pages, and automation percentages are weak proxies for value. Measure whether decisions improved, queues shrank, errors fell, people learned, customers succeeded, and resilience increased.

Eighteenth, design for correlated overload.

Fallback systems fail when every exception arrives at once. Size reserves for peaks. Create graceful degradation. Decide which services continue and which defer before the crisis.

Nineteenth, make identity and delegation legible.

People and systems should know when they are dealing with an agent, whom it represents, and what authority it carries. Authentication, signatures, and scoped credentials will become part of everyday cognition.

Twentieth, keep constitutional questions political.

Do not let convenience decide which public powers are delegated, which surveillance is acceptable, which rights can be scored, or which human encounters disappear. Some allocations require democratic legitimacy rather than product optimization.

These principles sound conservative in the literal sense: they conserve attention, agency, memory quality, human skill, and institutional capacity while allowing machine capability to expand. They are not arguments for slowing useful technology indiscriminately.

In many places the right design will be more automation.

Let agents reconcile routine records. Let them translate, search, schedule, test, monitor, simulate, draft, and classify. Let them act autonomously in well-bounded, reversible domains. Let strong systems handle work that exhausts people without requiring human dignity to be present.

Use the saved cognition for better things.

The test is whether automation increases the effective agency of the system’s participants or merely the throughput demanded from them.

This distinction will shape the politics of AI more than abstract arguments about machine consciousness in the near term. A worker cares whether the system gives her more control or monitors every pause. A patient cares whether the system helps the doctor see her sooner or blocks access behind an unappealable bot. A citizen cares whether government becomes easier to understand or more efficient at classification. A student cares whether the tutor helps him learn or quietly performs learning’s visible products in his place.

Allocation is where intelligence meets life.

The future will not be evenly intelligent. Some domains will become saturated with machine capability. Others will remain constrained by data, infrastructure, regulation, trust, or physical labor. Some people will carry agents that remember years of context. Others will encounter AI mainly as a system judging them. Some institutions will become nimble cognitive networks. Others will use the same technology to centralize control.

There will be no single “AI society.” There will be many allocation regimes.

That is reason for optimism as well as concern. Design is still open.

A technology that makes analysis cheap can widen expertise. It can help small organizations do work once reserved for large ones. It can lower language barriers. It can give people private tutoring, navigation through bureaucracy, and tools for creativity. It can let experts spend less time on routine work and more on difficult cases. It can make public systems more responsive.

None of these benefits arrives automatically.

The machine can generate intelligence. Institutions decide whether intelligence becomes capability, interruption, surveillance, advice, authority, or care.

This is why the dispatcher from the first chapter remains the right final image.

The dispatcher sits between a world of needs and a world of capabilities. The work is incomplete information, clocks, categories, escalation, and judgment. The dispatcher does not extinguish every fire. The intelligence lies in directing the right response toward the right place before the time for response is gone.

Civilization has always done this with minds.

Families decide who handles a crisis. Firms route questions through hierarchies. Professions certify expertise. Markets bid for labor. States create schools and research agencies. Libraries preserve memory. Courts allocate hearing. Networks allocate bandwidth. Every institution is partly a system for deciding whose cognition counts, where, and when.

Artificial intelligence enlarges the pool and changes its composition.

We can respond by chasing capability alone, adding more minds to every problem and trusting abundance to organize itself. That path will produce impressive local results and strange global failures: attention floods, verification bottlenecks, brittle centralization, dormant human skill, automated inequality, and systems too fast for their own controls.

Or we can treat allocation as a first-class design problem.

That path is less theatrical. It requires budgets, queues, standards, interfaces, rights, memory policy, training, procurement, public infrastructure, and the occasional deliberate pause. It looks like administration because administration is the technology through which collective intelligence becomes action.

The age of abundant intelligence will need better administration of mind.

That may be the most human task left to us, not because machines cannot help with it, but because allocation ultimately asks what we are trying to preserve and what we are willing to spend.

Intelligence answers questions.

A constitution decides which questions are allowed to become power.
