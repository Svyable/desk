There is a particular kind of mistake people make when a scarce thing becomes abundant. We imagine scarcity has ended. Usually it has only moved.

Cheap printing did not end the struggle over knowledge; it changed the struggle into literacy, distribution, attention, and trust. Cheap computation did not end the struggle over calculation; it moved value into software, interfaces, data, and decisions. If machine intelligence continues along the same path, the consequential question will not be whether we possess enough intelligence but how we allocate it.

Allocation sounds dull beside intelligence, which is one reason it matters. Civilizations are often transformed less by the existence of a capability than by the mundane arrangements that decide who may use it, what they may use it for, how quickly it reaches them, what happens when it fails, and who is permitted to overrule it. This book is about those arrangements.

Its argument is simple enough to state and difficult enough to live with: intelligence is not one resource. It arrives bundled with attention, time, authority, memory, verification, communication, and responsibility. Making one component abundant can make another scarce. A system that can generate a million recommendations per second can create a human verification crisis. A company that gives every employee an excellent adviser can produce more proposals than its managers can authorize. A government that can predict more risks can still fail because it cannot decide which risks deserve action. A hospital can possess superb models and remain short of nurses, operating rooms, blood, and minutes. The interesting unit, then, is not the answer alone. It is the path from a situation to a consequential act.

The chapters ahead visit places where that path has been engineered under pressure: emergency triage, NASA mission control, aircraft cockpits, Toyota production lines, financial markets, public weather services, classrooms, and accident investigations. None is a perfect analogy for artificial intelligence. Analogies become dangerous when they turn into costumes. These cases are useful for a narrower reason: they show what institutions learn when information, expertise, speed, and authority have to be coordinated rather than admired separately.

If machine cognition becomes cheap enough to be ordinary, that coordination will matter more than the spectacle of producing another capable mind. The harder institutional question is where to put it, what authority to give it, and what scarce thing its abundance makes newly expensive.
