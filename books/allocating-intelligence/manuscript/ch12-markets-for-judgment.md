# Markets for Judgment

A price is a compressed argument.

It may contain research, fear, rumor, private information, liquidity needs, imitation, incentives, and noise. The people behind it do not have to agree about why they are buying or selling. They do not even have to know what everyone else knows. They place different judgments into one mechanism and a number emerges.

That number is useful precisely because no committee had to produce it first.

Friedrich Hayek’s famous argument about dispersed knowledge was not that markets are wise in some mystical sense. It was that important knowledge is scattered through local circumstances no central planner fully possesses: inventories, preferences, skills, timing, shortages, plans, opportunities, and observations whose significance may be visible only to the person standing near them. A price can coordinate some of that knowledge without requiring anyone to assemble the whole picture in advance.

Prediction markets turn the cognitive ambition into a more explicit experiment. Instead of trading claims on wheat or copper, participants trade claims on whether an event will happen. A contract resolves according to a specified outcome. The price can then function as a collective forecast under suitable conditions.

The Iowa Electronic Markets became an important research setting for this idea. Beginning in the late 1980s, the University of Iowa operated small real-money experimental markets around elections and other events. Their value was never that they proved markets infallible. They showed that a market could serve as a disciplined way to aggregate beliefs, while leaving plenty of room for the usual complications: thin participation, question design, incentives, herding, noise, and the possibility that the crowd is simply wrong.

Those complications are the point.

Prediction markets belong in a book about intelligence allocation because they create a route for disagreement that does not require winning the meeting first.

Suppose a project team is convinced a launch will happen by June. One engineer thinks the integration schedule makes that impossible. In an ordinary organization, she may have to persuade her manager, then the program lead, then a steering committee. Social hierarchy and rhetorical skill enter the signal before the forecast is recorded.

A forecasting mechanism can let her state the disagreement directly. If the institution uses probabilities, she can record twenty percent while everyone else records eighty. If it uses a market, she can take the other side. The dissent becomes visible before anyone knows who is right.

That is a genuine cognitive service.

Now keep the hypothetical launch market running for several weeks.

At first the contract is simple: will the product be generally available by June 30? Engineering trades near sixty percent. Sales is more optimistic. The program office sits somewhere in between. The number moves a little with each planning update, but nothing dramatic happens.

Then a security engineer discovers that one dependency will require work no one scheduled.

The engineer does not control the launch plan and may not yet know the final delay. What she knows is narrower: the current schedule assumes a condition that is no longer true. In a meeting hierarchy, she may wait until the issue is fully characterized before escalating. In a forecasting system, she can move her estimate immediately.

The price falls.

That movement is useful even before leadership knows the whole story. It says that someone with enough conviction to act believes the shared plan has become less likely.

But what happens next determines whether the market remains a sensor or becomes theater.

Suppose the program lead sees the lower forecast and asks why. That is healthy. Suppose instead the lead announces that the market is “demoralizing the team” and begins calling the most pessimistic traders into meetings. The formal mechanism still exists, but the social cost of dissent has changed. Prices will become more optimistic without the underlying schedule becoming more achievable.

An internal market is still inside an organization.

The mechanism does not suspend hierarchy, career incentives, confidentiality, or fear.

Now suppose the company gives every employee an AI forecasting assistant. Participation rises. People who would never have built a probability estimate can ask the assistant to review project documents, compare the remaining work with historical schedules, and suggest a forecast.

The market becomes more liquid.

It may also become less independent.

If everyone’s assistant reads the same program summary, uses the same model, and interprets the same historical data in similar ways, the new liquidity can increase confidence in a shared representation rather than introduce new information. The security engineer’s local observation matters precisely because it was not yet in the common archive. Once she enters it, all of the assistants may update together.

The price jumps again.

From the outside, the market looks wonderfully responsive.

Inside, the cognitive ecology may contain one human observation followed by a thousand correlated machine updates.

This is why provenance matters even in a market designed to aggregate rather than explain. The institution does not need to publish every trader’s private reasoning. It does need enough structure to distinguish a broad independent shift from a common-source update. A forecast that moved because five departments independently encountered trouble is not the same object as a forecast that moved because one new memo entered the retrieval system everyone shares.

Both movements may be correct.

They imply different confidence about robustness.

Then the forecast falls below forty percent and management allocates more engineers to the launch. Two weeks later the probability rises. What should the institution conclude?

Part of the improvement may be genuine new evidence. Part may be caused by the forecast itself. The market did not merely predict the schedule; it helped redirect resources into the schedule.

That is not failure. It is exactly why organizations forecast.

But the record must preserve the intervention. Otherwise the market will later appear better calibrated than the causal system really was. A forecast that triggers corrective action can be “wrong” in the most useful possible way: it identifies a risk early enough that the institution prevents the predicted outcome.

This is a recurring problem in safety and management. The warning that works destroys some of the evidence that it was necessary.

A mature forecasting institution therefore evaluates more than closing prices. It asks what information entered, how independent it was, which interventions followed, and whether pessimistic signals remained socially safe to express after leaders started watching the market.

The cognitive value lies in the whole loop, not in the number alone.

Markets make one additional move that conversation does not: they can make confidence costly. In a meeting, a person can say “I am certain” at almost no price. In a market, acting on certainty requires exposure to loss. Money is not the only way to create this discipline. Forecasting tournaments use scoring rules and track records rather than financial stakes. What matters is that belief is recorded before the outcome and evaluated afterward.

This is why markets and forecasting institutions matter for AI.

Machine intelligence will make opinions cheap.

An executive can ask one model whether a product will ship, then ask five models, then fifty agents. Every department can have its own forecasting assistant. Every analyst can produce an instant scenario tree. The quantity of apparent judgment can rise much faster than the amount of genuinely independent evidence.

That distinction is easy to miss.

Imagine five agents all predicting an eighty-five percent chance of launch. The display looks like consensus. But suppose all five use the same model family, retrieve from the same project documents, inherit the same missing data, and were tuned against similar benchmarks. The five votes may be one opinion wearing five interfaces.

AI can make consensus cheaper than independence.

A market does not automatically solve that problem. If every trader uses the same model, the price can become a beautiful summary of a common blind spot. Diversity of participants is not the same as diversity of belief formation.

The ecology behind the forecast therefore matters.

Who supplied information? Which models were used? Which data sources overlap? Did participants form views independently before seeing the market? Are they operating on different time horizons? Does anyone have local knowledge that never entered the common archive? Are the apparent competitors all optimizing against the same benchmark?

A robust institution should care about these questions before admiring the precision of the final number.

This is one reason prediction markets are more interesting as mechanisms for surfacing disagreement than as machines for declaring truth.

A market price is a signal. A signal still needs interpretation.

Liquidity is an obvious example. A contract can display a precise price even when little activity supports it. A thin internal market may be dominated by a handful of enthusiastic participants. A single trade can move the number sharply. If the interface shows only “63 percent,” the compression hides the facts needed to judge the compression.

Participation, spread, volume, and recent movement may matter as much as the price itself.

Question wording matters even more.

“Will the project succeed?” is almost useless as a forecast unless success is defined. “Will the product reach 100,000 paid users by December 31 according to the billing database?” is resolvable, but the crispness narrows the question. The product could hit the number through discounting, churn immediately afterward, damage trust, or miss a more important strategic opportunity.

Forecastability creates jurisdiction.

The mechanism becomes powerful where the question is empirical and resolvable. It becomes weaker where the question is moral, political, interpretive, or dependent on values no probability can choose for us.

A prediction market may help estimate whether a congestion-pricing proposal will reduce traffic. It cannot decide whether the burdens are fairly distributed. It can estimate whether a military operation is likely to achieve a stated objective. It cannot decide whether the operation is legitimate. It can forecast revenue from a new medical product. It cannot determine what informed consent requires.

The market can inform the judgment without owning it.

This sounds obvious until a successful mechanism starts expanding beyond its cognitive jurisdiction. Markets solve some information problems, so market enthusiasts apply prices to rights. Expert committees solve some technical problems, so experts drift toward political authority. Voting protects equal standing, so votes are sometimes asked to settle questions that depend on specialized evidence.

Allocating intelligence means choosing the mechanism as well as choosing the mind.

The most useful market question may therefore be the one asked before trading begins: what kind of uncertainty is this?

If the uncertainty concerns an observable future event, forecasting can help. If it concerns what an institution should value, another process is required. If it concerns evidence that does not yet exist, science or investigation may be needed. If it concerns who has legitimate authority, governance enters.

No mechanism gets the throne.

This boundary becomes especially important when the people forecasting can also change the outcome.

Suppose an internal market asks whether a software team will ship by Friday. The engineers trading the contract are also the people who decide when the product counts as shipped. Their knowledge is valuable precisely because they are close to the work. Their control over the outcome also creates a conflict.

Prediction and action are entangled.

AI agents can make that entanglement tighter. The same system may forecast that a marketing campaign has an eighty percent chance of success, recommend the campaign, allocate budget to it, optimize it during execution, and later report the outcome. If the campaign succeeds, the original forecast appears accurate. But the prediction partly caused the event by directing resources toward it.

A useful institution should preserve the difference between what the system believed and what the system later made true.

That requires temporal memory. Record the forecast before the intervention. Record what resources were allocated because of it. Record the outcome separately. Otherwise calibration becomes self-congratulation.

Calibration is more subtle than ordinary performance measurement anyway.

If a forecaster assigns a twenty percent probability to an event and the event occurs, that one result does not prove the forecast was bad. Twenty-percent events happen. Performance emerges across many forecasts. An institution needs enough history to ask whether events assigned twenty percent happen roughly one time in five, whether ninety-percent events occur far more often, and where the forecaster is systematically overconfident.

This kind of scorekeeping is valuable for humans and models alike.

Current AI interfaces encourage one-shot impressions. The model answers. The answer sounds persuasive. The user moves on. Long-run forecasting records create a different relationship. A model that speaks elegantly but calibrates poorly in a domain should lose authority there. A less impressive system that repeatedly predicts well should earn more workload.

This begins to resemble a market for cognitive labor.

An organization can route a portion of tasks across several models, observe verified outcomes, and adjust allocation. Compute, latency, privacy, and verification burden become real costs. A routine classification may go to a cheap fast model. A difficult legal question may justify slower reasoning and retrieval. A high-consequence decision may justify two genuinely independent methods plus human review.

The organization does not need to announce one universally best model. Capability earns work where evidence supports it.

The market metaphor helps because it makes opportunity cost visible.

Every expensive reasoning call consumes something that could have been used elsewhere. Every verification step consumes time. Every specialist review enters a queue. If a task can be resolved reliably by a cheaper method, routing it to the most expensive model wastes scarce complements even when inference itself feels abundant.

But internal pricing creates its own distortions.

Once a resource threshold becomes visible, teams learn to present work in ways that qualify for it. An agent may learn that expressing uncertainty triggers access to a stronger model. Managers may cut verification because its value appears mainly in failures that did not happen. Benchmarks can become targets, and providers can optimize toward the benchmark rather than the real task.

The market-maker can become the hidden sovereign.

If every model competes on one evaluation, the evaluation determines what competition means. Apparent diversity can conceal a single objective function. This is the cognitive version of a market with many firms but one buyer writing all the specifications.

Healthy competition needs plural evidence.

The same principle governs the participants themselves. Critical forecasts should not all depend on one model provider, one data pipeline, one analyst culture, or one methodology. Independence looks inefficient because duplication costs money. The cost buys protection against common-mode failure.

Organizations accustomed to optimizing away duplication will have to learn where duplication is actually reserve capacity.

Markets also create a useful discipline before any trade occurs: they force vague claims toward resolution criteria.

A manager says a reorganization will improve collaboration. A market designer has to ask what observable event would count as improvement. A team says a launch is on track. A forecasting process has to ask for a date and a probability distribution. A strategy group says customers will love a feature. Someone has to specify the measure and the period.

This pressure is healthy because it exposes hidden assumptions.

It is dangerous when the measurable proxy quietly replaces the original purpose.

A contract resolving on paid users is not a contract resolving on durable customer value. A forecast about regulatory approval is not a forecast about social benefit. The crisp question is easier to trade precisely because it discards context.

The institution must keep the discarded context alive somewhere else.

This is where markets meet the interface of judgment. A naked probability can look more authoritative than the evidence warrants. Good displays should show what the contract actually resolves on, who participates, whether liquidity is thin, when the price moved, and which information changed recently. The user needs enough of the ecology to know whether the number deserves attention.

Sometimes the disagreement is more valuable than the consensus.

A wide spread can indicate uncertainty. A persistent minority position can reveal that one group holds information the rest of the organization lacks. A contract nobody wants to trade may be badly specified. A sudden move can surface news before the formal reporting chain absorbs it.

The market is not only a calculator. It is a sensor network with incentives attached.

Like any sensor network, it can fail in several ways. Sensors can share the same defect. Participants can herd. Thin markets can jump. Incentives can be gamed. Automated trading can move faster than human supervision. Financial markets learned long ago that price formation does not eliminate the need for market-access controls, testing, surveillance, circuit breakers, capital rules, and institutions capable of containing failure.

The same will be true of cognitive markets.

If agents bid for tasks, forecast events, or allocate compute, their interactions will need limits outside the strategies themselves. The market cannot be expected to design its own emergency brake while it is failing.

This does not weaken the case for markets. It clarifies it.

A market is valuable because it coordinates certain kinds of dispersed judgment without requiring prior consensus. It gives dissent a standardized route. It can reward calibration. It can update continuously as evidence arrives. It can shift cognitive resources toward systems with better track records.

Those are substantial powers.

They are not the same as authority to decide what matters.

An organization might therefore use several mechanisms in sequence. Independent forecasters record views before seeing one another. A market or scoring process aggregates empirical expectations. Experts investigate the highest-consequence disagreements. Leaders or public authorities deliberate about values, constraints, and legitimacy. Accountable decision makers choose. Outcomes later return to the forecasting record.

The sequence matters because prediction, deliberation, decision, and action are different cognitive jobs.

Collapse them together and the loudest mechanism colonizes the rest.

Keep them distinct and each can contribute what it does well.

AI will make this separation harder because one general system can appear competent at every stage. The same interface can forecast, argue, recommend, draft policy, and execute. Convenience invites consolidation.

Institutional design should resist that convenience where different stages benefit from different forms of independence.

A machine forecast should be allowed to be wrong without automatically becoming a policy failure. A decision maker should be allowed to reject the highest-probability option for legitimate reasons and record why. A market should be allowed to reveal an uncomfortable estimate without being asked to decide what the institution ought to do about it.

This is how disagreement remains useful instead of becoming insubordination.

The future will contain more forecasts than any human can inspect. Prices, scores, ranks, and model routers will compress them. The scarce resource will not be another opinion. It will be confidence that the opinions were formed differently enough, measured honestly enough, and placed inside the right jurisdiction.

A number is not knowledge merely because many minds contributed to it.

But a well-designed market can do something meetings often fail to do: let dispersed evidence become visible before hierarchy has finished smoothing it, make some forms of confidence costly, and preserve a route for the person or model willing to say the consensus is wrong.

That is enough to earn markets an important place in the cognitive portfolio.

Not the throne.
