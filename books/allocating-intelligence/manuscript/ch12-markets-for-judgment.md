# Markets for Judgment

A price is a compressed argument.

It can contain research, fear, rumor, liquidity needs, private information, imitation, regulation, incentives, and noise. The elegance of a market is that participants do not need to agree on why they trade. They place different beliefs into a common mechanism and a price emerges.

Prediction markets make the cognitive ambition explicit. Contracts pay according to future events, so the market price can be interpreted, under suitable conditions, as a collective forecast. The Iowa Electronic Markets, operating since the late 1980s as small real-money experimental markets, became an important research setting for studying whether markets can aggregate information about elections. Their record is interesting precisely because the answer is neither “markets are omniscient” nor “markets are casinos.” Design, liquidity, participant behavior, incentives, and the structure of the question matter.

Prediction markets belong in a book about intelligence allocation because they route attention through stakes.

A person who believes the consensus is wrong can act on that belief. The mechanism gives disagreement a channel. Accurate dissent can be rewarded. Unlike a meeting, the loudest voice does not automatically win. Unlike a survey, participants can revise continuously and have incentives to consider what others already know.

The market is a cognitive institution.

AI will change these institutions in at least three ways. It will lower the cost of analysis. It will create agents capable of trading or forecasting continuously. And it will make internal prediction markets possible at much larger scale because agents can help employees turn vague beliefs into explicit probabilities or contracts.

This could improve collective judgment. It could also produce a strange new form of correlated intelligence.

Suppose thousands of traders use the same model to interpret news. The market appears to contain many participants, but their analyses share a cognitive source. A model error can become consensus rapidly. Diversity of accounts is not diversity of belief formation.

This is the same common-mode problem seen in verification.

Markets usually gain strength from heterogeneous information, time horizons, models, needs, and incentives. AI can increase diversity by giving small participants access to sophisticated tools. It can reduce diversity if one dominant system becomes everyone’s analyst.

The effect depends on distribution.

There is a second challenge: markets allocate intelligence toward what can be priced.

A prediction contract needs a resolution condition. Will a candidate win? Will a product ship by a date? Will revenue exceed a threshold? These questions force clarity, which is valuable. But many important questions resist clean resolution. Was the strategy wise given what was known? Did the policy improve dignity? Did the company preserve trust? Did the research direction create option value?

A market can sharpen some forms of uncertainty while ignoring others.

Organizations already suffer from this measurement bias. What fits a metric receives attention. AI can intensify it by making forecasts cheap. A company may create probabilities for hundreds of measurable outcomes and gradually treat the forecastable world as the important world.

The right response is not to reject markets but to understand their cognitive jurisdiction.

Prediction markets can be excellent for bounded empirical questions. Expert committees can be better where deliberation and shared interpretation matter. Democratic processes are necessary where preferences and legitimacy, not just factual prediction, are at stake. Scientific methods are needed where evidence must be generated rather than merely anticipated.

No single allocation mechanism deserves to govern every uncertainty.

This principle is easy to forget because successful mechanisms acquire philosophical fans. Markets solve information problems, so market logic expands into places where rights or care do not belong for sale. Expert systems solve technical problems, so expertise expands into political decisions. Democratic voting protects equality, so voting is used on questions where technical competence is decisive.

Allocating intelligence means choosing the mechanism as well as the mind.

Markets offer one particularly useful property: they make confidence costly. In a meeting, a person can say “I am certain” without consequence. In a market, acting on certainty requires a position that can lose. This does not eliminate bluffing or manipulation, but it connects belief to exposure.

Forecasting tournaments create a related discipline without necessarily using money. Participants assign probabilities, predictions resolve, and performance is scored over time. The practice separates people who sound convincing from people whose forecasts are well calibrated.

AI agents should face similar scorekeeping.

Current model interfaces encourage one-off impressions. A system gives an answer; the user judges whether it seems good. Long-term calibration is harder to see. An organization can instead maintain records: the agent predicted delivery by June with seventy percent confidence; what happened? The system forecast a thirty percent risk of churn; how often do such customers leave? Which model performs better on which domain?

This turns model selection into a market-like allocation based on track record.

Models can compete for tasks. A router sends a portion of cases to several systems, observes verified outcomes, and adjusts allocation. The mechanism resembles portfolio management or online learning. No single model is declared universally best. Capability earns workload where evidence supports it.

This could become an internal economy of cognition.

The metaphor has limits. Models do not need wages. But compute, latency, and verification are real costs. An organization might attach shadow prices to them so a task router can choose among options. A routine classification gets a cheap fast model. A difficult legal question earns expensive reasoning and retrieval. A high-stakes decision receives independent models and human review.

Pricing can make opportunity cost visible.

The danger is Goodhart’s law: once the internal price becomes a target, behavior shifts around it. Teams may structure tasks to qualify for expensive resources. Agents may learn to present uncertainty in ways that trigger stronger models. Managers may cut verification because its value appears only when something goes wrong.

Cognitive markets need governance like financial markets do.

Financial history is useful here because speed and automation did not remove the need for rules. The Flash Crash and Knight Capital incident showed that automated markets can propagate effects quickly. Circuit breakers, market-access controls, testing, surveillance, and capital requirements are forms of institutional intelligence around the intelligence of traders.

A market is not self-governing merely because prices aggregate information.

The same will be true of markets for AI services. As organizations buy reasoning from multiple providers, prices may reflect compute cost, latency, privacy, reliability, and scarcity. We can imagine spot markets for inference or specialist agents bidding on tasks. The market could improve allocation. It could also create perverse incentives to overstate capability, conceal failure, or race toward risky speed.

Standards, audits, warranties, liability, and reputation would become cognitive market infrastructure.

There is a deeper distributional question. Markets allocate by ability and willingness to pay. That is efficient for many goods and morally unacceptable for others. If high-quality intelligence becomes essential to education, legal defense, health navigation, or civic participation, pure market allocation can reproduce inequality even when the underlying technology is cheap.

The price of inference may approach zero while the price of trustworthy service remains high because trustworthy service includes context, verification, privacy, and accountable humans.

This is how abundance can coexist with exclusion.

A person may have free access to a strong general model and lack the specialized legal data, professional representation, or procedural standing that makes legal intelligence consequential. A rural clinic may have excellent diagnostic advice and lack the specialist, equipment, or transport needed to act. A student may have a tutor and lack a quiet room or stable internet.

Markets reveal willingness to pay, not moral urgency.

Societies therefore combine market allocation with public provision, insurance, rights, subsidies, professional duties, and universal services. Intelligence will join this mixed economy.

One of the most interesting possibilities is public prediction infrastructure. Governments already produce forecasts—weather, budgets, demographic projections, disease surveillance, economic statistics. These are collective intelligence goods. AI can broaden the range and granularity of forecasts, but public systems need rules about uncertainty, transparency, political pressure, and access.

A government forecast can move behavior and markets. It cannot be treated as a casual model output.

Prediction also changes incentives. If an agency is rewarded for optimistic forecasts, calibration suffers. If a company punishes managers for reporting risk, internal probabilities become theater. Good forecasting institutions protect the ability to be wrong honestly.

This is why scorekeeping should distinguish process from outcome. A twenty-percent event happening does not prove the forecast was bad. Over many events, calibration matters. Organizations unfamiliar with probabilistic thinking often punish the messenger when low-probability risks materialize and reward overconfidence when luck cooperates.

AI can help teach probabilistic language if institutions allow it.

Markets are appealing partly because they tolerate disagreement. Two people can hold opposite views and trade. Many bureaucracies seek consensus before acting. Consensus can be valuable for commitment and destructive for forecasting because social pressure compresses uncertainty prematurely.

An intelligent institution may use different mechanisms in sequence. First gather independent forecasts. Then reveal aggregate information. Then deliberate about values and action. Finally assign an accountable decision maker. Mixing these stages can contaminate each one.

This sequence illustrates the broader thesis of the book. Intelligence is not a single act. It is a pipeline of noticing, predicting, interpreting, valuing, deciding, acting, and learning. Markets are excellent at some links and irrelevant or dangerous at others.

The future will contain more machine forecasts than any human can inspect. Prices, scores, and rankings will compress them. The question will be which compression mechanisms deserve trust.

A number is not knowledge merely because many minds contributed to it.

But a well-designed market, scoring rule, or competitive allocation system can do something meetings often fail to do: make disagreement visible, reward calibration, and let evidence shift resources over time.

That is enough to earn markets a place in the cognitive portfolio.

Not the throne.
