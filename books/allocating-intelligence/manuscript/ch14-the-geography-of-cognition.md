# The Geography of Cognition

Intelligence has an address.

The phrase sounds wrong because thought feels immaterial. A mathematical proof can travel across a page. A software program can be copied. A model appears in a browser from nowhere in particular. But every machine inference occurs on hardware in a place, drawing electricity from a grid, moving data through cables and radio links, occupying land, consuming cooling, depending on supply chains, and existing under a jurisdiction.

The cloud has always been a geographic system wearing an atmospheric name.

Artificial intelligence makes the geography harder to ignore because frontier computation concentrates enormous physical resources. Data centers cluster where power, fiber, land, cooling, skilled labor, tax policy, and market access align. Semiconductor manufacturing depends on highly specialized facilities and global supply chains. Network latency makes physical distance matter for some applications even when software abstractions try to erase it.

Allocating intelligence is therefore partly allocating infrastructure.

A region without reliable electricity cannot participate equally in compute abundance. A hospital with weak connectivity cannot depend on remote inference for time-sensitive care. A country reliant on foreign cloud infrastructure faces different sovereignty risks from one with domestic capacity. A rural school may have access to a global model in theory and a congested connection in practice.

The distribution of intelligence follows the distribution of its complements.

This is not new. Libraries were geographic. Universities concentrated scholars. Telegraph lines changed which cities could receive information quickly. Telephone networks expanded social reach. Radio changed distance again. The internet lowered many geographic barriers while creating new centers around cables, exchanges, data centers, and firms.

AI continues the pattern rather than abolishing it.

One important geography is latency. Some intelligence can be remote. A person asking for a book recommendation can tolerate a network round trip. A machine controlling an industrial process may not. Local inference becomes valuable where milliseconds matter, networks are unreliable, or privacy argues against moving data.

This creates a layered geography: edge systems for immediate or sensitive cognition, regional systems for heavier work, centralized frontier systems for the most expensive reasoning or training.

The architecture resembles transportation hubs. Not every trip goes through the national capital.

A second geography is data. Some knowledge is global and some is stubbornly local. Weather models need local observations. Medical systems need population context and local clinical practice. Agricultural systems need soil, climate, crop, and market conditions. Legal systems need jurisdiction. Language systems need dialect and cultural usage.

A globally powerful model can still be locally ignorant.

This is why local data institutions matter. Communities need ways to produce, govern, and correct the data through which AI understands them. Otherwise the places richest in machine-readable records become the places machines understand best, and the visibility gap compounds.

The problem resembles mapmaking. A blank area on a map may be experienced as empty by the map reader and full of life by the people who live there. Data absence can become policy absence when systems allocate resources based on what they can measure.

Cognitive geography therefore includes measurement capacity.

A city with extensive sensors and administrative data can optimize services more precisely than a city without them. That advantage can improve welfare and increase surveillance. The governance of sensing becomes part of intelligence allocation.

Who gets observed? Who controls the records? Which neighborhoods become legible to government? Which forms of informal life disappear because they do not fit categories? Geography and power meet inside datasets.

A third geography is energy. AI is not literally made of electricity, but large-scale computation depends on power availability and grid capacity. As data-center demand grows, siting decisions become energy policy. Communities may compete for investment while confronting water use, transmission needs, land use, and electricity prices.

This physicality should change how we talk about marginal intelligence.

A token generated somewhere has a material cost, even if the user sees a zero price. Efficient models can reduce energy per task while total demand rises as use expands. The Jevons-like dynamic discussed earlier reappears physically: cheaper inference can create more inference.

Allocation policy may eventually distinguish tasks by energy intensity as well as monetary cost.

A trivial query does not deserve the same computational path as a difficult scientific problem if a smaller model suffices. Routing to efficient models is therefore environmental policy at scale.

This need not become moral accounting for every prompt. Infrastructure operators already optimize workload placement, hardware utilization, and energy cost. The broader point is that “use the strongest model for everything” is physically inefficient as well as cognitively lazy.

A fourth geography is jurisdiction.

Data crossing a border can enter a different legal environment. Models hosted elsewhere may be subject to foreign law, sanctions, export controls, surveillance obligations, or commercial policy. A multinational organization cannot assume that one cognitive architecture fits every country.

This will encourage sovereign and regional AI infrastructure.

The phrase “AI sovereignty” can cover many agendas, from reasonable continuity planning to protectionism. The practical core is legitimate: societies may want control over critical cognitive infrastructure the way they care about energy, communications, food, finance, and defense.

Control does not require self-sufficiency in every component. No modern economy is independent of all supply chains. It requires knowing which dependencies can be tolerated, diversified, substituted, or stockpiled.

For AI, these dependencies include chips, cloud providers, model weights, training data, software frameworks, connectivity, and skilled people.

A fifth geography is talent. Intelligence clusters around institutions that attract people. AI can distribute expertise remotely, but people still build trust, companies, laboratories, and communities in places. Knowledge spillovers often occur through movement, conversation, and labor markets.

Remote machine expertise may reduce some advantages of proximity and increase others. If routine advice becomes universally available, the value of being near rare equipment, capital, decision makers, research communities, or customers may grow.

Abundance changes the reason for clustering rather than ending clusters.

This matters for regional policy. A town that imagines AI will make location irrelevant may underinvest in infrastructure and institutions. A city that assumes all AI value belongs in a few technology centers may miss opportunities to combine general models with local industries.

The most valuable applications may be geographically specific: agriculture, manufacturing, logistics, energy, health systems, local government, and education.

AI becomes consequential where it meets a real production system.

That meeting requires people who understand both the model and the place.

A sixth geography is language. Major languages receive more data, tools, benchmarks, and commercial attention. Smaller languages can be technically supported and still receive worse quality, weaker moderation, and fewer domain resources.

Language access is not a cosmetic localization issue. It shapes who can use intelligence without translating themselves into someone else’s conceptual world.

Public investment may be especially valuable here because market size alone can underfund smaller languages. Community partnerships are necessary because data extraction without cultural authority can reproduce old forms of exploitation.

A model that speaks a language is not necessarily part of the language community.

Cognitive infrastructure should support communities’ ability to correct, extend, and govern language resources. This turns localization from product adaptation into institutional capacity.

The geography of cognition also affects resilience.

Concentration is efficient until the concentrated resource fails. A small number of cloud regions, chip suppliers, model providers, or network routes can become common dependencies across industries. A localized outage can produce geographically distant cognitive failures.

Redundancy costs money because duplication always looks inefficient before the incident.

Critical systems should know how to degrade. A hospital can run a smaller local model if the network disappears. A logistics company can preserve basic routing. A government can access essential records without one vendor. A person can export the memory of a personal agent.

Graceful degradation is geographic intelligence.

It recognizes that connectivity is a condition, not a law of nature.

The final geography is political. Communities that host infrastructure may not capture its benefits. Data centers can consume local resources while serving distant users. Workers may produce training data while model ownership sits elsewhere. Countries may export raw data and import expensive intelligence services.

These are familiar patterns from resource economies.

The analogy is imperfect because data and models are copyable, but bargaining asymmetry is real. Regions need strategies for retaining value: skills, local firms, public data infrastructure, research institutions, energy agreements, procurement policy, and rights over sensitive data.

Otherwise intelligence abundance can recreate a center-periphery economy in cognitive form.

The right response is not autarky. Knowledge benefits from circulation. Science thrives on exchange. Models improve through diverse use. The objective is resilient interdependence rather than dependence disguised as frictionlessness.

Once intelligence has an address, allocation becomes concrete.

Where should compute be built? Which tasks run locally? Which data may move? Who pays for grid upgrades? Which languages receive support? What public services need offline capability? Which dependencies are acceptable? Who benefits from the infrastructure a community hosts?

These questions sound like industrial policy because they are.

The age of artificial intelligence is also an age of substations, fiber routes, cooling systems, fabs, warehouses, permits, and technicians.

The mind in the machine still lives somewhere.

The geography also begins at the device in a person’s hand.

National statistics about internet access can hide the difference between a household with fiber, several modern computers, and reliable backup connectivity and a household sharing one aging phone across a limited data plan. Both may count as connected. Their practical access to persistent agents, multimodal tools, large file uploads, private local models, and long study sessions is not the same.

This is why the old digital divide survives inside apparently universal connectivity.

The relevant questions become more granular. Is there a device suitable for the task? Is connectivity affordable enough for sustained use rather than occasional messaging? Is the interface accessible to a person with a disability? Does the service work in the local language? Is latency low enough? Can the person authenticate? Does the household have privacy for sensitive use? Does the school or workplace block the needed tools?

Effective cognitive access is a chain, and the weakest link can dominate.

A public policy that distributes model access without repairing those complements may produce impressive usage counts and modest gains. The people already well equipped to orchestrate technology will capture more value. The places with weak complements will receive an interface to abundance and continue to experience scarcity.

This is where geography becomes a theory of complements rather than merely a map of data centers.

A rural clinic might benefit from world-class diagnostic support, yet a correct recommendation is useless if the nearest specialist is hours away, the required imaging equipment is unavailable, or transport cannot be arranged. A farmer can receive precise agronomic advice and still face seed, credit, water, insurance, or market constraints. A student can receive a perfect explanation and still lack time because of work or caregiving.

Intelligence often increases the value of whatever remains scarce.

This is economically familiar. When one input becomes cheaper, complementary inputs can become more valuable. AI may reduce the price of analysis while raising the return to trusted relationships, local execution capacity, physical infrastructure, regulatory permission, and skilled maintenance.

Places rich in those complements can compound advantage.

That suggests a regional strategy different from competing only for giant data centers. A region may gain more by improving the interfaces between general AI and its existing industries: machine shops, hospitals, farms, ports, construction firms, energy systems, schools, or laboratories. The local advantage is not owning the smartest model. It is having enough tacit knowledge and institutional capacity to make the model consequential.

This is one reason place-based expertise will survive abstraction.

A general model can know thousands of pages about welding and still not know why one particular shop’s fixture vibrates after lunch. It can understand agricultural science and still need field observations. It can explain building codes and still need a local inspector to know which assumptions survive the actual site.

The geographic edge is often the last meter between description and reality.

Local models and sensors can help bridge that meter. An industrial system can combine general reasoning with measurements from its own equipment. A city system can combine broad planning models with local traffic, weather, maintenance, and service data. A school can combine general tutoring with the curriculum and learning context actually used there.

The key is not merely local data residency. It is local feedback.

A system improves when consequences return. Did the repair work? Did the student learn? Did the crop improve? Did the bus arrive more reliably? Local institutions that can measure outcomes and revise practice will gain more from the same base model than institutions that merely consume answers.

This is why cognitive geography is partly institutional density.

Some places contain thick networks of universities, hospitals, suppliers, financiers, regulators, skilled trades, and experienced managers. Others have fewer nodes. AI can connect a thin region to distant expertise, which is valuable, but connection does not instantly create the local network that turns advice into coordinated action.

Policy should therefore resist two fantasies at once: that geography no longer matters, and that every region needs to reproduce Silicon Valley.

Different places can build different cognitive specializations.

A port city may become unusually good at logistics intelligence. An agricultural region can build crop and water expertise. A manufacturing corridor can combine robotics, quality control, and supplier knowledge. A multilingual city can become a center for translation and cross-border services. The general model is shared; the local system of complements becomes the differentiator.

There is a final temporal geography too.

Places live on different upgrade clocks. A frontier model can change every few months while a hospital device, grid component, school procurement cycle, public contract, or factory machine may remain in service for years or decades. The newest cognitive layer must coexist with older physical systems.

This creates translation work. An agent may reason with state-of-the-art software and act through a decades-old database. A technician may receive a sophisticated diagnosis and repair a machine whose manuals exist only as scanned PDFs. A public agency may deploy a modern assistant on top of rules encoded in mainframe-era systems.

The geography of cognition is therefore also the geography of legacy.

Rich organizations do not necessarily have less legacy; they may have more accumulated infrastructure. What matters is whether they can connect layers safely. Adapters, APIs, documentation, migration plans, and people who understand old systems become part of the intelligence stack.

The dream of placeless cognition tends to erase all of this.

Real intelligence arrives through a location, a device, a grid, a network, a language, a legal regime, an institution, a person, and a set of physical possibilities. The quality of the model matters enormously. So does every bridge between the model and the world.

The places that prosper will not necessarily be those closest to the largest machines.

They will be those that learn how to turn remote cognition into local capability.
