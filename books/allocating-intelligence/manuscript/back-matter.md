# Back Matter

## About the Author

Sven Hardy Benson writes about institutions at the point where engineering, economics, technology, and public life collide.

## Sources and Method

The research notes in this book's `research/` directory are part of the publication record. They distinguish sources used for concrete historical or institutional claims from the author's synthesis and include falsification notes for claims that would materially weaken the argument if contrary evidence emerged.

The book is written around a practical proposition: abundant cognition does not abolish allocation. It makes allocation more important. The useful unit of design is therefore not the isolated model or person but the full decision system—who notices, who thinks, who checks, who may act, who remembers, and who bears the consequence.

## A Field Note for Applying the Book

There is a simple way to apply that proposition without converting the book into another governance checklist.

Take one real decision.

Not the company’s “AI strategy.” Not a model card. Not a diagram of the future organization. Pick something that happened recently enough that people remember the friction: a customer was refunded, a patient was escalated, a server was restarted, an invoice was rejected, a student was flagged, a contract was approved, a shipment was rerouted, a candidate was screened out, a machine was taken offline.

Then reconstruct the path.

Who noticed the situation first? What information did they actually have at that moment? Which part of the problem was uncertain? What happened next because of that uncertainty? Did somebody ask a person, query a database, call a model, consult a rule, wait for a meeting, escalate to a manager, or simply act?

This reconstruction usually reveals more than a capability inventory.

An organization may discover that its strongest model sits near the beginning of the process while the consequential bottleneck is a manager who approves only twice a day. A team may learn that expensive reasoning is being spent on cases a simple deterministic check could resolve. A public office may find that it can explain eligibility beautifully while citizens still cannot correct the underlying record. A software group may have excellent automated review and no practiced way to operate when the shared identity provider fails.

The point is not to find an embarrassing inefficiency. The point is to see where intelligence becomes action and where it stops.

Follow the clock next.

Some parts of the decision become more expensive with delay. Others become more dangerous when rushed. Mark the moments where another minute changes the consequence and the moments where another minute improves the evidence. If those moments are different, the system may need a fast reversible action followed by slower judgment rather than one universal response time.

Then look at authority.

Ask what each participant could do, not merely what each participant knew. A model may have been capable of recommending an action but unable to execute it. A junior worker may have seen the problem but lacked stop authority. A manager may have held formal authority but lacked the local context to use it well. A customer may have possessed the missing fact but no route through which that fact could alter the decision.

These are allocation failures even when everybody involved is intelligent.

Now inspect verification.

What counted as a check? Was it independent or merely repeated? Did the checker see different evidence? Was there a hard constraint the acting system could not waive? Did somebody compare the result with the world afterward? Could the process detect a case that had been routed incorrectly at the beginning, or did it only measure whether each later step followed procedure?

Outcome feedback matters because routing errors often leave no obvious artifact. A bad answer can be inspected. The expert who was never called is harder to see. The case that remained low priority until it became a crisis may look, in the logs, like a sequence of ordinary events.

Then inspect memory.

What did the system remember from the last similar case? What should it have remembered and did not? Which old fact was carried forward after it stopped being useful? Could a correction reach the summaries and downstream tools that had already copied the error? Was the provenance of a remembered claim visible, or had a previous interpretation hardened into fact?

Memory changes the cost of future intelligence. Good memory compounds learning. Bad memory compounds mistakes.

At this point a second picture usually appears: the physical and institutional complements around the decision.

Perhaps the answer was correct and nothing happened because there was no bed, no inventory, no engineer, no appointment, no permit, no budget, no transportation, no person authorized to sign, or no language channel through which the affected person could respond. These constraints are easy to overlook in AI projects because the cognitive part is the part changing fastest.

Do not punish the model for a missing truck. Do not celebrate the model when the truck was the real reason the system worked.

Finally, ask who experienced more agency after the system was introduced.

Did the worker gain leverage or merely a higher quota? Did the citizen gain understanding or merely encounter a faster classifier? Did the expert gain more time for difficult cases or more alerts? Did the customer gain a quicker route to resolution or lose the ability to reach a person? Did managers receive better judgment or simply more proposals demanding attention?

The answer may differ across participants. That difference is often the political substance of the deployment.

A useful review ends with changes small enough to test.

Move an escalation threshold. Add a receipt. Separate recommendation from execution. Give one role stop authority. Replace a second model review with a deterministic check. Preserve a source link in memory. Delete an alert that never changes action. Add a fallback that can absorb a surge rather than an ordinary failure rate. Give affected people a route to correct data before it travels further. Let a reversible action happen automatically and reserve human attention for the irreversible one.

Then watch what changes downstream.

The book’s argument does not require every institution to become cautious, slow, or committee-driven. In many systems the right allocation will be more automation, more delegation, and less human interruption. The claim is narrower: capability should not be confused with a complete design. Intelligence has to be routed through clocks, permissions, memory, verification, physical capacity, and legitimate authority before it becomes useful action.

That is why the same question can be asked of a person, a model, an agent, a team, a market, or a government office.

What should receive the next unit of intelligence?

The answer is never only about intelligence.
