# Chapter Briefs and Falsification Notes

These are research and argument briefs, not prose outlines. Each chapter should keep its narrative shape and only add evidence that changes the reader's understanding of the allocation problem.

## Chapter 1 — The Dispatcher

**Job:** Establish allocation as the governing problem through people who already sit between unlimited need and limited response capacity.

**Evidence to seek:** 911 call-taking and dispatch roles; NASA mission-control organization; emergency triage systems; examples where routing quality mattered more than producing another diagnosis or analysis.

**Counterevidence:** Some systems are bottlenecked primarily by raw expertise or compute, not routing. Do not imply dispatch is universally the highest-value cognitive task.

**Falsification note:** The chapter fails if it treats a dispatcher as a neutral switchboard. Dispatch criteria encode priorities, jurisdiction, and acceptable delay.

## Chapter 2 — Intelligence Is a Budget

**Job:** Make cognition legible as something organizations spend through people, software, meetings, queues, and review.

**Evidence to seek:** Bounded-rationality literature; empirical AI productivity work; cases where lowering the cost of one cognitive task increases demand for adjacent review or action.

**Counterevidence:** AI can create genuine net cognitive surplus rather than simply moving bottlenecks. Preserve that possibility.

**Falsification note:** Do not turn a metaphorical budget into fake accounting precision. The claim is about opportunity cost and constrained capacity, not a universal unit of intelligence.

## Chapter 3 — The Price of Attention

**Job:** Show that cheap generation can make human attention more valuable by increasing the volume of plausible things worth inspecting.

**Evidence to seek:** Simon's information-rich-world argument; cognitive-load and selective-attention research; alerting and workload guidance from safety-critical domains.

**Counterevidence:** Good automation can reduce interruption and cognitive load substantially. The chapter should distinguish generation from filtering.

**Falsification note:** The chapter should not imply human attention is fixed in every context. Training, interface design, batching, and organizational structure alter effective attention capacity.

## Chapter 4 — Triage

**Job:** Develop triage as a dynamic allocation practice based on urgency, likely benefit, uncertainty, and resource conditions.

**Evidence to seek:** WHO routine and mass-casualty triage; reassessment requirements; Toyota abnormality signaling as a nonmedical analogue.

**Counterevidence:** Triage can systematically misclassify unusual or under-observed cases and can reproduce historical inequality.

**Falsification note:** Avoid reducing triage to a three-variable formula. Real protocols contain clinical judgment, changing resource conditions, and explicit exceptions.

## Chapter 5 — Matching Minds to Problems

**Job:** Show that intelligence is heterogeneous. The value of a mind depends on task, context, tools, and the surrounding team.

**Evidence to seek:** NASA flight-controller specialization; empirical evidence that AI assistance has heterogeneous effects by worker experience; organizational information-processing research.

**Counterevidence:** General-purpose systems may outperform specialization on a widening range of tasks. Matching still matters if cost, reliability, latency, and authority differ.

**Falsification note:** Do not romanticize human expertise. Measure whether expert intervention changes outcomes rather than assuming seniority equals value.

## Chapter 6 — The Cost of Waiting

**Job:** Treat latency as part of correctness. A late perfect answer can be operationally wrong.

**Evidence to seek:** Knight Capital's 45-minute failure window; power-grid cascading failure; emergency care where delay changes outcome; precommitment and circuit-breaker mechanisms.

**Counterevidence:** Speed can magnify error. Some high-stakes decisions benefit from deliberate delay or independent review.

**Falsification note:** “Faster” is never the chapter's universal recommendation. The relevant question is which clock governs the consequence.

## Chapter 7 — Verification

**Job:** Explain why abundant generation increases the value of checks that are independent, narrow, and tied to consequence.

**Evidence to seek:** NASA IV&V; NIST AI risk management; software testing and safety assurance; examples of correlated verification failure.

**Counterevidence:** Redundant review can become expensive theater, and deterministic checks can miss semantic error.

**Falsification note:** Asking the same model to reconsider is not automatically independent verification. Record the source of independence.

## Chapter 8 — Memory Is an Institution

**Job:** Separate stored information from institutional memory. Useful memory requires provenance, retrieval, interpretation, and application.

**Evidence to seek:** NASA Lessons Learned Information System; NRC knowledge-management preservation after Three Mile Island; records-management practices.

**Counterevidence:** Memory systems can fossilize obsolete practice and spread a misleading lesson if context is stripped away.

**Falsification note:** A large archive is not evidence of learning. Look for mechanisms that cause stored lessons to alter training, checklists, policy, or design.

## Chapter 9 — Autonomy Budgets

**Job:** Make authority a separately allocated resource. Capability does not imply permission.

**Evidence to seek:** NIST and OECD governance language on oversight and lifecycle risk; aviation and industrial controls that limit automated action; examples of reversible versus irreversible operations.

**Counterevidence:** Requiring human approval can introduce delay, inconsistency, fatigue, and automation bias of its own.

**Falsification note:** Avoid “human in the loop” as a sufficient safeguard. Specify visibility, competence, response time, and stop authority.

## Chapter 10 — The Interface of Judgment

**Job:** Show that interface design determines which state becomes available to human judgment and which actions become salient.

**Evidence to seek:** Three Mile Island instrumentation lessons; FAA flight-deck human factors; research on mode awareness, alerts, workload, and automation status.

**Counterevidence:** More visible information can overwhelm rather than clarify. A good interface suppresses as well as displays.

**Falsification note:** Do not present Three Mile Island as an interface-only accident. Equipment, procedure, training, and operator actions all mattered.

## Chapter 11 — Organizations Are Cognitive Machines

**Job:** Treat org charts, meetings, queues, escalation paths, and decision rights as information-processing architecture.

**Evidence to seek:** Organizational information-processing and bounded-rationality literature; Toyota's stop-and-escalate practice; NASA mission operations.

**Counterevidence:** Informal networks often outperform formal structure, and organizational cognition cannot be reduced to message routing.

**Falsification note:** Preserve conflict, incentives, status, and power. Organizations do not merely process information; participants also have interests.

## Chapter 12 — Markets for Judgment

**Job:** Give price systems and prediction markets their strongest case as distributed mechanisms for aggregating information, then identify the boundaries.

**Evidence to seek:** Hayek on dispersed knowledge; prediction-market accuracy and interpretation literature; market microstructure and failure cases.

**Counterevidence:** Under useful conditions markets can outperform experts and polls; do not caricature them as gambling or irrationality.

**Falsification note:** A market price is not automatically an objective probability. Contract wording, liquidity, participation, incentives, and settlement matter.

## Chapter 13 — Public Intelligence

**Job:** Ask which cognitive capabilities should function as public infrastructure rather than premium private service.

**Evidence to seek:** GPS as freely available public infrastructure; public-sector AI guidance; public information, translation, navigation, and education cases.

**Counterevidence:** Public provision can be slow, politicized, brittle, or inferior to competitive private services.

**Falsification note:** “Public” should not mean one centralized official model. Preserve pluralism, contestability, privacy, and routes to independent information.

## Chapter 14 — The Geography of Cognition

**Job:** Show that useful intelligence still has a map because connectivity, devices, electricity, language, institutions, and physical services are unevenly distributed.

**Evidence to seek:** NTIA internet-use and device data; rural/low-income access gaps; infrastructure constraints that determine whether an answer can become action.

**Counterevidence:** Mobile networks and low-cost models can leapfrog older infrastructure and reduce geographic disadvantage quickly.

**Falsification note:** Do not conflate U.S. connectivity patterns with the world. Geography includes institutional and linguistic distance, not only broadband coverage.

## Chapter 15 — Education After Answers

**Job:** Reframe education around formation of judgment when answer production becomes cheap.

**Evidence to seek:** U.S. Department of Education and UNESCO guidance; research on tutoring and AI-assisted learning; apprenticeship and skill-formation evidence.

**Counterevidence:** Fast access to explanations can improve learning and broaden individualized tutoring, especially where teachers are scarce.

**Falsification note:** Do not define “real learning” as unaided performance. Tools have always been part of expertise; the question is which capabilities must remain internalized.

## Chapter 16 — Intelligence and Power

**Job:** Track who gains authority when AI allocates attention, work, opportunity, scrutiny, or explanation.

**Evidence to seek:** OECD work on algorithmic management and public-sector AI; appeal and transparency requirements; evidence on data asymmetry between institutions and individuals.

**Counterevidence:** AI can redistribute power downward by giving individuals analytic, legal, linguistic, and organizational capability once reserved for large institutions.

**Falsification note:** Avoid treating every asymmetry as abuse. Identify the actual decision right, information advantage, and available remedy.

## Chapter 17 — Failure Cascades

**Job:** Explain correlated and high-speed failure when many actions depend on shared systems, models, feeds, or assumptions.

**Evidence to seek:** 2003 North American blackout; Knight Capital; software deployment failures; common-mode risk and staged rollback practices.

**Counterevidence:** Standardization can also improve reliability, speed patching, and make failures easier to diagnose.

**Falsification note:** Distinguish a common component from a common failure mode. Shared infrastructure does not guarantee correlated failure.

## Chapter 18 — The Human Reserve

**Job:** Treat retained human competence as reserve capacity rather than nostalgia.

**Evidence to seek:** NTSB Flight 1549 investigation; aviation automation/human factors; training and simulator practices; examples of skill degradation after automation.

**Counterevidence:** Human manual operation can itself be dangerous, and machines may remain superior in degraded conditions.

**Falsification note:** Hero stories are not a staffing model. Identify the training, procedures, equipment, and institutions that made successful intervention possible.

## Chapter 19 — Institutions for Abundance

**Job:** Translate the book's observations into institutional design without pretending one governance framework fits all domains.

**Evidence to seek:** NIST AI RMF; OECD AI Principles and accountability work; high-reliability practices from aviation, nuclear safety, manufacturing, and software assurance.

**Counterevidence:** Governance can become compliance theater and freeze beneficial experimentation when burdens are detached from consequence.

**Falsification note:** Prefer outcome-linked controls to paperwork volume. A rule that produces documentation but no improved visibility, reversibility, or accountability is weak evidence of safety.

## Chapter 20 — A Constitution for Intelligence

**Job:** End with durable allocation principles: consequence, attention, clocks, authority, verification, provenance, appeal, reserve, diversity, complements, and public capacity.

**Evidence to seek:** The chapter should synthesize cases already earned in the book rather than introducing a fresh parade of examples.

**Counterevidence:** Some principles conflict. Transparency can consume privacy; diversity can reduce interoperability; human override can slow response; local discretion can reduce consistency.

**Falsification note:** The final principles should remain trade-offs, not commandments. The reader should leave with questions that improve design decisions, not a promise that the constitution removes uncertainty.
