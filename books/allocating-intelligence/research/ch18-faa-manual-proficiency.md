# Chapter 18 research note — automation and retained manual proficiency

Accessed 2026-09-06.

## Why this note exists

Chapter 18 argues that human competence can function as reserve capacity only if it remains practiced. The manuscript already uses aviation as its opening case and the source ledger points generally to FAA human-factors material, but the specific claim that automation can erode manual proficiency deserves a more direct operational source.

## Primary source

Federal Aviation Administration, Advisory Circular 120-123, *Flightpath Management* (November 21, 2022): https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/AC_120-123.pdf

The active FAA advisory circular gives operators and training centers guidance on flightpath-management policy, procedures, and training. Its scope explicitly includes manual flight operations, management of automated systems, pilot monitoring, and energy management. FAA says the guidance grew from extensive FAA and industry study of accidents and incidents in which ineffective flightpath management was causal or contributory.

The important boundary for Chapter 18 is institutional rather than heroic: maintaining manual capability is treated as part of operating a highly automated system, not as an argument for rejecting automation. The circular addresses both effective use of automation and manual-flight proficiency within the same flightpath-management framework.

A second current FAA source makes the skill-retention point more explicit. Advisory Circular 61-98E, *Currency Requirements and Guidance for the Flight Review and Instrument Proficiency Check* (October 30, 2024), warns that overreliance on automated flight systems can erode manual flying skills and recommends that pilots who fly with automation manually control the aircraft at times to maintain those skills. It also says pilots should be able to operate without aircraft automation and recommends simulated failures of critical automation components during flight reviews when safe and practical: https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/AC_61-98E.pdf

## What this supports

This supports the chapter's narrower claim that retained human capability may require deliberate practice when reliable automation reduces ordinary opportunities to exercise it. It also supports the chapter's emphasis on degraded-mode training rather than merely keeping a nominal human present.

## Strongest counterargument / disconfirming boundary

The same evidence does not establish that manual control is generally safer than automation. FAA guidance treats automation as an important safety capability and teaches pilots to manage it effectively; the problem is overreliance, misunderstanding, or loss of proficiency, not automation itself. Manual operation can introduce its own errors, workload, and inconsistency, and some degraded conditions may still be handled better by automated systems.

The transferable lesson for AI therefore should remain conditional. Do not infer that every automated workflow needs routine manual duplication or that humans should take over merely to preserve status. Preserve human reserve where consequence, failure modes, and recovery requirements justify it, and test whether the reserve can actually perform under degraded conditions.
