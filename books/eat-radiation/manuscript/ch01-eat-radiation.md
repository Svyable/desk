# 1. Eat Radiation

The sentence is irresistible because it is almost certainly wrong.

**There are fungi at Chernobyl that eat radiation.**

You can feel the headline assemble itself. Black mold crawls across the inside of a ruined nuclear reactor. It does not merely tolerate the radiation. It turns toward it. Feed it gamma rays and it grows. Put its pigment under a spectrometer and the pigment changes. Send a relative to orbit and the fungal lawn sits between a detector and space.

By the time the story reaches the internet, photosynthesis has acquired a gothic cousin.

Plants eat sunlight. Black fungi eat radiation.

Wonderful. Print it.

Except carbon is still carbon.

A fungus cannot build a hypha out of a gamma ray. It needs atoms. It needs nitrogen, phosphorus, sulfur, water, carbon skeletons. If ionizing radiation contributes useful energy to some part of its metabolism, that is extraordinary enough. We do not need to promote the fungus to a creature that breakfasts on reactor accidents.

The correction makes the story stranger, not smaller.

In 1997 and 1998, researchers surveyed fungal growth inside the Shelter enclosing the destroyed fourth reactor at Chernobyl. They found extensive colonization: thirty-seven species belonging to nineteen genera. The distribution was not random. In the more heavily contaminated locations, melanin-containing species dominated. *Cladosporium sphaerospermum* was among the commonly encountered organisms.

That result alone proves very little about energy capture. A black umbrella survives rain better than a white paper napkin. Nobody says the umbrella metabolizes weather.

Melanin is old biological equipment. Fungi use it in environments that have nothing to do with nuclear reactors. It can stiffen cell walls, alter permeability, quench reactive species, absorb electromagnetic energy and generally make life harder to kill. If melanized fungi are overrepresented where ionizing radiation kills their competitors, ordinary natural selection has already supplied an explanation.

Then the fungi did something less comfortable.

Researchers working with isolates from the Chernobyl region built experiments in which carbon-rich radioactive debris could not serve as the obvious attractant. They exposed fungal spores and growing hyphae to directional beta and gamma radiation. Some grew preferentially toward the radiation source.

Not all fungi. Not even close.

That detail matters enough to put in ink twice.

A later comparison of forty-seven species from radioactive substrates found positive radiotropism in only about nineteen percent. The response was strain-related and was not confined neatly to dark fungi. Any story in which "melanin makes fungi seek radiation" has already outrun the organisms.

But a directional response to ionizing radiation is still a directional response to ionizing radiation.

What is the fungus sensing?

That is the first good question.

The second came from a different laboratory tradition. In 2007, Ekaterina Dadachova, Arturo Casadevall and colleagues irradiated melanin and melanized fungi. They reported changes in electron-spin-resonance signatures consistent with altered electronic structure. Irradiated melanin showed an increased capacity to participate in an NADH redox reaction. Melanized *Cryptococcus neoformans* and *Wangiella dermatitidis* exposed to ionizing radiation grew more than comparison conditions by several measures; the paper also reported roughly threefold greater incorporation of carbon-14 acetate under the tested conditions. *Cladosporium sphaerospermum* showed enhanced growth under limited nutrients.

This is where the word *radiosynthesis* enters the room and begins stealing furniture.

The experiment did not show a fungus living on radiation alone. It did not identify a complete pathway analogous to photosynthesis. It did not demonstrate that a gamma photon became a particular ATP molecule and that the ATP paid for a particular unit of fungal biomass. It showed something simultaneously narrower and more provocative: ionizing radiation altered a biological pigment's measurable electronic behavior, and radiation exposure was associated with enhanced growth and carbon assimilation in melanized fungi under specific experimental conditions.

There is a vast scientific country between those two sentences.

This book is about that country.

## A pigment with several jobs

The problem begins with the word *melanin*, singular, as though nature ordered it from a catalog.

Melanins are chemically heterogeneous pigments assembled through different pathways. Their structures are notoriously difficult to specify with the satisfying neatness of a small molecule. They contain stable radical populations. They absorb broadly. Their physical organization matters. Their chemistry depends partly on how they were made and what surrounded them while they were made.

This ambiguity is not mystical. It is materials science performed by cells.

In subsequent work, Dadachova and colleagues showed that fungal melanization could protect cells against substantial ionizing-radiation doses. Chemical composition, radical-quenching capacity and spatial arrangement all mattered. That gives us one strong mechanism before we invoke energy harvesting: **melanin can make radiation less lethal**.

Protection changes ecology.

Imagine two organisms living on the same carbon source in a radioactive niche. One loses reproductive capacity faster. The other has a pigment that absorbs some radiation, dissipates energy and quenches reactive chemistry. Over generations, the pigmented organism occupies more of the niche. A field ecologist arrives and finds black fungi where radiation is highest.

Nothing in that sequence requires the black fungus to derive useful metabolic energy from radiation.

Now change one thing. Suppose irradiation also shifts the pigment's electron-transfer behavior in a way the cell can couple to redox metabolism. The pigment is no longer merely armor. It is armor with an electrical side effect.

Does the cell use the side effect?

That is the whole case.

Not whether melanin interacts with radiation. It does.

Not whether melanization can protect fungi. It can.

Not whether radiation can alter growth. Under some conditions, experiments say yes.

The hard question is whether a reproducible fraction of radiation-derived energy crosses the border from **physics happening to a pigment** into **work available to the organism**.

The border needs customs officers.

## Five claims pretending to be one

The popular version compresses five propositions:

1. an organism survives radiation;
2. an organism detects or responds directionally to radiation;
3. radiation changes its growth rate;
4. radiation contributes usable energy to metabolism;
5. radiation can function as a major ecological energy source.

Evidence for claim one is not evidence for claim five.

This sounds pedantic until you notice how often frontier science dies in precisely this compression. A sensor anomaly becomes an object. An object becomes a craft. A craft becomes a technology. A statistical deviation becomes perception. A molecule found in tissue becomes a neurotransmitter. A protective pigment becomes a solar panel.

The interesting phenomena survive decomposition. The weak ones depend on blur.

Radiation-responsive fungi survive decomposition remarkably well.

There really were melanized fungi inside the Chernobyl Shelter.

There really are experiments reporting fungal radiotropism.

There really are controlled studies in which ionizing radiation changes melanin's measured electronic/redox properties.

There really are growth experiments reporting a melanization-dependent component to radiation-associated proliferation.

There really was an experiment with *Cladosporium sphaerospermum* aboard the International Space Station. A later peer-reviewed report found that the organism grew in orbit and measured a small attenuation difference beneath the fungal biomass, while the authors were careful enough to discuss passive shielding as a major part of the engineering value.

What remains unsettled is the phrase everyone remembers.

Do they eat it?

## Chernobyl is a selection experiment nobody designed

Nuclear accidents are terrible experiments because the controls are awful and the stakes are real.

Chernobyl changed temperature, chemistry, human activity, water movement, nutrient availability, species interactions and radiation simultaneously. Reactor interiors add concrete, graphite, metals, dust, leaks and gradients that would make a laboratory biologist reach for a larger notebook. Finding a fungus near a radiation source cannot tell you why it is there.

But accidents can reveal possibility space.

A reactor creates combinations evolution has rarely encountered at that intensity. Organisms arrive with old machinery built for other problems. Pigments that evolved under ultraviolet light, oxidative stress, predation or desiccation suddenly confront gamma fields and radioactive particles. Most combinations fail. A few turn out to have useful cross-protection. Some responses may be accidents of chemistry. Some may become selectable traits.

Evolution does not care what a molecule was *for*.

This is why Chernobyl belongs in astrobiology without requiring any extraterrestrials.

Habitability models often begin with energy: sunlight at a surface, chemical disequilibria around vents, redox gradients in rock, hydrogen from water-rock reactions. Ionizing radiation is usually booked on the expense side. It breaks bonds. It damages DNA. It generates radicals. Shield against it.

That accounting is correct for most familiar biology.

But biology is a professional embezzler. It takes a waste stream and discovers a gradient. Oxygen was poison before it became respiratory fortune. Hydrogen sulfide is lethal to us and dinner to somebody else. Ultraviolet light damages DNA and also drives photochemistry. Electrical fields, magnetic fields, heat gradients, pressure gradients: if a persistent physical asymmetry exists long enough, the question is not whether life finds it pleasant. The question is whether evolution can couple anything useful to it.

Ionizing radiation is a persistent physical asymmetry in some places.

A radioactive mineral grain is not merely dangerous. It is also an energy source in the literal physical sense. The biological question is whether the energy arrives in a form a cell can exploit faster than the same radiation destroys the machinery doing the exploiting.

That ratio—not the adjective *radiotrophic*—is the astrobiology.

## The annoying possibility that nothing exotic is required

There is a version of this book in which the most dramatic hypothesis loses and the science gets better.

Radiation may stimulate growth through stress signaling. Low-dose exposure may produce hormetic responses. Melanin may protect cells and alter local redox chemistry without functioning as an energy-harvesting antenna. Directional hyphal growth may use damage gradients or secondary chemistry as cues. Organisms from contaminated sites may simply have been selected for unusually effective repair and stress response. Space-grown fungi may benefit from microgravity, culture geometry or other orbital conditions while their biomass passively attenuates a little radiation because wet carbon-rich matter attenuates radiation.

If that model explains everything, we should use it.

The book will not defend radiosynthesis from defeat. It will try to arrange the defeat properly.

A decisive experiment would not ask whether irradiated black fungus grows better than some vaguely comparable unirradiated fungus. It would use matched genetic backgrounds with and without melanization. It would measure nutrient and carbon budgets. It would vary photon energy and dose rate. It would separate generic stress signaling from pigment-specific electron transfer. It would quantify the energetic contribution required to explain any biomass difference. Ideally it would interrupt the proposed redox bridge and restore it independently.

Then the organism gets to answer.

## The black wall on Mars

There is an engineering temptation here too.

A radiation shield that grows is an excellent sentence.

Mass is expensive in spaceflight. Water, polyethylene and regolith are attractive shielding materials partly because hydrogen-rich matter is useful against important components of the space-radiation environment. Fungal biomass contains a great deal of water. Melanin has interesting attenuation and radical-handling properties. A living layer might repair itself. Feed it waste carbon and perhaps the wall becomes part shield, part recycler, part biological manufacturing system.

This does not require radiosynthesis either.

That distinction is liberating.

The engineering case for melanized biomass can succeed even if the strongest metabolic claim fails. Passive attenuation plus self-repair plus local manufacture could be valuable. A fungus does not need to eat cosmic rays to earn a ticket to Mars.

Conversely, a petri-dish attenuation result does not prove a practical habitat shield. Galactic cosmic radiation is a mixed high-energy field. Secondary particles matter. Geometry matters. Areal density matters. Water content matters. Maintenance matters. Containment matters. Fungi have inconvenient opinions about where they grow.

Mars is not Chernobyl with better branding.

## What the word is allowed to mean

I am going to keep *radiotrophic* because the literature uses it and because throwing away a word does not solve an epistemic problem.

But the word gets a leash.

Sometimes it will mean an organism associated with high-radiation environments. Sometimes it will refer to radiation-responsive growth. Sometimes authors use it for the stronger hypothesis that ionizing radiation can contribute metabolically useful energy. Those meanings are not interchangeable. Each time the book crosses from one to another, the evidence has to cross with it.

The same goes for *radiosynthesis*.

Photosynthesis is not "a pigment absorbs a photon and the organism later grows." It is a worked-out biochemical architecture connecting photon capture to electron transport, electrochemical gradients, ATP/NADPH production and carbon fixation. Calling fungal radiation responses analogous to photosynthesis is a hypothesis about architecture, not a compliment.

Show me the architecture.

Until then, I will happily settle for something weirder than the headline: a class of ancient pigments with semiconductor-like and radical chemistry; fungi that can flourish in places where ionizing radiation is an ecological force; some strains that orient growth toward radiation; experiments in which irradiation changes pigment chemistry and growth; and a live argument over whether biology has found a way to skim useful work from one of nature's most destructive energy streams.

That is enough.

More than enough, actually.

The reactor exploded in 1986. Decades later, one of its quieter scientific legacies may be that the border between **energy source** and **environmental hazard** was never where we drew it.

The fungus does not care what column the accountant used.
