# Wild Speculation

There is a particular kind of investor who becomes most confident when the world becomes least knowable.

A missile crosses a border. A carrier group changes course. An embassy closes. A pipeline burns. A cable is cut. A minister says the sentence diplomats use when they want to sound calm and make everyone else nervous. Futures open red. Oil jumps. Gold jumps. Defense stocks jump. Someone on television says the word *historic* before breakfast.

Then the investor opens a brokerage account and decides, with astonishing speed, that history has finally become easy.

Buy oil. Buy gold. Buy weapons. Short Europe. Sell Taiwan. Own dollars. Avoid dollars. Buy Bitcoin because states are losing control. Sell Bitcoin because the grid might go down. Buy farmland. Buy uranium. Buy canned food. Buy the company that makes the cans.

The story changes by the hour. The confidence does not.

This book is about that confidence.

The title is deliberately alarming. *Investing on the Brink of World War 3* sounds like a forecast because fear prefers a forecast. It wants somebody to say that the thing everyone dreads is coming, that the map can be drawn in advance, that there will be a winner, that the winning asset can be purchased before everyone else understands what is happening.

I cannot give you that forecast.

Nobody can give it to you honestly.

The phrase *World War 3* is itself a narrative shortcut. It compresses nuclear deterrence, proxy wars, cyber operations, maritime blockades, trade restrictions, sanctions, sabotage, conventional invasion, political coercion, industrial mobilization, terrorism, miscalculation, alliance commitments, and the possibility of de-escalation into one cinematic object. It suggests a clean beginning and a recognizable shape. Real crises usually arrive as a series of ambiguous thresholds, crossed unevenly and often denied while they are being crossed.

In 2026 the world has enough evidence of danger without inventing any. Global military expenditure reached a level that the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute described as the highest in its records. Nuclear-armed states continued modernizing arsenals. War in Ukraine had already demonstrated how a regional conflict could rearrange European energy, defense budgets, corporate investment, commodity routes, sanctions law, and the status of sovereign reserves. Fighting in the Middle East had again forced investors to think about the Strait of Hormuz not as a label on a map but as a narrow passage through which a material share of the world's oil and liquefied natural gas moves. Shipping disruptions had already taught companies that a vessel going around Africa instead of through the Red Sea is not a geopolitical abstraction. It is more days, more fuel, more insurance, more inventory, more working capital, and eventually a different price on a shelf.

Those are facts.

A prediction that they add up to a particular global war is something else.

Investors get into trouble when they confuse the two.

Markets make the confusion irresistible because they put a price beside the story. If a stock rises after an attack, it feels as if the market has confirmed the narrative. If gold rallies, fear seems validated. If oil falls after a dramatic escalation, the decline can look absurd until you remember that prices are not moral verdicts. They are arguments about future cash flows, future scarcity, future policy, positioning, liquidity, and what everybody had already expected five minutes earlier.

The same war can be bullish for one company, bearish for another, inflationary for one country, deflationary for another, politically stabilizing for one government, ruinous for the government next door, and nearly irrelevant to an investor whose liabilities are thirty years away and whose portfolio was diversified before the first alert hit the phone.

That last investor is less interesting on television.

The uninteresting investor is the person I want to study.

This is not a book about making money from apocalypse. It is a book about keeping uncertainty from turning you into a speculator at exactly the moment speculation feels like prudence.

There is a difference between preparing for geopolitical risk and wagering on a geopolitical script.

Preparation asks what can break. It asks where assets are held, which currencies liabilities are in, whether a company depends on one port, one supplier, one jurisdiction, one cloud provider, one commodity, one government contract, or one assumption about peace. It asks how much liquidity a household needs if markets are open but life is expensive, or if markets are closed but bills are not. It asks what inflation, recession, sanctions, capital controls, cyber disruption, mobilization, and reconstruction do to different balance sheets.

A script says China invades Taiwan in a particular year, therefore buy this. A script says the Strait of Hormuz closes, therefore oil goes to that price. A script says NATO enters a direct war with Russia, therefore the dollar does this and gold does that. A script feels rigorous because it contains nouns from the news and numbers from a spreadsheet. Its weakness is that every arrow depends on another arrow.

The invasion happens. The response is what you assumed. The sanctions work as expected. The central bank behaves as expected. The market was not already positioned. The government does not change the rules. The company you bought benefits from the event but is not already priced for it. Its suppliers keep delivering. Its margins survive. Its executives allocate the windfall intelligently. Taxes do not change. The exchange stays open. You stay calm.

By the time the chain reaches your brokerage statement, the confident geopolitical forecast has become a tower of conditional sentences.

History is unkind to towers like that.

When the First World War began, the New York Stock Exchange did not merely fall. It closed for more than four months. The event is useful because it violates the way modern investors imagine risk. We picture a market as a continuous scoreboard. Prices move, sometimes violently, but they move. In 1914 the mechanism itself disappeared from ordinary view. Investors still owned securities. Claims still existed. People still needed cash. Informal trading emerged. But the clean public price we treat as a fact of nature was unavailable.

That is a different category of risk.

So is discovering that a reserve asset can be immobilized by another sovereign. So is discovering that a stock you own trades in a country imposing capital controls. So is learning that the company with the beautiful margins depends on a component made in a place suddenly difficult to insure. So is holding the correct asset in the wrong account, at the wrong institution, in the wrong jurisdiction, with the wrong time horizon.

War does not simply move prices. It changes the plumbing around prices.

This book moves through that plumbing.

It begins with headlines because that is where most bad geopolitical trades begin. It goes back to 1914 because market closure is a useful antidote to the assumption that liquidity is permanent. It examines the war premium, oil, shipping chokepoints, sanctions, reserve currencies, gold, cash, bonds, defense companies, semiconductor concentration, cyber risk, household resilience, bunker fantasies, the ethics of profiting from conflict, inflation, recession, reconstruction, and finally the hardest investment position of all: admitting that you do not know.

There will be no model portfolio promising to survive World War 3.

A literal global nuclear exchange would make the precision of an asset-allocation table grotesque. In less extreme conflicts, the right portfolio depends on a person's country, job, tax system, age, liabilities, family obligations, time horizon, existing wealth, and capacity for loss. The Securities and Exchange Commission's basic guidance on diversification remains more useful than most apocalypse portfolios precisely because it is boring: spread risk, match risk to horizon and tolerance, rebalance rather than chase.

Boring principles survive because they do not require the world to follow a screenplay.

The book will still speculate. The word is in the title for a reason.

We will speculate about blockades, sanctions, energy shocks, broken payment networks, war finance, rearmament, industrial policy, reconstruction, and the possibility that the next crisis does not resemble the last one. But the speculation will be used as a stress test, not a prophecy. The question will not be, *What will happen?* It will be, *What would this expose if it did?*

That is a smaller question.

It is also one you can do something with.

This manuscript is for general informational and educational purposes. It is not individualized investment, legal, tax, or financial advice, and it does not recommend any security, commodity, currency, or strategy for any particular reader. War can destroy wealth, institutions, infrastructure, and lives on a scale that financial hedges cannot meaningfully offset. Any discussion of assets in crisis should remain subordinate to that reality.

The markets will keep putting numbers beside frightening events.

Our job is to remember that a number is not the same thing as knowledge.
