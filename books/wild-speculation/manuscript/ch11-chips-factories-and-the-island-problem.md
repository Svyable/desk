# Chips, Factories, and the Island Problem

A semiconductor is small enough to disappear under a fingernail and important enough to reorganize national budgets.

That combination helps explain why Taiwan appears so often in geopolitical investment stories. The island sits inside one of the most consequential industrial systems in the world. Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company became the leading manufacturer of advanced logic chips used by companies that design processors for phones, servers, artificial intelligence, communications, and other high-performance applications. The concentration is real enough that governments have spent extraordinary amounts trying to reduce it.

The investment story usually stops there.

Taiwan risk. Buy domestic fabs.

The industrial story is harder.

A modern semiconductor is not produced by moving one factory from one country to another. The factory itself is a system of extraordinary complexity, but it sits inside a larger system of design software, process equipment, specialty gases and chemicals, silicon wafers, masks, advanced packaging, testing, precision parts, clean-room operations, power, water, skilled engineers, technicians, suppliers, and customers whose own products are designed around particular manufacturing processes.

A fab is not an island even when the company operating it comes from one.

This is the first correction the semiconductor trade needs.

Geographic concentration can be reduced without being eliminated. The U.S. CHIPS program illustrates the scale of the attempt. In 2024 the Department of Commerce finalized an award of up to $6.6 billion in direct incentives for TSMC Arizona, supporting a planned investment of more than $65 billion in three leading-edge fabrication plants. Commerce also made a multibillion-dollar award to Intel for projects across several states. These were not ordinary factory tax breaks. They were industrial-policy responses to the belief that leading-edge semiconductor capacity had become an economic and national-security dependency.

The investment is enormous because the capability is enormous.

It is also slow.

A government can pass a law in months. A semiconductor ecosystem compounds over decades.

This does not mean the effort is futile. TSMC reported strong early production performance at its first Arizona facility, and the Commerce award tied disbursement to milestones. The point is that diversification is a process rather than a switch. A new fab can produce advanced chips in another country while still depending on equipment, materials, engineers, intellectual property, and customer relationships spread across the world.

Supply-chain sovereignty is usually partial.

That matters for investors because the cleanest geopolitical narrative often prices the least clean industrial reality. A domestic fab may reduce exposure to one location and increase exposure to construction costs, labor scarcity, power demand, water, permitting, and execution. Subsidies can improve the project’s economics and attach conditions. A company can be strategically favored and financially challenged at the same time.

National security is not a waiver from return on capital.

The semiconductor industry makes this unusually visible because the capital intensity is so high. A leading-edge fab costs billions before it produces saleable wafers. Equipment is ordered years ahead. Process technology changes while the building is under construction. Customers decide which products to tape out on which node. Yields improve through learning. A delayed ramp can erase years of optimistic spreadsheet assumptions.

The state wants capacity.

The shareholder wants profitable capacity.

Those are related but not identical objectives.

This is the same distinction that appears in defense production. A strategically essential industry can attract public money because the government values redundancy more than the private market would. That can be rational public policy even if the subsidized factory earns only ordinary returns. The social return includes resilience that the shareholder cannot capture.

Investors should not confuse the social return with their own.

The second correction is that “semiconductors” is too broad a category for geopolitical analysis. The chip inside a washing machine and the accelerator inside an artificial-intelligence server share a family name and live in different economic worlds. Leading-edge logic receives the political attention because it supports advanced computing. Mature-node chips remain critical to automobiles, industrial equipment, power systems, appliances, medical devices, and military platforms. Memory has different cycles. Analog chips have different economics. Power semiconductors solve different problems.

A blockade or export control would not affect each category equally.

Neither would domestic subsidies.

This is why the investor who buys a semiconductor index as a pure Taiwan hedge may own businesses whose risks point in several directions. Some design chips and outsource fabrication. Some manufacture. Some sell equipment. Some provide design software. Some sell memory. Some depend heavily on China as a customer. Some depend on Taiwan as a supplier. Some benefit from new fabs regardless of which manufacturer wins because every fab needs equipment.

The theme is a network of opposing exposures.

That can be useful diversification.

It is not a one-direction bet.

The third correction concerns the famous idea of a silicon shield: the notion that Taiwan’s importance to global technology helps deter conflict because disruption would impose catastrophic economic costs on everyone. There is an intuitive logic to this. Interdependence raises the cost of war.

It does not make war impossible.

Europe before 1914 was economically integrated enough to make a continental war costly. The cost arrived anyway. States do not always choose the economically optimal outcome. Leaders can miscalculate. National identity, security concerns, domestic politics, alliance commitments, and military perceptions can outweigh commercial logic.

A supply-chain concentration can be both a deterrent and a vulnerability.

Investors should be wary of treating either interpretation as a guarantee.

The island problem is therefore not “what happens to chip stocks if Taiwan is attacked?” The scenario is too large for that question to be useful by itself.

The better exercise is to trace the layers.

Which companies lose access to fabrication?

How much inventory do they have?

Which products can move to another process node or foundry?

How long does qualification take?

Which customers can redesign?

Which equipment companies lose service access to fabs in the affected region?

Which manufacturers elsewhere gain pricing power?

Can packaging and testing continue?

Do export controls expand?

Does shipping continue?

Does insurance?

Do engineers remain at the facilities?

Does power remain available?

A fab is only productive when the entire operating environment survives.

This is why military maps alone are inadequate. Semiconductor vulnerability includes electricity. A fabrication plant cannot simply coast through a prolonged grid failure. It includes water treatment. It includes chemical deliveries. It includes software updates and spare parts. It includes skilled maintenance. An industrial system can be interrupted without the building being physically destroyed.

Cyber risk and semiconductor risk overlap.

So do energy risk and semiconductor risk.

So does workforce risk.

The island is one node in a stack of dependencies.

That makes the semiconductor chapter a bridge between physical chokepoints and cyberwar. The most consequential disruption may not look like a conventional attack. It can be an export restriction on equipment, a cyber intrusion into a supplier, a power shortage, an earthquake, a port closure, or a political decision that prevents engineers from servicing installed tools.

Geopolitics is often experienced by companies as operations.

That is why corporate contingency planning matters more than television certainty. A firm that depends on advanced chips cannot meaningfully diversify by placing an order with a second broker if both brokers source the same fab. True redundancy may require redesigning products to accept different components, qualifying multiple suppliers, carrying more inventory, or accepting lower performance in an emergency configuration.

Resilience can be an engineering property.

Investors can look for evidence of it. Does management know its sub-tier suppliers? Has it qualified alternatives? Are supply agreements long-term? How much inventory is held? Are critical components sole-sourced? Can product architectures substitute? Does the company have contractual priority or merely a purchase order? Is the chip custom or commodity?

These questions are less glamorous than predicting a blockade.

They are also answerable.

The same discipline should be applied to the supposed beneficiaries. A foundry outside Taiwan may appear to receive a geopolitical windfall. Can it actually produce the same node? At what yield? With what capacity? Is the customer willing to redesign? Does the fab have the same equipment? How long does the move take? A company can be “the alternative” in a strategy deck and irrelevant to the next two years of production.

Time-to-substitute returns again.

The semiconductor industry is unforgiving because technology moves while substitution occurs. A factory built to reduce dependence on today’s leading process may open into tomorrow’s competitive landscape. That does not make the investment strategically pointless. It changes the shareholder calculation.

Industrial policy is buying an option on future capacity.

Options have premiums.

Taxpayers can rationally pay a premium for resilience that investors would not pay on expected cash flow alone. The public debate becomes confused when every subsidized project is expected to prove both that it was necessary for national security and that it was the highest-return commercial use of capital. Those are separate tests.

The private investor can respect the first and still evaluate the second.

This matters because geopolitical themes create valuation halos. A company becomes strategically important and investors start treating strategic importance as permanent pricing power. Yet governments can demand domestic investment, restrict exports, attach guardrails to subsidies, or pressure companies to serve national priorities. Strategic importance can increase regulation as easily as it increases revenue.

The semiconductor industry is already deeply shaped by export controls. Restrictions on advanced chips and manufacturing equipment are intended to limit access to capabilities with military and intelligence applications. The commercial effect is not simply lost sales. Controls can change product design, customer strategy, manufacturing location, research priorities, and the incentives of other countries to develop substitutes.

Policy becomes part of the technology roadmap.

This adds an unusual risk to valuation. A company can create a technically superior product and discover that it cannot sell the product to a major customer. Another can receive domestic subsidies and face limits on expansion elsewhere. A third can benefit from restrictions on a competitor and then face reciprocal measures.

The addressable market is partly diplomatic.

Investors should treat this as a reason for wider valuation ranges, not more confident point estimates.

The same is true of artificial intelligence. The boom in AI demand increases the importance of advanced chips and the capital flowing into data centers. That can strengthen the strategic case for domestic capacity while also making semiconductor valuations more sensitive to an entirely different uncertainty: whether AI demand and economics justify the investment already being built.

A geopolitical hedge can therefore contain a technology-cycle bet.

The categories overlap.

This is why a portfolio designed around “Taiwan risk” can accidentally become concentrated in the same semiconductor and AI stocks already dominating the broad market. The investor believes a hedge has been added when the portfolio has actually doubled down on the existing factor exposure.

Look through the fund.

Look through the customer list.

Look through the supply chain.

The island problem is an invitation to look through labels.

For a household investor, there is a more modest conclusion. You do not need to decide whether a conflict over Taiwan will occur. You need to recognize that a globally diversified portfolio already contains a great deal of exposure to the semiconductor ecosystem because chips sit inside much of the modern economy. Adding a large thematic position may increase rather than reduce geopolitical concentration.

The hedge could be broader than the theme.

Cash-flow resilience, geographic diversification, moderate position sizes, unleveraged holdings, and exposure to multiple sectors may protect against a semiconductor shock more reliably than identifying one supposed winner in advance.

For a company, the answer is operational redundancy.

For a government, the answer may include industrial policy and alliances.

For the speculator, the answer is often a ticker.

The ticker is the easiest part.

Factories are harder.

Factories have to work.
