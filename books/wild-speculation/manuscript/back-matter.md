# Back Matter

## About the Book

*Wild Speculation: Investing on the Brink of World War 3* is a book about what happens when geopolitical fear becomes an investment thesis.

The title is intentionally dramatic. The argument is not. This book does not claim that a third world war is inevitable, imminent, or forecastable on an investment timetable. It uses the possibility of major-power conflict as a stress test for a more practical question: what happens to a portfolio when markets close, energy routes lengthen, sanctions change ownership rights, inflation redistributes purchasing power, industrial bottlenecks become strategic, cyber systems fail, and stories become more certain than the evidence beneath them?

The recurring conclusion is that price is only one layer of exposure. Custody, jurisdiction, settlement, currency, access, financing, physical capacity, household liabilities, and the investor's own behavior can matter just as much.

The objective is not to build a portfolio that requires catastrophe. It is to build one that remains useful across several futures, including the possibility that the most dramatic forecast is wrong.

## Notes and Sources

The manuscript was researched chapter by chapter using a source ledger maintained alongside the book.

Primary and official sources are preferred where they can answer a claim directly: Federal Reserve research and historical records, U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission materials, Treasury and OFAC sanctions records, U.S. Energy Information Administration data, Government Accountability Office reports, Commerce Department semiconductor-program records, Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency guidance, Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation materials, Stockholm International Peace Research Institute expenditure data, World Bank reconstruction assessments, company filings, and contemporaneous historical financial records.

The sourcing discipline is intentionally conservative. A current estimate is treated as a snapshot. A government projection is treated as a projection. A military budget is not treated as contractor revenue. A backlog is not treated as cash. A strategic need is not treated as a funded project. An asset freeze is not automatically described as confiscation. A rerouted commodity flow is not described as vanished supply. A geopolitical-risk index is evidence about measured geopolitical risk, not a mechanical trading signal.

The source ledger also records counterevidence and boundary conditions. Sanctions can reroute trade rather than eliminate it. Strategic industries can receive more public money and face more public control at the same time. Gold can diversify some monetary risks without becoming a universal crisis hedge. Government bonds can be strong in a deflationary shock and weak in an inflationary one. Reconstruction needs can be enormous without becoming immediate private-sector revenue.

Several figures in the draft are deliberately dated because geopolitical and market conditions move quickly. Chokepoint flows, military expenditure, reserve composition, sanctions rules, reconstruction estimates, industrial-capacity programs, and current market structures should all be rechecked before a Shelf release.

The research files accompanying this manuscript are the canonical source record for the Desk draft. They can be converted into publication-format endnotes during final production.

## Acknowledgments

This book depends on institutions and people who preserve records when events are moving too quickly for memory to remain reliable: statisticians, regulators, researchers, archivists, engineers, inspectors general, public agencies, company filers, and historians.

The historical record repeatedly shows that crises look cleaner after they are over than they did while people were living through them. The documents help restore some of that uncertainty.

Any remaining errors of interpretation belong to the author.
