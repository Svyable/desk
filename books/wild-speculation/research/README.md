# Research Brief

## Thesis under test

*Wild Speculation* asks what an investor can responsibly infer from geopolitical danger when the consequences are enormous but the path from event to portfolio return is conditional, institution-dependent, and often already reflected in price.

The strong claim — that a third world war is imminent and therefore a particular set of assets must outperform — is intentionally rejected. The useful claim is narrower and falsifiable:

> Geopolitical shocks matter to investors primarily through specific transmission mechanisms — physical supply, financing, payment and custody systems, law, state capacity, corporate exposure, and household liabilities — rather than through a universal “war trade.” A resilient portfolio should therefore reduce concentrated dependencies and preserve optionality instead of requiring one geopolitical screenplay to come true.

The manuscript uses “World War 3” as a stress-test label, not as a forecast. It should remain valid if tensions ease, if a severe conflict stays regional, or if the next major disruption arrives through sanctions, cyber operations, shipping, industrial mobilization, or market plumbing rather than a cinematic global war.

## Core empirical tests

1. **Headline versus exposure:** Do geopolitical-risk shocks affect firms and sectors heterogeneously enough to invalidate a single directional “war trade”?
2. **Market plumbing:** Under extreme stress, can trading suspensions, sanctions, custody rules, capital controls, bank access, or settlement constraints dominate the quoted price of an asset?
3. **Energy geography:** How much does chokepoint risk depend on rerouting capacity, inventories, spare production, destination markets, and duration rather than the existence of a narrow strait alone?
4. **Sanctions and money:** When states weaponize access to reserves, payments, trade finance, and property, what exactly changes about the practical meaning of liquidity and reserve assets?
5. **War finance:** How do fiscal expansion, administered yields, central-bank choices, rationing, taxes, and inflation redistribute wartime costs across bondholders, workers, consumers, and firms?
6. **Defense economics:** Does higher military spending reliably translate into superior shareholder returns once procurement structure, capacity limits, valuation, labor, suppliers, and political constraints are included?
7. **Industrial concentration:** Which semiconductor and critical-material dependencies are true single points of failure, and which can be substituted, stockpiled, rerouted, or rebuilt at a cost?
8. **Cyber continuity:** How much of financial resilience is operational — identity, backups, authentication, communications, custody, and recovery — rather than an asset-allocation decision?
9. **Household resilience:** Which vulnerabilities are best addressed by liquidity, insurance, low leverage, documentation, geographic flexibility, and diversified income rather than speculative hedges?
10. **Reconstruction:** Who captures value after destruction, and how often do financing, governance, procurement, security, and timing prevent a large reconstruction need from becoming a simple equity bull market?
11. **Portfolio robustness:** Does a diversified, liability-aware portfolio with predetermined rebalancing rules outperform crisis-script concentration across multiple plausible geopolitical scenarios?

## Falsification and counter-evidence

Every chapter should preserve evidence that cuts against its preferred intuition.

- A simple geopolitical trade can sometimes work. A durable physical shortage can raise the cash flows of producers whose capacity is available and whose valuation did not already price the shortage.
- Gold, cash, sovereign bonds, reserve currencies, and defense equities can each be useful in specific crises. The argument is not that “havens do not work”; it is that the job, institution, jurisdiction, price, and time horizon matter.
- Strategic concentration can create persistent scarcity rents. Not every bottleneck is quickly arbitraged away.
- Sanctions can be highly effective even when evasion and substitution occur, and institutional control over payments can increase rather than reduce the usefulness of incumbent reserve currencies.
- War spending can support aggregate demand and selected corporate revenues even while reducing civilian welfare.
- Reconstruction can create genuine long-duration investment opportunities when security, institutions, financing, and procurement align.
- Diversification cannot eliminate loss in a global shock, and correlations can rise sharply during stress. Robustness is not immunity.
- If repeated evidence shows that a stable, simple bundle of “war assets” delivers superior risk-adjusted returns across distinct conflicts without requiring precise timing, the manuscript’s skepticism toward universal crisis portfolios should be reduced.
- If geopolitical events consistently overwhelm prior valuation and expectations, the book should place less weight on “relative to what price?” and more weight on structural directional exposure.
- If capital controls, market closures, custody impairment, or sanctions prove too rare to matter outside directly affected jurisdictions, the manuscript should narrow its emphasis on institutional plumbing for globally diversified investors.

The book should change when these tests fail. It should never turn a dramatic scenario into evidence for itself.

## Evidence lanes

### I. Geopolitical risk and asset pricing

Use the Caldara-Iacoviello geopolitical-risk research and later Federal Reserve work on firm and bank exposure to separate the common shock from cross-sectional exposure. Treat event-day prices as expectations-relative observations, not as moral or causal verdicts.

### II. Market closure, custody, and liquidity

Use the 1914 exchange closure, banking-holiday history, modern sanctions, and market-structure records to test the assumption that an investor can always sell, settle, transfer, or access a claim on demand.

### III. Energy and shipping chokepoints

Track physical flows, destination markets, bypass routes, transit time, spare capacity, inventories, and insurance. EIA chokepoint data is the primary quantitative anchor; current figures must be date-stamped.

### IV. Sanctions, reserves, and the dollar

Separate reserve composition, transaction currency, funding currency, settlement infrastructure, and legal access. Use Treasury/OFAC for sanctions mechanics and IMF/BIS data for reserve and transaction roles.

### V. War finance, bonds, and inflation

Use Federal Reserve historical records for wartime yield pegs and monetary-fiscal conflict; pair them with current inflation and fiscal data where a chapter makes a contemporary claim. Do not project one war-finance regime onto another without identifying the policy mechanism.

### VI. Defense and industrial capacity

Use SIPRI for comparable military-spending series, GAO and Department of Defense records for production constraints, and procurement evidence for the gap between appropriated demand and realizable deliveries or margins.

### VII. Chips and export controls

Use Commerce/BIS rules and program materials for advanced-computing restrictions and domestic capacity policy. Keep fabrication, packaging, equipment, materials, design, and end-market exposure separate.

### VIII. Cyber and operational resilience

Use CISA threat advisories and NIST risk-management guidance. The relevant investment question is not whether “cyber stocks go up,” but which dependencies can interrupt ownership, operations, payments, or recovery.

### IX. Reconstruction and portfolio design

Use World Bank damage-and-needs assessments to distinguish social need from investable cash flow. Use SEC/Investor.gov materials for the deliberately boring baseline of allocation, diversification, horizon, risk tolerance, and rebalancing.

## Source discipline

- Prefer official datasets, primary legal materials, regulatory records, original academic research, contemporaneous records, and direct institutional reporting.
- Date-stamp all current military-spending, reserve-share, shipping-flow, reconstruction, sanctions, and policy claims.
- Separate a threat from a realized disruption, an appropriation from a contract, a contract from delivery, backlog from profit, and social reconstruction need from shareholder return.
- Do not infer a universal asset response from one conflict or one event window.
- Record where a source is descriptive rather than causal.
- Keep scenario assumptions visibly conditional; never cite a scenario as evidence that the scenario will occur.
- For every major portfolio claim, ask what would make the recommendation fail even if the geopolitical forecast were correct.
- Recheck volatile facts immediately before any Shelf release.
