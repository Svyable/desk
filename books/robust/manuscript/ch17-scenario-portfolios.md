# Scenario Portfolios

The future is easiest to predict when you are allowed to choose only one.

The presentation demands a base case. The budget needs a number. The board wants the plan. The hiring model wants headcount. The product road map wants dates. A spreadsheet cannot leave every cell as “it depends.”

So uncertainty gets compressed. AI capability improves at this rate. Inference cost falls by that amount. Customers adopt on this curve.

Agents become reliable enough for this category of work in this quarter.

Regulation lands here.

Competitors respond there.

The organization selects one internally coherent future and begins behaving as though coherence made it likely.

This is how strategy becomes brittle.

The alternative is not indecision.

It is a **scenario portfolio**: a strategy composed of actions that perform acceptably across several plausible futures, plus a smaller number of deliberate bets that pay strongly in specific ones.

The idea comes naturally once we stop treating strategy as prediction.

A prediction says what you think will happen.

A scenario says what could happen in a way that would materially change your decision.

A portfolio says how much you are willing to expose yourself to each possibility.

This is especially useful in AI because the uncertainty is layered.

There is technical uncertainty.

How capable will models become, and at what tasks?

There is economic uncertainty.

How quickly will capability translate into lower prices, higher productivity, new demand, and durable profits?

There is adoption uncertainty.

Will people delegate quickly, slowly, or unevenly by domain?

There is organizational uncertainty.

Can firms actually redesign work around the technology, or will bottlenecks move into governance, data, trust, and management?

There is political uncertainty.

Which uses become encouraged, restricted, licensed, litigated, or standardized?

There is competitive uncertainty.

Does intelligence remain concentrated among a few providers, become broadly commoditized, or split into layers with different economics?

These uncertainties interact.

A model can become dramatically more capable while adoption remains slow in consequential institutions. Prices can fall while switching costs rise. Agents can become excellent at long tasks and still be limited by permissions, liability, or poor organizational data. Open models can close capability gaps in some domains while proprietary systems retain advantages in others.

The range of plausible futures is wide enough that one-point strategy becomes an act of faith.

A robust company does not need twenty scenarios.

Three or four that change behavior are enough.

Imagine a professional-services firm.

**Scenario One: AI improves steadily but remains mostly assistive.** Models get better at research, drafting, analysis, and coding, but consequential autonomy remains limited. Clients expect faster work and lower prices. Human experts remain central.

**Scenario Two: reliable agents arrive quickly.** Systems can execute multi-step work with high enough reliability that whole workflows can be redesigned. Competition shifts from “who uses AI well” to “who has rebuilt the operating model around delegated intelligence.”

**Scenario Three: capability continues improving but economics commoditize.** Powerful models become cheap and widely accessible. Differentiation moves away from model access toward proprietary context, distribution, trust, workflow, brand, and customer relationships.

**Scenario Four: adoption hits institutional friction.** Regulation, liability, security incidents, customer resistance, or disappointing production results slow deployment in high-consequence work even while frontier capability rises.

No single scenario is the forecast.

The strategic question is what actions make sense across several.

Investing in clean, permissioned, well-organized internal data helps in all four.

Building workflow-specific evaluation capability helps in all four.

Teaching employees problem framing, verification, and delegation helps in all four.

Keeping customer relationships direct helps in all four.

Designing software with reversible model boundaries helps in all four.

These are **robust moves**.

Then there are **option moves**.

Pilot an agentic delivery model with one service line.

Train a small team deeply on local models.

Negotiate rights to use client data for specified AI workflows.

Build a prototype whose economics become compelling only if agent reliability crosses a threshold.

These moves are modest investments that become valuable if a particular future strengthens.

Finally there are **bets**.

Acquire a company because you believe one layer of the market will consolidate.

Build a large proprietary model.

Replace a major workforce process before the evidence is complete.

Sign a long capacity contract.

Exit a business you believe AI will commoditize.

Bets are not un-robust.

A portfolio with no bets is often just a plan to be average in every future.

Robust strategy asks whether the organization can survive being wrong enough to keep playing.

That is different.

The scenario portfolio therefore separates **survival**, **optionality**, and **upside**.

What must remain true in every plausible world for us to continue functioning?

What low-cost actions keep future paths open?

Where do we have a view strong enough to deserve concentrated exposure?

This structure prevents a common strategic mistake: treating every uncertain question as though it deserves the same level of commitment.

Some uncertainties should be hedged. Some should be tested. Some should be ignored. Some should be bet on.

The art is matching commitment to evidence and reversibility.

AI creates another problem for scenario planning: the scenarios can age quickly.

A static annual exercise becomes theater if a model release, price collapse, regulation, or competitor move invalidates the assumptions two months later.

So a robust scenario portfolio has **triggers**.

Not predictions of dates.

Observable conditions that change the next action.

If workflow eval performance crosses this threshold, expand autonomy.

If model cost falls below this level, revisit the build-versus-buy decision.

If more than this percentage of customers adopt the new interface, change onboarding.

If a provider changes data terms, activate migration review.

If the human exception queue falls below this rate for three months, redesign staffing.

If regulation requires a new audit trail, shift the architecture.

Triggers turn scenarios into operating systems.

They also reduce political attachment to forecasts.

The organization does not have to admit “we were wrong.”

It says, “The trigger fired. The portfolio changes.”

This is a subtle cultural advantage.

Many bad strategies survive because too much identity is attached to the forecast. The CEO predicted rapid adoption. The transformation office promised savings. The investment committee believed open models would commoditize the market. Evidence changes, but reversing course feels like confessing incompetence.

A scenario portfolio normalizes conditionality from the start.

If A, do this.

If B, do that.

We currently weight A more heavily.

The language preserves conviction without turning conviction into a trap.

This is how good scientists hold hypotheses and how good traders manage positions. A view can be strong and still contain a price at which you change it.

Organizations need the same humility.

There is a practical way to build scenarios without producing science fiction.

Start with two uncertainties that genuinely change the business.

For AI, one might be **capability**: incremental versus discontinuous improvement in the workflows you care about.

The other might be **adoption or constraint**: fast institutional adoption versus slow adoption due to trust, economics, regulation, or integration.

Four quadrants appear. Incremental capability / slow adoption. Incremental capability / fast adoption. Discontinuous capability / slow adoption. Discontinuous capability / fast adoption.

Now ask what your business looks like in each.

Do not write a novel. What happens to customer willingness to pay? Which costs change? Which assets become more valuable? Which roles change?

Which competitors appear? Which dependency becomes dangerous? Which current investment still pays? Which one becomes embarrassing?

Then identify the moves with the widest usefulness.

This exercise often reveals that companies spend too much time predicting the frontier and too little time building assets that matter across frontiers.

Proprietary customer trust. Clean data. Distribution. A reputation for accountability. Evaluation infrastructure.

Talent that learns quickly. Modular systems. Cash. The ability to run experiments.

These can sound boring beside “own the winning model.”

Boring assets survive forecasts.

The same approach applies to careers.

A person can build a scenario portfolio too.

Suppose you are a software engineer.

Scenario A: coding agents improve rapidly and perform most routine implementation.

Scenario B: they improve but remain unreliable on large, context-heavy systems.

Scenario C: implementation becomes cheap, causing software demand to expand dramatically.

Scenario D: organizational bottlenecks, security, and regulation slow agent autonomy.

What investments pay across these? System design. Debugging. Security. Understanding users.

Working with agents. Reading code. Learning quickly. Owning a domain. What options are cheap?

Build something with the newest agent. Learn how evals work. Contribute to an open project.

Study a domain where software meets physical or regulated reality.

What bets might you choose?

Join an agent-native startup.

Move into AI infrastructure.

Specialize in a domain you believe will explode.

A robust career does not require predicting which future wins.

It requires avoiding a portfolio in which one narrow prediction destroys all your human capital.

The same applies to education.

A school cannot know the exact division of labor between students and AI in 2035.

It can still teach skills that matter across scenarios: reasoning, evidence, communication, quantitative fluency, collaboration, self-directed learning, tool use, and domain depth.

It can preserve options by exposing students to AI rather than banning it wholesale, while still designing assessments that reveal whether students can think without invisible assistance when the objective requires it.

It can test new forms of apprenticeship.

It can avoid building an entire curriculum around the current interface.

Robustness is not knowing the future of education.

It is refusing to make one uncertain answer irreversible for a generation.

Governments need scenario portfolios even more because their commitments can last decades.

AI infrastructure policy depends on assumptions about compute, energy, chips, security, labor markets, and national capability. Regulation depends on assumptions about risk and adoption. Education policy depends on assumptions about skills. Procurement depends on assumptions about vendor maturity.

A robust state distinguishes no-regret capacity from concentrated bets.

Digital infrastructure, research talent, evaluation capability, cybersecurity, energy reliability, competitive markets, and public-sector technical competence can matter across many futures.

Specific subsidies, restrictions, or industrial bets should have review triggers tied to evidence.

The larger the institution, the more valuable conditionality becomes because reversal is slower.

Scenario portfolios also protect against one of the strangest effects of AI news: **temporal whiplash**.

A breakthrough makes the future feel immediate. A disappointing product makes it feel distant. A benchmark falls and everyone compresses timelines. A deployment fails and everyone expands them.

Strategy should not be a mood ring for the latest demonstration.

The portfolio lets new evidence change weights without rewriting the worldview every week.

The best scenario system is therefore not a deck.

It is a set of maintained beliefs. What are our current scenario weights? What evidence moved them? Which actions are robust across them? Which options should we fund now?

Which bets are we consciously carrying? What would make us exit the bet? This can be done on one page. Complexity does not make the thinking stronger.

The goal is to reduce the number of hidden forecasts embedded in irreversible decisions.

That is what brittleness often is: a prediction nobody remembers making.

We hired for this workflow because we assumed the work would remain manual.

We removed this team because we assumed automation would be reliable.

We signed this contract because we assumed prices would stay high.

We built this interface because we assumed users would want chat.

We centralized this data because we assumed regulation would converge.

Every architecture contains a forecast.

Every organization does too.

Robust strategy brings the important forecasts into the open and refuses to make all of them at maximum size.

There will be a future.

You must act before you know which one.

That is not a flaw in planning.

It is the reason portfolios exist.
