# Taste Is a Control System

The cheaper it becomes to make something, the more expensive it becomes to know what is worth keeping.

This is the abundance problem hiding inside generative AI.

A person once paid with time for every variation. Another headline meant writing another headline. Another design meant moving the elements again. Another coding approach meant implementing enough of it to learn whether it worked. Another strategic scenario meant finding another afternoon.

Scarcity performed quality control by exhaustion. Not good quality control. But a limit. Generative systems remove the limit.

Twenty names before breakfast. Ten layouts in minutes. Five memos with different recommendations. Three implementations. Fifty images. A hundred personalized messages. Endless revisions, because revision no longer has the friction that once forced a person to decide whether the change was worth making.

The new bottleneck is selection.

This is where taste stops sounding decorative.

Taste is often treated as the mysterious property possessed by designers, editors, chefs, musicians, investors, and founders who “just know” what is good. The mystery is useful to people who sell taste and frustrating to everyone else.

For the purposes of robustness, taste can be made less mystical.

**Taste is a control system for abundance.**

It is a set of learned discriminations that helps a person decide what to keep, reject, question, or improve when the cost of producing alternatives falls.

Good taste is not simply preference.

“I like blue” is preference.

“This shade makes the warning state too similar to the normal state for a user scanning quickly” is discrimination.

“I prefer short writing” is preference.

“This paragraph is shorter but removed the condition that makes the claim true” is discrimination.

“This code looks ugly” is preference until the speaker can point to the hidden coupling, ambiguous state, or maintenance cost that the ugliness signals.

Taste becomes robust when it can explain itself enough to be taught, tested, and applied under pressure.

AI makes this valuable for a simple reason: production is becoming easier to outsource than judgment.

Not all judgment.

Models can judge too. They can rank alternatives, critique drafts, compare outputs against rubrics, run tests, and identify flaws. Use them.

The human advantage is not that machines cannot evaluate.

The robust advantage is to avoid having one undifferentiated intelligence produce the work, define the standard, grade the work, and decide what happens next without an external constraint.

A system that writes its own exam and marks its own paper can be brilliant.

It is difficult to audit. Taste provides one source of independence. Tests provide another. Customers provide another. Reality provides the final one.

The point is to create multiple signals of quality rather than turn fluency into proof.

This matters because generative output has a powerful surface property: it tends to arrive finished-looking.

A rough human draft advertises uncertainty. Crossed-out sentences, placeholders, missing transitions, comments in the margin — the artifact tells you it is unfinished.

AI output often arrives with the seams hidden. The headings are aligned. The paragraphs are complete. The tone is calm. The argument has an introduction and a conclusion. A generated software interface can look like a product before the state model is sound. Generated code can look idiomatic before the edge cases have been considered.

Finish is no longer evidence of finishing.

Taste has to penetrate the surface.

The same problem appears in research.

An assistant can summarize a topic into five clean themes. The themes may be reasonable. The organization of the evidence can feel so inevitable that the reader forgets someone — human or machine — selected the categories.

A person with research taste asks different questions.

Which claim carries the conclusion?

What source supports that claim?

Is the source measuring what the prose says it is measuring?

What would a contrary source look like?

Which facts are current and which are being smuggled across time?

Where did uncertainty disappear between the paper and the summary?

These questions are not romantic resistance to automation.

They are how the human stays useful when synthesis becomes abundant.

Taste is built through exposure plus feedback. You see many examples. You make choices. Reality answers.

The editor cuts a sentence and the paragraph improves.

The designer changes hierarchy and users find the button.

The engineer adds an abstraction and six months later discovers whether it simplified change or merely hid it.

The investor learns that a persuasive narrative did not make the cash flow arrive.

The manager promotes the brilliant individual contributor and learns whether brilliance transferred to leadership.

Taste is compressed consequence.

That is why it is hard to accelerate with explanations alone.

A rubric helps.

A mentor helps.

A model explaining its critique helps.

But the deeper calibration comes from repeated contact with outcomes.

This creates another apprenticeship problem for the AI era.

If the machine produces the first hundred drafts, does the human still get enough exposure to understand why the hundred-and-first is better?

The answer can be yes if the workflow is designed for it.

A young designer can generate many more variants than before and compare them against real user behavior. A programmer can inspect several implementations and run tests. A writer can ask for deliberately bad versions, identify what is wrong, then see how revisions alter the effect. A student can receive immediate counterarguments and examples.

AI can multiply feedback.

It can also numb discrimination by flooding the person with competent sameness.

The difference is whether the human is asked to choose for a reason.

A robust AI workflow should make evaluation active.

Do not ask only, “Do you like this?”

Ask what it optimized.

Ask what it sacrificed.

Ask which constraint it violated.

Ask what would make it fail in the real environment.

Ask what evidence would change the judgment.

Ask which version you would ship if your name were on the consequence.

These questions convert taste from vibe into governance.

This is especially important in management because AI makes it easier to hide value judgments behind generated analysis.

A model can rank applicants, summarize performance, prioritize customers, draft budgets, recommend cuts, and produce a rationale for each. The rationale can make a subjective tradeoff look procedural.

But somewhere there is still a standard.

What counts as a promising employee?

Which customer deserves exception handling?

What is the organization protecting during a budget cut?

Which kind of error is worse?

Who bears the cost of speed?

The system cannot eliminate these questions. It can make them less visible.

Taste, in its broad managerial sense, is the ability to keep them visible.

This is why experienced people can become more valuable as AI gets stronger, provided experience has produced judgment rather than merely seniority.

A person who has seen ten product launches knows that a launch plan can be complete and still be unserious. The calendar is filled. The owners are named. The deck has risks. But perhaps the customer-support team has not used the feature, the data migration is assumed rather than rehearsed, or the success metric cannot distinguish curiosity from retention.

The model can learn these patterns too.

Good.

Then the experienced person can use it to inspect more deeply.

The durable value is not exclusive possession of the pattern. It is having enough calibrated judgment to know when the pattern applies.

Taste is contextual.

A sparse interface can be elegant in one product and irresponsible in another. A concise answer can be merciful or evasive. Aggressive automation can be visionary in a reversible sandbox and reckless in a system of record. Redundancy can be prudent or absurd.

This contextual nature makes taste difficult to standardize and therefore tempting to remove.

Organizations prefer rules because rules scale.

Rules also ossify.

The robust answer is to separate **constraints** from **judgments**.

Constraints should be explicit where the organization truly means them.

Never send customer secrets to an unapproved service.

Never transfer above this amount without authorization.

Never deploy code without these checks.

Never publish a factual claim without a recoverable source trail.

Judgments remain where context matters. Is this argument fair? Does this design create trust? Is this customer actually exceptional? Is the technically passing code maintainable?

Does this recommendation fit the institution we are trying to become?

AI can assist with both. But confusing a judgment with a constraint produces brittle policy, while confusing a constraint with judgment produces arbitrary governance.

Taste helps keep the border clean.

There is another reason to cultivate it: **AI can make mediocre choices feel less painful.**

When revision is expensive, choosing poorly hurts. If you commission the wrong illustration, rewrite the wrong codebase, or spend a week pursuing the wrong argument, the cost leaves a mark.

When regeneration takes seconds, the penalty disappears.

This is mostly wonderful.

It can also weaken commitment to the act of choosing.

Why decide? Generate another.

Why learn what went wrong? Ask for a different version.

Why establish a standard? Keep iterating until the feeling changes.

The person becomes a slot-machine operator for adequacy.

Robust taste resists this by making the standard legible before the options become infinite.

What are we trying to preserve? What matters most? What can be traded? What would make this clearly wrong? What would make it unmistakably ours?

These questions give generation edges.

They also make collaboration with AI dramatically better. Models respond well to a world with constraints. “Make it better” invites generic polish. “Cut the opening unless it establishes stakes in the first five lines; preserve the factual caveat; remove any sentence that repeats the thesis without advancing it” creates a tractable editorial problem.

The better your taste becomes, the more precisely you can delegate.

That is the paradox.

Strong judgment does not make the machine less useful.

It makes more of the work safe to hand over.

A person with weak standards must supervise everything because she does not know where quality will fail.

A person with strong standards can encode some checks, delegate broadly, inspect strategically, and focus attention where discrimination still matters.

Taste is therefore not the last human refuge after machines take production.

It is infrastructure for higher delegation.

And machines can help build it.

Ask the model to compare two drafts under a rubric.

Then disagree with it.

Ask why.

Ask for the strongest case against your preference.

Ask it to identify the tradeoff you are making.

Run the output against users, tests, evidence, or consequences.

Record where your judgment was wrong.

Taste that never meets correction becomes mannerism.

The robust version is willing to update.

This matters because the AI landscape will keep changing what “good” looks like.

A writing style prized because it signaled effort may become cheap. A coding convention built around human typing cost may become irrelevant. A customer experience designed to conceal automation may become stranger than simply admitting it. A workflow that once required a specialist may become accessible to a generalist with a strong model and good taste.

Standards should not be frozen merely because they were earned through experience.

The point of taste is not to preserve the old hierarchy.

It is to notice quality under new conditions.

Production is becoming abundant.

Judgment will become more visible because of it.

The person who can make one thing will be useful.

The person who can make ten things will be powerful.

The person who can tell which one deserves to exist — and explain why — will be sturdy.
