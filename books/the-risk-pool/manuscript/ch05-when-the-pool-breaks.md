# When the Pool Breaks

Insurance failure rarely begins with a dramatic bankruptcy filing.

It begins earlier, when the assumptions holding a pool together stop matching the people and losses inside it.

The symptoms can look ordinary.

Claims cost more than expected. Customers most likely to claim remain while low-risk customers leave. A legal interpretation expands coverage across old contracts. Medical utilization rises. Repair inflation accelerates. A new technology creates a category of loss the policy language did not anticipate. A series of storms exposes the fact that a portfolio thought to be diversified is concentrated in the same weather pattern.

For a time, the insurer can absorb the difference.

That is what capital is for.

But capital is not a substitute for pricing. If new premiums continue to be collected below the true cost of new risk, the institution is manufacturing future losses.

The insurance business is especially vulnerable to this because growth can make trouble look like success.

A company that underprices coverage can sell a great deal of it.

Premium volume rises. Market share grows. Brokers like the product because it is competitive. Customers like the product because it is cheap. Claims may take years to mature in long-tail lines. By the time the loss ratio reveals the problem, the company may have accumulated obligations far larger than the profits that made the expansion look attractive.

This is one reason insurance people sometimes distrust growth.

A retailer can discount inventory and know immediately that the margin is thin.

An insurer can discount future uncertainty without learning the real margin until much later.

The gap between sale and true cost creates a temptation every generation of insurers rediscovers.

Cash arrives before bad news.

This timing problem helps explain insurance cycles. When capital is abundant and recent losses are light, insurers compete aggressively. Rates fall. Terms broaden. Underwriting discipline can soften. New entrants arrive. The market becomes "soft."

Then losses expose weak pricing, capital gets scarcer, reinsurers demand more, and carriers retreat. Rates rise. Deductibles grow. Exclusions tighten. The market becomes "hard."

The cycle is not perfectly mechanical, and different lines move differently, but the pattern reveals something important.

Insurance supply is not fixed.

Capacity responds to memory and capital.

A quiet period can create confidence. A catastrophe can create scarcity. New capital can later restore competition. The customer experiences these financial cycles as changing access to an ostensibly stable promise.

Adverse selection can accelerate the process.

Imagine a health plan charging one price to a broad group while participation remains voluntary. Healthier people compare the premium with their expected costs and decide to leave. The average risk of the remaining pool rises. The plan raises the price. More healthy people leave.

Nothing supernatural occurs.

The average price is chasing a deteriorating average customer.

This is sometimes called an adverse-selection spiral. The phrase is most familiar in health economics, but the logic appears in many insurance settings.

A property insurer that must hold rates below the level it believes high-risk homes require may respond by reducing new business in exposed areas. Low-risk homeowners may still have access to multiple carriers, while high-risk homeowners migrate toward the carriers or residual pools willing or required to take them. The portfolios separate.

The residual market becomes riskier precisely because it is performing its social function.

The insurer of last resort inherits the people the ordinary market found hardest to price.

This creates a political optical illusion.

The public pool can appear badly managed because its losses are high.

Sometimes it is badly managed.

But a residual pool designed to accept rejected risks should be expected to contain rejected risks. Comparing its claim profile with a voluntary insurer without adjusting for selection misses the point.

The harder question is whether the residual institution charges enough, mitigates enough, and holds enough capital for the risks it intentionally concentrates.

Moral hazard creates a different break.

Insurance can weaken the relationship between behavior and consequence.

The crude version is fraud: someone burns a building to collect insurance. Contract law, investigation, deductibles, insurable-interest rules, and criminal law all exist partly to stop such behavior.

The more important version is ordinary adaptation.

People take more risk when somebody else shares the cost.

A business with cyber insurance may feel less urgency about security, though good cyber insurers increasingly try to reverse this by making controls a condition of coverage. A flood-insured homeowner may remain in a hazardous location longer than an uninsured homeowner. A bank protected by public guarantees may accept risks depositors would otherwise punish.

The effect need not be conscious.

Insurance changes the budget constraint.

Changing the budget constraint changes decisions.

The policy can counter this through deductibles and coinsurance. Keeping the policyholder exposed to the first layer of loss preserves an incentive to avoid small claims and care about prevention.

But high deductibles create another problem.

The policy may become technically present and practically weak.

A homeowner with a twenty-thousand-dollar wind deductible is insured against total ruin but still exposed to a large repair bill. A health plan with high cost-sharing protects against extreme medical expense while making ordinary care expensive. A business interruption policy with narrow triggers may protect against a category of shutdown smaller than the customer imagines.

The difference between insured and protected matters.

Insurance contracts can fail socially without failing legally.

A policy can perform exactly as written and still surprise the policyholder because the policyholder misunderstood what was written.

This became painfully visible during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Businesses closed or lost revenue on a scale that looked, from the owner's perspective, exactly like business interruption. Many had business interruption insurance as part of commercial property coverage. Yet coverage commonly depended on physical loss or damage, and many policies contained virus exclusions. Litigation followed over language, causation, and what counted as physical loss.

Private insurance played a limited role in compensating the enormous business losses from the pandemic. The Government Accountability Office later examined proposals for federal pandemic insurance and reached a conclusion that reveals the limit of ordinary pooling: the losses could be so large, widespread, and correlated that government would bear most or all of the risk under plausible designs.

This is the accumulation problem in its pure form.

Insurance works by having the unlucky few draw from contributions made by the many.

A pandemic can make the many unlucky at once.

The same structure appears in war risk.

A localized accident can be pooled. General war can destroy assets across regions, interrupt trade, move markets, trigger sanctions, impair currencies, and affect the insurer's own investments. Nuclear risk can create losses whose geographic and temporal scale overwhelms ordinary private balance sheets.

This is why exclusions matter.

An insurance contract is partly a list of risks the insurer is willing to pool.

It is also a list of risks the insurer refuses to pool.

Customers often experience exclusions as fine print designed to avoid claims. Some are exactly that: aggressive drafting can shift uncertainty back to the policyholder in ways regulators or courts later challenge.

But the existence of exclusions is not itself evidence of bad faith.

A solvent pool has to define its perimeter.

If an insurer priced ordinary property risk and silently included unlimited war, nuclear, pandemic, and systemic cyber exposure, the policy might look generous until the first event revealed that the premium never funded the promise.

The honest insurance problem is not maximizing covered perils.

It is matching promises to capital.

Long-tail liability makes this matching particularly difficult.

Some claims arise years or decades after the policy was sold. Exposure to asbestos is the classic example. Companies wrote liability policies long before the eventual scale of disease, litigation, and legal interpretation became clear. Environmental liabilities created similar challenges. The insurer may discover decades later that old policy language covers losses nobody priced when the contract was written.

This is reserve risk.

An insurer must estimate how much today's known and unknown claims will eventually cost.

If the estimate is too low, reported profits are too high.

The error can persist for years.

Long-tail business therefore requires a kind of institutional humility. A company is making a statement about liabilities that are not fully observable yet.

The longer the tail, the more room for law, medicine, inflation, social norms, and discovery to change the final bill.

Climate-related property risk has a different tail but a similar epistemic problem.

The claims themselves arrive quickly after fire, wind, flood, or hail. The uncertainty lies in the future frequency, severity, exposure, and cost environment.

Historical loss data may no longer provide a stable estimate if weather patterns, development, vegetation, construction costs, or building density are changing.

The past is still evidence.

It is no longer a complete baseline.

This is sometimes described as the end of stationarity: the idea that the statistical properties of the hazard can be assumed stable enough for historical frequencies to represent future frequencies.

The phrase can sound grander than the operational problem.

The operational problem is that yesterday's loss ratio may be a bad price for tomorrow's renewal.

Insurers respond by using catastrophe models, forward-looking hazard data, scenario analysis, and shorter feedback cycles. Regulators respond by asking whether the models are credible, whether prices are discriminatory, and how much weight companies should be allowed to place on proprietary forecasts.

Homeowners respond by wondering why a house that did not move suddenly became harder to insure.

All three are living in the same changing distribution.

The pool can also break through concentration of information technology.

Insurance companies increasingly rely on common cloud providers, software vendors, data brokers, catastrophe models, and AI systems. Policyholders do too.

A vulnerability in one widely used piece of infrastructure can create claims across many insureds while simultaneously impairing the insurer's own operations.

This is double exposure.

The company can be both the payer and the victim.

Cyber insurance has forced underwriters to think about this because traditional diversification categories—industry, geography, revenue—may not reveal common dependencies. Two companies on different continents can share the same identity provider, endpoint software, cloud platform, or managed-service provider.

The true accumulation map is technical rather than geographic.

EIOPA, Europe's insurance regulator, has increasingly treated cyber risk as a financial-stability concern for exactly this reason. Its 2025 and 2026 work emphasizes technological interdependence, data gaps, and accumulation risk. By mid-2026 its insurance risk dashboard classified digitalisation and cyber risk at a high level.

The market can still insure cyber risk.

The challenge is knowing how much of one event sits inside the portfolio.

That problem is familiar to catastrophe insurers. What cyber changes is the speed.

A hurricane takes time to form and follows physical geography. A software exploit can propagate globally in hours. The loss can cross property, liability, business interruption, crime, and specialty lines before anyone has a complete picture.

The pool may learn about its concentration during the event.

There is one more way a pool breaks: political exit.

Insurance requires participation from capital providers as well as policyholders.

Regulators often focus on consumer access, which is understandable. But an insurer forced to write a class of risk on terms it believes unprofitable has another option if the law allows it.

It can stop writing.

A company may reduce exposure, pause new business, nonrenew selected policies, withdraw from a state, or restructure the product. These actions can be unpopular and, in some cases, restricted. But capital is mobile enough that sustained underpricing eventually appears somewhere else in the system.

This creates a basic constraint on rate regulation.

A regulator can set the legal price.

It cannot permanently force private capital to regard the price as attractive.

The distinction matters because price controls can solve an immediate affordability problem while creating an availability problem.

If a premium that would clear the market is eight thousand dollars and the approved premium is four thousand, consumers who receive a policy benefit enormously.

Consumers who cannot find a carrier may prefer the eight-thousand-dollar policy to no policy at all.

The market can ration by price or by quantity.

Insurance regulation often shifts the mix between them.

Neither outcome is politically easy.

A functioning pool therefore needs more than correct mathematics.

It needs enough participants, trustworthy contracts, responsive pricing, capital, prevention, regulation, and social legitimacy.

Break any one of these badly enough and the others come under pressure.

Underprice risk and capital leaves.

Overprice or oversegment risk and low-risk participants or politically vulnerable customers leave.

Make claims unpredictable and trust leaves.

Make coverage incomprehensible and customers discover gaps only after disaster.

Make mitigation impossible and the premium becomes a punishment without an escape route.

Hide public subsidies and the fiscal cost appears later.

The pool is stable when people understand, at least roughly, why they are paying, what they are receiving, and why the institution will still be there after a bad year.

That last part is easy to forget during calm periods.

The product is not the policy document.

The product is the payment after the event.

Everything before the event exists to make that payment possible.

When a pool breaks, the failure is rarely that uncertainty existed.

The failure is that the institution misjudged which uncertainties it could carry together.

A broken pool should also be distinguished from a pool that merely had a bad year.

The difference is crucial.

Insurance exists to absorb bad years. A hurricane, wildfire, hail season, liability verdict, or cyber event can generate losses above expectation without proving the underlying institution is unsound. Capital, reserves, reinsurance, and retained earnings are there precisely because realized loss will wander around the expected value.

If every large claim were evidence that insurance had failed, insurance would be judged a failure whenever it did its job.

The real warning sign is persistence.

Does the company replenish capital after the event? Can it still buy reinsurance? Do new policies earn an adequate expected return? Are reserves for old claims stabilizing or deteriorating? Are underwriting restrictions targeted responses or signs that management no longer understands the book? Is the company losing customers because price reflects risk, or keeping customers because price still does not?

A hard market can be painful and healthy at the same time.

Higher rates and tighter terms can attract new capital and repair an underpriced pool. The adjustment hurts customers, especially when the insured asset cannot move or adapt quickly, but the alternative can be a policy whose apparent affordability rests on an insurer becoming progressively less able to honor it.

Recovery therefore requires diagnosis.

A capital problem can sometimes be solved with new equity or reinsurance.

A pricing problem needs different rates.

A claims-cost problem may need engineering, fraud control, medical management, repair networks, or legal change.

An adverse-selection problem may require broader participation or redesigned eligibility.

A correlation problem may require lower limits, geographic or technological diversification, reinsurance, or a public catastrophe layer.

A trust problem may require clearer contracts and better claims handling.

Calling all of these insolvency hides the intervention that could work.

The insurer's balance sheet gives clues.

When losses develop worse than expected, reserves rise. When catastrophe exposure consumes too much of the available cushion, management reduces concentration or buys protection. When rating agencies or regulators doubt the capital position, the company can face constraints before it misses a single claim payment. These early interventions can look frustrating from the customer's perspective because the insurer is withdrawing while still solvent.

That is often the point.

Prudential supervision is supposed to notice fragility before the cashier's window closes.

Once an insurer actually fails, the problem changes character. Policies may need to be transferred, claims may enter statutory resolution processes, guaranty mechanisms may become relevant, employees and vendors are disrupted, and competing carriers reconsider whether the same business was mispriced across the market.

Insolvency is contagious even when the legal obligations are not.

It changes beliefs.

A competitor sees the failure and asks whether its own assumptions are next. Reinsurers revisit exposure. Brokers move clients. Regulators increase scrutiny. Capital becomes more expensive. A company that made one set of mistakes can cause the whole market to rediscover caution.

This is how institutional memory enters the cycle.

The memory eventually fades.

New managers arrive. New capital enters. The last catastrophe recedes. Competition makes yesterday's disciplined price look like today's opportunity. Underwriting exceptions accumulate one account at a time. A rule designed after the last crisis becomes an inconvenience to sales.

Insurance repeatedly confronts the same human weakness as banking.

The quiet period is when resilience feels most expensive.

Nothing is happening, so capital looks idle, reinsurance looks overpriced, exclusions look unnecessarily cautious, and engineering looks like overhead. Then the event arrives and all four turn out to have been part of the product.

A healthy pool therefore needs mechanisms that survive success.

Capital standards should not disappear because recent losses were light. Exposure monitoring should not stop because a model performed well last year. Claims data should challenge underwriting assumptions rather than be massaged to defend them. Management incentives should not reward premium growth without regard to the uncertainty being accumulated behind it.

The hardest discipline is saying no while competitors are saying yes.

That is why insurance is not only a mathematics business.

It is a governance business.

The pool breaks when numbers are wrong, but it can also break when institutions know the numbers are uncertain and choose not to act because the short-term rewards belong to the present while the claims belong to the future.

A catastrophe tests capital.

A cycle tests memory.

A durable insurer needs enough of both.
