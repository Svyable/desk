# Who Pays for the Agent?

The first insurance question about an autonomous agent is not whether the agent is intelligent.

It is who pays when it is wrong.

A software assistant books the wrong flight. An enterprise agent sends confidential data to an outside service. A coding agent introduces a vulnerability. A purchasing agent accepts a fraudulent invoice. A medical support system recommends an action that contributes to harm. An industrial agent changes a setting that damages equipment. A customer-service agent makes a binding promise the company did not intend to make.

The technical failure can be novel.

The legal question is ancient.

Who had the duty?

Who breached it?

Who caused the loss?

Who has money behind the answer?

Insurance enters at the last question and quickly discovers it cannot avoid the first three.

Liability insurance has always depended on legal architecture. A policy can cover negligence, errors and omissions, product liability, professional liability, cyber loss, directors-and-officers claims, or other categories only because law defines relationships and remedies around the underlying conduct.

Artificial intelligence destabilizes those categories by moving decision-making across organizational boundaries.

A company builds a model.

Another company fine-tunes or configures it.

A cloud provider hosts it.

A software platform supplies tools.

A business deploys an agent.

An employee writes the instruction.

The agent calls a third-party service.

A customer relies on the result.

When the chain works, modularity is a strength.

When it fails, modularity becomes a liability argument.

Each participant can point to another layer.

The model provider says the deployer controlled the use case.

The deployer says the model behaved unpredictably.

The tool vendor says the agent was given excessive permissions.

The customer says she reasonably relied on the company offering the service.

The insurer asks which policy was supposed to respond.

This is why AI liability will become an insurance-design problem before courts settle every doctrinal question.

Businesses need to operate under uncertainty.

They cannot wait twenty years for appellate law to become complete.

Insurance contracts can allocate some risk in advance.

The challenge is that insurers need enough information to price it.

Traditional errors-and-omissions coverage is a natural starting point. A software company already faces liability when its product fails to perform promised services or causes customer losses. Cyber insurance covers defined security and privacy events. Product liability can apply where software is embedded in physical goods. Professional liability covers advice and services in regulated professions.

AI does not erase these categories.

It creates overlaps and gaps.

The same agent failure can look like software error, professional negligence, privacy breach, fraud, employment action, intellectual-property dispute, or physical product failure depending on context.

A general-purpose model used inside many industries can touch liability lines that were historically separate.

This creates silent AI risk—the equivalent of silent cyber.

An insurer may have written policies before anyone asked explicitly how AI-related losses fit. The policy language might cover the claim even though the underwriter never priced autonomous systems as a distinct exposure.

The industry will respond the way it did with cyber.

Some carriers will clarify affirmative coverage.

Some will add exclusions.

Some will create sublimits.

Some will require disclosures about model use.

Specialty products will emerge around high-risk deployments.

The transition will be messy because customers prefer broad language until they need a claim, while insurers prefer precise boundaries before the claim exists.

The most important distinction will be between ordinary AI error and systemic AI failure.

An agent making one bad booking is ordinary frequency risk.

A widely used model version causing the same class of harmful action across thousands of insured companies is accumulation risk.

This is where insurance becomes nervous.

The software industry is highly concentrated in shared dependencies. Many companies use the same foundation models, cloud providers, orchestration frameworks, vector databases, identity systems, and APIs.

A common defect can propagate horizontally across industries.

The portfolio looks diversified by customer.

The technology stack is not.

This resembles cyber accumulation but adds a behavioral dimension.

The system does not merely fail to respond.

It can actively take the same wrong action many times.

An automated claims system can deny similar legitimate claims across a population. A financial agent can execute correlated strategies. A coding model can introduce the same insecure pattern into many repositories. A procurement agent can respond similarly to one manipulation technique across thousands of companies.

Shared intelligence creates shared mistakes.

This is a novel insurability problem because the model's usefulness encourages concentration.

The better a foundation model becomes, the more companies may adopt it.

The larger the installed base, the larger the potential correlated liability when something goes wrong.

The vendor can reduce this risk through testing, staged rollouts, rate limits, sandboxing, version control, and monitoring.

Insurance can reinforce those practices.

An insurer covering AI liability may ask which model versions are used, how changes are approved, what permissions agents have, whether high-impact actions require confirmation, how logs are preserved, whether outputs are independently verified, and how incidents are rolled back.

These questions resemble cyber underwriting.

The difference is that security controls are designed mostly to keep unauthorized actors out.

Agent controls must also constrain authorized software doing the wrong thing.

Least privilege becomes a liability control.

An agent allowed to draft an email creates a smaller tail than an agent allowed to send payments, modify production code, sign contracts, or change industrial equipment.

Insurance will therefore price authority.

This is a significant shift.

Traditionally liability insurance prices industries, revenue, payroll, claims history, products, procedures, geography, and other proxies for exposure.

Agentic systems create a new variable: the scope of delegated action.

Two companies using the same model can have radically different risk because one uses it as a suggestion engine and the other lets it transact autonomously.

The model is not the unit of risk.

The permission is.

This insight can improve product design.

A company seeking lower insurance costs may limit agent authority, add approval thresholds, separate planning from execution, require deterministic policy checks, preserve audit logs, and create rapid shutdown mechanisms.

The insurance premium becomes a price on autonomy.

This is not necessarily a brake on innovation.

It can be the condition that makes innovation deployable.

A hospital may accept an AI system sooner if liability allocation is clear. A customer may trust an autonomous financial service more if execution errors are covered by a guarantee. A board may approve agents with financial authority if the company can insure specified losses.

Insurance converts fear into a governed risk budget.

The best outcome is not zero liability.

It is predictable liability.

Markets function when participants know which failures belong to which balance sheets.

Uncertainty about liability can be more damaging than high liability because nobody knows how much capital to hold or what contract to buy.

This is why safe-harbor debates around emerging technology matter. A legal regime can encourage specified controls by limiting or clarifying liability when companies follow recognized practices.

Insurance can then price around the standard.

The standard becomes a common language between regulators, vendors, customers, and capital providers.

The danger is premature standardization.

If regulators freeze one technical architecture into law, the industry can optimize compliance while the technology changes underneath it. A control appropriate for 2026 agents may be obsolete by 2030.

Outcome-based standards are more durable.

Can the system prove authorization?

Can high-impact actions be reversed?

Can a company reconstruct what happened?

Can agents be isolated when compromised?

Can permissions be scoped?

Can a human or another system intervene before irreversible harm?

Can the provider detect correlated failures across customers?

These are insurability questions because they determine both frequency and severity.

The liability chain also raises a familiar insurance conflict: should the party best able to prevent the loss carry the risk?

If a foundation-model provider is always immune, it may have weaker incentives to reduce systemic failure. If the provider is liable for every downstream misuse, it may refuse broad access or charge prices that only large firms can afford.

If deployers bear all liability, small businesses may face risks they cannot evaluate. If users bear all liability because they clicked accept, consumer protection becomes fiction.

The efficient allocation probably varies by control.

The model provider should carry risks it uniquely controls, such as defects in its infrastructure or failures to follow promised safeguards.

The deployer should carry risks created by configuration, permissions, domain use, and failure to supervise.

The user should carry ordinary consequences of authorized choices within understandable boundaries.

Insurers can make these distinctions concrete by writing policies around them.

Contract markets often mature before legal doctrine does because businesses need a workable answer now.

Indemnities between vendors and customers already allocate software risk. AI contracts will become more detailed about model outputs, data rights, security, uptime, agent authority, and liability caps.

Insurance sits behind the indemnity.

A contractual promise without insurance or balance-sheet capacity can be weak.

This creates a new procurement question.

A company evaluating an AI vendor will ask not only what the system can do, but what insurance the vendor carries and whether the policy actually covers AI-related errors.

Coverage becomes evidence of institutional maturity.

The effect can favor large vendors because they can buy broader insurance or self-insure.

Small vendors may struggle if insurers require long loss histories that do not exist.

This is where specialty markets and pooled industry data can help.

New risks become insurable faster when claims and incident information are shared enough for underwriters to learn.

The cyber market went through this process.

AI liability will too.

One source of data will be near misses.

Insurance traditionally learns from claims because money forces reporting. But autonomous systems can generate thousands of incidents that are corrected before financial loss occurs. A coding agent writes insecure code that a test catches. A purchasing agent almost sends money to a fraudulent account but an approval rule blocks it. A medical system produces a dangerous suggestion that a clinician rejects.

These events are gold for risk management.

They reveal failure rates before catastrophic claims.

Insurers may eventually offer better terms to companies that share structured near-miss data, much as aviation safety systems learn from incidents that did not become crashes.

This would turn insurance into an observability layer for AI safety.

The challenge is legal discoverability and reputation. Companies may fear that reporting near misses creates evidence against them.

Public policy can improve learning by creating protected reporting channels for specified safety data.

The idea has precedent in other safety-critical fields.

A mature AI insurance market will therefore require more than premiums.

It will require incident taxonomy, control standards, loss databases, legal clarity, model provenance, and mechanisms for pooling information without exposing confidential systems.

This is why AI risk is not simply a new product line.

It is an institutional coordination problem.

The hardest part will come when the market encounters an event that looks truly systemic.

Suppose a common model defect causes widespread financial loss across many insured firms. Private insurers can pay ordinary claims. Reinsurers can absorb a larger layer. Catastrophe bonds might eventually transfer some modeled AI risk.

But if the event reaches the scale of national economic disruption, private capital may not be enough.

The government will face the same question it faced with terrorism, flood, and pandemic risk.

Does society need a public catastrophe layer to preserve private coverage below it?

The answer should not be decided after the first giant AI loss.

The market can begin designing thresholds now.

A government backstop should not cover routine model mistakes. Those belong to vendors, deployers, insurers, and ordinary liability systems.

A public layer should be reserved for truly correlated events that threaten the availability of coverage across the market.

Otherwise the backstop becomes a subsidy for weak engineering.

The purpose is not to make AI safe for investors.

It is to prevent one systemic event from making useful AI uninsurable for everyone.

This distinction will matter as autonomy grows.

The most economically powerful agents will be the ones allowed to act without constant human confirmation.

That is also where liability becomes largest.

A society that wants autonomous systems will need institutions willing to stand behind autonomous mistakes.

The engineer can build the agent.

The lawyer can write the terms.

The regulator can set duties.

The insurer answers the question that eventually reaches every real technology.

How much confidence can be converted into capital?

That question becomes much easier when authority is described precisely.

"Uses AI" is almost useless as an underwriting fact.

A system that summarizes documents and a system that can move ten million dollars may use the same model family. A system that drafts code and waits for review is different from one that merges and deploys it. A customer-service agent that proposes a refund is different from one that can issue the refund, waive a fee, modify an account, and promise future service.

The relevant exposure is a ladder of verbs.

Read.

Recommend.

Write.

Send.

Spend.

Commit.

Delete.

Execute.

Bind.

Each step changes the tail.

The underwriter will care about which verbs are available, to which resources, under what limits, and with what path to reversal. A thousand-dollar purchasing limit is a different risk from an unlimited purchasing mandate. The ability to create a draft database migration is different from authority to run it against production. The right to negotiate a contract is different from the right to sign one.

This makes permission architecture legible as an insurance control.

A mature agent deployment should be able to answer a simple question after any consequential action: what authority caused this action to be possible?

That answer may include a user instruction, a standing organizational policy, a tool permission, an API credential, a model decision, and a validation rule. The chain matters because claims attribution will otherwise collapse into a vague argument that "the AI did it."

The AI did not open its own bank account.

Someone connected it to one.

This is why provenance and logging become more than debugging conveniences. They are evidence about authorization, causation, and control. A log that shows which model version acted, which tools were available, which data was retrieved, which policy check ran, which human approved an exception, and what output was actually sent can turn a chaotic liability dispute into a tractable one.

The insurer does not need omniscience.

It needs reconstruction.

Reconstruction also makes near misses more valuable. If an agent attempted an unauthorized transfer and a deterministic policy engine blocked it, the company has learned something about both the model and the control. If the same attempt happens fifty times after a model update, the pattern may justify intervention before a claim occurs.

This is how loss prevention becomes continuous rather than annual.

The insurance policy can reinforce the architecture by distinguishing routine errors from severe delegated failures. Small, frequent mistakes may be better retained through deductibles or self-insurance because the company is in the best position to fix them. A larger layer can protect against rare losses that exceed ordinary operating tolerance. Reinsurance or specialty capacity can sit above still larger accumulation risk.

The familiar insurance stack reappears inside autonomy.

Do not transfer every typo.

Transfer the error that can sink the enterprise.

This has a behavioral benefit. If every minor agent mistake is reimbursed, the deployer has weaker reason to improve the system. Retention keeps some cost close to the party controlling configuration and supervision. Catastrophe coverage protects the balance sheet without turning the insurer into a maintenance contract for bad automation.

The difficult losses will be the ones caused by interactions rather than a single defective component.

A model may produce an ambiguous instruction. A retrieval system may surface stale data. A tool may interpret the instruction literally. A permission policy may be too broad. A human may have approved the workflow months earlier. A monitoring system may fail to notice that conditions changed.

Which component caused the loss?

Possibly all of them.

Insurance already knows this problem. Industrial accidents, medical errors, construction defects, and cyber incidents often emerge from chains of small failures rather than one cartoonishly negligent act. Agentic systems add speed and repeatability. Once a flawed chain is executable, it can run many times before anyone notices.

That makes rate limits and circuit breakers financial controls.

If an agent can make one bad decision per hour, the maximum loss distribution differs from an agent that can make ten thousand in a minute. The probability of error may be identical. The severity is not.

Insurance has always cared about maximum probable loss.

Autonomous systems give that old idea a software expression.

A spending cap, transaction throttle, deployment window, approval threshold, sandbox, rollback mechanism, or reversible queue limits how far one bad inference can travel before the institution regains control.

This is safe autonomy in a form an underwriter can understand.

The phrase matters because autonomy is too often discussed as a binary property: human in the loop or human out of the loop. Real organizations will operate on a gradient. Some actions will be autonomous within bounded authority. Others will escalate. Some will be reversible by design. Others will require human confirmation because the action changes legal rights, moves large sums, affects health, or creates physical risk.

Insurance can help make the gradient explicit.

A premium does not need to reward theatrical human oversight where the human simply clicks approve. It can reward controls that actually reduce loss: separation of duties, independent policy checks, capped authority, test environments, monitoring, provenance, rollback, and meaningful escalation for exceptional actions.

This is important because weak oversight can be worse than no oversight if everyone assumes someone else is paying attention.

The human rubber stamp is not a control.

It is a liability ritual.

A more useful division asks which decisions humans are uniquely positioned to make and which machine checks are more reliable when applied consistently. A deterministic rule that blocks payments above a threshold can be stronger than an exhausted employee scanning hundreds of routine transactions. A human may be better at recognizing that a technically valid transaction makes no sense in context.

Good systems combine different failure modes rather than stacking identical ones.

The accumulation problem follows the same principle.

If thousands of firms use the same model, the same orchestration framework, the same policy engine, and the same agent templates, adding a second insurer does not necessarily diversify the underlying behavior. The market must eventually track technological commonality across insureds just as cyber underwriters track cloud and identity concentration.

This may create an odd new form of exposure reporting.

An insurer could care about model-family concentration the way a property carrier cares about coastal concentration. A reinsurer could ask how much delegated payment authority in its portfolio depends on one vendor or one model release. A common software update could be treated less like an ordinary product revision and more like a weather front moving across many balance sheets at once.

The analogy is imperfect.

The important part is correlation.

A safe model used everywhere can still create a large tail if its rare failure is common to everyone.

This is why version control matters to insurability. If a provider can identify affected deployments, halt a release, preserve old versions where necessary, communicate defects, and support rollback, a common failure can be bounded. If every customer silently receives a behavioral change at once with no ability to reconstruct or reverse it, the same technical quality produces a different insurance risk.

Operational discipline becomes catastrophe mitigation.

None of this implies that insurers already know how to price autonomous-agent liability cleanly. They do not have decades of stable loss history, and the technology, legal duties, and deployment patterns are still changing. Early underwriting will rely on proxies, conservative limits, exclusions, qualitative engineering judgment, and experience borrowed from cyber, software errors and omissions, professional liability, crime, product liability, and other existing lines.

That uncertainty should be stated rather than concealed behind a futuristic product name.

The market will learn by paying claims.

It can learn faster if the failures are observable, the permissions are legible, and the losses are attributed with enough discipline to distinguish a bad model from a bad deployment from a bad tool from a bad business decision.

That is the institutional opportunity.

Insurance does not need to decide whether an agent is intelligent enough to count as an actor.

It needs to decide whether the organization deploying the agent has made action governable enough that a promise can be written behind it.

The frontier of autonomous technology will therefore be shaped by an unexpectedly old requirement.

Someone must be able to say, in advance, how much the mistake can cost.
