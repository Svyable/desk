# Cyber Has No Coastline

A hurricane knows where it is.

Cyber risk often does not.

This sounds obvious, but it changes nearly every intuition the insurance industry developed around catastrophe.

Property insurers think geographically because physical hazards accumulate geographically. A hurricane affects a coast. An earthquake affects a fault region. A wildfire moves through vegetation and built environments. Even a flood follows water and elevation.

Cyber losses can begin in a software dependency nobody on the insurance application thought to list.

A vulnerability appears in a widely used product. A malicious update enters a trusted supply chain. An identity provider fails. A cloud region goes down. A ransomware group discovers a repeatable way into one class of systems. Thousands of companies in different countries and industries discover that their supposed diversification was architectural theater.

They all depended on the same thing.

Insurance has a word for the resulting problem: accumulation.

The word is too polite for what underwriters fear.

Accumulation means one event can trigger many policies at once.

Cyber accumulation is difficult because the common cause can be invisible until the event occurs.

A property underwriter can map every insured roof relative to a coastline.

A cyber underwriter may not know every piece of software running inside every insured company, every outsourced service provider, every cloud dependency, every library inside every vendor product, or every privileged relationship between systems.

The map is hidden inside the stack.

This is why cyber insurance has become a laboratory for the future of insurability.

The peril is economically important, fast-changing, hard to model, strategically adversarial, and potentially systemic. It rewards good security controls but can still punish a well-defended company through a vendor it does not control.

The attacker learns too.

Weather does not read the policy language.

A ransomware crew can.

Cyber criminals can change tactics when defenses improve. They can target insured companies if they believe coverage increases the probability of payment. They can exploit incident-response vendors, data about insurance limits, or known control weaknesses. States can conduct cyber operations for strategic reasons unrelated to ordinary criminal profit.

The hazard is partly endogenous to the defenses built against it.

This makes cyber insurance different from a static natural peril.

It is also why insurance can improve the risk.

A serious cyber underwriter asks questions a small or midsize company may never have forced itself to answer.

Do you use multifactor authentication for privileged access?

Are backups offline or otherwise protected from the production environment?

How quickly do you patch critical vulnerabilities?

What remote access is exposed?

Do you segment networks?

Can an attacker disable security tooling with one administrator account?

Who responds at three in the morning?

Can the company restore operations without paying a ransom?

Insurance can turn these questions into money.

The company with stronger controls may receive better terms. The company without them may face a higher premium, a sublimit, an exclusion, or no offer.

Underwriting becomes a distributed security standard.

This is one of cyber insurance's most valuable functions.

Regulation tells companies what they must do.

Insurance can create an additional price for what they failed to do.

The mechanism is imperfect. Underwriters can rely on checklists that reward documentation rather than resilience. Controls that mattered against last year's attacks may not matter against next year's. A company can buy every fashionable security product and still fail through human error, vendor compromise, or a novel exploit.

But the insurer has an advantage that individual companies do not.

It sees claims across many organizations.

A security vendor can say its product prevents ransomware.

An insurer can observe whether customers using the product actually suffer fewer or smaller claims.

The pool becomes a real-world security experiment.

This loss data is strategically important. It can tell insurers which controls deserve discounts, which industries are underpriced, which vendors create concentration, and which attack patterns are spreading.

It can also create public value if aggregated carefully.

EIOPA has argued that cyber-incident data can function like a public good because a stronger market needs better information about frequency, severity, and accumulation. The problem is that organizations are often reluctant to share cyber incidents for legal, reputational, and security reasons.

The market needs data most when participants have reasons to hide it.

This is the adverse-selection problem in informational form.

A company that has already discovered serious vulnerabilities has more incentive to buy coverage. An insurer that cannot distinguish it from a safer company charges an average price. Safer companies may resent subsidizing weaker ones.

Underwriting controls try to separate them.

Attackers try to erase the separation.

The cycle can be fast.

Ransomware made this visible during the early 2020s. Claims rose sharply. Insurers increased premiums, reduced limits, demanded stronger controls, and changed coverage. The market hardened.

Companies adapted. Security improved in some areas. Insurers gathered more data. Competition returned. Pricing changed again.

This is an insurance cycle operating on software time.

The most difficult issue, however, is not ordinary ransomware frequency.

It is systemic cyber catastrophe.

Imagine a cloud-service outage lasting days across a large share of the economy.

The event may not destroy data. It can still stop payment systems, retail operations, logistics, software services, reservations, manufacturing, healthcare administration, and communications. Thousands of insured businesses could claim business interruption.

Now imagine the cause is a malicious attack rather than operational failure.

Now imagine it crosses from digital disruption into physical damage.

The same initiating event can touch multiple insurance lines.

Cyber.

Property.

Business interruption.

Crime.

Errors and omissions.

Directors and officers.

Marine or cargo.

Workers' compensation in unusual circumstances.

The accumulation travels through policy categories the industry historically managed separately.

Lloyd's made this point starkly in its older "Business Blackout" scenario, which modeled a cyber attack on the U.S. power grid and estimated enormous economic losses spreading across more than thirty lines of insurance. The exact numbers were scenario assumptions, not forecasts. The durable lesson was cross-line accumulation.

Cyber catastrophe does not respect the insurer's org chart.

This is why silent cyber became an industry concern.

A policy written for property or liability may not explicitly discuss cyber events, yet cyber could be the cause of a covered loss. Insurers worried they had accumulated cyber exposure inside policies that were never priced as cyber policies.

The industry responded by clarifying exclusions and affirmative coverage.

Customers sometimes experienced this as insurers narrowing protection.

From the insurer's perspective it was an attempt to know which pool contained the risk.

Ambiguity is dangerous at catastrophe scale.

A small wording dispute can be litigated policy by policy.

A common wording across thousands of policies can become a solvency issue.

This is another reason standardization is double-edged.

Standard forms reduce transaction costs and make insurance understandable across a market.

They also create common legal exposure when a clause is interpreted unexpectedly.

The systemic dependency can be legal as well as technical.

War exclusions illustrate the problem.

Cyber attacks can be conducted by states, state-linked groups, criminals, contractors, patriotic hackers, or actors whose identity remains uncertain. Traditional insurance exclusions for war were developed around physical conflict between states. Applying them to cyber operations has produced disputes over attribution and meaning.

The insurer wants to exclude truly systemic war risk it cannot price.

The policyholder wants cyber coverage that does not disappear precisely when a sophisticated attacker is involved.

The boundary is hard because cyber conflict does not always look like war.

A destructive malware campaign can spread globally after being launched for geopolitical purposes, harming companies that were not intended targets.

Who owns that loss?

Insurance contract language becomes foreign policy in miniature.

The market has been revising war exclusions to make cyber treatment clearer. This is necessary, but it does not solve the underlying capital problem.

If a state-level cyber attack could generate losses beyond private insurance capacity, somebody still carries the tail.

The government may eventually become the reinsurer of last resort for defined systemic cyber events, just as it does for terrorism or flood in other structures.

This possibility should not be treated as an industry bailout plan.

It should be designed before the event around clear thresholds, private retention, security standards, and public purpose.

The worst time to design catastrophe finance is during catastrophe.

Cyber insurance also raises a concentration problem around security vendors themselves.

Suppose every insurer decides that a particular endpoint-security product is best and rewards policyholders for using it.

Adoption rises.

The control improves average security.

It also becomes a common dependency.

A defect or compromise in the product now reaches more insureds.

The safer portfolio can become more correlated.

This is a deep systems problem.

Standardization reduces individual risk and can increase systemic risk.

The same principle applies to cloud platforms, identity providers, payment processors, operating systems, and AI models.

Insurance therefore needs to price both security quality and dependency concentration.

A vendor can be excellent and still be too central.

This is analogous to earthquake engineering. A strong building is safer individually. If an entire city depends on one bridge, the bridge can be superbly engineered and still represent concentration risk.

Cyber underwriters are beginning to think like infrastructure planners.

They need inventories of common dependencies.

The effort is difficult because software supply chains are nested. A company may know its cloud provider but not every library embedded in a vendor application. A vendor may know its code but not every transitive dependency. Mergers and SaaS integrations change architectures constantly.

The exposure graph moves faster than a parcel map.

Artificial intelligence will make it move faster still.

Agents can autonomously use APIs, buy services, execute code, browse the web, and connect tools. This expands the number of machine-to-machine relationships inside businesses. Each relationship can create authentication, authorization, data leakage, fraud, and operational risks.

The old cyber questionnaire asked what systems a company has.

The new one may have to ask what systems its agents are allowed to discover and use dynamically.

Static inventories become less complete.

At the same time, AI can improve defense. Models can detect anomalies, summarize incidents, generate patches, monitor configurations, and help small organizations operate security practices that previously required larger teams.

Attack capability and defense capability both become cheaper.

Insurance sits between them measuring realized loss.

This makes cyber insurance unusually important as an empirical institution.

Security debates can become theoretical. Vendors advertise prevention. Researchers demonstrate vulnerabilities. Regulators mandate controls.

Claims answer a cruder question.

What actually cost money?

The answer should shape pricing.

It should also shape product design.

A good cyber policy does more than reimburse loss. Many policies bundle incident-response services, breach counsel, forensic vendors, notification support, and crisis management. This is insurance as operational infrastructure.

The insurer wants the incident handled quickly because delay increases the claim.

The insured wants expert help because the worst day of its digital life is not the day to start shopping for a forensic firm.

Prearranged response turns the policy into a capability.

This may be the future of insurance more broadly.

Coverage paired with prevention, monitoring, and response.

A property policy can include sensors and mitigation support. A health plan can support prevention. An auto insurer can use telematics and crash response. An AI liability policy can require model governance and incident logging.

Cyber arrived early because the hazard changes too fast for a once-a-year promise to be enough.

The insurer has to stay involved.

Yet the systemic tail remains.

EIOPA's late-2025 financial-stability work described cyber as a potential macrofinancial threat because technological interdependencies can create large economic and insured losses from single events. By July 2026 the regulator's risk dashboard rated digitalisation and cyber risks high, citing frontier AI, geopolitical tensions, and underwriting exposure.

This is not evidence that cyber insurance is impossible.

It is evidence that cyber has crossed from a niche technology policy into infrastructure finance.

The market's task is to separate what can be pooled from what must be backstopped.

Ordinary breach and ransomware risk can be priced, controlled, and diversified.

Common-vulnerability and provider-outage risk can be modeled and limited.

True systemic cyber catastrophe may require a larger layer of capital than private insurers can hold alone.

The same ladder appeared in property over centuries.

The difference is that cyber has no coastline to tell us where to draw the map.

A useful cyber map therefore cannot look like a map at all.

It looks more like a graph.

At one node sits the insured company. Around it sit cloud providers, identity systems, managed-service firms, payment processors, telecommunications carriers, software vendors, open-source components, data platforms, customers, suppliers, and the authentication paths that connect them. Some nodes are replaceable. Others are single points of failure disguised as convenience.

The underwriting question is not only whether each node is secure.

It is what happens when one important node disappears.

This changes the meaning of resilience.

Traditional security programs often emphasize prevention: stop the intrusion, block the malware, patch the flaw. Those controls remain essential. Insurance, because it pays after prevention fails, has reason to care just as much about recovery.

How long can the company operate without its primary cloud environment?

Can it authenticate employees if the identity provider is unavailable?

Are backups merely present, or can they be restored at scale?

Does the business know which manual processes still work?

Can it communicate with customers when normal systems are down?

How many suppliers share the same dependency?

These are not glamorous security questions.

They determine business-interruption severity.

A company that is breached and restored in four hours represents a different insurance risk from an equally breachable company that cannot function for ten days. Frequency matters. Duration does too.

This suggests a more mature underwriting language: not simply security controls, but time to recover.

The shift would parallel property engineering. A sprinkler does not make fire impossible. It changes how far the fire spreads before it is controlled. Compartmentation does not prevent ignition. It limits severity. Cyber resilience needs the same attention to containment.

Network segmentation, access boundaries, restore procedures, alternate communications, vendor substitution, and tested incident authority are digital fire doors.

The metaphor is useful because it resists a common technology fantasy: that enough prevention will eventually produce perfect security.

Insurance has never required a world without fires.

It requires losses that can be bounded.

Bounding cyber loss is particularly difficult when firms outsource critical capabilities to a handful of efficient providers. Centralization can improve ordinary reliability and security because specialist platforms can invest more than small customers. It can simultaneously enlarge the catastrophe radius of a rare failure.

This is not an argument for every company running its own infrastructure badly.

It is an argument for measuring concentration explicitly.

An insurer that rewards a strong cloud provider should also know how much of its portfolio depends on that provider. The two facts belong in the same risk model.

The same goes for AI agents. Giving thousands of firms access to a capable common model can improve productivity and even security. If those firms use the model with similar permissions, similar tools, and similar integrations, a shared failure mode can become an accumulation event.

The model may be safe on average and dangerous in concentration.

Cyber insurance is where the industry can learn to price this distinction before it appears everywhere else.

The claims process can help, but only if incidents are described consistently enough to aggregate. One company's "vendor outage" may be another company's "system failure" and a third company's "cyber incident." If the taxonomy is sloppy, the insurer cannot see concentration until a very large event forces the cases together.

Definitions become a form of sensing.

The market needs to know not merely how much it paid but what common dependency connected the losses.

That requires cooperation among insurers, insureds, vendors, regulators, and incident-response firms without turning sensitive security information into a public exploit manual. The balance is difficult. Too little sharing leaves every insurer partially blind. Too much careless disclosure can create new risk.

The useful middle is structured, privacy-conscious incident information that exposes patterns without exposing victims.

This is one place public institutions can add value even when the underlying insurance remains private.

A common incident language, safe reporting channels, and credible aggregation can improve the entire pool's ability to distinguish ordinary loss from emerging systemic dependence.

The coastlines of property insurance were given by geography.

Cyber's coastlines have to be discovered in the dependency data.

They may move every time a software architecture changes.

That does not make the risk uninsurable.

It makes inventory, recovery, and concentration management part of the policy's financial core.
