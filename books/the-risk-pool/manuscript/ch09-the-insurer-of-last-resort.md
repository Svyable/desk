# The Insurer of Last Resort

The insurer of last resort begins where an ordinary insurance market stops.

A homeowner applies to multiple carriers and is declined. A coastal property cannot obtain wind coverage at a price the owner can carry. Flood risk is too concentrated for private companies to offer broad standardized policies under the existing legal and market structure. Terrorism risk becomes so uncertain after a major attack that insurers pull back from commercial coverage. A pension sponsor fails while workers still expect benefits promised decades earlier.

At that point government usually faces an awkward choice.

Let the risk remain uninsured and accept the economic consequences.

Or widen the pool.

The widened pool can take many forms. It can be a state-created property plan such as California's FAIR Plan or Florida's Citizens. It can be a federal program such as the National Flood Insurance Program. It can be a guarantee corporation such as the Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation. It can be a public-private catastrophe backstop in which private insurers retain ordinary losses and government absorbs some extreme layer.

These institutions are often discussed as exceptions to insurance.

They are better understood as insurance's constitutional edge.

They show which risks a society has decided are too important to leave entirely to voluntary private capacity.

The National Flood Insurance Program is the classic American example because its contradictions are unusually visible.

Flood is a difficult peril for ordinary homeowners insurance. It is spatially concentrated. People most exposed to it have the strongest incentive to buy. Major events can hit many policyholders at once. Mapping and pricing require detailed geographic information. Political pressure for affordable coverage is strong because flood-prone homes are often ordinary homes, not speculative luxury assets.

Private flood insurance existed in limited forms, but the federal government created the NFIP in 1968 to make coverage broadly available and to encourage communities to adopt floodplain-management standards.

The program was never only an insurance company.

It was a bargain between property owners, local land-use decisions, federal risk sharing, and mapping.

A community joined the program and adopted specified floodplain-management rules. Property owners gained access to federal flood insurance. Mortgage requirements later tied coverage to certain federally related loans in designated high-risk zones.

Insurance became a lever for land-use policy.

The bargain also contained the seeds of long-term fiscal tension.

If premiums fully reflect flood risk, some households cannot afford them.

If premiums are subsidized, the program may not collect enough to pay catastrophic claims.

If Congress provides disaster relief regardless of insurance, households can rationally underinsure.

If maps lag changing risk, the price signal weakens.

If development continues in hazardous areas because coverage exists, the program accumulates more exposure.

Every solution changes another part of the system.

The NFIP's debt makes the tension concrete. The Government Accountability Office reported that as of February 2025 the program owed the Treasury about $22.5 billion, even after Congress canceled $16 billion of debt in 2017.

The debt did not arise because every policyholder behaved irresponsibly.

It arose from the collision between catastrophic losses and a program designed with competing goals: affordability, broad access, floodplain management, and fiscal soundness.

Those goals cannot be reconciled by actuarial technique alone.

GAO's work on Risk Rating 2.0 captures the problem well. FEMA's newer pricing methodology improved the relationship between premiums and property-level flood risk. But GAO also warned that moving toward full-risk premiums raises affordability problems and that NFIP's legacy debt is unlikely to be repaid under the existing structure.

The choice is not between truth and subsidy.

The choice is whether the subsidy is explicit.

If government believes some households should receive help buying flood insurance, it can provide means-tested assistance through the budget.

That approach lets the premium continue to carry information about the risk while social policy addresses ability to pay.

A discounted premium hides both.

It tells the homeowner the risk is cheaper than it is and tells the taxpayer less about the support being provided.

This principle is politically difficult because explicit subsidies have constituencies and budget scores.

Hidden subsidies feel like prices.

The same pattern appears in state residual property markets.

California's FAIR Plan is not funded like a federal entitlement. It is an association of licensed insurers operating under state law, with a structure that can ultimately assess participating insurers if losses exceed available resources. As its exposure grew, the question of who bears a catastrophe loss became increasingly important.

The answer does not stop at FAIR Plan policyholders.

If assessments reach insurers, those costs can affect the broader market. If insurers finance assessments, customers elsewhere may eventually feel part of the burden. State reforms can add financing mechanisms or clarify recoupment.

The risk pool expands after the event.

This is one of the defining features of last-resort insurance.

It concentrates difficult risks on the front end and often distributes extreme losses more broadly on the back end.

Florida's Citizens has a different structure but similar logic. It exists to provide property coverage to eligible applicants who cannot find comparable private coverage. Its financing has included premiums, reserves, reinsurance, capital-market protection, and the possibility of assessments under specified conditions.

The assessment mechanism reveals the public character of the institution even though the loss originates with policyholders.

A severe event can reach a wider population through charges imposed on policies or other financing arrangements.

This is social insurance conducted through property markets.

The political difficulty is that residual plans are often asked to be two incompatible things.

They are expected to be available when private insurers refuse.

They are also expected not to compete too aggressively with private insurers.

If the residual plan is much cheaper, customers will prefer it even when private coverage exists. The plan grows and concentrates risk.

If it is much more expensive, households with no alternative experience the institution as a penalty for geography.

States therefore design eligibility rules, rate caps, surcharges, depopulation programs, and coverage limits to keep the residual market residual.

The engineering is messy because the boundary moves with the private market.

When private capacity returns, the public pool is supposed to shrink.

Florida's recent Citizens depopulation demonstrates this. Hundreds of thousands of policies moved back to private carriers as market conditions improved. The institution had to manage not only claims and underwriting but its own contraction.

A last-resort insurer should be capable of getting smaller.

That sounds obvious.

Public risk programs are politically easier to create than to unwind.

Once households, brokers, lenders, employees, contractors, and local economies adapt to them, withdrawal creates its own disruption.

The most dangerous public insurance programs therefore become permanent without ever deciding what permanent role they are meant to play.

Temporary crisis capacity quietly turns into infrastructure.

This is not always a failure.

Some risks genuinely require durable public participation.

The question is whether the institution's design acknowledges that fact.

The Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation makes the logic clearer in a different domain. PBGC insures defined-benefit pension promises made by private employers within statutory limits. If a covered pension plan terminates without enough assets, PBGC can become responsible for guaranteed benefits.

The program exists because a pension promise can outlive the company that made it.

Workers cannot diversify a failed employer retroactively. Their wages, career, and retirement promise may have been tied to the same institution.

Pension insurance pools some of that sponsor-failure risk across the system.

PBGC's 2025 annual report showed both its single-employer and multiemployer programs in positive net financial positions, a substantial improvement from earlier periods of severe concern.

The example is useful because public insurance is not inherently insolvent.

Design, premiums, investment, statutory changes, economic conditions, and government support matter.

A public backstop can become stronger.

It can also become weaker if political promises outrun financing.

The same is true of deposit insurance.

Although this book focuses on insurance companies rather than banking, deposit insurance is one of the clearest examples of a government guarantee changing behavior and stabilizing a system at the same time.

Before deposit insurance, bank customers had strong reason to run at the first sign of trouble because arriving early could determine whether they recovered money. Insuring deposits reduced that incentive for ordinary depositors and helped make banking more stable.

It also reduced depositor monitoring of bank risk.

Regulation had to replace some of the discipline the market no longer supplied.

This is the universal backstop tradeoff.

Public protection can stop panic.

It can also weaken private incentives to avoid the protected risk.

The solution is not to reject backstops.

It is to pair them with rules that reduce moral hazard.

Flood insurance should be connected to floodplain management and mitigation.

Deposit insurance should be connected to bank supervision and capital requirements.

Pension insurance should be connected to funding rules and sponsor premiums.

Property residual plans should be connected to mitigation, underwriting, and development policy.

A guarantee without risk discipline is a subsidy to fragility.

A guarantee with impossible conditions is not a useful guarantee.

This balance becomes especially important for catastrophe risk because the private market can be healthy right up until a tail event overwhelms ordinary capital.

Terrorism insurance after September 11 offers a model of layered public-private risk sharing. The attacks created enormous insured losses and, more importantly for future coverage, revealed a class of risk that was deliberate, potentially correlated, and difficult to model from historical frequency.

Insurers sharply restricted terrorism coverage. Commercial lenders and property markets then confronted a problem: buildings and projects could be required to carry coverage that the market no longer wanted to sell broadly.

Congress created a federal terrorism insurance backstop.

The goal was not to nationalize all terrorism insurance.

It was to make private coverage possible by placing a federal layer behind extreme losses.

This layered structure is likely to become more common as new systemic risks emerge.

Pandemic insurance is an obvious candidate, though GAO's analysis shows why it is difficult. A pandemic can close businesses across the country simultaneously. Private pooling works poorly when nearly every insured has a correlated business-interruption claim.

Government would have to carry a very large share of the tail.

Cyber risk may eventually create similar pressure if a catastrophic event exploits common infrastructure across thousands of policyholders.

AI-related liability could too if widely used systems create correlated failures or courts expand duties unexpectedly.

Climate catastrophe is already pushing states toward larger residual pools and renewed debate over federal roles.

The design question is where the public layer should attach.

If government covers ordinary predictable losses, private underwriting loses much of its purpose.

If government covers only truly extreme, correlated layers, private insurers can retain incentives to price and mitigate ordinary risk while society absorbs the tail it has decided cannot be left to market withdrawal.

This resembles reinsurance at national scale.

The state becomes the reinsurer of last resort.

There are good reasons to be cautious. Government catastrophe protection can be mispriced for political reasons. It can encourage development in dangerous places. It can transfer wealth toward owners of expensive exposed assets. It can create enormous contingent liabilities that remain invisible in ordinary budgets.

A government guarantee is still a liability even when no check has been written this year.

Public accounting often makes contingent risk harder to see than direct spending.

Insurance accounting has the same temptation. A quiet year produces premium revenue and few claims. The true cost of the promise sits in the tail.

Public programs need the same discipline private insurers do: scenario analysis, transparent exposure, reserves or financing plans, and rules for how losses will be distributed.

They also need something private insurers do not.

A democratic theory of who deserves support.

Should public insurance preserve every existing property regardless of risk?

Should subsidies be limited by income?

Should second homes or luxury properties receive the same support as primary residences?

Should new construction in known high-risk areas qualify for the same backstop as older communities built under different assumptions?

Should repeated claims trigger buyouts or coverage changes?

These are not actuarial questions.

They are allocation questions.

The insurer of last resort cannot avoid them because it exists precisely where private pricing produced an outcome society rejected.

That is why these institutions are so revealing.

They expose the values hidden inside ordinary insurance markets.

A private insurer can say: this risk does not meet our underwriting criteria.

A public insurer eventually has to answer a harder question.

If not us, then who?

A serious answer should begin before the public program is created.

Last-resort insurance needs an attachment point, even when nobody uses that phrase. The institution should know what failure in the private market is meant to trigger its role. Is the problem that no carrier will quote? That available quotes exceed a defined affordability threshold? That the market has withdrawn after a catastrophe? That a peril is structurally correlated across nearly everyone? That the underlying activity—homeownership, commercial lending, pensions, terrorism coverage—is socially important enough that interruption would create larger economic damage?

Different triggers justify different institutions.

A residual property plan is not the same thing as a catastrophe reinsurer. A residual plan writes individual policies private carriers reject. A catastrophe backstop can leave the individual policy with private insurers and enter only after aggregate losses cross a threshold.

Confusing those roles can make government carry too much risk too early.

The cleanest public layer is often the one that preserves as much private information and discipline below it as possible.

Private insurers still compete for customers. They still inspect buildings, evaluate controls, adjust claims, price ordinary frequency, and retain meaningful loss. Reinsurance and capital markets can sit above them. Government attaches only where correlation becomes so large or politically essential that private withdrawal would damage the broader economy.

This is not always feasible.

Some residual markets exist precisely because the private layer has already disappeared for certain customers.

But the principle remains useful: do not socialize the risk that private actors can still efficiently price and manage merely because public financing looks cheaper in a quiet year.

Government appears to have cheap capital partly because taxpayers stand behind it.

That backing is real economic capacity, not free money.

The next design question is entry.

Who qualifies for the public pool?

If eligibility is broad and the price is attractive, the public program can pull good risks out of the private market and grow far beyond its original purpose. If eligibility is too narrow, households can be left with nominal access and no usable option.

The boundary should be visible enough that policymakers can tell whether growth reflects genuine market retreat, aggressive public pricing, or a change in eligibility rules.

Enrollment is not merely an administrative statistic.

It is a measurement of where risk is migrating.

Then comes exit.

Can the policyholder move back to private insurance when capacity returns? Can a private carrier assume blocks of residual business? Are rates and coverage structured so that the public plan does not become the permanent cheapest option? Does the institution have an operational incentive to shrink, or does every participant around it benefit from continued scale?

A last-resort program without an exit mechanism can become a first-resort market by inertia.

That may eventually be a legitimate policy choice.

It should be named as one.

The third test is prevention.

A public pool should know whether it is financing resilience or merely financing repetition. If a property can be hardened, if a community can reduce flood exposure, if a pension sponsor can improve funding, or if a cyber program can require controls, access to the backstop can be connected to those actions.

The connection must remain realistic. A low-income homeowner cannot rebuild a neighborhood's drainage system alone. A small business cannot eliminate a national cloud concentration. Requirements should attach to the actor capable of changing the risk.

Otherwise risk discipline becomes blame assigned to the weakest participant.

The fourth test is loss allocation.

Every public insurance program should be able to answer, in ordinary language, what happens after a loss larger than current premiums and reserves can absorb.

Who is assessed?

Who can borrow?

Who guarantees the debt?

Which policyholders can be surcharged?

When does the treasury enter?

What is the maximum plausible contingent liability?

If these answers are obscure before catastrophe, they will become political emergencies afterward.

Transparency does not make the tail smaller.

It makes the social contract visible.

The final test is whether the backstop preserves the possibility of saying no to new risk.

This is the most uncomfortable one.

A society may reasonably protect a family that bought a home decades ago under different hazard assumptions. It need not make the same promise to every developer proposing new construction in a place where the public pool already carries concentrated danger.

Insurance of last resort should not automatically become permission to create unlimited new exposure for the last resort to absorb.

Existing vulnerability and newly chosen vulnerability are not morally or economically identical.

The distinction gives public insurance a chance to be humane without becoming self-defeating.

Protect people caught by a changing risk landscape.

Make future decisions confront the landscape more honestly.

That is a more durable purpose than pretending the public pool has made the hazard disappear.
