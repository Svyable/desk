# The Insurability Frontier

Insurance works best when losses are uncertain at the individual level and stable enough at the group level.

That sentence is doing more work than it appears to.

If an event is certain, the premium becomes a prepayment plan.

If an event is impossible to estimate, the insurer cannot know what to charge.

If one event can hit nearly everyone at once, diversification fails.

If the loss can be manipulated by the insured, incentives collapse.

If courts can expand liability unpredictably for decades after the policy is sold, the tail can outrun the price.

If the insurer cannot obtain reliable data, capital markets and regulators will doubt its reserves.

The boundary around insurable risk is therefore not fixed by nature.

It moves with information, law, technology, capital, engineering, and institutions.

Risks once considered too strange to insure can become ordinary.

Risks once considered ordinary can become difficult.

This moving boundary is the insurability frontier.

The history of insurance can be read as a long campaign to push that frontier outward.

Ocean voyages became more financeable when underwriters learned to price ships, cargoes, routes, seasons, and war.

Urban fire became more financeable when cities, building practices, and loss records improved.

Life insurance expanded with mortality tables, medical underwriting, and larger pools.

Aviation liability became insurable as flight operations, safety standards, legal regimes, and reinsurance developed.

Catastrophe models turned hurricane and earthquake risk from a regional intuition into a portfolio calculation.

Cyber insurance emerged from a collection of technology, crime, privacy, and business-interruption risks that traditional policies had not been designed to separate cleanly.

Insurance does not merely discover a frontier.

It builds one.

Data makes some uncertainty measurable.

Engineering makes some losses preventable.

Reinsurance makes some accumulation financeable.

Contract language makes some boundaries enforceable.

Government backstops make some tails survivable.

Courts make some liabilities predictable enough to reserve.

Capital markets create another place to send catastrophe risk.

Remove enough of those supports and a familiar risk can move backward across the frontier.

This is happening now in several places at once.

Climate is making some property risk less stationary.

Cyber is making correlation less geographic.

Artificial intelligence is creating new liability paths and new concentrations of infrastructure.

Geopolitical fragmentation is changing war, trade, sanctions, supply-chain, and political-risk exposures.

Longevity changes the duration of life and pension liabilities.

Medical technology can extend life while raising treatment cost.

The same technological systems that reduce some risks can create dependencies that concentrate others.

The frontier is not moving in one direction.

It is folding.

Consider the data center.

At first glance, it looks like an industrial property risk.

There is a building.

There are servers.

There is electricity.

There is cooling equipment.

There are fire systems and physical security.

The insurer can price property damage and business interruption.

Then scale changes the object.

By 2026, some proposed AI data-center projects had capital costs measured in tens of billions of dollars. They combined enormous electrical demand, dense computing hardware, batteries, liquid cooling, specialized construction, network connectivity, and dependence on regional power systems. A single campus could represent extraordinary insured value. Multiple tenants and customers could depend on it. The same cloud or model provider might support thousands of insured businesses downstream.

The property risk becomes an accumulation risk.

A fire is not just a fire.

It may interrupt a shared service.

A grid failure is not just a utility loss.

It may halt computation across a portfolio of companies.

A cyberattack is not just a breach at one insured.

It may spread through a provider that sits underneath thousands of insureds.

Swiss Re has explicitly highlighted this accumulation problem in its 2026 work on insuring AI data-center expansion. The challenge is not that insurers have never covered expensive buildings.

It is that physical value, digital dependency, and common infrastructure are being concentrated in the same places.

This is what moves a risk toward the frontier.

The loss channels start talking to one another.

Traditional insurance lines are built partly around separation.

Property covers physical damage.

Cyber covers defined digital events.

Professional liability covers errors in service.

Directors-and-officers coverage addresses certain management liabilities.

Business interruption covers income loss under specified triggers.

Each line has exclusions, limits, and definitions intended to make the exposure legible.

A systemic event ignores the organizational chart of the insurance industry.

The same event can touch all of them.

The insurer then has to ask an uncomfortable question: how many policies are secretly one risk?

This is the accumulation problem.

A portfolio can look diversified because it contains thousands of customers.

If all of them depend on the same cloud provider, identity service, payments network, model API, operating system, chip architecture, logistics node, or power region, the diversification can be imaginary.

The policy count is large.

The dependency count is small.

Modern insurance will increasingly need dependency maps, not just policy maps.

Cyber taught this lesson first.

A ransomware campaign or software vulnerability can produce losses across sectors simultaneously. A vulnerability in widely used software can become a common cause hidden beneath unrelated policyholders. Insurers responded by tightening terms, improving aggregation modeling, asking more detailed security questions, buying reinsurance, and developing exclusions for warlike or systemic scenarios.

The market did not disappear.

It evolved because the risk could still be bounded enough to sell.

That phrase—bounded enough—is the practical definition of insurability.

The insurer does not need certainty.

It needs a credible maximum loss distribution relative to its capital and reinsurance.

When the upper tail becomes impossible to bound, capacity vanishes quickly.

This is why war is so often excluded.

War is not merely severe.

It is strategic, correlated, legally complex, and capable of destroying the economic system in which the insurance contract is denominated. A private insurer cannot diversify a national war the same way it diversifies house fires.

Nuclear war is the extreme example.

The problem is not that actuaries have failed to find the correct premium.

The problem is that the insurer, reinsurer, banks, courts, policyholders, currency, and physical infrastructure may all fail together.

Some events sit outside private insurance because they attack the container in which insurance operates.

This distinction matters when people ask whether everything should be insurable.

No.

Some risks should remain with the state.

Some with the firm.

Some with the individual.

Some should be prohibited rather than insured.

Some should be reduced until they become insurable.

Insurance is one layer of risk governance, not the final destination of every danger.

The frontier can also move because law changes.

Liability insurance depends on legal predictability more than policyholders often realize.

An insurer selling coverage today may pay claims years later after courts interpret duties, causation, damages, exclusions, and aggregation. If legal standards change gradually, pricing can adapt. If liability expands retroactively or unpredictably, a policy sold under one expectation can produce losses under another.

This is called social inflation when rising claim severity reflects litigation trends, larger awards, legal strategies, and related forces beyond ordinary price inflation.

The phrase is industry language and can sometimes be used too broadly.

Still, the underlying issue is real.

Long-tail insurance is a promise made under one legal environment and paid under a later one.

AI liability could intensify this problem because the law is still forming.

When an autonomous system causes harm, responsibility may be divided among the model developer, application provider, deployer, data supplier, user, hardware operator, and professional who relied on the output. Different jurisdictions may treat the chain differently. Contractual indemnities may shift losses between firms. Regulators may create new duties. Courts may develop standards slowly through cases.

An insurer can cover uncertainty.

It struggles with undefined responsibility.

This is why insurance often pushes industries toward clearer standards.

A policy application can ask whether a company follows a recognized control framework.

A warranty can require certain safeguards.

Coverage can distinguish tested deployments from experimental ones.

Insurers can price organizations with audit logs differently from organizations without them.

Loss data can reveal which controls actually matter.

Over time, an emerging risk can move inward from the frontier and become a normal line of business.

Insurance participates in the domestication of technology.

The process is not automatic.

It requires enough independent experience to separate signal from hype.

AI creates a peculiar problem because the technology itself may change faster than the claims history can mature.

A model released this year may be obsolete before long-tail liability emerges.

The deployment architecture may change.

The agent may gain new tools.

Regulation may change.

The loss experience of yesterday’s system may be a poor guide to tomorrow’s.

Underwriters then rely more heavily on scenarios, controls, limits, and conservative capital.

This makes insurance expensive precisely when society wants a new technology to scale quickly.

That friction can look anti-innovation.

It can also be the market asking whether the innovation has learned how to fail.

Mature technologies do not merely work.

They fail in bounded ways.

Airplanes have redundant systems, maintenance programs, incident reporting, certification, operating procedures, trained crews, investigation regimes, and liability structures. Banks have capital rules, liquidity requirements, settlement infrastructure, deposit insurance, audits, recovery planning, and resolution regimes. Pharmaceuticals have trials, manufacturing standards, pharmacovigilance, labeling, and liability.

These systems remain imperfect.

Their failures have shape.

Insurability grows when failure acquires shape.

AI systems are still acquiring it.

The same is true of climate adaptation.

A wildfire-exposed community can move toward greater insurability if building standards improve, vegetation is managed, utilities harden equipment, access roads improve, water systems become more resilient, and insurers can verify those changes.

A floodplain can move toward greater insurability if drainage, barriers, elevation, land-use restrictions, and relocation reduce expected loss.

The physical hazard may remain.

The insured loss distribution changes.

This is why insurability is not a permanent label attached to a place or technology.

It is a relationship between hazard and capacity.

Capacity includes money.

A reinsurer with more capital can take a larger risk than a small mutual insurer.

A global market can absorb what a local market cannot.

A catastrophe bond can add capacity outside traditional insurance balance sheets.

A government backstop can support a tail private markets reject.

As capital increases, the frontier moves outward.

Then concentration can move it back.

The AI infrastructure boom illustrates this double movement.

It creates enormous insurance demand for construction, property, equipment, liability, energy, cyber, and interruption.

It also creates assets so large and interconnected that the same buildout can stress the industry’s ability to provide limits.

Insurance can enable the investment and become a bottleneck to it.

That tension is likely to become familiar across the energy transition too.

New grids, batteries, offshore wind, hydrogen systems, carbon capture, nuclear projects, and new industrial processes all require risk capital. Some have limited loss histories. Some use unfamiliar technologies at enormous scale. Some are exposed to natural catastrophe. Some depend on changing subsidies or regulation.

Developers often discover that a project is technically viable and financially attractive before it is conventionally insurable.

The insurance market becomes part of the engineering process.

Lenders ask for coverage.

Insurers ask for design changes.

Reinsurers ask about aggregation.

Governments consider guarantees.

Standards bodies formalize practice.

A technology crosses the frontier through negotiation among these institutions.

This is why insurance deserves to be considered part of innovation policy.

A society that wants rapid deployment of new technologies needs systems for financing their failure.

Subsidizing success is only half of industrial policy.

Someone must decide what happens when the factory burns, the battery ignites, the software fails, the turbine breaks, the autonomous system injures someone, or the project underperforms because a hazard nobody modeled appears.

If every failure can destroy the developer, innovation becomes fragile.

If every failure is socialized, innovation becomes reckless.

Insurance sits between those extremes.

It allows failure without making failure free.

That role changes the scale of who can participate.

A giant corporation can retain risks that would destroy a smaller firm. It can self-insure, spread projects across regions, negotiate bespoke contracts, fund captives, post collateral, and absorb uninsured losses from cash flow. A startup or municipal utility cannot do these things as easily.

When a new technology remains beyond ordinary insurance, deployment naturally favors the largest balance sheets.

This is an underappreciated competition effect.

The absence of insurance is not neutral. It can create a private minimum-capital requirement more severe than any regulator wrote. Only firms wealthy enough to carry the tail themselves can enter.

When insurance becomes available, smaller firms can rent balance-sheet strength.

The premium may be expensive. It can still democratize participation compared with requiring every entrant to survive the maximum loss alone.

This is one reason standardization matters so much at the frontier.

Bespoke risk can be insured, but bespoke underwriting is expensive. An insurer may spend weeks understanding a novel battery chemistry, an autonomous industrial process, or an AI deployment. The transaction can justify that effort for a billion-dollar project. It cannot for ten thousand small businesses each seeking a modest policy.

Standards compress underwriting cost.

A recognized fire code, cybersecurity framework, product certification, incident taxonomy, or model-governance protocol lets the insurer rely on shared evidence instead of reconstructing the technology from first principles every time.

The frontier therefore moves inward through boring documents.

Test protocols.

Definitions.

Inspection records.

Version histories.

Loss reports.

Contract wording.

The institutions that create these artifacts rarely look like engines of innovation. They are.

They turn one-off experiments into categories capital can recognize.

There is a danger here too.

A standard can freeze the wrong architecture. An incumbent can shape certification so smaller rivals face disproportionate compliance cost. An insurer can demand controls that are easy to audit rather than controls that actually reduce loss. A regulator can treat the existence of a checklist as proof of safety.

The frontier can be pushed outward cosmetically without the underlying risk becoming better.

Claims eventually expose the difference.

This is why near-miss and failure reporting are as important as certification. Aviation did not become insurable because airplanes received certificates and then stopped failing. The system learned because incidents were investigated, causes were distributed, maintenance changed, designs changed, and responsibility became more legible.

Emerging technologies need the same institutional memory.

An insurer should care about the incident that nearly caused a loss, not only the claim that did. A regulator should care about patterns across operators. A reinsurer should care when multiple insureds share a control weakness. A standards body should revise practice when failure data contradicts the assumption behind the standard.

Insurability is not a one-time gate.

It is a learning loop.

The frontier also has a political dimension because societies can choose to move it with public capital.

A government guarantee can make an otherwise unfinanceable project appear insurable by placing the extreme tail on taxpayers. This can be justified for infrastructure, national security, or technologies with large public benefits. It can also disguise a project whose private economics do not support its risk.

The right question is not whether government moved the frontier.

Governments always shape it through law, liability, standards, emergency response, and credit.

The right question is whether the public is being paid or compensated for the risk it takes, whether private parties still retain enough exposure to care about failure, and whether the public benefit is large enough to justify the contingent liability.

A public backstop should make a new market possible without making bad engineering cheap.

The same principle applies to property in a changing climate. Public reinsurance can keep private insurers writing in a difficult region. If it is paired with stronger codes, mitigation, land-use discipline, and a transition plan, it can buy time for adaptation. If it simply makes the tail cheaper while development continues unchanged, it moves the frontier on paper and the hazard in the opposite direction.

Insurability should be treated as a diagnostic, not a political trophy.

A place or technology becoming easier to insure can mean risk genuinely fell, information improved, capital became cheaper, or government assumed more of the tail. Those are different achievements.

A place becoming harder to insure can mean hazard rose, legal uncertainty increased, capital withdrew, market competition weakened, or models became more pessimistic. Those are different failures.

The premium alone does not tell us which occurred.

The institutional job is to decompose the signal before acting on it.

That decomposition matters because the wrong remedy can push the frontier further away. Subsidize a risk that needs engineering and exposure grows. Mandate engineering when the true problem is market power and households spend money without gaining capacity. Cap prices when the problem is capital scarcity and availability shrinks. Create a broad public guarantee when the risk is ordinary and private prevention weakens.

The frontier is a systems property.

Moving one boundary moves others.

The insurability frontier therefore tells us something about institutional maturity.

A risk far beyond the frontier is either too unknown, too correlated, too manipulable, too politically unstable, or too destructive for ordinary private pooling.

A risk comfortably inside the frontier has enough data, controls, capital, legal clarity, and diversification to support a market.

The movement between those states is one of civilization’s quieter forms of progress.

The next great technologies will not be fully real when the demo works.

They will be real when society knows how to absorb their failure.
