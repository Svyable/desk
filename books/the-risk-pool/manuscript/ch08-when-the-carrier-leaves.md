# When the Carrier Leaves

An insurer can say no in several ways.

The bluntest is a cancellation or nonrenewal notice.

More often the refusal happens upstream. The company stops accepting new policies in a region. It raises underwriting standards. It requires a newer roof. It caps the share of homes it will cover in a ZIP Code. It refuses houses near brush. It reduces agent appointments. It changes reinsurance. It offers a policy, but with a deductible or exclusion that makes the customer keep more of the risk.

The market retreats before the logo disappears.

This distinction matters because insurance availability is easy to mismeasure.

A state may have dozens of licensed carriers while individual homeowners can obtain only one or two realistic quotes. A carrier may be active statewide while avoiding specific areas. A customer may technically receive an offer that is unaffordable. A policy may exist but exclude the peril the customer fears most.

Availability is not a count of company names.

It is the existence of usable capacity at a price and on terms that let the underlying activity continue.

California's homeowners market turned this idea into a public argument during the first half of the 2020s.

Large insurers reduced new business or announced limits on new policies, citing wildfire exposure, rebuilding costs, reinsurance, and the state's regulatory environment among other factors. Homeowners in exposed areas reported difficulty finding coverage. The California FAIR Plan, designed as a basic insurer of last resort, grew rapidly.

The debate quickly split into familiar camps.

Insurers argued that prices and models had to reflect current risk and cost.

Consumer advocates warned that companies could use catastrophe models and withdrawal threats to demand large rate increases.

Regulators faced the most difficult position because both failure modes were real.

Approve inadequate prices and private capacity can leave.

Approve unaffordable prices and households can be priced out.

This is the insurance regulator's version of a problem central banks know well: the policy tool affects more than one objective.

Rate regulation looks simple from outside the industry. A company proposes a price. The regulator reviews it. If the price is excessive, reject it.

The difficulty is that the "correct" price depends on expectations about future losses, expenses, capital, reinsurance, investment income, and behavior. The most important future catastrophe has not happened yet. If it had, the problem would be accounting rather than underwriting.

A regulator is reviewing a forecast disguised as a filing.

The forecast can be manipulated.

It can also be right before the public is ready to hear it.

California historically placed significant weight on observed loss experience and regulatory approval. As wildfire losses increased and insurers sought greater use of catastrophe modelling and reinsurance costs in pricing, the state moved toward reforms intended to restore market participation while requiring insurers to write more business in high-risk areas.

The details are specific to California, but the structural bargain is general.

Private capital says: let us price the risk we believe exists.

Government says: if we allow that price, you must serve more of the market.

The arrangement recognizes that price and availability are linked.

It also recognizes the insurer's market access as a form of leverage.

A company that wants access to millions of lower-risk customers can be asked to maintain some presence in harder markets.

This resembles universal-service obligations in utilities and telecommunications. Profitable service areas help support a broader social expectation of access.

Insurance is not legally a public utility in the ordinary sense.

Yet when a policy becomes necessary for housing finance, the political pressure starts to look similar.

A household cannot simply decide that homeowners insurance is an optional luxury if the mortgage requires it.

This makes market exit different from a restaurant closing.

The consumer is not merely losing a brand.

She may be losing access to credit.

Florida reached the problem through a different path.

The state's property market has long been shaped by hurricane risk, a large coastal population, volatile reinsurance costs, litigation, roofing claims, assignment-of-benefits practices, fraud allegations, insolvencies, and periodic legislative reform. Several carriers failed or reduced business after difficult loss years. Citizens Property Insurance Corporation grew as the insurer of last resort.

Then the cycle changed.

Florida enacted legal reforms intended in part to reduce litigation-related costs. New and existing private insurers expanded. Citizens used a large depopulation program to transfer hundreds of thousands of policies back to private carriers.

By late 2025 Citizens expected a sharply lower policy count than earlier in the year. Its 2026 operating budget described the operational challenge of shrinking an institution that had recently grown much larger.

The story is useful because insurance crises are often described as one-way decline.

They can reverse.

Capital returns when the expected return changes.

Prices rise. Legal conditions change. Reinsurance becomes cheaper. Competitors enter. Loss experience improves. A residual pool shrinks.

This does not mean the underlying hurricane risk disappeared.

It means the market's ability and willingness to carry that risk improved.

Insurability is economic, not purely physical.

A hurricane-prone house can become easier to insure without becoming less hurricane-prone if capital conditions or legal costs improve.

The reverse is also true. A physically unchanged house can become harder to insure because reinsurance costs rise, construction inflation accelerates, or the insurer's portfolio becomes too concentrated.

This is why every premium increase should not be read as a climate verdict, and every premium decrease should not be read as proof that climate concerns were exaggerated.

Insurance prices multiple moving systems at once.

Market exit is one of the mechanisms that reveals which system dominates.

An insurer can leave because risk is too high.

It can leave because rates are too low.

It can leave because litigation costs are too unpredictable.

It can leave because reinsurance is scarce.

It can leave because headquarters wants to deploy capital elsewhere.

It can leave because a rating agency is worried about catastrophe concentration.

It can leave because the company itself underwrote badly and needs to shrink.

A public debate that assumes every exit has one cause will usually produce one-dimensional policy.

The customer rarely cares which reason dominates.

She needs a policy.

This mismatch between causal complexity and consumer urgency makes insurance crises politically explosive.

The household receives a notice with a date.

The policy debate produces reports.

The notice wins.

Residual markets exist partly to bridge that difference in time.

California's FAIR Plan, Florida's Citizens, coastal wind pools, and similar mechanisms preserve a baseline of coverage when voluntary insurers refuse or restrict it. Their presence can prevent an immediate collapse in mortgage availability and property transactions.

But residual plans carry a dangerous temptation.

They can make market failure less visible.

If every rejected homeowner can be placed somewhere, politicians can postpone the harder question of whether the risk has been correctly priced, mitigated, or relocated.

The residual pool grows quietly until a catastrophe exposes how much risk accumulated there.

California's FAIR Plan published roughly $768 billion in exposure by June 2026. The number is not the same as probable loss; only a fraction would be affected by any one event. Still, it indicates how much property value had migrated into a mechanism built for risks the voluntary market did not want to carry normally.

A residual plan can concentrate the exact risks diversification would usually spread.

This is why its funding structure matters.

If premiums and reserves are insufficient after a major event, who pays next?

The answer can include assessments on participating insurers, reinsurance, debt, policyholder surcharges, or public mechanisms depending on the system.

A loss that began in a hard-to-insure neighborhood can eventually reach customers and companies far from the fire line.

The pool becomes statewide after the disaster.

This creates an argument about fairness that is almost impossible to settle cleanly.

Why should a renter in a low-risk city indirectly pay for an expensive house in a fire corridor?

Why should a homeowner who followed every mitigation rule lose access because the wider region is risky?

Why should a family that bought thirty years ago under different hazard assumptions be treated like a developer choosing to build new luxury homes in a dangerous area today?

Insurance markets tend to compress these morally different cases into risk classes.

Public policy can distinguish them if it chooses.

That distinction is one of the most important tools available.

Existing households and new development do not have to receive identical treatment.

A state can provide means-tested support for existing low-income homeowners while requiring new construction to pay closer to full risk. It can subsidize mitigation rather than premiums. It can impose stricter codes in exchange for access to public insurance. It can buy out repeatedly damaged properties. It can restrict development where evacuation or infrastructure cannot support the exposure.

These policies recognize a basic principle.

Affordability assistance and risk pricing do not need to be the same instrument.

When they are combined, the subsidy becomes hidden inside the premium.

Hidden subsidies are politically easy at first because nobody receives a tax bill labeled "support for high-risk property."

They are economically dangerous because the price no longer communicates the risk.

A better design can charge the risk and subsidize the person transparently.

This is not painless. Somebody still pays.

But the system preserves information.

The same logic applies to insurer exits.

A government can prohibit nonrenewals temporarily after disaster to prevent chaos. It can require notice, appeals, mitigation credits, or data transparency. It can condition market participation on serving distressed areas. It can operate a residual plan.

What it cannot do indefinitely is declare a private risk cheap by statute.

If expected losses and required returns remain above allowed prices, the gap will reappear as reduced capacity, weaker coverage, capital stress, or public exposure.

Markets ration somehow.

The form of rationing changes.

This is why the phrase insurer greed, while emotionally satisfying in some cases, is not a complete theory of market exit.

Insurance companies seek profit. That is unsurprising. Competition, regulation, and capital requirements exist partly to constrain how they pursue it.

But profit-seeking can produce both entry and exit.

A state with attractive pricing draws capital. A state with unattractive pricing loses it.

The policy task is to make the competitive return consistent with socially acceptable access.

That may require reducing the underlying loss cost rather than merely bargaining over margins.

Florida's experience with litigation reform illustrates this. Whether one agrees with every provision or distributional effect, changes that reduced expected legal costs could lower the actuarially indicated premium without changing the wind.

The market became more attractive because one component of loss changed.

This is a powerful lesson for climate adaptation.

Not every dollar of insurance pressure is physically immutable.

Some risk can be engineered down.

Roof standards can reduce wind losses. Defensible space and building materials can reduce fire vulnerability. Drainage can reduce flood severity. Land-use decisions can reduce future concentration. Claims processes can reduce fraud and legal costs. Data can improve underwriting. Reinsurance structures can spread tail risk.

An insurance crisis should therefore trigger a forensic question.

Which part of the price can actually be changed?

If the answer is hazard, invest in mitigation.

If the answer is exposure, reconsider where and how to build.

If the answer is litigation, improve the legal system without destroying legitimate claims.

If the answer is reinsurance scarcity, examine public catastrophe layers or capital-market alternatives.

If the answer is market power, improve competition.

If the answer is household poverty, subsidize the household rather than corrupting the risk signal.

This decomposition is less satisfying than a villain.

It is more useful.

The insurer's threat to leave can itself be a source of market power. Companies know regulators fear availability crises. A carrier can use capacity decisions as leverage in rate negotiations or legislative debates.

That possibility requires scrutiny.

Transparency about underwriting changes, exposure, claims, reinsurance, and rate assumptions helps distinguish credible retreat from strategic pressure.

Regulators need independent modelling capability rather than relying entirely on company submissions.

Consumers need enough notice to shop and mitigate.

Competition policy matters because a concentrated market gives each exit more weight.

The goal is not to make exit impossible.

The goal is to make the reason legible.

Insurance markets need the ability to say no. Without it, underwriting ceases to be underwriting.

Societies also need to decide what happens after the no.

That decision determines whether a private refusal becomes a household crisis, a public pool, a mitigation program, a lower property value, a development restriction, or a taxpayer liability.

The carrier's exit is not the end of risk allocation.

It is the moment the allocation becomes visible enough that someone else has to answer.

There is one more institution in that answer that consumers rarely see: the insurer's own capital committee.

Insurance capacity is not produced policy by policy. It is allocated across a portfolio. A company may be perfectly willing to insure one fire-prone house in isolation and unwilling to insure the ten-thousandth similar house because the next policy adds too much to a catastrophe concentration already sitting on the balance sheet.

The marginal no can therefore be caused by all the earlier yeses.

This is why underwriting restrictions sometimes feel arbitrary from the sidewalk. The house may be well maintained. The owner may have no claims. The carrier may still refuse because the relevant unit of risk is not the parcel but the portfolio.

The same logic reaches upward to reinsurance and ratings. A primary insurer that keeps adding concentrated catastrophe exposure may have to buy more reinsurance, hold more capital, accept a different return, or explain the concentration to agencies and investors that judge its financial strength. The retail underwriting rule that annoys one homeowner can be the last visible link in a chain of wholesale constraints.

Capacity is a stack.

The policyholder sees the bottom layer.

That stack also explains why market recovery can be nonlinear. A modest improvement in expected profitability may attract no one when capital is scarce and uncertainty is high. Then legal rules change, reinsurance softens, new investors enter, loss experience improves, and several carriers decide nearly at once that the same market is worth writing again.

The houses did not all become safer on Tuesday.

The capital stack crossed a threshold.

This makes timing central to regulation.

If a state responds to a temporary hard market with permanent subsidies, it can lock in a public role just as private capital is preparing to return. If it assumes every private return proves the crisis was invented, it may unwind a residual mechanism before the voluntary market is durable. The regulator has to distinguish a cyclical shortage of capital from a structural increase in loss.

That distinction is difficult because both can occur together.

Brokers and agents often see the transition first. They watch the number of quotes fall, the questions multiply, the binding authority narrow, and the exceptions disappear. Their experience is anecdotal at one office and informational when aggregated across a market.

A useful availability system would therefore measure friction, not merely policies in force.

How many carriers will quote a new risk? How long does placement take? How often does a customer fall to a residual plan? How much coverage is being moved to specialty markets? How much more risk is the household retaining through deductibles and exclusions? How often does a lender require a different policy before closing?

These are leading indicators of retreat.

Insolvency is a lagging one.

By the time an insurer fails, the argument about whether prices were adequate is over. Claims remain, capital is gone, guaranty mechanisms may be involved, and other carriers reconsider the same market. A prudential system therefore has to allow companies to become unpopular before they become insolvent.

That is one reason the politics are so hard.

The socially convenient insurer is the one that always renews, always covers more, and rarely raises price.

The financially durable insurer sometimes does the opposite.

The challenge is separating discipline from opportunism.

A carrier should not be able to invoke solvency as a magic word that ends scrutiny. It should be able to show the regulator the loss assumptions, capital constraints, reinsurance costs, concentration, and return thresholds behind a restriction. The regulator in turn should not force a company to hide a real problem inside thinner coverage simply because a visible rate increase is politically harder.

Price, quantity, terms, and solvency belong on the same dashboard.

Otherwise policy squeezes one corner and congratulates itself while the risk leaks out another.

The household ultimately needs something more modest than a perfect market.

It needs a credible path from no to next.

If a private carrier withdraws, can the owner understand why? Is there a mitigation action that can reopen eligibility? Is another private market genuinely available? Is residual coverage adequate for the mortgage? Is there temporary support while a roof or electrical system is improved? If the property has become structurally hard to insure, is the public policy goal to preserve occupancy, finance adaptation, or help the household exit without losing everything?

Those are different answers.

A system that treats every no as the same event will reach for the same remedy every time.

A durable insurance market needs the right to refuse.

A durable society needs more intelligence about what the refusal means.
