# Living Longer Than the Spreadsheet

Life insurance has a strange relationship with good news.

People living longer is good news.

For an annuity provider, a pension plan, or a long-term care insurer that priced a different future, it can also be expensive news.

This is not because these institutions prefer people to die.

It is because they sell promises tied to time.

A life insurer generally worries about death arriving sooner than expected. An annuity provider worries about death arriving later. A pension plan promising lifetime income faces the same longevity risk. A long-term care insurer faces a more complicated combination: how long people live, how many require care, when claims begin, how long claims last, how much care costs, and how many policyholders keep paying premiums instead of lapsing.

The spreadsheet is trying to price a human life without knowing its length.

That uncertainty is unusually well suited to pooling when the population is large and the mortality assumptions are stable enough.

One person cannot know how long retirement savings must last.

A pool can make a better estimate across thousands or millions of people.

This is the economic magic of the annuity.

If retirees self-fund individually, each person has to protect against the possibility of living much longer than average. That can mean spending less than necessary during retirement because nobody wants to exhaust savings at ninety-six.

A life annuity pools the risk. People who die earlier leave resources in the pool that help finance payments to people who live longer.

This is sometimes called mortality credit.

The phrase can sound cold because it is a financial description of human death.

The mechanism is valuable precisely because nobody knows which side of the average life they will occupy.

The pool turns longevity uncertainty into stable income.

This is insurance working at its cleanest.

Then the assumptions change.

Mortality improvement has historically mattered enormously to pensions and annuities. If people live longer than expected, the institution makes more payments. A small change in average longevity can create a large present-value change across a vast pension population.

This is why actuaries care about a year that has not happened yet.

A pension liability is not the check mailed this month.

It is the stream of checks expected over decades.

Interest rates matter too because future payments are discounted back into today's dollars. When rates fall, the present value of long-duration liabilities generally rises. When rates rise, the valuation moves the other way, though assets and hedging strategies complicate the effect.

The pension plan is exposed to both life and finance.

A company can be operating successfully while its pension economics deteriorate because workers live longer or interest rates move. A public pension can accumulate underfunding across political cycles because benefits are promised now and paid decades later.

Insurance institutions are forced to make the future visible on today's balance sheet.

They do not always succeed.

The Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation exists because employer pension promises can fail.

A worker may spend thirty years with one company expecting a defined benefit. If the employer later fails and the pension plan is underfunded, the worker cannot rewind the career. PBGC provides a federal insurance layer for covered plans, subject to statutory limits and rules.

The institution is a risk pool around failed risk pools.

Its history contains periods when its own financial condition looked dangerous. By fiscal 2025, however, both its single-employer and multiemployer insurance programs reported positive net positions for a fifth consecutive year, according to PBGC.

The improvement matters because public backstops are often described as one-way paths toward insolvency.

They are not.

Policy, funding, premiums, investment outcomes, economic conditions, and targeted interventions can change the balance sheet.

The lesson is not that guarantees are free.

It is that guarantee design matters.

Long-term care insurance provides a harsher lesson in assumption risk.

The product promised benefits decades in the future for people who might eventually need help with daily living, nursing care, or related services. Early generations of policies were sold when insurers had limited experience with how policyholders would behave over long periods.

Some assumptions proved optimistic.

Policyholders lapsed less often than expected. More people kept the coverage.

That sounds like commercial success.

It increased claim exposure.

Claim incidence and duration also proved more expensive than some original pricing expected. Low interest rates reduced investment income relative to earlier assumptions. Insurers sought significant premium increases on older policies.

The economic problem was clear.

The old price did not fully fund the future promise.

The human problem was worse.

The customers receiving rate increases were often older people who had paid premiums for years precisely because they wanted protection late in life. Shopping for a replacement policy could be impossible or prohibitively expensive because age and health had changed.

The policyholder was locked in after the insurer discovered the price was wrong.

This is the most dangerous kind of insurance error.

A one-year property policy can be repriced at renewal. The homeowner dislikes it, but the market is at least conceptually annual.

A decades-long personal insurance promise can create dependence that cannot be repriced without changing the meaning of the original purchase.

The insurer cannot ignore solvency.

The regulator cannot ignore reliance.

This is where actuarial correctness and contractual fairness collide.

NAIC's long-term care work has increasingly tried to create more consistent approaches to reviewing rate increases, preserving insurer solvency while reducing the shock imposed on older policyholders who have already absorbed large cumulative increases.

There is no painless formula.

Leave rates too low and the company may be unable to pay future claims.

Raise them fully and customers may lapse after decades of premiums, just before they are most likely to need benefits.

Reduce benefits and the protection they planned around shrinks.

Inject capital and shareholders absorb the loss, if enough capital exists.

The original pricing error cannot be erased.

It can only be allocated.

This is the recurring truth of insurance.

When assumptions fail, somebody inherits the difference.

Longevity risk will become more interesting if medical innovation meaningfully changes healthy life expectancy.

The insurance consequences would reach far beyond life companies.

Pensions would pay longer.

Annuities would become more valuable to individuals and more expensive to guarantee.

Long-term care patterns could improve if added years are healthy, worsen if disability periods lengthen, or change in mixed ways. Health insurers would face different age distributions and treatment costs. Governments would collect and pay taxes over different lifetimes.

The economic effect depends on the kind of longevity.

Adding ten healthy working years is different from adding ten years of expensive frailty.

Insurance must model function, not only survival.

This distinction is often lost in broad discussions of lifespan.

For an annuity, survival is the key cash-flow variable.

For long-term care, disability matters.

For health insurance, morbidity and treatment intensity matter.

For life insurance, mortality timing matters.

The same medical breakthrough can help one line and hurt another.

This creates natural hedges in theory.

A company with life insurance and annuity exposure can partially offset longevity shifts: fewer early deaths reduce life-insurance claims while increasing annuity payments. Markets have developed longevity swaps and other instruments designed to transfer these risks.

The broader idea is familiar now.

When a risk becomes large enough, institutions try to turn it into a tradable layer.

Mortality becomes a financial variable.

This makes people uncomfortable for understandable reasons. There is something jarring about investors taking positions linked to how long populations live.

But the alternative does not make mortality disappear from finance.

Pensions, governments, households, and insurers already carry the risk.

The question is whether it can be distributed to those most able and willing to bear it.

The ethics depend on structure.

A life insurer paying a death benefit is not betting that a person dies for entertainment. An annuity pool is not wishing for shorter lives. A longevity hedge does not need to create an incentive to harm people.

Good insurance architecture separates risk bearing from control over the event.

This is why insurable interest and anti-wagering principles matter historically.

The person taking the financial position should not gain dangerous power over whether the insured event occurs.

New longevity markets need the same moral clarity.

The private annuity market also illustrates adverse selection beautifully.

People who expect to live longer have more reason to buy lifetime income. If insurers price using average population mortality while buyers are healthier or longer-lived than average, the product becomes underpriced.

Underwriting, product design, and pricing respond.

This is a case where the insured may have private information that works against the insurer in the opposite direction from life insurance.

In life insurance, someone who expects shorter life may value coverage more.

In annuities, someone who expects longer life may value coverage more.

The same human body sits on opposite sides of two risk markets.

This is why combined products can sometimes reduce selection. A policy offering both life and longevity features can attract a broader range of customers because the direction of private information partly offsets.

Insurance design becomes a way of balancing adverse selection.

The household problem remains simpler to state.

Nobody knows how long retirement will last.

This uncertainty causes people to hold precautionary wealth, delay spending, depend on family, or accept the possibility of outliving assets.

Annuities can solve part of the problem, yet voluntary annuity markets are often smaller than economists might expect.

People value liquidity, bequests, flexibility, and control. They distrust irreversible products. They may expect family or public programs to support late-life needs. They may underestimate longevity or overestimate investment returns.

The mathematically elegant pool has to compete with human preferences.

Insurance repeatedly runs into this gap between theoretical welfare and lived behavior.

A policy can be economically useful and still be hard to sell because the customer dislikes paying now for a contingent future.

This is why defaults and institutions matter.

Defined-benefit pensions automatically pooled longevity for workers. As retirement systems shifted toward individual defined-contribution accounts, more longevity risk moved from institutions onto households.

The worker gained portability and ownership of the account.

The worker also inherited the problem of turning a balance into lifetime income.

Risk was not eliminated.

It was individualized.

This is one of the largest quiet shifts in modern retirement finance.

Insurance can move risk back into pools, but only if households choose or are defaulted into products they trust.

The public sector remains a major longevity pool through Social Security and Medicare. These programs spread risk across generations and taxpayers rather than relying only on actuarially individualized premiums.

Their fiscal debates demonstrate the same principle at national scale.

Longer life is a social achievement.

Financing longer life requires adaptation.

The system can raise contributions, change benefits, adjust retirement ages, increase productivity, encourage later work, modify taxes, or accept greater public debt.

Every option allocates the cost differently.

Insurance does not solve longevity.

It organizes who carries its uncertainty.

This is the deeper meaning of all long-duration coverage.

The risk is not only that something bad happens.

The risk is that a good or bad future lasts longer than the price assumed.

A building can be inspected.

A cyber system can be patched.

A life keeps moving through time.

The insurer has to make a promise anyway.

That is why the most dangerous word in a long-term actuarial model may be not catastrophe.

It is assumption.

And assumptions do not fail evenly.

Averages are useful because insurance needs them. They are dangerous because people do not live average lives. Mortality improvement can be stronger in one income group than another. Disability can arrive early for a manual worker and late for an office worker. A married couple can face a different retirement horizon from a single person. A family with adult children nearby carries a different long-term-care risk from one whose relatives live a continent away.

The pool absorbs some of those differences. Product design decides which ones remain with the household.

That distinction becomes especially important when retirement finance is discussed as though it were merely an investment problem. A portfolio answers the question, how much money is here? Longevity insurance answers a different question: how long must the money keep arriving? Long-term care asks a third: what happens if living longer requires expensive human assistance rather than merely more groceries and rent?

The three questions are connected, but they are not interchangeable.

A household can die wealthy because it protected too aggressively against longevity. It can also spend freely in its seventies and discover in its nineties that the remaining problem is not investment return but the price of care. The financial industry often separates these products into different shelves because institutions are organized by product line. The household experiences them as one life.

That is why inflation matters differently across long promises.

A nominal pension can keep paying exactly what it promised and still buy less care. A long-term-care benefit defined as a fixed dollar amount can remain contractually intact while the labor required to deliver care becomes more expensive. An annuity with inflation protection transfers more of that uncertainty back to the provider and therefore has to be priced differently.

The future is not only longer.

It has prices.

Care is particularly difficult because much of it remains stubbornly human. A machine can improve scheduling, monitoring, mobility, diagnostics, or paperwork. It may reduce some labor needs. But bathing a frail person, noticing confusion, persuading someone to eat, changing a dressing, or sitting with a frightened family member still contains a kind of time that cannot simply be compressed like a database query.

If societies live longer while the supply of caregivers grows slowly, longevity risk becomes partly a labor-market risk.

The insurance contract may promise dollars. The claimant needs hours.

That gap matters.

It also explains why family structure belongs in a serious account of long-term risk. Unpaid care provided by spouses, daughters, sons, siblings, neighbors, and friends is an enormous informal insurance system. It has no claims department and usually no premium. Its capital is time, proximity, affection, obligation, and sometimes guilt.

Commercial insurance sits on top of that invisible pool.

When families become smaller, more geographically dispersed, or composed of older members caring for one another, the informal pool changes. A product designed in an era when a surviving spouse or nearby adult child could provide substantial care may encounter a different claim environment decades later.

Again, the assumption is not merely actuarial.

It is social.

This is also why longevity risk cannot be solved by telling individuals to save more. Saving is important, but individual accumulation does not create pooling. If everyone must finance alone for the possibility of being the unusually long-lived, unusually frail, unusually expensive case, society strands enormous precautionary balances in accounts that may never be needed while still leaving unlucky households exposed to ruin.

Pooling exists to make that uncertainty cheaper collectively than it is individually.

The difficult question is what kind of pool deserves trust.

A private insurer must hold capital and price the promise honestly enough to survive. A pension sponsor must fund benefits rather than treating future workers as an accounting footnote. A government program must admit when demographic change alters the burden across generations. A family cannot be assumed to contain limitless unpaid labor. None of these institutions escapes arithmetic merely because its promise is socially valuable.

Nor does arithmetic decide the social bargain by itself.

Two pension systems can be equally solvent and distribute risk very differently. One may place investment and longevity risk on retirees; another may place more on employers or taxpayers. Two long-term-care systems can spend the same amount while relying on radically different mixtures of family care, private insurance, public benefits, and out-of-pocket payment.

Solvency answers whether the promise can be paid.

Politics answers who was asked to make it.

There is a tendency in insurance to imagine that better data will eventually tame this problem. More medical records, better mortality models, genetic information, wearable sensors, and richer behavioral data can certainly improve prediction. They can also make classification more granular.

But perfect prediction would change the nature of the pool.

If an insurer knew with certainty that one person would need ten years of intensive care and another would need none, the first person's premium would begin to resemble prepayment rather than insurance. The social value of the pool comes partly from uncertainty shared before individual fate is known.

Longevity therefore sits at the same frontier as health and AI underwriting: prediction can improve pricing while simultaneously eroding the solidarity that makes insurance useful.

The institution must decide what it wants to know and what it intends to do with the knowledge.

That is not a technical footnote.

It is the design of the pool.

A long life should not be treated as a financial defect. Neither should its costs be hidden until the people who incurred them are too old to adapt.

The durable arrangement is the one that makes uncertainty explicit early, spreads the parts worth pooling, prices the parts that must be priced, and admits where public or family support is carrying what the contract does not.

The spreadsheet will never know exactly how long a person will live.

The point is not to make it omniscient.

The point is to make sure its errors do not arrive forty years later as somebody else's emergency.
