# The State as Reinsurer of Last Resort

On September 12, 2001, the insurance industry knew two things.

The first was that the attacks of the previous day had caused enormous insured losses.

The second was harder to price: nobody knew whether the event belonged to a distribution that could be modeled, diversified, and sold in the ordinary way.

Terrorism risk had always existed. What changed was the scale, concentration, and uncertainty revealed by the attacks. A single coordinated event had struck office towers, aviation, workers, businesses, life insurers, property insurers, liability insurers, and reinsurers at once. The loss did not fit neatly inside one product line.

Insurers responded the way private capital often responds to an unbounded tail.

They narrowed coverage.

Reinsurers withdrew.

Terrorism exclusions spread.

Commercial property owners found that the coverage lenders expected was becoming difficult or expensive to obtain.

The problem moved immediately beyond insurance.

A building that could not obtain terrorism coverage could have trouble obtaining financing. A construction project could stall. A mortgage covenant could be breached. A landlord could discover that a risk once buried inside an ordinary policy had become a separate prerequisite for doing business in a major city.

Congress responded in 2002 with the Terrorism Risk Insurance Act.

TRIA did not nationalize commercial insurance.

It built a federal loss-sharing layer behind it.

Private insurers continued to write coverage. They retained losses up to specified deductibles and co-shares. Above defined thresholds, the federal government would share qualifying losses from certified acts of terrorism, subject to program rules and an overall cap.

The state became a reinsurer of last resort.

Not literally in every contractual sense. The federal program is not a conventional reinsurance company collecting a market premium in advance for each policy. But economically it performs a related function: it absorbs a portion of catastrophe severity that private insurers might otherwise be unwilling to hold.

The effect is visible in the market that followed.

The Government Accountability Office has repeatedly found that the terrorism insurance market has remained broadly stable under the program and that the federal backstop has helped support availability. In its 2025 work on reauthorization, GAO again warned that significant market disruption could occur if no federal terrorism risk-sharing mechanism existed. Insurers could restrict coverage or exit certain risks. Projects could face financing problems reminiscent of the period after September 11.

This is the hidden power of a public backstop.

It may never pay.

Its mere existence changes what private actors are willing to insure today.

A contingent government promise can support current economic activity even in years when it costs the treasury nothing in claims.

That sounds magical until the balance sheet is remembered.

The promise has value because the state can tax, borrow, and compel payment across a much larger population than a private insurer can. It can operate across generations. It can absorb losses that are too correlated to diversify inside a single year. It does not face policyholder flight in the same way. It can remain after private capital retreats.

These are extraordinary advantages.

They are also reasons to be careful.

A government that becomes the effortless absorber of every unpriceable tail can encourage the private sector to keep gains while socializing catastrophe.

That is moral hazard at the institutional level.

The design problem is therefore not whether the state should ever backstop risk.

Modern states already do.

The problem is when, how, and on what terms.

The United States has built public insurance or guarantee systems around deposits, floods, crops, pensions, nuclear accidents, terrorism, mortgages, health, and other exposures. Other countries have their own mixes of catastrophe pools, flood schemes, export-credit guarantees, agricultural insurance, social insurance, and public-private disaster facilities.

The pattern appears whenever three conditions converge.

The activity is economically or socially important.

The private market cannot reliably supply enough coverage at an acceptable price.

The public sector believes the consequences of noninsurance would eventually fall on it anyway.

That third condition matters most.

Government often enters insurance because it is already the implicit insurer.

If a nationally important bank fails and deposits vanish, the state may intervene even without deposit insurance.

If a hurricane destroys an uninsured region, emergency spending follows.

If a terrorist attack renders major commercial buildings uninsurable, political pressure to stabilize the market appears.

If a pandemic overwhelms health systems and business interruption, governments support firms and households directly.

The absence of an explicit backstop does not necessarily mean the taxpayer is protected.

It may mean the rules are unknown until after the disaster.

Unknown rules are a poor form of insurance.

They create uncertainty about who will pay, how quickly, and under what conditions. They encourage lobbying after losses occur. They reward political visibility. They can produce unequal treatment across events. They make it hard for private insurers and lenders to understand the boundary of their own responsibility.

An explicit backstop can be better precisely because it draws a line before the event.

Private capital absorbs this layer.

Government shares that layer.

Losses above this cap are handled under these rules.

Premium surcharges or recoupment may restore part of public outlays afterward.

The details matter because every line changes incentives.

TRIA illustrates the design problem. Congress has repeatedly changed insurer deductibles, federal co-shares, industry retention, and program triggers. GAO has observed that these adjustments shift federal fiscal exposure and private-sector responsibility. Raise the insurer’s retention and the private market carries more tail. Lower it and the government absorbs more. Raise the program trigger too far and smaller catastrophes may sit awkwardly between ordinary insurance and federal support.

There is no neutral number.

The architecture is a negotiation over who must have capital available before the event.

Flood insurance exposes a different version.

The National Flood Insurance Program was created because private flood insurance had long been difficult to sustain broadly. Flood loss is geographically correlated. Repeated flooding is predictable in many locations. Adverse selection can be severe because people most likely to buy coverage are often those facing the highest hazard. Mapping and land-use policy are deeply political. Congress also wanted insurance to support mortgage markets and reduce dependence on ad hoc disaster relief.

The program has paid claims to millions of policyholders.

It has also accumulated substantial debt to the Treasury after catastrophic years.

That debt is not an accounting accident.

It reveals the collision between three goals that do not automatically coexist: actuarially adequate premiums, broad affordability, and the ability to pay extreme losses without taxpayer support.

A program can often achieve two more easily than three.

Charge full risk and some households cannot afford coverage.

Hold rates down and premium revenue may not cover expected loss and capital needs.

Guarantee claims anyway and the public balance sheet fills the gap.

This is why public insurance debates can become circular.

Critics say the program is subsidized.

Supporters say the private market will not serve everyone.

Critics say subsidies encourage risky development.

Supporters say millions of existing owners cannot simply move.

Both can be right.

The policy question is where to put the transition cost.

A public backstop is most defensible when the risk is genuinely systemic, difficult to diversify privately, and attached to activity society wants to preserve.

Terrorism after September 11 fits this logic more comfortably than subsidizing routine loss on a repeatedly damaged property.

One is a tail risk whose probability and severity are shaped by strategic adversaries and national security.

The other may be a known recurring hazard whose cost can be reduced through mitigation, land-use change, or relocation.

Both may deserve public involvement.

They do not deserve the same kind.

This distinction will matter for cyber insurance.

Cyber risk has features that make insurers nervous for the same reason terrorism did: one event can create losses across many policyholders at once. A vulnerability in widely used software, cloud infrastructure, identity systems, payment networks, or managed service providers can produce accumulation that ignores geography.

A hurricane has a cone.

A software flaw has dependencies.

The federal government has already had to ask whether existing terrorism frameworks are enough. GAO noted in 2025 that the current terrorism program can cover qualifying cyber losses when an incident is certified under the law, but many catastrophic cyber events may not fit terrorism’s statutory requirements. An attack can be economically devastating without being easily classifiable as violent, coercive terrorism.

This leaves a possible backstop gap.

Private insurers may cover ordinary cyber losses while fearing the true systemic event.

The state may end up paying after such an event through emergency support, national-security measures, loans, or industry rescue.

The question is whether to formalize that possibility before the loss.

AI may produce a similar debate.

Not because every model error is systemic.

Most will not be.

But consider a widely deployed model used by hospitals, financial firms, law offices, industrial systems, or software agents. A defect, security failure, corrupted update, or shared reasoning flaw propagates through thousands of insured organizations. Liability claims emerge across sectors. Business interruption follows. Professional indemnity, cyber, product liability, errors-and-omissions, directors-and-officers, and other lines may all become implicated.

The loss looks less like ten thousand independent mistakes and more like one event expressed through ten thousand contracts.

That is the moment insurers start asking where the public boundary sits.

The answer should not be “government pays for AI.”

A broad guarantee would subsidize reckless deployment and weaken the discipline insurers can impose through underwriting.

A better structure, if one ever becomes necessary, would force the private market to retain meaningful first-loss exposure, require mitigation and controls, price participation, and reserve public capacity for clearly defined extreme accumulation.

The state should be last in line, not first.

This principle appears in well-designed catastrophe systems generally.

The insured retains a deductible.

The insurer retains a layer.

Reinsurance takes another.

Capital markets may take another.

Only after private capacity is meaningfully exposed does the public layer activate.

Why bother with all these layers if the state can simply pay?

Because each layer performs a different function.

The deductible reduces small claims and preserves incentives.

The insurer prices and underwrites individual risk.

The reinsurer diversifies across companies and geographies.

Capital markets bring another pool of risk-bearing capacity.

The state absorbs what remains too correlated, politically unavoidable, or economically destabilizing for the others.

A public backstop should not replace underwriting.

It should make underwriting possible at the frontier.

This is also why government guarantees can be a form of industrial policy.

Certain industries would be smaller or more expensive if all catastrophic liability had to be carried privately without limit.

Commercial aviation developed alongside liability frameworks and insurance markets.

Nuclear power has long required special liability arrangements because a severe accident can create losses far beyond the balance sheet of a single operator.

Space launch, energy infrastructure, terrorism-exposed real estate, and other capital-intensive activities depend on some answer to the question of catastrophic loss.

The answer influences what gets built.

A government can subsidize an industry with grants and tax credits.

It can also subsidize it by making extreme risk financeable.

This support is less visible because it sits in contingent liability rather than annual spending.

That does not make it free.

A guarantee is economically valuable even before it is exercised.

If a private insurer would demand a huge premium to absorb a tail that the government takes for little or no upfront charge, the difference is a transfer.

Sometimes that transfer is justified.

It should still be counted.

Public backstops therefore need a discipline of explicitness.

What event activates them?

What losses qualify?

How much private capital must be exhausted first?

Is the public share recouped later through assessments or surcharges?

Does participation require mitigation?

Can the government change terms prospectively without destabilizing contracts?

What happens when losses exceed the cap?

Who audits claims?

How are political decisions separated from claim decisions?

A vague guarantee is dangerous because markets capitalize the benefit while taxpayers discover the terms during crisis.

There is an additional problem: the state is not infinitely diversified.

Governments can spread loss across taxpayers and time, but large catastrophes often arrive alongside other fiscal stress. A severe hurricane can reduce local tax receipts while raising public spending. A pandemic can cut economic output while increasing healthcare and income-support costs. A cyberattack can disrupt payment systems and government operations at the same time.

The sovereign balance sheet is enormous.

It is not supernatural.

A country with weak fiscal capacity can be pushed toward debt distress by disasters that a richer sovereign can absorb comfortably.

This is one reason public backstops work differently across countries.

The credibility of the promise depends on the state’s own capacity.

A guarantee from a government that cannot borrow in crisis is not the same instrument as a guarantee from one with deep capital markets and monetary sovereignty.

The state can be reinsurer of last resort only if someone believes the state can pay.

This brings the argument back to the deepest layer of insurance.

Insurance is organized trust in a future payment.

The policyholder trusts the insurer.

The insurer trusts the reinsurer.

The reinsurer trusts capital markets, models, and contract law.

At the outer edge, all of them trust the political order enough to believe money, courts, property rights, and public promises will still function after catastrophe.

The ultimate risk pool is institutional.

That is why some losses are uninsurable in failed states regardless of actuarial sophistication.

There is no stable promise behind the promise.

In functioning states, the public backstop should be treated as scarce capacity.

Used well, it can stabilize markets that private capital cannot support alone.

Used casually, it becomes an invitation to underprice danger.

The difference is whether the state absorbs the risk nobody else can reasonably carry—or simply the risk nobody else wants to charge for honestly.

There is a budgeting implication hidden inside that distinction.

Ordinary public spending appears when legislators appropriate money. A guarantee can create economic value without an appropriation this year. That makes it easy to treat contingent support as though it were outside the budget until the event occurs.

But a contingent liability is still a claim on future fiscal capacity.

The state should therefore model backstops the way a prudent insurer models catastrophe: exposure, attachment, plausible severity, correlation with other public costs, financing after activation, and the conditions under which the promise grows.

This is especially important when the same catastrophe hits both sides of the sovereign balance sheet.

A hurricane can trigger insurance support while damaging public infrastructure and shrinking tax receipts. A pandemic can activate business support while increasing health expenditure and unemployment payments. A systemic cyber event can create private claims while disrupting government systems and financial markets.

The public tail is often correlated with the government's own operating stress.

That makes the timing of financing crucial.

A backstop funded only by the assumption that cheap borrowing will always be available after catastrophe is not fully designed. The government may still borrow, but it should know what happens if interest rates are high, markets are dislocated, or several emergencies overlap.

Pre-event authority matters too. Treasury officials, regulators, insurers, and claims administrators need to know who can certify an event, release funds, assess industry participants, or change terms. A beautifully calibrated loss-sharing formula can fail operationally if nobody has authority to move money quickly.

Public insurance is administration under stress.

The institution should rehearse the stress before it arrives.

There is also a legitimacy test.

A public backstop can survive economically and still fail politically if people believe it protects sophisticated firms while leaving households exposed. Terrorism insurance for commercial buildings may be defensible because it preserves financing and employment, but the public rationale has to be stated. A cyber backstop for critical infrastructure may be defensible because cascading failure would injure the public, not merely shareholders. A future AI backstop would need an equally clear reason.

The beneficiary cannot simply be the industry asking to transfer its tail.

The public layer should purchase a public outcome: continuity of credit, essential services, broad coverage availability, recovery capacity, or protection against systemic collapse.

That requirement helps determine the conditions attached to support.

If taxpayers absorb extreme cyber loss, participating firms can reasonably be required to meet security and incident-reporting standards. If public catastrophe capacity supports property markets, building and land-use policy should not ignore the hazard. If government stands behind an AI accumulation layer, deployers should not be free to expand autonomous authority without controls while assuming the tail is public.

Backstops should buy prevention as well as solvency.

The discipline is reciprocal.

Government should not demand impossible risk elimination from participants merely to claim the guarantee is conditional. The point is to reduce avoidable loss while preserving the activity the program exists to support.

A useful condition changes behavior before catastrophe.

A decorative condition produces paperwork.

This is where public and private insurance look most alike.

Both are promises about money after uncertainty.

Both become stronger when they shape safer behavior before uncertainty resolves.

The state has a larger balance sheet.

It does not get an exemption from underwriting.
