# Insurance as Permission

A permit says the government allows you to build.

A loan says a bank is willing to finance it.

An insurance certificate often decides whether either permission can be used.

This is easiest to see in construction because the paperwork is explicit.

A developer wants to put up an apartment building. The lender requires property coverage during construction. The general contractor carries liability insurance. Subcontractors provide certificates. Workers' compensation covers job-site injuries. The owner may require builders risk coverage for the structure as it goes up. The project can involve surety bonds guaranteeing performance or payment. Architects and engineers carry professional liability. After completion the operating entity needs property and casualty coverage. A lender may specify limits, deductibles, named insureds, and loss-payee language.

The building is made of concrete, glass, steel, wiring, plumbing, labor, debt, permits, and insurance promises.

Remove enough of the promises and the project may become unfinanceable even if the engineering still works.

This is what I mean by insurance as permission.

The insurer rarely has formal authority to approve the building. It has something almost as consequential: the right to refuse to stand behind specified losses.

The same structure appears wherever modern institutions require proof that somebody can survive a bad outcome.

A landlord requires liability coverage from a tenant because one fire or injury should not become the landlord's unrecoverable loss. A city requires a contractor to be bonded because public work cannot depend entirely on the contractor's promise to finish. A hospital requires physicians to carry malpractice coverage because the financial consequences of error can exceed an individual's resources. A corporate board buys directors-and-officers coverage because serving as a director creates personal litigation exposure that can make the job unattractive without indemnification and insurance.

A vendor contract may require cyber insurance.

A film production may not start until cast, equipment, liability, workers' compensation, travel, and completion risks are addressed.

A cargo owner may not accept a shipment arrangement without marine coverage.

An event venue may require an organizer to produce proof of liability insurance before opening the doors.

None of these requirements makes the activity safe.

They make the consequences financeable.

Modern economies are full of entities that want other people to take risk but do not want those people to be judgment-proof.

Insurance is the bridge.

The legal system can impose liability after harm. Insurance provides a source of funds capable of satisfying at least part of that liability.

This distinction matters because law without collectible resources can become symbolic. A plaintiff can win a judgment against a person or small company that does not have the money to pay it. Required insurance turns some legal rights into financially credible rights.

Auto liability insurance is the familiar example. Many jurisdictions require drivers to carry minimum liability coverage because a driver can cause losses far beyond personal savings.

The mandate is not primarily for the driver's benefit.

It protects strangers.

That is another form of insurance permission: society allows a risky activity on the condition that the participant pre-fund some capacity to compensate others.

Workers' compensation systems perform a related function inside employment. Industrial work creates injury risk. Historically, injured workers had to prove employer negligence through litigation, while employers faced uncertain tort liabilities. Workers' compensation systems replaced much of that conflict with a more structured bargain: employees receive defined benefits for workplace injuries without ordinary negligence litigation, and employers obtain protection from many tort claims.

Insurance became part of the employment institution.

The result illustrates a pattern the insurance industry sometimes obscures with product categories.

Insurance is often constitutional in the small-c sense.

It defines rights, obligations, remedies, and who bears loss before the event occurs.

The premium funds the arrangement, but the arrangement is bigger than the premium.

Consider professional liability.

A surgeon can make a mistake whose financial consequences exceed a lifetime of ordinary earnings. A lawyer can miss a deadline that destroys a client's claim. An engineer can approve a design that later fails. An accountant can issue work that investors rely on.

If professionals had to personally hold enough liquid wealth to cover every plausible liability, only the very wealthy could practice independently.

Insurance broadens participation by externalizing a portion of catastrophic personal exposure into a pool.

That can improve access to professions and services.

It also changes accountability.

A malpractice insurer may impose risk-management requirements, review claims patterns, negotiate settlements, and sometimes influence whether a practitioner remains insurable. Hospitals and licensing bodies may care about coverage status. A doctor who becomes extraordinarily difficult to insure can discover that legal permission to practice is not the same as practical permission to practice.

Insurability becomes reputation with a balance sheet attached.

The effect can be constructive when the underwriting signal identifies real danger.

It can become exclusionary when models rely on weak proxies or when a market becomes so concentrated that one carrier's judgment effectively controls access.

This is the recurring double edge.

Insurance makes activity possible by spreading risk.

The insurer's ability to refuse can become a private regulatory power.

Banks understand this because insurance and credit are tightly joined.

A mortgage lender does not merely ask whether the borrower can make monthly payments. It cares about the collateral surviving fire, wind, and other hazards. A commercial lender may require business interruption coverage because a building can remain physically repairable while a long shutdown destroys cash flow.

Lenders can therefore transmit insurance conditions into the credit market.

A property that is hard to insure becomes harder to finance.

A property that is hard to finance can lose buyers.

A property with fewer buyers can lose value.

This chain can begin before physical loss occurs.

That is one of the most important economic consequences of changing climate-related insurability.

The hazard does not need to destroy the asset to reprice it.

The expectation of future insurance cost can do part of the work.

Mortgage markets have not fully standardized how to incorporate this future premium path into valuations. Appraisals traditionally focus on comparable sales, property characteristics, and market conditions. Yet a home facing rapidly rising insurance costs has a different carrying-cost trajectory than an otherwise identical home with stable coverage.

The difference may emerge gradually through buyer behavior rather than through a formal line in the appraisal.

A family can afford principal and interest but fail the real household budget once taxes, utilities, homeowners association fees, flood coverage, wind coverage, and homeowners insurance are included.

Insurance does not sit outside housing affordability.

It is part of it.

The Federal Reserve's 2026 household survey made this visible from the other direction: some homeowners reported going without coverage because they could not afford it, while lower-income homeowners were much more likely to be uninsured.

For homeowners without mortgages, dropping insurance can be a desperate form of self-insurance.

The family retains the entire tail risk because the premium itself became a near-term financial stress.

The choice is rational in one time horizon and dangerous in another.

This tradeoff appears across small business.

A restaurant may operate on thin margins and face rising liability, property, workers' compensation, and health-insurance costs. A small contractor may have enough demand but fail to qualify for the bond required on a larger public project. A technology startup may be asked by an enterprise customer to carry cyber and errors-and-omissions insurance beyond what the startup can easily obtain.

The contract requirement can function as a market-access filter.

Large companies often have dedicated risk managers and brokers capable of assembling coverage across carriers. Small firms encounter the requirement as one more threshold they have to clear before they can sell.

Insurance becomes part of the fixed cost of legitimacy.

This can protect customers from fragile vendors.

It can also entrench incumbents that already have scale, history, and access to sophisticated insurance markets.

The effect is especially strong in industries where one catastrophic error can impose losses on many people.

A cloud provider serving thousands of companies can create enormous business-interruption exposure. A payment processor can transmit fraud or outage losses across merchants. A software vendor with privileged access can become a cyber accumulation point.

Enterprise customers increasingly respond by demanding insurance in vendor contracts.

The requirement is sensible up to a point.

Yet the policy limit can become a ritual number rather than a true measure of capacity. A small vendor may present a five-million-dollar cyber policy while a systemic failure could create hundreds of millions in customer losses. The certificate satisfies procurement, but the pool behind it cannot make every affected customer whole.

Insurance permission can create false comfort when the contract requirement substitutes for actual risk analysis.

The same problem appears in construction bonds.

Surety is often grouped with insurance, but its logic is different in an important way. A surety bond guarantees performance or payment and typically expects recourse against the principal that fails. The surety is not pricing a random loss in quite the same way as a property insurer expecting some policyholders to suffer covered events.

The distinction matters because the presence of a bond can signal prequalification.

The surety has examined the contractor's financial strength, experience, and ability to perform. A project owner relies partly on the surety's underwriting.

Private risk assessment becomes delegated due diligence.

Credit-rating agencies play a similar role in finance. Insurance underwriting plays it in physical and professional activity.

This delegation can save enormous transaction costs. Every landlord does not need to inspect every tenant's full balance sheet if an insurance requirement transfers specified liabilities. Every customer does not need to investigate whether a contractor can personally pay after an accident. Every bank does not need to hold the full fire risk of every building it finances.

Insurance standardizes trust.

Standardized trust is one of the foundations of scale.

The economic history of shipping makes the point especially clearly. Long-distance trade concentrates goods far from their owners, across weather, ports, jurisdictions, and political risks. Marine insurance lets merchants detach some of the commercial value of cargo from the physical fate of one voyage.

This does not merely protect merchants after shipwreck.

It changes how much trade can be financed in the first place.

The same is true of trade credit insurance, which protects sellers against customer nonpayment. A manufacturer can extend payment terms to buyers it might otherwise require to pay cash in advance. Insurers collect information about buyer creditworthiness and can withdraw limits when risk deteriorates.

The insurance decision becomes an early signal of commercial stress.

When trade credit insurers cut limits during a downturn, companies can suddenly lose the ability to buy inputs on normal terms. Suppliers become cautious. Working-capital needs rise. A credit event spreads through supply chains.

Insurance was supposed to reduce risk.

Its withdrawal can amplify a crisis.

This is not necessarily because the insurer acted irrationally. The company is responding to deteriorating risk.

The macroeconomic problem is that many rational withdrawals can produce a collective contraction.

Banking has the same dynamic. Each bank can rationally reduce lending during a crisis, making the overall crisis worse.

Insurance markets can become procyclical too.

After large catastrophe losses, carriers and reinsurers seek higher prices and tighter terms. The capital needed for rebuilding becomes more expensive exactly when the affected region most needs it. After a wave of cyber losses, underwriters demand stronger controls, exclusions, or premium increases just when firms discover how exposed they are.

The risk signal is valuable.

The timing can be painful.

Public policy sometimes responds by making insurance mandatory or creating public alternatives.

The logic is not always to replace private markets. It can be to preserve the permission structure when private capacity becomes too volatile.

Federal flood insurance supports mortgage lending in flood-prone areas. State residual property plans preserve a minimum level of insurability. Terrorism insurance backstops emerged because private insurers sharply restricted terrorism coverage after the September 11 attacks, creating problems for commercial real-estate finance and other sectors.

The Terrorism Risk Insurance Act was not only a response to insurance-company balance sheets.

It was a response to the fact that missing terrorism coverage could obstruct ordinary economic activity.

The government entered because insurability had become a macroeconomic constraint.

That example is important for future AI and cyber risk.

If a class of liability becomes both mandatory in contracts and difficult to insure privately, economic activity can get trapped between requirements.

Customers demand coverage because the risk is real.

Insurers restrict coverage because the risk is too uncertain or correlated.

Vendors cannot transact because they cannot satisfy the requirement.

A new backstop, liability regime, or standardized risk-control framework may eventually emerge not because government wants to subsidize technology, but because commerce needs a workable allocation of tail risk.

Autonomous vehicles, AI agents, synthetic biology, advanced energy systems, and other technologies can all encounter this boundary.

Technical capability is not sufficient for deployment.

Someone has to accept liability for the bad states of the world.

Insurance markets are one of the places society asks whether that acceptance is credible.

This makes insurers de facto participants in technological adoption.

They can accelerate adoption by making risks financeable.

They can slow adoption by refusing them.

They can shape design by requiring controls.

They can improve safety by rewarding evidence of lower risk.

They can also make conservative errors, relying on sparse data and excluding new activities until loss experience matures.

Innovation policy usually focuses on patents, capital, regulation, talent, and demand.

It should pay more attention to insurability.

A technology that cannot be insured may still be built in a laboratory.

It is harder to put inside a hospital, a school district, a public company, a transport network, or a mortgage-financed asset.

Institutions require someone to stand behind failure.

The insurer is rarely the only candidate. Companies can self-insure, form captives, create industry pools, issue guarantees, or rely on government backstops. Wealthy firms can retain risks that small firms cannot.

This creates another competitive advantage from scale.

A giant technology company can absorb a liability layer on its own balance sheet.

A small startup may need an insurer to make the same promise credible.

The larger company can therefore enter some uncertain markets sooner even if its technology is no better.

Balance-sheet capacity becomes innovation capacity.

Insurance can democratize risk-bearing by lending smaller actors the strength of a larger pool.

When insurance capacity disappears, that democratizing effect reverses.

Only entities rich enough to self-insure remain.

This is the point at which insurance stops looking like a support service.

It becomes part of market structure.

The question "Can I get a policy?" is often another way of asking "Am I allowed to participate at scale?"

That question will become more common as familiar risks get harder to price and unfamiliar risks get larger.

There is a practical flaw in the permission system that deserves attention because it is so mundane: the certificate can become more important than the contract behind it.

Organizations often ask for proof of insurance as a procurement checkbox. A certificate lists carrier, limits, dates, and categories. The document can create the impression that the requested risk has been transferred cleanly.

It has not necessarily been.

The actual policy contains definitions, exclusions, retentions, sublimits, endorsements, territorial limits, notice requirements, and aggregation language. A five-million-dollar limit means little if the relevant event is excluded. A cyber policy can exist while a particular systemic event is carved out. A contractor can satisfy a general-liability requirement while professional errors sit elsewhere. A vendor can meet the requested limit while dozens of customers each believe the same finite policy exists principally for them.

The certificate is evidence of a promise, not the promise itself.

This distinction matters more as contracts become increasingly dependent on insurance requirements drafted by people far from the risk. Procurement teams copy clauses. Customers demand limits because similar customers demand them. Small vendors buy whatever policy lets them check the box. Nobody asks whether the requested coverage maps to the failure that could actually interrupt the relationship.

Permission becomes ceremonial.

A better system starts with the loss scenario rather than the certificate.

What could this counterparty do that would hurt us badly?

Which balance sheet should absorb the first loss?

Which policy responds?

How much limit is shared with other claimants?

What exclusions matter?

Can the counterparty survive the deductible and uninsured portion?

Does the requested insurance create better controls, or merely more paperwork?

These questions turn insurance from a procurement ritual back into risk allocation.

They also expose a limit of compulsory insurance as policy. Requiring coverage can protect third parties only if coverage is available on workable terms and the required limit has a plausible relationship to the harm. Set the minimum too low and the mandate becomes symbolic. Set it too high and the rule can exclude smaller participants without creating proportional social benefit.

The permission system therefore needs calibration.

A good requirement is large enough to make the promise credible, targeted enough to match the real exposure, and flexible enough that strong firms can sometimes demonstrate equivalent financial capacity through retention, guarantees, captives, or other structures.

The goal is not to make everyone buy a policy.

The goal is to prevent risky activity from being financed by an empty promise.

The coming insurance debate is therefore not only about how much premiums rise.

It is about where economic permission migrates when the private promise becomes scarce.
