# The Politics of the Premium

A premium looks like a price.

That is only partly true.

A price for bread tells you what the baker will accept in exchange for a loaf. A price for gasoline tells you what it costs to acquire a gallon at a particular moment. A premium tells you something stranger. It is a price placed on a future that has not happened yet, calculated from a population of possible futures, adjusted for uncertainty, expenses, capital, regulation, competition, and the insurer’s willingness to remain exposed.

Then politics enters.

It enters because the number is rarely allowed to be only a number.

If a flood insurer says a house should cost four thousand dollars a year to insure rather than one thousand, the homeowner hears more than actuarial information. The homeowner hears a judgment about the value of a place, the safety of a family decision, the future marketability of an asset, and perhaps the competence of officials who approved the neighborhood in the first place. A lender hears something about collateral. A mayor hears something about the tax base. A builder hears something about future demand. A legislator hears from all of them.

The premium becomes political because it sits where risk, affordability, property, and blame meet.

Insurance has always contained this tension. The clean actuarial ideal is easy to state: charge each risk enough to cover its expected claims, expenses, uncertainty, and capital cost. The social reality is messier. People want insurance to reveal risk until the revealed risk becomes unaffordable. They want prices to be accurate, but not so accurate that a house becomes unsellable. They want insurers to distinguish careful owners from careless ones, but not to sort people so finely that the unlucky become a market of one.

Risk-based pricing sounds neutral because the adjective refers to risk rather than identity.

But risk lives inside geography, income, construction quality, occupation, credit, health, age, behavior, and history. The closer a model gets to the true expected loss of an individual household, the closer it may also get to the accumulated inequality of the society in which that household lives.

That does not make risk-based pricing wrong.

It makes it political even when the mathematics is correct.

The United States National Flood Insurance Program offers a useful example because its long history makes the tradeoff visible. For decades, flood premiums often reflected broad zones, statutory discounts, and policy choices that muted the difference between one property and another. That made coverage cheaper for many households and less informative about the underlying risk. It also left the program repeatedly dependent on federal borrowing after major disasters.

Risk Rating 2.0 was an attempt to move closer to property-level pricing. FEMA began implementing it in 2021. The methodology used more variables, including distance to water, type of flooding, replacement cost, and other characteristics, to estimate risk more precisely than the older zone-based system.

The Government Accountability Office judged the new method a substantial improvement in actuarial soundness.

Then came the other half of the sentence.

More accurate prices also exposed the degree to which some existing premiums had been held below full risk. GAO found that, as of late 2022, the median annual premium was $689 while the estimated median full-risk premium was $1,288. Some policyholders would eventually need increases of more than 300 percent to reach full risk. Statutory caps slowed those increases for many households.

That is insurance politics in miniature.

The old price can be wrong and still be affordable.

The new price can be more accurate and still be destabilizing.

A government cannot make the underlying flood risk disappear by slowing the premium increase. But a sudden jump in price can itself create hardship, mortgage stress, political backlash, and coverage drop-off. If too many owners abandon insurance, the public may face larger post-disaster demands anyway.

So the problem is not simply whether to charge the correct price.

It is how to move from an incorrect price to a more correct one without pretending the transition has no distributional consequences.

This distinction matters well beyond flood.

Homeowners insurance in wildfire country faces the same argument. So does coastal wind coverage. Auto insurance regulators confront it when insurers request rate increases after repair costs, medical expenses, litigation, theft, and severe weather change. Health insurance lives inside a far more explicit political settlement because individualized medical risk pricing has been constrained by law in many markets. Life insurance is allowed to use some individual risk characteristics that health insurance cannot. Workers’ compensation prices occupations and workplace histories. Commercial liability prices industries, limits, claims experience, and legal environment.

Every insurance line contains an answer to the same question:

Which differences in risk are legitimate to price, and which differences should the pool absorb?

The answer is never purely scientific.

Suppose two houses face very different wildfire probabilities. Charging them the same rate asks the safer house to subsidize the riskier one. That may be defensible if the alternative would destroy a town’s property market, strand long-time residents, or punish owners for decisions made under older building codes. It may be indefensible if the subsidy encourages another decade of construction in the most dangerous part of the landscape.

Suppose the insurer charges each house a highly individualized rate based on roof material, vegetation, slope, access roads, wind exposure, fire history, water supply, and ember-resistant construction. The price signal becomes sharper. Owners who can afford mitigation receive a reward. Owners who cannot may discover that the market has accurately measured a danger they cannot afford to fix.

Accuracy does not distribute capacity.

This is one reason insurance arguments become moral arguments faster than economists expect.

The insurer says, “This is the risk.”

The homeowner hears, “You should not live here.”

The regulator says, “People need time.”

The insurer hears, “You must write a loss-making policy.”

The environmentalist says, “Stop subsidizing dangerous development.”

The local official hears, “Abandon our community.”

Each statement can contain some truth while answering a different question.

The Federal Insurance Office’s nationwide homeowners-insurance study made the conflict harder to dismiss as a handful of dramatic anecdotes. Using data covering hundreds of insurers and hundreds of millions of policy records aggregated at the ZIP Code level, Treasury reported in January 2025 that homeowners insurance had become more expensive and harder to obtain in areas exposed to elevated climate-related hazards. Households in the highest-risk ZIP Codes paid more on average, experienced higher nonrenewal rates, and faced greater market disruption.

This is what a price signal looks like when the price is attached to the right to maintain a mortgage.

Economists often say that prices transmit information.

Insurance premiums do.

But sometimes the information is socially explosive.

A home may be the largest asset a family owns. It may be the main source of retirement wealth, the collateral behind a loan, the inheritance intended for children, and the anchor of a neighborhood. If insurance costs rise enough, the market value of the home can fall even before the next disaster. The premium is not merely reflecting the risk of property destruction. It is helping capitalize that risk into the property today.

This makes the insurance market an early repricing mechanism for climate and catastrophe exposure.

A scientific report can say that wildfire probability has increased.

An insurance nonrenewal changes a family’s options this month.

That immediacy is one reason politicians intervene.

Rate regulation can take several forms. Some jurisdictions require prior approval before insurers can change rates. Some permit more rapid adjustment. Some limit the factors that may be used. Some restrict how quickly rates can rise. Some allow insurers to incorporate catastrophe models or reinsurance costs more directly. Some create residual-market plans for people unable to obtain ordinary private coverage.

Every choice shifts risk somewhere.

Hold premiums down and the insurer may reduce new business, tighten underwriting, or leave the market. Permit rapid increases and households may lose affordability. Force broad availability and the state-backed residual plan may accumulate concentrated high-risk exposure. Let the residual plan charge fully risk-based rates and it may cease to serve its political purpose.

There is no regulatory setting called “make everyone happy.”

There are only different allocations of cost, visibility, and time.

The most dangerous policy is the one that hides these transfers.

Cross-subsidies are not inherently bad. Insurance itself is a cross-subsidy after the fact: the many people without losses fund the few people with losses. The more important distinction is between pooling random loss and systematically underpricing known risk.

If two otherwise similar houses have uncertain outcomes, pooling works naturally. One burns; the other does not. Before the year begins, nobody knows which one will suffer.

If one house is repeatedly flooded and the other sits on a hill, charging them the same price is no longer merely pooling uncertainty. It is transferring the predictable cost of one location to another.

A society may choose that transfer.

It should know that it is choosing it.

This is where insurance differs from welfare policy in a politically useful way. A subsidy can be embedded in a premium without appearing in a budget line. That makes it easier to enact and harder to debate honestly. The safer policyholder pays more. The insurer absorbs less margin. The public plan accumulates exposure. Future taxpayers inherit contingent liabilities. The subsidy exists even if nobody writes a check labeled subsidy.

Hidden subsidies become especially unstable when the underlying risk rises.

A transfer that was modest when severe losses were rare can become enormous when hazards intensify, asset values rise, repair costs climb, or more people move into exposed areas. The pool can carry a little distortion for a long time. Then the distortion becomes the business model.

At that point, the insurer stops behaving like a reluctant public agency and begins behaving like a private institution trying to survive.

It raises rates.

It changes deductibles.

It buys more reinsurance.

It narrows coverage.

It declines new business.

It leaves.

The politics often treats the departure as the beginning of the problem.

Economically, it may be the moment the problem became impossible to hide.

There is another complication: granular pricing can become a form of sorting.

Insurance has always classified. Marine underwriters distinguished routes, cargoes, seasons, hulls, captains, and wars. Life insurers distinguished ages and health. Auto insurers distinguish vehicles, territories, driving histories, mileage, and other variables. Classification is necessary because a pool collapses if high-risk members can enter at prices designed for low-risk members while low-risk members leave.

This is adverse selection.

But classification can continue until very little pooling remains.

Imagine an insurer with perfect information about every house, driver, worker, body, and business. It knows the expected loss of each individual with extraordinary precision. The company can charge each person almost exactly what that person is expected to cost.

At the limit, insurance begins to disappear.

If the premium for a particular loss becomes nearly equal to the expected loss, plus expenses and capital, the customer is no longer buying much redistribution across risk types. The customer is prepaying for his own predicted future.

Insurance requires ignorance of a productive kind.

Not ignorance about everything. An insurer must understand the distribution well enough to price it. But the institution works by grouping people whose exact outcomes remain uncertain.

This creates a paradox for the age of machine underwriting.

Better prediction can improve underwriting and reduce fraud.

Too much segmentation can destroy solidarity inside the pool.

The danger is not limited to protected classes or obviously sensitive variables. A model can reconstruct risk indirectly through hundreds of innocuous signals. Geography can stand in for income or historical segregation. Purchase patterns can reveal health conditions. Device data can reveal work schedules. Property imagery can reveal maintenance. Driving telemetry can reveal behavior in exquisite detail.

A regulator that bans one variable may discover that ten others approximate it.

The political question therefore shifts from “Is this variable allowed?” to “What kind of pooling do we want this market to preserve?”

That is a harder question because it admits that insurance has a social design.

The cheapest possible insurance for the safest people is not automatically the best insurance system.

Nor is universal cross-subsidy automatically fair.

A homeowner who spends thousands hardening a roof may reasonably object to paying the same as a neighbor who does nothing. A careful driver may resent a rate that ignores behavior. A low-income family may be unable to afford the premium that accurately reflects a risk inherited from neighborhood infrastructure rather than personal choice.

Fairness has multiple meanings.

Actuarial fairness means similar expected losses should receive similar prices.

Procedural fairness means the rating process should be transparent enough to contest errors.

Social fairness asks whether people should bear the full price of risks they have limited power to change.

Political fairness asks whether public subsidies should be explicit and broadly financed rather than hidden inside private premiums.

These meanings can collide.

The useful policy distinction is between pricing and assistance.

When possible, let the premium tell the truth about risk.

Then decide separately whether society wants to help someone pay it.

This is cleaner than forcing the insurer to lie about the risk because the truthful number is uncomfortable. An explicit means-tested subsidy, mitigation grant, tax credit, relocation payment, or public investment can preserve affordability while keeping the risk signal visible.

That separation is not always politically easy.

It is intellectually honest.

If a house costs six thousand dollars a year to insure because the expected catastrophe loss is high, reducing the premium to two thousand does not reduce the hazard. Someone must absorb the four-thousand-dollar difference. The homeowner, another policyholder, the insurer’s capital, a reinsurer, a state plan, or the taxpayer.

The accounting can move.

The risk does not.

This principle becomes more important as insurance confronts risks that are changing rather than merely being revealed.

A pricing system assumes that the past, adjusted intelligently, says something about the future. Climate change weakens that assumption for some perils. Cyber risk changes as software changes. AI liability changes as deployment changes. Medical costs change with technology, provider consolidation, demographics, and regulation. Litigation changes liability tails. Supply chains change business-interruption severity.

The premium is therefore not simply a census of yesterday’s losses.

It is a bet on tomorrow’s distribution.

When that bet becomes more uncertain, the insurer charges not only for expected loss but for model risk and capital uncertainty.

This is why people sometimes see premiums rise faster than recent local claims would seem to justify. The insurer is pricing a future distribution, reinsurance costs, replacement costs, and the possibility that the model is wrong.

A politician can challenge the model.

Sometimes the politician should.

Models can be biased, stale, opaque, or opportunistic. Insurers can overreach. Consumers need appeal rights and regulatory scrutiny. Competition matters. Data quality matters. Market conduct matters.

But politics cannot repeal correlation.

If many houses can burn together, the capital requirement is real.

If one cyber vulnerability can hit thousands of insured businesses, the accumulation is real.

If medical inflation outruns premiums, the shortfall is real.

Insurance politics becomes dangerous when it treats every painful price as evidence of unfair pricing and every insurer retreat as evidence of bad faith.

Sometimes the painful price is information.

The mature response is not to worship that information.

It is to decide what to do with it.

Build differently.

Retrofit.

Restrict development.

Fund drainage.

Harden grids.

Subsidize vulnerable households explicitly.

Move public infrastructure.

Create a backstop for truly systemic risks.

Or accept the cost of maintaining settlement and economic activity in a dangerous place.

The premium is where the argument begins.

It should not be where the argument ends.

The missing institution in many premium debates is transition finance.

A regulator can know that the old price is unsustainable and still know that the full new price cannot be imposed overnight without creating a second kind of damage. A household can deserve time even when the hazard is real. A municipality can need several years to change drainage, building standards, evacuation routes, or land use. A lender can need a workable path for collateral that was financeable when the loan was made and becomes much harder to insure midway through the mortgage.

Time has a price too.

The honest question is who finances it.

A transition can be financed by capped annual premium increases, explicit public subsidies, insurer assessments, tax credits, mitigation grants, concessional loans, temporary public reinsurance, or some combination. Each instrument places the bridge cost on a different balance sheet.

What matters is whether the bridge leads somewhere.

A temporary subsidy that buys three years for a community to harden homes, improve infrastructure, or organize voluntary buyouts is different from a permanent subsidy that promises the old price indefinitely. One finances adjustment. The other converts the changed risk into a long-term transfer.

This is why sunset provisions and review points matter in insurance policy.

A subsidy can begin with a legitimate emergency rationale and become invisible infrastructure after the emergency passes. A residual plan can begin as a last resort and become the dominant carrier in a region. A rate cap can begin as consumer protection and become the main reason private capacity cannot return.

Political systems are better at creating relief than ending it.

Insurance is better when the exit is designed at the same time as the entry.

What conditions end the subsidy?

What mitigation earns faster transition?

What happens to new construction after the rules change?

How are low-income owners treated differently from new speculative development?

How does a public pool shrink if private capital returns?

These questions determine whether affordability policy preserves a community while it adapts or quietly protects a risk arrangement that no longer makes sense.

The distinction is also fairer across generations.

A household that bought under old assumptions may deserve transition support because it cannot move a house or refinance a life instantly. A developer proposing new construction after the hazard is well known has weaker grounds to demand the same subsidy. Treating both identically converts compassion for existing reliance into an incentive for new exposure.

Time changes the moral claim.

Premium politics rarely says this clearly enough.

The cleanest policy therefore has three separate ledgers.

One ledger records the risk price.

One records the affordability support.

One records the investments intended to make the future risk smaller.

Mix them together and nobody can tell whether a lower premium came from a safer house, a larger subsidy, or a transfer of tail risk to the public.

Keep them visible and political disagreement becomes harder to hide but easier to govern.

That is a worthwhile trade.

Insurance cannot depoliticize risk.

It can make the politics legible enough that society knows which bill it has chosen to pay.
