# The Map Is Moving

Insurance likes maps because hazards have addresses.

A coastline can be drawn. A floodplain can be shaded. A wildfire corridor can be modeled. A roof can be attached to coordinates, construction type, replacement cost, elevation, vegetation, distance from hydrants, distance from fire stations, and a history of prior claims.

The map creates an illusion of permanence.

The house stays where it is.

The risk does not.

This is the climate-insurance problem in one sentence.

The physical world is changing, but the assets, mortgages, roads, tax bases, and settlement patterns built on top of it move slowly.

Insurance reprices in the middle.

That middle position makes the industry one of the first places where changing hazard becomes an annual household bill.

Climate change is not the only reason property insurance costs rise. This distinction matters because public arguments often become less accurate as they become more moralized.

Insured losses grow for several reasons at once.

There are more people and more assets in exposed places. Houses are larger. Equipment is more expensive. Construction labor and materials cost more. Litigation and claims practices matter. Building codes differ. Reinsurance prices change. Inflation can raise every repair estimate. A hailstorm over open farmland is cheaper than the same hailstorm over a suburb full of roofs and cars.

Hazard and exposure interact.

This is why reinsurers such as Swiss Re and Munich Re emphasize both climate-related changes in some perils and the steady growth of economic values in harm's way.

The 2025 loss year is useful because it did not require a major U.S. hurricane landfall to produce enormous insured losses.

Swiss Re estimated global natural-catastrophe insured losses at about $107 billion in 2025. The Los Angeles wildfires alone generated roughly $40 billion in insured losses, the largest wildfire insurance event on record. Severe convective storms—tornadoes, hail, damaging wind, and related events—again produced tens of billions in claims.

Swiss Re described secondary perils such as wildfire, severe convective storms, and floods as accounting for a record share of insured natural-catastrophe losses in 2025.

The phrase secondary peril can be misleading to anyone whose house is underneath one.

It is an industry classification, not a statement about human importance.

The category historically distinguished events such as hail, flood, and wildfire from peak perils such as major hurricanes and earthquakes that can dominate catastrophe portfolios. Yet the so-called secondary perils have become persistent, expensive, and geographically broad.

The insurance problem is no longer only the rare monster event.

It is the accumulation of expensive ordinary catastrophes.

That change matters for pricing because frequent medium-to-large losses erode the calm years that help insurers rebuild capital between extremes.

A market can survive a rare hurricane if the premium across many years reflects the possibility.

It becomes harder when fires, hail, flood, storm, and repair inflation continually raise the baseline while the peak event remains possible on top.

In March 2026 Swiss Re noted that a peak-loss scenario could generate insured natural-catastrophe losses around $320 billion in a single year.

The exact scenario is not a forecast.

The point is the tail.

A $107 billion year can occur without the most feared U.S. hurricane event. A much larger year remains possible.

Insurance prices the distribution, not the latest headline.

This helps explain one of the recurring misunderstandings after a quiet season.

If a region avoids the hurricane, fire, flood, or earthquake everyone feared, customers may expect rates to fall. Insurers may still raise them because catastrophe pricing responds to multi-year trends, model updates, reinsurance costs, exposure growth, and capital requirements rather than to one lucky season.

A year without a major landfall can be good fortune without being evidence that the risk disappeared.

Munich Re made exactly this point in its review of 2025. The United States avoided a direct hurricane landfall for the first time in years, yet wildfire and severe thunderstorms still drove major losses. Its first-half 2026 review again emphasized that lower losses over six months did not change the underlying direction of climate and exposure risk.

Insurance is forced to distinguish weather from climate financially.

A household can look at the sky.

An insurer has to price decades of possible skies into annual contracts.

The models used for this purpose are among the most consequential private forecasting systems in the economy.

Catastrophe models combine hazard, exposure, vulnerability, and financial terms. They simulate possible events, estimate how structures respond, and translate physical damage into insured loss according to deductibles, limits, and coverage.

They are not crystal balls.

They are structured arguments about uncertainty.

A model contains choices about data, frequency, event sets, correlations, construction vulnerability, demand surge, and climate conditions. Competing models can disagree. Insurers adjust vendor models with internal experience. Regulators decide how much modeled loss can be reflected in rates.

The model becomes political when its output becomes a premium.

A homeowner can reasonably ask why a proprietary simulation should determine whether her family can afford a house.

An insurer can reasonably ask why it should be forced to ignore a forward-looking risk signal simply because historical losses were lower.

Both questions become sharper as historical stationarity weakens.

Traditional actuarial credibility comes from experience. The longer and cleaner the loss history, the more confidence an insurer can have that the sample says something about the underlying risk.

Climate change complicates this because the underlying risk can move during the observation period.

The hundred-year flood is not a promise that one flood occurs every hundred years. It is shorthand for an annual probability under a modeled distribution.

Change the distribution and the shorthand changes too.

The same problem appears in wildfire.

Fire risk depends on temperature, moisture, wind, vegetation, land management, ignition sources, development patterns, topography, construction, and emergency response. Climate is one driver inside a larger system.

A house can become more exposed even if the house itself never changes.

The landscape changes around it.

The insurer sees this through a portfolio lens.

One property may be defensible.

Thousands of properties connected by roads, vegetation, electrical infrastructure, water systems, and evacuation constraints can create accumulation risk that is difficult to see one parcel at a time.

The January 2025 Los Angeles fires demonstrated how dense high-value development can turn wildfire into an enormous insured event. The burned acreage did not need to be a statewide record. The fire intersected expensive structures and communities.

Value density matters.

A city is a concentration of human opportunity.

It is also a concentration of insured value.

This creates a paradox for adaptation.

The places with the most economic value often have the greatest resources to harden infrastructure, enforce codes, improve response, and finance mitigation.

They also have more dollars available to lose.

Insurance tracks both.

The Federal Insurance Office's 2025 nationwide study made the consumer side visible. It found that homeowners in areas with high expected climate-related losses paid more for insurance and faced higher nonrenewal rates. The study also documented large variation across ZIP Codes.

This is important because the climate-insurance problem is not a single national market failure.

It is spatial.

One county can have abundant competition while another relies increasingly on residual coverage. One neighborhood can see renewals while another receives nonrenewals. One house can earn mitigation discounts while the house across the canyon remains expensive because of access, vegetation, or construction.

The national average conceals local fragility.

Insurance availability therefore behaves more like infrastructure than like a consumer product.

A place either has enough functioning capacity or it does not.

The distinction is not perfectly binary. Markets deteriorate along a gradient.

More applications go to specialty carriers. Brokers spend longer placing business. Deductibles rise. Coverage is split among policies. Exclusions expand. Residual-plan enrollment grows. Homeowners accept policies with less generous terms. Prices move faster than incomes.

The market can remain technically present while becoming socially thin.

This is why the phrase insurance retreat needs care.

An insurer can reduce exposure without leaving an entire state. It can pause new policies in a set of ZIP Codes, tighten underwriting, require newer roofs, limit coverage in brush areas, raise deductibles, or rely more heavily on nonadmitted specialty markets.

From the company's perspective these are portfolio-management actions.

From the household's perspective they are shrinking choices.

A changing map produces changing selection.

The highest-risk properties tend to be the hardest to place in voluntary markets, so they migrate toward FAIR Plans, wind pools, flood programs, or other residual mechanisms.

The voluntary market becomes safer as the public or quasi-public market becomes more concentrated in danger.

This segmentation can make both markets appear healthier or sicker depending on which numbers are quoted.

A private carrier can improve its loss profile by shedding exposure.

The risk did not leave the state.

It changed balance sheets.

California's FAIR Plan shows the scale this can reach. By June 2026 it reported roughly $768 billion in exposure and nearly 700,000 dwelling and commercial policies in force. Those numbers had risen sharply from 2022 as voluntary-market availability tightened in parts of the state.

A residual plan at that scale is no longer a footnote.

It is part of the state's financial infrastructure.

The public consequences extend beyond claims.

If insurance becomes expensive enough, home values can adjust. If values adjust, property-tax bases can adjust. If buyers avoid a region, development patterns can change. If lenders worry about insurance availability over the life of a mortgage, underwriting standards can change.

The insurance signal can migrate into municipal finance.

A local government depends on buildings remaining valuable and occupied. It may therefore have a stronger incentive to invest in mitigation once insurance problems become visible.

This is the optimistic adaptation loop.

Higher risk produces higher premiums.

Higher premiums create political pressure.

Political pressure creates investment in firebreaks, drainage, codes, grid resilience, roof standards, or managed retreat.

Mitigation reduces expected loss.

Lower expected loss improves insurability.

The loop is economically elegant.

It is institutionally difficult.

Mitigation benefits are distributed across time and property owners. The cost arrives now. A city may need to spend on drainage before the flood that proves the investment was wise. A homeowner may pay for a fortified roof and sell the house before receiving years of premium savings. An insurer may fund a discount and lose the customer to a competitor later.

Adaptation has a coordination problem.

Insurance can help solve it if prices are credible and discounts reward measurable risk reduction.

It can worsen it if policies are so short-term or volatile that nobody believes mitigation will be recognized consistently.

The physical sciences also create a communication problem.

People want a single explanation for a premium increase.

The true explanation may be a stack: wildfire hazard, rebuilding inflation, reinsurance, capital costs, litigation, roof age, insurer appetite, rate regulation, and exposure concentration.

Blaming only climate can obscure solvable local problems.

Ignoring climate can leave the baseline wrong.

A serious insurance system has to hold both ideas at once.

Hazard is changing.

So are the number, value, and vulnerability of things placed in harm's way.

The policy response should target both.

Do not build where the risk cannot be carried.

Build differently where development remains valuable.

Price risk honestly enough that information survives.

Support households for whom an honest price is unaffordable through transparent policy rather than pretending the risk is smaller.

Create public catastrophe layers where private capital cannot efficiently carry the tail.

Use insurance data to identify where prevention produces the largest return.

These principles sound clean on a page.

They collide in neighborhoods.

Imagine a working-class community built decades ago near a river. Residents did not choose the drainage system. Many inherited homes or bought when flood maps looked different. Property values are the largest household asset. A new risk model implies much higher premiums.

An actuarially pure response says charge the risk.

A social response says protect the households.

A resilience response says reduce the risk.

A fiscal response asks who pays.

The durable solution probably needs all four.

This is the climate-insurance challenge in miniature.

It is not a debate between people who believe in risk and people who believe in fairness.

It is a debate about how quickly a moving map should be allowed to move the economy built on top of it.

Insurance cannot stop the map from changing.

It can make the change visible earlier than many other institutions.

That is both its value and the source of its political trouble.

The next problem is deciding who gets to draw the map.

For most homeowners, catastrophe modeling is experienced as authority without visibility. The policyholder receives a premium, a deductible, perhaps a nonrenewal notice, and sometimes a terse explanation about risk concentration. Behind that result may sit vendor models, insurer adjustments, aerial imagery, public hazard data, engineering assumptions, and portfolio constraints the customer cannot inspect.

Opacity is tolerable when the outcome feels stable.

It becomes combustible when the outcome threatens a home.

This is why model governance is moving from a technical insurance question toward a public-infrastructure question. California's work on a public wildfire catastrophe model is significant for this reason even before any model becomes a pricing standard. A public model can create a common reference point for mitigation, planning, and debate. It does not eliminate legitimate private disagreement. It gives the disagreement something visible to disagree about.

That matters because a risk model is not useful only when it is accurate in the abstract.

It must also be actionable.

If a model says a community is dangerous but cannot show whether stronger roofs, cleared vegetation, water-system improvements, undergrounded utilities, or better access would materially change the result, it behaves more like a sentence than a diagnostic tool. The household learns that it is risky without learning what risk reduction will be recognized.

An adaptation-oriented insurance system needs the second answer.

What can be changed?

The distinction between hazard and vulnerability helps. A homeowner cannot move the regional wind climate. A city cannot move a mountain. But buildings can resist ember intrusion better. Roofs can be strengthened. Drainage can be improved. Critical equipment can be elevated. Vegetation can be managed. Construction can be moved away from the most exposed parcel on a site. Emergency systems can be hardened.

A good map should not merely identify danger.

It should help rank interventions.

That creates another problem: the scale of the intervention may not match the scale of the insurance contract.

A homeowner can replace vents and clear brush. The homeowner cannot build a regional flood-control system or redesign an electrical grid. A city can upgrade drainage but cannot change ocean temperature. A utility can reduce ignition risk but cannot decide where every future subdivision is approved.

Climate risk is nested.

So mitigation must be nested too.

Insurance pricing at the parcel level can accidentally obscure this. A precise premium creates the impression that one owner controls one risk. In reality, a house can be well hardened and still sit inside a community whose roads, water, power, evacuation capacity, or neighboring structures create shared exposure.

The pool has to see the neighborhood as well as the roof.

This is where insurance and land-use policy meet most directly. If a community allows new development whose premiums are subsidized or whose catastrophe tail is shifted to a public plan, the private decision changes the public risk pool. If the same community requires safer construction, limits development in the most dangerous zones, and invests in shared mitigation, it can improve the economics of insurability for existing residents too.

The map is therefore not only descriptive.

It is an incentive system.

Draw it badly and it can induce the wrong behavior. Draw it too coarsely and safer properties subsidize more dangerous ones without anyone seeing why. Draw it with false precision and households may believe a model can distinguish risks more confidently than the science supports. Change it abruptly and families can experience a legitimate model improvement as confiscation through a collapsing asset value.

There is no perfect cartography for a moving world.

There can be better institutional handling of uncertainty.

That means ranges instead of theatrical certainty where appropriate. It means explaining which inputs drive a classification. It means rewarding verified mitigation. It means updating models on a schedule people can understand. It means separating affordability assistance from the claim that a hazard does not exist. And it means giving public officials enough visibility to see when private portfolio management is pushing concentrated risk into a residual plan.

The last point is easy to miss.

An insurer can be individually rational and the system can still become collectively fragile.

Each carrier may decide to reduce exposure in the same high-risk area. Each decision protects that company's capital. Taken together, the decisions can push thousands of properties into a state-backed pool, narrow mortgage options, and concentrate catastrophe exposure on a balance sheet ultimately connected to a much broader public.

No single insurer caused the concentration.

The map coordinated them.

This is a preview of the cyber and AI problem later in the book. Shared data and shared models can make actors smarter individually while making their behavior more correlated collectively.

The more sophisticated the map becomes, the more important it is to ask not only whether it predicts loss but what institutions do when they all believe it.

Climate adaptation is often described as a physical project: seawalls, roofs, drainage, vegetation, grids.

It is also an information project.

People must know which risks are rising, which protections work, who benefits from them, and whether the savings will survive into insurance prices, lending decisions, and property values.

Otherwise the household is asked to invest against a moving target.

The map will keep moving.

The question is whether the institutions built on top of it can learn to move deliberately rather than merely react at renewal.
