# Health Is Not a House

A house can be left uninsured.

A person cannot be left unalive.

That difference is why health insurance never behaves like ordinary property insurance for very long.

The same mathematics appears in both. People pay into pools. Some people incur larger claims. Insurers estimate expected costs, manage networks, detect fraud, negotiate prices, hold capital, and administer contracts.

But the social object is different.

If a house is too risky to insure at an affordable price, a market can eventually say the property should be worth less, be rebuilt differently, or perhaps not be occupied at all.

If a person is expensive to insure because she has cancer, diabetes, a congenital condition, pregnancy, disability, or simply age, the analogous conclusion is morally unacceptable in most societies.

The risk cannot be solved by moving the person uphill.

Health insurance therefore exposes the deepest conflict inside the idea of risk-based pricing.

The more precisely an insurer can predict medical cost, the less defensible it becomes to price each person according to that prediction.

A perfect health underwriter would be socially horrifying.

It would identify the people who most need insurance and charge them the most.

This is not a paradox accidentally created by regulation.

It is the reason health insurance is heavily regulated, subsidized, pooled, or publicly provided almost everywhere.

The central question is not whether healthcare has risk.

It is which risks society refuses to individualize.

The United States has answered this through an unusually layered system: employer-sponsored insurance, Medicare, Medicaid, individual-market coverage, military and veterans programs, public subsidies, regulated private insurers, self-funded employer plans, and large government roles in payment and coverage.

The complexity is often treated as administrative failure.

It is also evidence that health risk does not fit neatly into one pool.

Employment became one of the main pooling mechanisms because a workplace gathers people for reasons unrelated to current health status. The group can spread risk across healthy and sick employees. Employer contributions hide part of the premium from the worker's direct view while making health benefits part of compensation.

The arrangement solves some adverse selection.

It creates other dependencies.

Lose the job and the insurance relationship can change when income is already under pressure.

A small employer with one severe claimant can experience large premium consequences depending on market rules. A large employer can self-fund and retain more risk because its workforce is large enough to create a more stable pool.

Scale again becomes insurance capacity.

This is why health-insurance market concentration matters. GAO reported in 2024 that private health-insurance enrollment was highly concentrated in many states, with a small number of insurers holding most of the individual and employer-group markets.

Concentration can create bargaining power against hospitals and doctors.

It can also reduce consumer choice and competitive pressure.

Insurance market power and provider market power interact.

A dominant insurer can negotiate lower reimbursement.

A dominant hospital system can demand higher reimbursement.

The premium paid by a family is the negotiated output of institutions with enormous market power on both sides.

This makes health insurance different from a simple transfer of random loss.

The insurer is not merely paying the market price of care.

It is helping create the market price.

Provider networks are one of its strongest tools.

An insurer promises lower out-of-pocket costs if patients use contracted doctors and hospitals. Providers accept negotiated rates in exchange for patient volume. The network becomes a private map of access.

Two people with the same disease and the same city can face different practical healthcare systems because their insurance networks differ.

The policy does not merely finance care.

It routes care.

This gives insurers a form of governance similar to the role they play in other sectors, but far more intimate.

A property insurer can require a stronger roof.

A health insurer can require prior authorization before a treatment.

Both mechanisms are attempts to manage expected loss.

The human consequences are incomparable.

Prior authorization can prevent unnecessary or unsupported care, control cost, and coordinate treatment.

It can also delay necessary care, create administrative burden, and place a financial institution between a patient and clinician.

This is insurance as permission in its most literal form.

The same mechanism that makes expensive medicine financeable can make expensive medicine conditional.

The tension is unavoidable because healthcare is full of decisions where the benefit is uncertain and the cost is large.

An insurer that pays every requested service without scrutiny can be exploited and can contribute to unsustainable spending.

An insurer that scrutinizes too aggressively can deny beneficial care.

The correct threshold cannot be read from an actuarial table alone.

It requires clinical evidence, medical judgment, law, patient rights, and values.

Health insurance is therefore where the limits of actuarial governance become obvious.

Expected cost is not the same as deserved care.

Artificial intelligence will make this boundary sharper.

Insurers can use models to identify fraud, predict hospitalization, target care management, automate claims, review documents, and prioritize cases. Providers can use AI to code, diagnose, summarize, and recommend treatment. Patients can use agents to appeal denials, compare plans, and navigate benefits.

Automation can reduce enormous administrative waste.

It can also scale bad policy.

A human reviewer can make an inconsistent denial one case at a time.

An algorithm can make the same flawed denial across thousands of people before anyone notices the pattern.

This is the health-insurance version of correlated model risk.

The harm does not require one catastrophic event.

The common cause is the decision system itself.

Insurance regulation will increasingly need to distinguish automated administration from automated medical judgment.

The insurer has legitimate reasons to use software.

The patient needs a path to contest decisions affecting care.

The model needs evidence and auditing.

The organization needs to know whether automation changes denial rates, appeals, treatment delays, and outcomes—not merely administrative cost.

This is a lesson other insurance lines can borrow from health.

A risk model should be evaluated by downstream consequences, not only prediction accuracy.

Health insurance also demonstrates why mandatory participation can stabilize a pool.

People with high expected medical costs have strong reason to obtain coverage. Young and healthy people may prefer to stay out if the premium exceeds what they expect to use. If too many low-cost participants leave, the average cost rises.

Regulated enrollment periods, employer-based pooling, public programs, subsidies, and other mechanisms all respond to this selection problem in different ways.

The political debate often presents mandates as a question of personal freedom.

The insurance mechanism sees a participation constraint.

A broad pool cannot remain broad if people can enter only when they expect to claim.

This does not mean every mandate is justified.

It means voluntary insurance has limits when risk is strongly predictable and the social system is unwilling to exclude high-risk people.

Health is where pure market underwriting collides most directly with solidarity.

The Affordable Care Act made this explicit by restricting how insurers in the individual and small-group markets can price based on health status, requiring coverage of essential categories, providing subsidies, and creating enrollment rules intended to support broader pools.

The policy argument since then has often been about who subsidizes whom and through which mechanism.

That is what health insurance arguments are always about beneath the labels.

Healthy subsidize sick.

Young may subsidize old depending on rating rules.

Higher-income taxpayers subsidize lower-income enrollees through public programs.

Employer tax preferences subsidize workplace coverage.

Government pays directly for major populations through Medicare and Medicaid.

Hospitals cross-subsidize across payers and services.

Every healthcare system pools somehow.

The fight is over the boundaries.

This is why proposals to make health insurance more like ordinary insurance often run into reality.

Catastrophic coverage for unpredictable major events sounds economically clean. Routine care could be paid directly. Competition could discipline prices.

The problem is that many expensive health conditions are not unpredictable once they begin.

A person diagnosed with a chronic disease is not experiencing a one-time house fire followed by a return to normal risk.

The expected cost can remain high for years.

The condition becomes a known liability.

The patient needs continued pooling after the risk is no longer uncertain.

This makes health insurance partly insurance and partly redistribution across health states.

The distinction is important for this book because it reveals a limit of insurability as a concept.

Some social goods remain financeable precisely because society refuses to let insurers apply full predictive precision.

The pool is preserved by law.

That choice has costs.

Premiums paid by lower-risk participants are higher than pure individual risk pricing would imply. Public subsidies cost taxpayers money. Mandated benefits raise premiums relative to narrower products. Cross-subsidies can be hidden. Provider prices can remain high if the system does not create effective bargaining.

Yet the alternative is not a frictionless market.

It is exclusion or ruin for people whose expected costs are known to be high.

Health insurance makes the moral content of pooling impossible to ignore.

It also illustrates the difference between affordability and total cost.

An insurance subsidy can make the premium affordable without reducing the underlying price of healthcare. A low deductible can make care cheaper at the point of use while raising premiums. A narrow network can reduce premiums by restricting provider choice. Prior authorization can reduce utilization by increasing friction.

The system can move cost without reducing it.

This is another lesson property insurance will increasingly confront.

Suppressing a homeowners premium does not reduce wildfire risk.

Public reinsurance can reduce the retail premium by moving catastrophe cost to taxpayers or public balance sheets.

A higher deductible moves cost to the homeowner.

Mitigation is different because it reduces expected physical loss.

Healthcare has the same distinction.

Negotiating lower prices, preventing disease, reducing administrative waste, and delivering effective care more efficiently can reduce underlying cost.

Changing the insurance design mostly changes who pays and when.

The difference is politically crucial.

Insurance debates often confuse financing reform with cost reform.

The pool can be made more generous without making the underlying activity cheaper.

Sometimes generosity is still justified.

But the fiscal consequences remain.

Long-term care insurance demonstrates another version of the problem. Insurers selling older long-term care policies made assumptions about lapse rates, claim incidence, duration, and longevity. Policyholders kept coverage at higher rates than expected, and claims proved more expensive than some original pricing anticipated. Insurers sought large rate increases on older blocks.

The people receiving the increases were often older and had paid premiums for years.

The insurer's actuarial argument was that the old premium no longer funded the promise.

The policyholder's moral argument was that the promise had organized decades of retirement planning.

Both claims could be true.

NAIC materials acknowledge that older long-term care policies were often priced with assumptions that later proved inadequate, requiring difficult rate actions to protect future solvency.

This is one of the clearest examples of time-horizon mismatch in insurance.

The customer buys a decades-long expectation.

The underlying variables move.

The institution has to decide whether to raise premiums, reduce benefits, accept losses, inject capital, or restructure.

Health and longevity risks cannot be repriced like an annual auto policy without human consequences.

A seventy-five-year-old cannot shop for a new long-term care policy on the same terms she could obtain at fifty.

Exit is not symmetric.

That asymmetry creates a special obligation for insurers and regulators when selling long-duration coverage.

The price today must contain humility about tomorrow.

Health insurance also reminds us that "moral hazard" can be overused.

People may consume more healthcare when the marginal price is lower. That is a real economic effect.

But nobody gets cancer for the pleasure of using insurance.

Some utilization is induced by coverage because previously unmet need becomes affordable.

Calling all additional use moral hazard confuses waste with access.

The insurer has to distinguish low-value care from high-value care.

That is a medical problem, not only a pricing problem.

The best health insurance is therefore not the policy with the least utilization.

It is the system that finances care people need while reducing waste, bargaining over prices, and preserving enough pooling that illness does not become bankruptcy.

The standard is social performance, not actuarial minimalism.

Other insurance lines will increasingly face similar questions as prediction improves.

If an AI system can forecast who is likely to suffer flood loss, unemployment, disability, cyber attack, or long-term care need with much greater precision, society will have to decide which predictions belong in prices and which belong in planning.

Health offers the warning.

A prediction can be accurate and still be the wrong basis for exclusion.

The future of insurance will not be decided by whether machines become better at identifying risk.

They will.

It will be decided by whether we remember why some risks were pooled in the first place.

Health also reveals something property insurance can usually avoid: the thing being financed and the thing producing the risk are inseparable.

A house can be repaired by a contractor who does not know the homeowner. A patient receives care through relationships with clinicians, hospitals, pharmacies, laboratories, caregivers, and institutions whose decisions affect both outcome and cost. The insurance claim is embedded in a service process.

That means the insurer's behavior can change the loss itself.

Delay a roof claim and the homeowner may suffer inconvenience and secondary damage, but the house remains the object being restored. Delay a medically necessary treatment and the patient's condition can worsen. The financing decision can alter the eventual medical cost and the human outcome at the same time.

Claims management becomes part of care management whether the insurer wants that responsibility or not.

This is why crude cost control can backfire. A denial that avoids one expensive service can produce a more expensive emergency later. A narrow network can negotiate better prices and still impose travel or access burdens that reduce adherence. High cost sharing can discourage low-value use and high-value use together because patients do not carry a utilization-review department in their pockets.

The insurance design changes behavior on both sides of the claim.

Patients respond to price and friction.

Providers respond to reimbursement rules.

Hospitals respond to network negotiations.

Employers respond to premium trends.

Drug companies respond to formularies and contracting.

The payer is not outside the healthcare market observing cost.

It is one of the institutions generating the incentives under which cost emerges.

This makes competition unusually complicated.

More insurers do not automatically mean lower total healthcare cost if each insurer has less bargaining power against concentrated providers. A large insurer can negotiate aggressively and also possess enough market power to offer employers and patients fewer alternatives. A hospital merger can strengthen clinical coordination in one case and pricing power in another. The useful question is not simply whether an actor is large.

It is what the scale allows the actor to do.

Insurance needs scale to pool risk and negotiate.

Markets need enough rivalry to discipline the insurer itself.

Those objectives can conflict.

The same conflict appears in data. Large health insurers and public programs observe enormous populations over time. In principle, those records can reveal which interventions prevent hospitalizations, which care pathways work, and where spending produces little health. That information could make insurance a powerful learning system.

But claims data sees the world through billing.

It records what generated a code and a payment, not everything that mattered to the patient. It may miss unpaid caregiving, transportation barriers, social support, fear, work constraints, or a clinician's judgment that prevented an unnecessary intervention.

The database is rich and incomplete.

AI can make it more legible without making it whole.

This is another reason health is a good warning for machine underwriting. The most measurable variable can become the most influential simply because it is measurable. An institution can confuse visibility with importance.

A durable health pool has to preserve room for information that does not arrive neatly coded.

The life-course nature of health makes the pooling question deeper still.

A twenty-five-year-old who is inexpensive today is not permanently a low-risk person. Age, illness, accident, pregnancy, disability, and chance move people through risk states. The cross-subsidy is therefore not only between different people at one moment. It can also be between different versions of the same person across time.

This is one reason solidarity in health insurance is easier to understand when viewed over decades rather than one premium year.

The healthy person paying more than expected cost is not necessarily donating forever to an unrelated class. She is buying entry into a system she may need later when private prediction becomes less flattering.

Insurance creates continuity across states of health that no one can schedule in advance.

Portability matters because that continuity can be disrupted by institutions that are temporary even when the human body is not. Jobs change. Employers fail. Families move. Insurers enter and leave markets. Providers enter and leave networks. A chronic condition follows the patient through all of it.

The risk pool is useful only if the person can remain attached to some financing system as these institutional relationships change.

That is why health policy repeatedly returns to continuity, eligibility, enrollment, and public backstops.

The human risk is long duration even when the insurance contract renews annually.

Property insurance is beginning to encounter the same mismatch as climate risk moves against thirty-year mortgages. Health has lived with it from the beginning.

The person is the long-duration asset that cannot be sold to solve the underwriting problem.

That fact changes everything.

It means some of the most important insurance questions cannot be answered with a more precise premium.

They require a decision about membership.

Who remains inside the pool when risk stops being uncertain and becomes known need?

Health insurance is society's clearest answer that sometimes the point of the pool is not to discover who is risky enough to exclude.

It is to make sure risk does not determine who gets to remain a person with access to care.
