# What Can Still Be Insured

A house changes hands.

The buyer signs a mortgage.

The lender records a lien.

The insurer binds coverage.

Nothing has happened yet.

No fire.

No flood.

No hurricane.

No lawsuit.

No illness.

No theft.

No software failure.

No death.

The transaction still depends on all of them.

It depends on the fact that the possibility of loss has been translated into a contract someone is willing to stand behind.

That is the argument of this book.

Insurance is not a side business attached to the economy after the real work is done.

It is one of the institutions that decides which risks can be carried forward into ordinary life.

A mortgage without property insurance is harder to make.

A building without liability coverage is harder to finance.

A surgeon without malpractice insurance may be unable to practice.

A ship without marine insurance may be unable to trade on ordinary terms.

A company without directors-and-officers coverage can have difficulty recruiting leadership.

A contractor without bonding cannot bid on some work.

A family without health coverage can face financial danger from ordinary illness.

A retiree without longevity protection can outlive assets.

A country without catastrophe financing can turn a storm into a debt crisis.

Insurance does not eliminate these dangers.

It makes them compatible with plans.

That is a quieter achievement than prediction.

Prediction asks what will happen.

Insurance asks whether life can continue if the prediction is wrong.

Modern societies need both.

The distinction matters because we are entering a period obsessed with forecasting.

Climate models improve.

Wildfire models improve.

Flood maps improve.

Cyber threat intelligence improves.

Artificial intelligence can classify risk at extraordinary granularity.

Sensors reveal driving, health, machinery, buildings, weather, and behavior in real time.

It is tempting to imagine that enough information dissolves uncertainty.

It does not.

Better prediction rearranges uncertainty.

It separates risks that once looked similar.

It identifies concentrations that once went unnoticed.

It moves some losses from surprise into expectation.

It can make pricing more accurate and pooling more difficult at the same time.

A world of perfect prediction would not be the ultimate insurance market.

It might be the end of insurance.

If everyone knew exactly who would suffer each loss, there would be no reason to pool uncertain outcomes. The unlucky would face the full expected cost in advance. The lucky would refuse to subsidize them.

Insurance lives in the space between ignorance and certainty.

We know enough to estimate a distribution.

We do not know exactly who will draw the bad result.

That uncertainty permits solidarity without requiring charity.

Everyone pays because everyone might be the one.

This is why the politics of granular underwriting matters so much.

Technology can make a pool smarter.

It can also split the pool into ever-smaller groups until the social mechanism disappears.

The future insurance system will have to decide deliberately which risks should be individualized and which should remain shared.

That question cannot be answered by machine learning alone.

It is a political choice about what kinds of bad luck a society wants people to face together.

We have already made many such choices.

Health insurance does not price every person at a bespoke rate in many regulated markets because societies decided that full medical-risk sorting would produce unacceptable outcomes.

Flood programs mute or phase some pricing changes because abrupt actuarial correction can destabilize households and communities.

Workers’ compensation socializes some workplace-injury risk through regulated systems because industrial activity creates predictable harm that individual workers cannot sensibly bargain over one by one.

Deposit insurance pools bank failure risk and uses public authority because bank runs can become self-fulfilling systemic events.

Terrorism insurance uses a federal backstop because the private market alone may not hold enough correlated tail risk to keep major commercial assets financeable.

These are not departures from insurance.

They are decisions about the architecture of the pool.

The next decade will require more of them.

Climate risk is already forcing societies to choose between truthful pricing, affordability, mitigation, retreat, and public support.

There is no version in which all five remain painless.

If premiums fully reflect increasing hazard, some assets will reprice downward.

If premiums are suppressed, private capacity can retreat.

If public plans absorb the exposure, taxpayers inherit the tail.

If governments fund mitigation, someone must pay before the disaster.

If communities retreat, people lose homes, tax bases, and social attachments even when the policy is economically rational.

The insurance crisis is difficult because there is no accounting trick that makes physical risk disappear.

The loss must land somewhere.

Insurance merely helps decide where and when.

That sentence should be kept in mind whenever a premium becomes politically intolerable.

The premium can be subsidized.

The deductible can be raised.

Coverage can be narrowed.

The insurer can leave.

The state can enter.

The homeowner can move.

The building can be hardened.

The loss can be borrowed against the future.

But the expected physical damage is not erased by changing the financial label.

The mature response to an insurance problem starts with a balance-sheet question:

Who is actually carrying this risk now?

Sometimes the answer is obvious.

Sometimes it is hidden.

A homeowner with a subsidized premium may appear to carry the risk while the residual-market plan and state treasury absorb the tail.

A company with a large deductible may appear uninsured while its parent balance sheet acts as a captive risk pool.

A country with little formal catastrophe insurance may appear exposed while foreign aid and concessional lending function as implicit protection.

A cloud provider may appear to diversify its customers while thousands of those customers share one dependency.

A government may claim to have no guarantee while markets correctly expect rescue after catastrophe.

Insurance literacy is partly the ability to see these hidden pools.

Once seen, they can be governed.

That is where the most productive policy lies.

Not in arguing that every risk belongs in a private policy.

Not in arguing that every painful premium is exploitation.

Not in pretending that public backstops are free.

Not in treating actuarial models as moral authorities.

The better approach is layered.

Retain the small losses people and firms can absorb.

Pool the uncertain losses that diversify well.

Use reinsurance and capital markets for larger tails.

Reduce risk where engineering and prevention are cheaper than recurring claims.

Use public balance sheets deliberately for risks that are systemic, socially necessary, or politically unavoidable.

Make subsidies explicit where possible.

Let prices communicate danger without assuming everyone has equal capacity to respond to the signal.

And stop building exposures that no plausible balance sheet wants to carry.

This last principle is the hardest because insurance often arrives after development decisions have already been made.

A subdivision is approved.

A road is built.

A grid line is extended.

A hospital is placed in a flood zone.

A data center cluster forms in a constrained power region.

A software ecosystem consolidates around one dependency.

Years later, the insurer is asked to price the accumulated result.

When the price becomes ugly, the insurer appears to be the villain.

Sometimes the insurer deserves criticism.

Sometimes the premium is a late invoice for decisions made elsewhere.

This is why insurance should be moved earlier in planning.

Ask about insurability before permitting the project.

Ask how the risk accumulates before financing the platform.

Ask whether the control system is auditable before granting the agent authority.

Ask whether the liability regime is clear before scaling the technology.

Ask whether retreat is financially possible before repeated loss destroys options.

Insurance should be part of design, not merely cleanup.

The same is true inside firms.

Risk management is often treated as a department that says no after the exciting work is already planned.

The more useful version helps make the work possible.

A cyber insurer that identifies a common dependency can motivate redundancy.

A property insurer that prices a hardened roof can make resilience financeable.

A liability insurer that demands testing and logs can improve AI deployment.

A reinsurer that identifies portfolio concentration can prevent a company from discovering correlation after catastrophe.

Insurance earns its place when it converts fear into design constraints.

That is the constructive side of underwriting.

Underwriting is a judgment about what conditions would make a promise sensible.

The popular image is exclusion: declined.

The more interesting answer is conditional acceptance.

We can insure this if the sprinkler system is installed.

We can insure this if the deductible rises.

We can insure this if the risk is shared with a reinsurer.

We can insure this if the software permissions are limited.

We can insure this if the building is elevated.

We can insure this if the government covers the extreme tail.

We can insure this if the exposure is diversified.

The word “if” is where risk becomes engineering.

That will matter enormously for artificial intelligence.

The AI economy will not mature merely when models become more capable.

It will mature when failure becomes legible enough to finance.

Who is responsible when an agent buys the wrong thing?

Who pays when a model causes professional harm?

What happens when one provider creates correlated losses across thousands of firms?

How are autonomous actions logged?

Can permissions be scoped?

Can systems be rolled back?

Can damages be separated between developer, deployer, operator, and user?

Can insurers distinguish safe deployment from reckless deployment?

The answers will shape adoption more than many benchmark scores.

The same is true of climate infrastructure, energy systems, biotechnology, autonomous machines, and space.

Every transformative technology eventually meets a boring question.

Can someone insure it?

The question is not conservative in the ideological sense.

It is practical.

A society that cannot finance failure can only innovate through actors wealthy enough to absorb failure themselves or through governments willing to socialize it implicitly.

Deep insurance markets democratize risk-taking because they let smaller balance sheets attempt things without staking their entire existence on one bad outcome.

This is one reason insurance and entrepreneurship are complements.

Limited liability prevents every business failure from consuming the founder’s entire future.

Insurance prevents certain accidental failures from consuming the firm’s entire balance sheet.

Bankruptcy law, insurance, corporate law, and capital markets together create a civilization in which failure can be survivable.

That institutional achievement is easy to underestimate because success is visible and absorbed risk is not.

A factory opens.

A ship sails.

A surgeon operates.

A mortgage closes.

A bridge is built.

A company tries something new.

Behind each act sits a chain of promises about what happens if things go wrong.

Civilization is partly the art of making those promises credible.

There is a temptation, after seeing insurance this way, to make the institution heroic.

That would be a mistake.

Insurance companies deny valid claims.

They write confusing contracts.

They classify people unfairly.

They retreat from markets.

They lobby for favorable rules.

They can exploit information advantages.

They can become complacent after long quiet periods and panicked after losses.

They are ordinary profit-seeking institutions staffed by fallible people using imperfect models.

The importance of insurance does not make insurers virtuous.

It makes their incentives consequential.

This is exactly why the institution deserves scrutiny.

A hidden permission system should not remain hidden.

People should understand when insurance is constraining a project because risk is genuinely high, when it is constraining a project because market competition is weak, when regulation prevents adequate pricing, when public subsidy is concealing danger, and when a new risk is simply waiting for better data and standards.

These are different failures.

They require different remedies.

Competition can solve some.

Regulation can solve some.

Public finance can solve some.

Engineering can solve some.

Time can solve some.

Nothing can solve all of them.

There is also a category that deserves more respect: deliberate retention.

Not every risk should be insured even when a policy could be manufactured for it.

A household with ample savings should not pay an insurer to handle every cracked phone screen or broken appliance. A company should not transfer predictable maintenance cost just because a contract can be written around it. A government should not purchase expensive private protection for losses its ordinary budget can comfortably absorb.

Insurance is most valuable where the loss is volatile enough to disrupt plans and large enough relative to the balance sheet to justify paying someone else to hold capital against it.

Everything below that threshold is a candidate for retention.

This matters because a culture of insuring everything can weaken the institution too.

Small claims create administrative cost. Rich coverage can encourage moral hazard. Transferring predictable expenses can turn insurance into an expensive payment plan. Public programs can become cluttered with losses that never threatened fiscal capacity while truly catastrophic layers remain underfunded.

The mature risk system has a deductible not only in the policy but in its philosophy.

Some discomfort should remain with the person or institution able to manage it.

That retention preserves incentives and reserves insurance capacity for what cannot be absorbed routinely.

The principle becomes morally complicated when balance sheets differ.

A five-thousand-dollar loss is retainable to one household and ruinous to another. A regional government may absorb a flood that would bankrupt a small municipality. A global technology company can self-insure a liability layer a startup cannot survive.

There is no universal deductible for society.

Capacity matters.

That is why fairness in insurance cannot be reduced to treating identical hazards identically. The same hazard can produce radically different welfare effects depending on who carries it.

Public policy has to decide when unequal capacity justifies subsidy, broader pooling, or a different risk-bearing institution.

But the opposite mistake is to treat every inability to pay as evidence that the underlying risk should be hidden.

A household can deserve assistance and still live in a dangerous house. A community can deserve transition support and still face a hazard that must eventually change its land use. A startup can be socially valuable and still need limits on what its autonomous systems are allowed to do before private capital will cover them.

Compassion does not require pretending the loss distribution is different.

It requires deciding explicitly who helps carry it and for how long.

This brings us to exit.

Insurance discussions tend to treat exit as failure: the carrier leaves, the customer leaves the pool, the project is abandoned, the community retreats. Sometimes it is failure. Sometimes exit is the mechanism that prevents a bad risk arrangement from becoming permanent.

A residual market should be able to shrink when private capacity returns. A public backstop should be able to tighten when a technology becomes conventionally insurable. A household should be able to switch carriers without losing the value of verified mitigation. A community should be able to stop rebuilding an exposure that has become indefensible.

Healthy risk institutions need entrances and exits.

Without entry, people cannot obtain protection.

Without exit, temporary subsidies become entitlements, emergency pools become permanent dumping grounds for risk, and old assumptions become impossible to retire.

The design of exit is one of the least glamorous parts of insurance policy and one of the most important.

It requires notice, transition periods, portability, financing, and rules that distinguish existing reliance from new risk creation.

A homeowner who bought thirty years ago deserves different consideration from a developer proposing another subdivision in the same newly understood hazard zone. A company that deployed an AI system before liability standards existed may deserve a transition period; a company deploying the same system after standards are clear has weaker grounds to demand public protection from known defects.

Time changes responsibility.

A good insurance regime notices.

This also suggests a practical test for public backstops.

They should answer four questions before the crisis: what private layer must be exhausted first, what behavior qualifies for support, how will the public recover or price some of the risk, and what conditions allow the program to contract later?

If a backstop has no answer to the fourth question, it is probably not merely a backstop.

It is an institution being created permanently without admitting it.

That can still be the right choice.

Permanent social insurance is a legitimate political form.

The problem is pretending permanence is temporary because nobody wants to debate the distribution openly.

Insurance is healthier when the hidden pool becomes visible.

The hardest risks will remain at the frontier.

War.

Systemic cyber failure.

Unbounded AI liability.

Climate extremes in places that cannot be defended economically.

Pandemics with broad business interruption.

Sovereign breakdown.

Events that disable the very institutions required to honor claims.

These risks remind us that insurance is not magic.

It cannot make aggregate loss vanish.

It can only distribute loss across people, places, capital providers, and time.

The system works when the distribution is credible enough that people can keep making plans.

That is the standard by which insurance should be judged.

Not whether premiums are always low.

Not whether every risk receives coverage.

Not whether insurers never fail.

The question is whether the system allows a society to confront uncertainty without either freezing activity or lying about danger.

A good insurance system tells the truth early enough to act.

It rewards prevention.

It preserves pooling where pooling remains socially valuable.

It makes public subsidies visible.

It carries losses with capital adequate to the promise.

It allows failure without making failure free.

And when a risk cannot be insured, it treats that absence as information rather than embarrassment.

The house from the beginning of this book still needs a policy before the mortgage closes.

That requirement can feel bureaucratic.

It is actually a question posed by the financial system to the future.

If this house burns, floods, or is destroyed, who has promised to absorb the loss?

If nobody can answer, the deal stops.

That is why insurance decides more than what happens after disaster.

It decides what can happen before one.
