# The House That Could Not Close

A house can fail at the closing table without anything being wrong with the house.

The roof can pass inspection. The title can be clean. The appraisal can support the price. The buyer can have a down payment, a job, a credit score the lender likes, and a moving truck booked for Saturday.

Then the insurance binder fails to arrive.

This is not an exotic legal technicality. A mortgage lender is advancing hundreds of thousands of dollars against a physical object that can burn, flood, blow apart, collapse, or become uninhabitable. The borrower owns the house subject to the loan. The lender owns a claim on the house. Both are exposed to physical loss. Hazard insurance is one of the devices that makes the loan possible.

The buyer may think she is purchasing a home.

The bank is also purchasing a risk structure.

If the structure cannot be insured on acceptable terms, the economics of the transaction change before anybody carries a box through the front door.

This is the first reason insurance deserves more attention than it gets. It is often described as a product purchased after a more important economic decision. Buy the house, then insure it. Start the business, then call the broker. Build the factory, then place the coverage. Hire employees, then arrange workers' compensation. Ship the cargo, then insure the voyage.

In practice, the order runs both ways.

The availability of insurance helps determine whether the underlying activity can happen at all.

A mortgage without property insurance is usually not a normal mortgage. A commercial lease without liability coverage may violate the lease. A construction contract without bonding may never be awarded. A hospital without malpractice coverage would have to hold much more capital or drastically change what it does. A public company unable to obtain directors-and-officers insurance may find it harder to recruit directors willing to take the job. A manufacturer whose suppliers cannot obtain trade credit insurance can watch a production plan fail in places far from its own balance sheet.

Insurance sits in the background because successful insurance is intentionally boring.

A premium is paid. A contract is issued. The risky activity proceeds. Most policies do not produce a dramatic claim during any one year. The economic value appears as an absence: the project was financed, the shipment sailed, the surgery happened, the board met, the family slept.

This makes the institution easy to underestimate.

Banking has buildings, balances, interest rates, and crises that everyone recognizes as macroeconomic. Insurance often looks like a support industry until it withdraws. Then people discover that risk transfer was embedded in the price and permission structure of the economy.

Homeowners in parts of the United States have been learning this quickly.

The Federal Insurance Office's nationwide analysis of homeowners insurance, released in January 2025, used data covering more than 246 million policy-years from 2018 through 2022. It found rising premiums and declining availability, with the most severe pressure concentrated in places exposed to larger climate-related losses. Homeowners in the fifth of ZIP Codes with the highest expected annual building losses paid substantially more than homeowners in the lowest-risk fifth, and nonrenewals were higher in high-risk areas.

That finding sounds almost tautological. Riskier places cost more to insure.

The important part is what happens when the difference becomes large enough to change behavior.

The Federal Reserve's 2026 report on household economic well-being found that six percent of homeowners went without homeowners insurance on their primary residence. Among low- and moderate-income neighborhoods the rate was higher. Cost was the most common reason among uninsured homeowners who answered the question. Some people said no insurer would cover the home.

A homeowner with no mortgage may be able to make that choice, though the word choice can be generous when the premium is unaffordable. A mortgaged homeowner usually has less freedom. The contract with the lender requires coverage because the lender does not want the collateral uninsured.

If the homeowner allows the policy to lapse, a servicer may obtain force-placed insurance. The name is unusually candid. The coverage is placed because the loan contract requires it, not because the homeowner went shopping for a preferred product.

That arrangement reveals the hierarchy.

Insurance is not merely there to make the homeowner feel safe. It protects a financial system built on secured claims.

The same logic operates in commercial property. A lender financing an apartment building wants assurance that a fire will not leave only a scorched lot behind the debt. A company using a warehouse as collateral faces similar requirements. Investors purchasing mortgage-backed securities are distant from individual roofs, but the asset pool still depends on the physical collateral retaining value.

The connection between insurance and property value becomes especially sharp when coverage gets scarce.

Suppose two identical houses sit on two streets. Both cost six hundred thousand dollars. Both are financed with the same mortgage rate. One can be insured for two thousand dollars a year. The other requires eight thousand dollars, plus a larger deductible, because wildfire or wind exposure has been repriced.

The second house is not economically identical anymore.

The buyer's annual carrying cost is higher. The lender may require proof of a policy with specified limits. The insurance payment does not build equity. If the premium is expected to keep rising, the future resale pool may narrow. A buyer who once compared kitchens and school districts now has to compare hazard models, carrier appetite, and the possibility of nonrenewal.

The insurance market has entered the house price.

This can happen gradually enough that nobody marks the date.

There is no bell announcing that a neighborhood has crossed from ordinary insurability into fragile insurability. Policies become harder to shop. Agents say only a few carriers remain. Renewal increases arrive. Deductibles change. Coverage for roofs or water damage narrows. A state residual market begins accumulating policies. Mortgage brokers add insurance questions earlier in the process.

Then one transaction fails, and the hidden system becomes visible.

California's FAIR Plan is an unusually clear measure of this pressure. It is designed as a basic property insurance option for people who cannot obtain coverage in the voluntary market, not as a normal first choice. Yet by June 2026 it reported 696,562 dwelling and commercial policies in force and roughly $768 billion of exposure. The exposure figure had increased dramatically from 2022.

The number matters less as a scoreboard than as a signal.

A residual mechanism created for hard-to-place risks was becoming a major holder of property exposure in one of the world's largest economies.

The January 2025 Los Angeles fires made the stakes physical. Swiss Re later estimated roughly $40 billion in insured losses from the fires, making them the costliest wildfire insurance event on record. A catastrophe that large tests claims operations and capital, but it also changes the information available to every participant in the market. Insurers see claim severity. Reinsurers see accumulation. Regulators see affordability and availability problems. Homeowners see renewal risk. Builders see a future cost of development.

One of the most consequential things about catastrophe is that the loss does not end when claims are paid.

A major event becomes data.

Insurance is a memory system. It takes individual losses and feeds them into the next set of prices, terms, underwriting rules, building standards, and capital requirements. The system's strength is that it can learn. The political difficulty is that learning produces bills.

After a disaster, everybody wants the insurer to pay based on the contract written before the event. Before the next disaster, the insurer wants to write a contract based on what the last event revealed.

These are different moments of justice.

The first is about keeping a promise.

The second is about whether the next promise can be made at a price people can bear.

The tension becomes acute when risk changes faster than households can adapt. A family bought a home under one set of assumptions. The roof has decades of remaining physical life. The mortgage may last thirty years. Local roads, schools, tax bases, and community ties were built around a settlement pattern that cannot be rearranged every time an actuarial model changes.

Insurance, however, is often repriced annually.

The mismatch in time horizons is enormous.

The house is a thirty-year decision sitting on a one-year policy.

This is one reason insurance crises become political crises. The homeowner experiences the premium not as new information about risk but as a threat to a life already organized. The insurer experiences a rate cap not as compassion but as a restriction on the price needed to cover future claims. The regulator is caught between them, with the added knowledge that forcing insurers to sell too cheaply can make them reduce new business or leave.

There is no mathematical formula that resolves the politics.

There is only a question of who carries the risk when the market price becomes socially unacceptable.

Sometimes the answer is other policyholders. Rates can be compressed across regions or risk classes. Sometimes it is the insurer's capital, at least until losses or expected losses make the position unattractive. Sometimes it is a state-created plan. Sometimes it is taxpayers. Sometimes it is homeowners through enormous deductibles or narrower coverage. Sometimes it is lenders through collateral risk. Sometimes it is future buyers through lower property values.

Risk does not vanish when a premium is suppressed.

It moves.

Florida offers a useful contrast because its property market has gone through repeated cycles of insurer stress, litigation, reinsurance pressure, residual-market growth, and attempted depopulation. Citizens Property Insurance Corporation exists as the state's insurer of last resort. Its policy count swelled when private capacity became scarce, then fell sharply as private carriers took policies back through depopulation programs and market conditions improved.

This movement is often described as an insurance-market story.

It is also a housing-market story, a public-finance story, and a capital-market story.

Citizens does not create riskless property. The FAIR Plan does not make wildfire disappear. The National Flood Insurance Program does not stop rivers from rising. These institutions change the balance sheet on which the risk lands.

That can be necessary. Private insurance has limits, and a society may reasonably decide that access to housing, credit, recovery, or continuity requires public participation.

But public participation changes incentives too.

If the premium on a repeatedly flooded property fully reflected expected losses, some owners would be unable or unwilling to pay it. If the premium is held below risk, the difference has to be financed somehow. If the government repeatedly pays disaster assistance after uninsured losses, that too becomes an implicit form of risk sharing. If a state pool offers coverage at rates below a private market's required return, it may preserve affordability while accumulating exposure that can later be assessed across a wider population.

Insurance policy is therefore land-use policy conducted through another door.

A town can approve a subdivision in a fire corridor. A coastal county can permit more development in a hurricane zone. A city can tolerate weak drainage. The local benefits are immediate: construction jobs, property taxes, housing supply, political support.

The tail risk may be nationalized through federal flood insurance, state residual programs, disaster aid, or future bailouts.

The person deciding to build and the person bearing the catastrophe loss can be separated by geography and time.

Insurance can narrow that gap by charging the risk back to the decision.

It can also widen the gap if the premium is politically disconnected from the risk.

This is why the debate about affordability is harder than either side's slogans.

Actuarially sound pricing can produce socially brutal outcomes. A family with modest income may live in a high-risk house not because it chose a speculative beach mansion but because cheaper land was available in a flood-prone area. Raising the premium to reflect the hazard can make the home unaffordable and reduce its resale value at the same time. The price is informative, but the household cannot retroactively choose a different geography.

Subsidized pricing can also produce brutal outcomes. If the subsidy encourages more people to build or remain in places where losses repeatedly occur, the eventual disaster is not made kinder by the lower premium. The public may end up paying more to rebuild the same vulnerability.

Both claims can be true.

Insurance forces them into the same contract.

The mortgage closing makes this concrete because the transaction requires somebody to say yes.

The buyer says yes to the house.

The bank says yes to the loan.

The insurer says yes to the hazard.

Remove the third yes and the first two may no longer be enough.

This is the hidden power of insurability.

A building code tells you what may legally be constructed. A zoning map tells you what may legally be located on a parcel. A bank tells you what may be financed. Insurance tells you, in a less visible but often equally consequential way, which uncertain futures someone is willing to stand behind with capital.

For a long time, many Americans could treat that yes as automatic.

The premium was another line in escrow.

The next decade may teach more households to ask about it before they fall in love with the kitchen.

The change will not be confined to the most spectacular hazard maps.

Insurance enters housing through several channels at once, and that makes its effect easy to misread. A premium can rise because expected hazard rises. It can also rise because the cost of replacing the house rose, because contractors and materials became more expensive, because claims litigation changed, because reinsurance became dearer, because an insurer's portfolio became too concentrated, or because a carrier decided that it already had enough exposure in one county. Two neighbors can therefore experience the same renewal shock for different reasons.

That ambiguity matters politically.

If every increase is called climate pricing, regulators will miss market structure and claims-cost problems. If climate is excluded from the explanation because other costs also rose, households will be left with a map drawn from yesterday's losses. The useful question is not which single story wins. It is which components of the premium are temporary, which are structural, and which can be changed by prevention.

The answer changes the remedy.

A rebuilding-cost problem may respond to construction supply and code choices. A litigation problem may respond to legal reform. A concentration problem may respond to new capital or reinsurance. A wildfire problem may respond partly to parcel hardening, vegetation management, utility investment, community design, and emergency access. A flood problem may respond to drainage, elevation, land-use choices, and retreat from places where repeated rebuilding becomes indefensible.

Insurance is valuable when it distinguishes among these causes instead of producing one opaque number.

That requires a better conversation at the point of purchase.

A buyer is often given a sophisticated inspection of the house and a primitive understanding of its insurance future. The inspector may identify a cracked foundation, aging wiring, or a roof near the end of its life. The lender discloses interest cost over decades. The title company traces ownership. Yet the annual policy can remain a last-minute quote whose future variability is barely discussed.

That sequencing is increasingly irrational.

In a fragile insurance market, insurability belongs in due diligence before an offer becomes emotionally irreversible. Buyers need to know not only today's premium but the structure of the policy: deductible, exclusions, replacement-cost assumptions, roof settlement rules, flood gaps, wildfire conditions, and whether the quoted carrier is representative of a competitive market or one of the last willing writers.

The point is not to predict a premium twenty years from now. Nobody can.

The point is to recognize that the household is buying a stream of future risk-financing needs along with the physical structure.

Lenders have an interest in this information too. Mortgage underwriting traditionally spends enormous effort on the borrower's capacity to pay principal and interest. A world of volatile property premiums adds another moving obligation. A borrower who qualified comfortably when insurance cost two thousand dollars can become strained if that expense multiplies. The credit model may have been correct about the borrower and incomplete about the collateral's future carrying cost.

That is a peculiar kind of transition risk.

Nothing has happened to the borrower's salary.

Nothing has happened to the mortgage rate.

Nothing has happened to the kitchen.

The risk market around the house has changed.

Communities face the same problem at larger scale. Property taxes depend on values. Local services depend on the tax base. Schools, fire departments, roads, water systems, and bond repayment all assume that a place remains economically inhabitable, not merely physically standing. If insurance scarcity reduces transactions, depresses values, or accelerates departures by households able to move, the fiscal effect can outlast any one renewal cycle.

This is why a retreat from risk can become self-reinforcing.

A stressed place may need more mitigation spending at precisely the moment its tax base becomes less secure. The fire district may need better equipment while homeowners are fighting larger premiums. The town may need drainage upgrades while lower-value properties weaken borrowing capacity. Insurance then becomes both a warning signal and part of the mechanism transmitting the warning.

There is a temptation to solve the problem by making the signal quieter.

Sometimes that is humane. Sudden repricing can destroy household wealth faster than families can reasonably adapt. Transition assistance, explicit subsidies, or public backstops can buy time.

But buying time is useful only if somebody uses it.

A subsidy that accompanies roof hardening, defensible space, drainage, elevation, safer rebuilding, or planned relocation is different from a subsidy that merely preserves yesterday's price while risk compounds. One finances adaptation. The other finances denial.

The distinction will define much of the politics of property insurance in the coming years.

The deepest problem at the closing table is therefore not that an insurer can say no.

It is that the insurer may be the first institution in the transaction forced to price a future the rest of the participants would rather postpone.

The buyer sees shelter.

The seller sees an asset.

The lender sees collateral.

The municipality sees a tax parcel.

The insurer sees all of those things exposed to a distribution of possible losses.

None of these views is complete by itself.

A functioning housing market needs them to coexist.

When they no longer do, the failed binder is not paperwork getting in the way of the house.

It is the market discovering that the house and its risk can no longer be priced as separate things.
