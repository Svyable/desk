# Chapter 8 research note — Florida's depopulation is real, but it is not a clean causal experiment

**Checked:** September 15, 2026

Chapter 8, “When the Carrier Leaves,” uses Florida as evidence that an insurance availability crisis can reverse when legal, pricing, reinsurance, and capital conditions improve. The reversal is real enough to keep. The causal story needs a tighter boundary.

Citizens Property Insurance Corporation has become dramatically smaller. But that shrinkage reflects both improving private-market capacity and a depopulation system deliberately designed to move eligible policies out of Citizens. The manuscript should therefore avoid presenting the fall in Citizens enrollment as if it independently proves that litigation reform caused the market recovery.

## Primary and official evidence

### Citizens Property Insurance Corporation — current policy count

Citizens' public news page reported **266,117 policies in force as of September 4, 2026**. That is an extraordinary decline from the approximately **1.41 million-policy peak in October 2023** cited by Citizens in its June 2025 market update.

Source: Citizens Property Insurance Corporation, *News*, policy-count banner current through September 4, 2026. https://www.citizensfla.com/news

Source: Citizens Property Insurance Corporation, *Citizens’ CEO: The Florida Insurance Market Is Strong*, June 25, 2025. https://www.citizensfla.com/-/20250625-citizens-ceo-the-florida-insurance-market-is-strong

The June 2025 update also reported that 677,920 Citizens policies had been transferred to private-market companies since January 2024 and that Citizens' exposure had fallen from $520.1 billion to $295.1 billion over the preceding year. Citizens attributed the improvement partly to reduced litigation and a stronger private market. Those are useful observations, but Citizens is an interested institution describing the success of a program whose statutory purpose includes shrinking Citizens itself.

### Citizens — the depopulation program is explicitly designed to reduce Citizens

Citizens describes depopulation as the legislatively authorized program used to reduce its insured-property count and exposure by allowing Office of Insurance Regulation-approved private insurers to assume Citizens policies.

Source: Citizens Property Insurance Corporation, *What is Depopulation?*, updated March 20, 2026. https://securesupport.citizensfla.com/app/answers/detail/a_id/383/kw/takeout

That matters for interpretation. A falling Citizens policy count is evidence that risk is moving back onto private balance sheets. It is not, by itself, a natural experiment measuring household preference or isolating the effect of one reform.

### Citizens — private offers can change eligibility for the residual market

Florida's 2022 special-session changes tightened the boundary between Citizens and the private market. Citizens explains that, for qualifying depopulation offers, a policyholder is ineligible to remain with Citizens when an approved private offer is **within 20% of the estimated Citizens renewal premium for comparable coverage**.

Source: Citizens Property Insurance Corporation, *Special Session Brings Positive Changes for Property Insurance Industry*. https://www.citizensfla.com/web/public/-/special-session-brings-positive-changes-for-property-insurance-industry

This is the strongest qualification on the book's current Florida example. Some movement out of Citizens is not simply customers shopping voluntarily and discovering that private insurance has become preferable. The eligibility rule intentionally pushes the residual market back toward last-resort status when a sufficiently close private offer exists.

### Citizens — 2026 risk transfer became easier as the pool shrank

In June 2026 Citizens reported that it had purchased $2.82 billion of reinsurance for the 2026 hurricane season and could withstand its modeled 1-in-360-year hurricane without assessing non-Citizens policyholders. Citizens tied that result to its much smaller policy count, legislative changes, lower litigation, and renewed private-market participation.

Source: Citizens Property Insurance Corporation, *Smaller Citizens Secures Coverage for 2026 Hurricane Season*, June 23, 2026, available from the Citizens news archive. https://www.citizensfla.com/news

The direction is important even if Citizens' attribution is interested: shrinking the residual pool can itself improve the financing structure around the remaining tail. Recovery can therefore be reinforcing rather than linear.

## Strongest counterargument

The Florida sequence does **not** establish that the post-2022 legal reforms, standing alone, caused the market recovery.

Several things moved together: litigation rules changed; Citizens eligibility and depopulation rules changed; private insurers entered or expanded; reinsurance conditions improved; Citizens rates continued moving under a statutory glide path; capital returned; and catastrophe experience affects both insurer appetite and reinsurance pricing. Citizens itself is not a neutral evaluator of which factor deserves how much credit.

The 20% eligibility rule is especially important because it means Citizens depopulation is partly an administrative outcome. A policy moving to a private carrier is still meaningful evidence of private capacity—the assuming company must be approved and willing to take the risk—but the transfer cannot be read as a simple consumer vote for the private market.

The opposite overcorrection would also be wrong. The rule cannot manufacture private capacity from nothing. A depopulation offer requires a private insurer willing and regulator-approved to assume the policy. The collapse in Citizens enrollment from its 2023 peak therefore remains powerful evidence that the market's ability to carry property risk changed materially.

## Manuscript boundary

A future prose pass should keep Florida, but sharpen the lesson from “legal reforms worked and capital returned” to something closer to this:

**Florida shows that a residual pool can shrink quickly when private capacity, legal economics, reinsurance, pricing, and the rules governing access to the residual market change together. The policy-count decline is evidence of recovery, not a clean estimate of which reform caused it.**

That formulation is more consistent with the chapter's own larger argument that insurance availability is produced by several moving systems at once.

It also produces a more useful contrast with California. California's FAIR Plan was still growing through June 2026 even as carriers announced new writing commitments; Florida's Citizens had already moved deep into realized depopulation by September 2026. The difference between **commitment**, **capacity**, and **realized migration off a residual plan** gives the chapter a better way to describe recovery without turning it into a binary event.

## Falsification / update trigger

Revisit this note if later evidence shows that Florida's private-market expansion is not durable—for example, a sustained return of rapid Citizens growth, widespread reversals by recent entrants, or renewed dependence on extraordinary assessments after ordinary catastrophe experience.

The recovery interpretation would strengthen if voluntary-market policy counts and competition remain durable through multiple severe loss years without renewed large-scale migration into Citizens. Even then, attribution among litigation reform, pricing, reinsurance, capital conditions, residual-market rules, and loss experience should remain separated unless stronger causal evidence becomes available.
