# Research brief — The Risk Pool

## Thesis

*The Risk Pool* argues that insurance is not merely a mechanism for compensating people after loss. It is a hidden permission system for modern economic life. Mortgages, construction, shipping, professional practice, public contracts, enterprise procurement, retirement promises and technological deployment often depend on someone being willing to stand behind failure with capital.

The book's core proposition is therefore operational: **when a risk can no longer be pooled, priced, transferred or credibly backstopped, the economic consequences can arrive before the physical disaster does.** A house may remain standing while insurance cost changes its carrying cost, financing and resale market. A technology may function in a laboratory while liability uncertainty makes deployment difficult. A public program may preserve nominal coverage while shifting catastrophe exposure onto taxpayers or future assessments.

## Research vintage

Research was refreshed through September 2026. Time-sensitive figures are dated in the manuscript and ledger. The manuscript should not silently promote an old policy count, loss estimate, regulatory proposal or program status into a permanent fact.

## Evidence lanes

### 1. Property insurance and household affordability

Primary evidence comes from the U.S. Treasury Federal Insurance Office and the Federal Reserve. These sources establish rising homeowners-insurance stress, regional variation, uninsured households, and the connection between expected loss and availability. They do not prove that climate change is the sole cause of every premium increase; exposure growth, inflation, litigation, reinsurance, insurer strategy and regulation can also matter.

### 2. Residual markets and public participation

California FAIR Plan, California Department of Insurance, Florida Citizens, FEMA, GAO and Treasury materials are used to distinguish voluntary markets, insurers of last resort, federal programs and catastrophe backstops. Residual-market exposure is treated as a measure of where risk has migrated, not as probable loss.

### 3. Natural catastrophe and reinsurance capital

Swiss Re and Munich Re catastrophe and market research are used for current insured-loss, peril-mix and ILS figures. These are industry sources and are labeled accordingly. They are useful for market scale, transaction data and independent industry views of catastrophe experience, not neutral proof that any particular policy response is correct. Scenario estimates are not forecasts, and differences between catastrophe datasets should not be mistaken for factual contradiction when definitions or estimation methods differ.

### 4. Flood, terrorism and pandemic limits

GAO, FEMA and Treasury sources show three different public-private structures. NFIP combines insurance and floodplain management while carrying major federal fiscal exposure. TRIP demonstrates a federal catastrophe layer behind private terrorism coverage. GAO's pandemic work shows a case where widespread correlated business losses could push most risk onto government. The book uses these as contrasts rather than claiming one backstop template fits every peril.

### 5. Cyber accumulation

Cyber chapters use EIOPA prudential analysis and Lloyd's *Business Blackout* scenario to focus on common dependencies, cross-line losses and the difficulty of defining one event. The Lloyd's scenario is used only as a stress-test illustration of cross-line accumulation, not as a forecast or realized loss estimate. The manuscript deliberately avoids pretending that a single scenario estimate is a realized loss distribution.

### 6. AI underwriting and AI liability

NAIC and EIOPA sources support claims about insurer AI governance, third-party models, correlated behavior and demutualisation pressure from increasingly granular segmentation. Swiss Re Institute research on AI data-centre value accumulation supports the discussion of very large infrastructure values, physical/digital dependencies and insurance-limit constraints. The manuscript's argument that agent authority itself will become an underwriting variable is a forward-looking inference, not a claim that a mature standardized AI-liability market already exists.

### 7. Health and long-term care

GAO supports claims about health-insurance market concentration. NAIC supports the historical explanation of older long-term-care blocks, including underestimated claim incidence/duration and lower-than-assumed lapses. Health chapters distinguish financing choices from changes in underlying medical cost.

### 8. Longevity and pension backstops

PBGC provides current evidence on the financial position of its pension-insurance programs. Broader statements about longevity risk, annuities and pension duration are conceptual actuarial arguments rather than predictions of near-term mortality improvement.

### 9. Risk classification

Actuarial Standards Board materials are used to anchor adverse selection, credibility and risk-classification concepts. The professional standard describes workable classification practice; it does not answer the political question of which predictive distinctions should be legal or socially acceptable.

### 10. Insurance as prevention

Lloyd's and FM institutional histories support the historical narrative around marine underwriting and loss-prevention engineering. Swiss Re Institute work supports current discussion of protection gaps and projected adaptation benefit-cost ratios. Current mitigation arguments still rely on the broader logic that a loss avoided can improve insurability when the reduction is measurable and recognized in pricing. Institutional histories and industry research are treated as interested sources and not as independent proof of the institutions' own excellence.

## Sourcing convention

Concrete figures, dates, program rules and claims about current institutional status should map to a source-ledger row. Conceptual explanations of pooling, adverse selection, moral hazard, deductibles, reinsurance and capital layers are common insurance economics, but the chapter briefs still identify professional or official anchors where useful.

Industry research is not automatically downgraded or upgraded. It is used for information the source is well positioned to observe—such as catastrophe-bond issuance, insured-loss estimates or market engineering constraints—and paired with regulator, government or multilateral material when a public-policy claim depends on it.

The source ledger contains unique documents. One document can support several chapters, but the same document should not be entered twice under different evidence labels and counted as independent corroboration.

## Falsification convention

Every chapter brief includes a falsification test. These are not predictions that must literally occur. They identify evidence that would materially weaken the chapter's central claim. Examples include sustained return of private capacity without higher rates or public backstops, reliable evidence that granular AI underwriting expands rather than narrows broad pooling without regulation, or repeated proof that mitigation produces no measurable change in insured losses or availability.

The purpose is to keep the book from converting a persuasive narrative into a closed belief system.

## Research gaps to revisit before a future edition

- Exact long-run effect of California's Sustainable Insurance Strategy on voluntary-market availability, FAIR Plan depopulation and household premiums.
- Post-2026 loss experience for new cyber catastrophe and ILS structures.
- Court decisions and policy wording that clarify AI-related errors-and-omissions, product, professional and cyber liability.
- Evidence on whether insurer-recognized home hardening and community mitigation materially change nonrenewal rates, not only modeled expected loss.
- Better household-level evidence on how insurance cost capitalizes into home prices and mortgage credit over time.
- Updated longevity assumptions if medical innovation materially changes healthy life expectancy rather than merely survival.
