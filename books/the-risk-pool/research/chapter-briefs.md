# Chapter briefs and falsification notes

## Chapter 1 — The House That Could Not Close

**Claim.** Homeowners insurance is part of the financing architecture of housing, not an optional accessory after purchase; declining affordability or availability can affect mortgageability and household balance sheets before physical loss occurs.

**Evidence anchors.** S01, S02, S03, S04.

**Counterevidence / caution.** Many homeowners still obtain ordinary coverage, and insurance pressure is geographically uneven. A premium increase can reflect rebuilding costs, litigation, market structure or portfolio management as well as physical hazard.

**Falsification test.** The argument would weaken if high-risk regions experienced persistently rising expected physical loss while insurance cost and mortgage availability remained economically irrelevant to transactions and home values.

## Chapter 2 — The Price of Uncertainty

**Claim.** A premium prices more than expected claim dollars: expenses, capital, volatility, adverse selection, uncertainty and the credibility of the promise also matter.

**Evidence anchors.** S24 plus the solvency and market evidence used throughout the book.

**Counterevidence / caution.** Market power, weak competition or inefficient distribution can also raise prices; a high premium should not automatically be interpreted as an actuarially necessary price.

**Falsification test.** The chapter would fail if insurance could sustainably charge only expected loss with no allowance for expenses, capital or uncertainty and still remain solvent across adverse years.

## Chapter 3 — The Coffeehouse and the Fire

**Claim.** Insurance became economically important by combining information, reputation, contract and distributed capital; loss prevention later became part of underwriting rather than a separate safety activity.

**Evidence anchors.** S22, S23.

**Counterevidence / caution.** Marine insurance predates Lloyd's and developed in multiple commercial centers. Institutional histories can overstate the uniqueness of their own lineage.

**Falsification test.** The historical argument would weaken if long-distance commerce and industrial expansion did not materially benefit from risk transfer, loss information or engineering-based loss prevention.

## Chapter 4 — The Pool

**Claim.** Scale helps insurance only when risks diversify; large portfolios can still fail when apparently separate exposures share one common cause.

**Evidence anchors.** S24, S12, S15, S16.

**Counterevidence / caution.** Correlation can be modeled and transferred; concentration does not automatically make a risk uninsurable.

**Falsification test.** The claim would weaken if portfolio size alone reliably eliminated volatility even when all policyholders were exposed to the same catastrophe or dependency.

## Chapter 5 — When the Pool Breaks

**Claim.** Pools become unstable through underpricing, adverse selection, changed legal interpretation, moral hazard, reserve error or unexpected accumulation; growth can conceal rather than cure mispricing.

**Evidence anchors.** S12, S15, S21, S24.

**Counterevidence / caution.** Hardening markets can attract new capital and later recover; a temporary availability problem is not necessarily permanent pool failure.

**Falsification test.** The chapter would weaken if persistent underpricing and deteriorating selection did not eventually appear through losses, capital stress, reduced coverage or exit.

## Chapter 6 — Insurance as Permission

**Claim.** Insurance requirements function as private economic permissions in housing, construction, professional practice, procurement and other activities because counterparties need credible resources behind failure.

**Evidence anchors.** S01, S02, S10.

**Counterevidence / caution.** Large firms can self-insure or retain more risk, and not every activity legally requires commercial insurance.

**Falsification test.** The argument would weaken if removing insurance and bonding requirements from credit and commercial contracts had little effect on transaction volume, lender behavior or participation by smaller firms.

## Chapter 7 — The Map Is Moving

**Claim.** Property risk changes while physical assets and settlement patterns move slowly, making annual insurance repricing an early channel through which changing hazard and exposure reach households.

**Evidence anchors.** S01, S03, S04, S27, S28, S30, S31.

**Counterevidence / caution.** Climate is not the sole driver of property-insurance cost; exposure, inflation, litigation, building cost and reinsurance also matter.

**Falsification test.** The climate-insurability thesis would weaken if forward-looking hazard changes proved to have little relationship to long-run insured losses, nonrenewals, required capital or mitigation economics.

## Chapter 8 — When the Carrier Leaves

**Claim.** Insurance availability can deteriorate through underwriting restrictions and reduced capacity before a company formally exits; rate regulation can shift rationing between price and quantity.

**Evidence anchors.** S03, S05, S06, S28.

**Counterevidence / caution.** Florida shows that private capacity can return after legal, pricing and capital conditions improve. Exit does not prove one specific cause.

**Falsification test.** The chapter would weaken if sustained rate suppression below insurers' required returns did not produce any change in capacity, underwriting or capital deployment.

## Chapter 9 — The Insurer of Last Resort

**Claim.** Residual and public programs reveal where societies deliberately widen the risk pool after voluntary private capacity proves inadequate or socially unacceptable.

**Evidence anchors.** S03, S06, S07, S08, S09, S10, S19.

**Counterevidence / caution.** Public programs can be financially sound; they are not inherently bailouts. Residual pools also contain adverse selection by design.

**Falsification test.** The argument would weaken if residual/public insurance could permanently charge below underlying risk without moving any cost to assessments, taxes, debt, reduced benefits or other participants.

## Chapter 10 — Reinsurance

**Claim.** Reinsurance globalizes local catastrophe risk and influences retail availability by changing the marginal cost of the tail carried by primary insurers.

**Evidence anchors.** S04, S17, S18, S30.

**Counterevidence / caution.** Primary insurers choose retention structures and can inefficiently buy reinsurance; wholesale pricing should not be accepted uncritically as a pass-through cost.

**Falsification test.** The chapter would weaken if material changes in catastrophe reinsurance cost and capacity had no observable influence on primary underwriting, capital or rates.

## Chapter 11 — The Capital Behind the Weather

**Claim.** Insurance-linked securities broaden catastrophe capacity by moving defined insurance risk into capital markets; trigger design trades speed and transparency against basis risk.

**Evidence anchors.** S17, S18, S25, S26.

**Counterevidence / caution.** High issuance does not prove protection is cheap or adequate. Shared catastrophe models can synchronize market beliefs.

**Falsification test.** The chapter would weaken if catastrophe securities repeatedly failed to supply usable risk-bearing capacity or if basis risk made non-indemnity structures economically unusable in practice.

## Chapter 12 — Cyber Has No Coastline

**Claim.** Cyber catastrophe is organized around shared dependencies rather than geography, creating accumulation across industries, policy lines and countries.

**Evidence anchors.** S12, S15, S16, S32.

**Counterevidence / caution.** Ordinary cyber loss is not necessarily systemic; controls, sublimits and portfolio management can keep much cyber risk privately insurable.

**Falsification test.** The chapter would weaken if large cyber portfolios showed negligible loss correlation from common cloud, identity, software and service-provider dependencies.

## Chapter 13 — Machines Underwrite Machines

**Claim.** AI increases the resolution of insurance classification and can improve prevention, but granular segmentation and common model dependencies can create fairness, demutualisation and correlated-behavior risks.

**Evidence anchors.** S13, S14, S15, S16, S24.

**Counterevidence / caution.** Better prediction can broaden access when it identifies safer risks previously misclassified; more granularity does not automatically reduce social pooling.

**Falsification test.** The chapter's demutualisation concern would weaken if much finer predictive segmentation consistently expanded broad affordable pooling without regulation or cross-subsidy changes.

## Chapter 14 — Who Pays for the Agent?

**Claim.** Agentic AI makes scope of delegated authority a liability variable; insurability will depend on permissions, logging, reversibility, incident control and a workable allocation among model provider, deployer and user.

**Evidence anchors.** S13, S14, S15, S16.

**Counterevidence / caution.** The AI-liability market is still immature and ordinary software, professional and cyber policies may absorb much of the risk without a wholly new insurance category.

**Falsification test.** The chapter would weaken if increasing agent autonomy produced no meaningful change in underwriting questions, contractual indemnities, coverage wording or loss severity.

## Chapter 15 — Health Is Not a House

**Claim.** Health insurance demonstrates the moral limit of individualized risk pricing because society often refuses to let known high medical need become a basis for exclusion; insurers also shape care markets through networks and authorization.

**Evidence anchors.** S20, S21.

**Counterevidence / caution.** Insurance design cannot by itself reduce underlying healthcare prices or utilization; broader provider and pharmaceutical markets matter.

**Falsification test.** The argument would weaken if a fully individualized voluntary health-insurance market reliably produced broad affordable coverage for people with known expensive conditions without subsidy or regulation.

## Chapter 16 — Living Longer Than the Spreadsheet

**Claim.** Longevity and long-duration insurance expose assumption risk: a socially good outcome such as longer life can become expensive for institutions that promised payments on older mortality, lapse or interest assumptions.

**Evidence anchors.** S19, S21.

**Counterevidence / caution.** Healthy longevity can improve labor supply and public finance; the insurance effect depends on morbidity, retirement behavior and product design rather than lifespan alone.

**Falsification test.** The chapter would weaken if material changes in longevity, lapses, morbidity or discount rates had negligible effect on pension, annuity and long-term-care liabilities.

## Chapter 17 — The Politics of the Premium

**Claim.** Premium debates combine three different questions—what risk costs, what households can afford and what society wants to subsidize—and policy becomes clearer when those questions are separated.

**Evidence anchors.** S01, S02, S05, S08, S24.

**Counterevidence / caution.** Separating subsidy from price can be administratively difficult and may not protect households from sudden asset repricing.

**Falsification test.** The chapter would weaken if hiding affordability policy inside premiums consistently preserved both accurate risk signals and stable private capacity better than explicit assistance.

## Chapter 18 — The Protection Gap

**Claim.** Uninsured catastrophe loss does not disappear; it reappears as household distress, public borrowing, migration, interrupted investment, aid or slower recovery. Risk finance is therefore part of development capacity.

**Evidence anchors.** S04, S25, S26, S34.

**Counterevidence / caution.** Self-insurance can be efficient for wealthy households, firms and sovereigns; a protection gap is not automatically proof that more commercial insurance is optimal.

**Falsification test.** The argument would weaken if otherwise similar communities and governments recovered equally well from severe shocks regardless of prearranged risk financing, liquidity or insurance penetration.

## Chapter 19 — The State as Reinsurer of Last Resort

**Claim.** Truly systemic or politically essential risk often requires a public catastrophe layer above private retention, but backstops should attach high enough to preserve private incentives and should make contingent public liability visible.

**Evidence anchors.** S07, S10, S11, S12, S19, S25.

**Counterevidence / caution.** Government backstops can crowd out private capacity, encourage risk-taking or become politically underpriced.

**Falsification test.** The chapter would weaken if private markets repeatedly provided stable affordable capacity for truly nationwide correlated catastrophes without public guarantees, extraordinary capital or post-event intervention.

## Chapter 20 — Adaptation Is an Insurance Product

**Claim.** Insurance can become a prevention system when verified mitigation changes expected loss, underwriting or capital cost rather than merely redistributing who pays.

**Evidence anchors.** S05, S09, S23, S25, S26, S27, S28, S34.

**Counterevidence / caution.** Mitigation investments can fail, benefits may take years to appear, and short insurer/customer relationships can weaken incentives to fund prevention.

**Falsification test.** The chapter would weaken if repeated high-quality evidence showed that verified physical or cyber mitigation had little effect on realized loss or insurability.

## Chapter 21 — The Insurability Frontier

**Claim.** New technologies and risks become scalable when frequency, severity, correlation, controls and liability acquire enough shape for capital to price them; concentration can move the frontier backward even as technical capability improves.

**Evidence anchors.** S10, S11, S12, S15, S16, S17, S18, S33.

**Counterevidence / caution.** Firms can self-insure and governments can authorize activity without commercial insurance; insurability is one constraint among several.

**Falsification test.** The thesis would weaken if high-liability technologies routinely achieved broad institutional deployment despite persistent inability to allocate or finance failure risk.

## Chapter 22 — What Can Still Be Insured

**Claim.** Insurance remains valuable not because every risk is privately insurable but because it creates a layered discipline for deciding what households retain, what private pools carry, what global capital absorbs and what the state ultimately guarantees.

**Evidence anchors.** S01-S34 as synthesis.

**Counterevidence / caution.** Insurance can become extractive, opaque or politically distorted; the institution should not be treated as a neutral oracle or substitute for regulation, prevention, redistribution or public investment.

**Falsification test.** The book's central thesis would weaken if the availability and design of risk-bearing institutions proved largely irrelevant to what households, lenders, companies and governments can build, finance, deploy and recover from.
