# An Archive of Impossible Weather

Before there were instruments, there were adjectives.

People wrote that the sky was fiery, that flames rose from hills, that globes moved above the ground, that a pale light hung over a mountain, that something like lightning appeared where there was no storm. Later readers gathered these accounts under a name that did not exist when many of them were written: earthquake lights.

This is useful and dangerous in equal measure.

Historical science often begins by making a corpus out of descriptions that were never meant to be data. Astronomers reconstruct old comets from court chronicles. Volcanologists use ship logs. Climate historians count frozen rivers, harvest dates and descriptions of unusual seasons. The method can work because nature leaves effects in human records long before somebody builds the correct instrument. But every reconstruction needs a theory of the archive. Who was likely to write? What language did they use for ordinary phenomena? What was copied from an earlier text? Which events were preserved because they were strange? Which mundane nights disappeared because nobody had reason to describe them?

Earthquake lights inherit all of these problems plus one more: the event around them is already memorable.

A strong earthquake edits memory. It gives significance to things that happened before it. A glow that would have been forgotten on an ordinary evening can become ominous once the ground moves. A dog barking, a headache, a strange smell, an electrical disturbance, a restless horse, a warm day, an odd cloud: after catastrophe, the mind has a reason to move them into the causal neighborhood. This does not mean witnesses lie. It means retrospective association is one of the strongest machines in human cognition.

The oldest reports are therefore best treated as evidence that people have long associated unusual light with seismic events, not as clean observations of a single geophysical mechanism. The difference sounds pedantic until someone builds a statistical argument from them.

Robert Thériault, France St-Laurent, Friedemann Freund and John Derr published one of the more ambitious modern attempts to extract structure from the historical record in 2014. Their *Seismological Research Letters* paper assembled 65 earthquake-light events and looked for geological patterns. They argued that the cases were disproportionately associated with rift environments and with faults that offered steep pathways toward the surface. If correct, the pattern would be important. It would imply that the lights are not randomly sprinkled around earthquakes but prefer particular crustal architecture.

The paper is easy to misuse because the result sounds more prospective than the dataset is.

Imagine a clean experiment. Start with every earthquake above some magnitude for fifty years. Before looking at any light reports, code the fault geometry and tectonic setting. Then search a fixed observational archive with a known probability of detecting lights. Count the earthquakes with and without reported luminosity. Correct for population, night versus day, cloud cover, electrical infrastructure, camera density and media attention. Now ask whether rift settings predict the phenomenon.

That is not the historical record we possess.

Instead we have famous cases, forgotten cases, published compilations, uneven witness quality and large changes in technology. A luminous report from an inhabited valley in 1988 is not observationally equivalent to an offshore rupture at noon in 1888. A modern city full of security cameras is not equivalent to a rural district where one person kept a diary. The denominator is mostly invisible: all the earthquakes during which nobody saw a light, or nobody was positioned to see one, or nobody thought the observation worth preserving.

This does not make the rift association meaningless. It changes the claim from `earthquake lights prefer rifts` to something like `within the compiled historical cases considered credible enough for study, rift and near-vertical fault environments occur at a striking rate that deserves prospective testing.` The second sentence is less useful on television. It is much more useful in science.

There is another archival trap. Once a phenomenon has a name, old reports are reread through it. A chronicler says the sky looked red and later writers call it earthquake light. Perhaps it was. It might also have been fire, aurora, lightning beyond the horizon, dust-lit sunset, meteors, electrical discharge, volcanic glow, or a metaphor the translator flattened into meteorology. The original observer did not supply a spectrum.

The proper response is not to throw away history. It is to grade it.

A witness account written that night is different from one recalled forty years later. A photograph with a known exposure and fixed location is different from an illustration. A light reported before rupture is different from one seen amid collapsing power lines. An observation from several separated witnesses with intersecting lines of sight is different from ten people standing together. A luminous cloud seen for ninety seconds is a different physical demand from a millisecond flash. A report from a tectonically quiet night matters differently from one during a magnitude-eight earthquake.

Those are not bureaucratic distinctions. They are how a heterogeneous archive becomes a set of testable subproblems.

The earthquake-light literature has often suffered from the opposite instinct: save the category by expanding it. If flashes, glows, globes, flames, columns and moving lights can all be earthquake lights, the category becomes robust in one sense and fragile in another. It is difficult to falsify because almost any unusual light can enter. The physical mechanisms then proliferate to match the forms. Corona discharge explains one class. Piezoelectric effects another. Gas ignition another. Triboluminescence or fracture light another. Atmospheric ionization another. The category survives because it is allowed to contain several phenomena.

Perhaps that is exactly what nature is doing. There is no rule requiring one mechanism.

But then the plural matters. We are studying lights associated with earthquakes, not necessarily `earthquake light` as a singular natural kind.

The same lesson will become painful in Hessdalen. Place-names are category factories. Once a valley is known for anomalous lights, every strange point in the camera becomes a Hessdalen light until somebody removes it. A car, aircraft, star, satellite, meteor, insect near the lens, internal reflection and genuinely unexplained luminous event can coexist in one archive. If the research program begins by asking what causes *the* Hessdalen phenomenon, it may already have compressed a dozen sources into one.

Historical earthquake reports show how early that compression can happen. A medieval glow and a modern security-camera flash can be placed in the same table because both occurred near earthquakes. A table is not a mechanism.

The reverse error belongs to skeptics. If one famous modern video is shown to be transformer arcing, that does not erase the old corpus. It removes one case. A good skeptical explanation should shrink the residual, not claim the right to own observations it has not analyzed. Debunking becomes unscientific when it uses the existence of false positives as proof that there are no true positives.

This sounds obvious until the subject becomes socially expensive. Strange lights create two reputational markets. In one, mystery is valuable. A researcher can attract attention by preserving anomaly, and a community organized around the phenomenon can become reluctant to lose it. In the other, closure is valuable. A skeptic can gain status by showing that believers missed a simple source. Both incentives are real. Neither changes the photon.

The archive needs adversarial cooperation: people who want the light to be interesting and people who want it to be ordinary, forced to agree in advance on what evidence would count.

If we were building the earthquake-light record from scratch now, the historical cases would not be discarded. They would be placed at the edge of a larger prospective system. We would tag their source quality, timing, geometry, geology and alternative explanations. Then we would stop asking them to carry the weight of modern causal inference. Their job would be to suggest where and what to measure.

That is a respectable job for an old story.

The strange thing is how often science progresses by refusing to decide too soon whether a story is nonsense. Ball lightning spent centuries in that condition. Meteorites did too, in a different era. Reports can precede mechanisms by a long time. But surviving ridicule is not evidence of truth. The history of science contains vindicated anomalies because the history of science contains anomalies. It also contains thousands of observations that dissolved when measurement improved.

The archive does not owe us a surprise.

It owes us candidates.
