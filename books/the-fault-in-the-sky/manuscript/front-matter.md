# The Fault in the Sky

*Earthquake Lights, Hessdalen, and the Geophysics of Things We Call UFOs*

Sven Hardy Benson

There is an old habit in the study of strange things: decide what category the thing belongs to before deciding what happened.

A light crosses a valley and somebody calls it an aircraft. A light hangs in place and somebody calls it a star. A radar paints a return and somebody calls it an object. A camera catches a flash during an earthquake and somebody calls it a transformer. Somebody else calls it plasma. Somebody else calls it a sign. If the footage is grainy enough, eventually somebody calls it a craft.

The trouble is not that one of these answers must be foolish. Several are often correct. The trouble is the order of operations.

This book starts lower, literally. It asks what the solid Earth can do to the air above it. Rocks under stress can change electrically in laboratory experiments. Fracturing granite can emit light. Lightning can make luminous balls whose spectra contain material from the soil. Strong earthquakes have been accompanied by flashes and glows that some researchers believe are generated by the crust, while other apparent examples turn out to be electrical infrastructure failing under the same shaking. In a Norwegian valley called Hessdalen, unusual lights were reported often enough in the early 1980s that engineers carried cameras, radar, spectrum analyzers, magnetometers and other instruments into the snow and tried to catch the problem before it became a story.

They caught measurements. They did not catch an explanation.

That distinction is the book.

I am not trying to prove that earthquake lights explain UFOs, that Hessdalen is plasma, that stressed rock predicts earthquakes, or that every stubborn aerial anomaly has a natural explanation waiting politely in a textbook. Those are four different claims. The evidence is uneven enough that combining them would be a way of losing information.

The strongest material in these pages is almost boring by the standards of mystery. In 2014, Jianyong Cen, Ping Yuan and Simin Xue published an optical spectrum of a luminous ball produced after a cloud-to-ground lightning strike. The event moved horizontally, lasted a little over a second, and showed spectral lines from soil elements. No philosophy is required. Something bright and ball-like moved through the air after lightning, and the instrument saw chemistry in the light. It is a useful control because it demonstrates a fact that popular taxonomies routinely forget: an apparently bounded, mobile luminous object can be a natural atmospheric event without being a conventional cloud, bolt, star, airplane or optical illusion.

The earthquake-light record is harder. It contains old chronicles, witness memories, photographs of disputed provenance, modern videos, instrument records and a great deal of hindsight. A 2011 paper by Jorge Heraud and José Lira analyzed flashes recorded by a fixed security camera in Lima during the 15 August 2007 Pisco earthquake and compared their timing with local ground acceleration. That is better evidence than a witness remembering a strange glow after the ground stopped moving. It is not the same as establishing the source. Earthquakes break electrical systems. Cities are bright machines. Any account that treats a flash during shaking as automatically geological has designed its conclusion into its label.

The physics is similarly split. Friedemann Freund and collaborators have spent years studying electrical charge activation in stressed rocks and have proposed a route from crustal stress to surface charge, air ionization and light. Laboratory currents are real measurements. The geological extrapolation is a model. Other laboratory work has produced luminescence during granite fracture and emphasized piezoelectric effects. Those differences matter. Nature is not obligated to use one mechanism merely because a field has found one that can be drawn cleanly on a diagram.

Then there is Hessdalen.

Hessdalen is tempting because it seems to offer what anomalous-aerial research always asks for after a frustrating single incident: recurrence. If the light comes back, bring instruments. If the witness account is ambiguous, point a camera at the valley. If one sensor lies, use several. Project Hessdalen did exactly that in 1984. Its report describes dozens of visual observations and multiple radar returns, along with attempts using spectroscopy, magnetometry, infrared equipment, a Geiger counter and even a laser. Later projects added more equipment. An automatic station has watched the valley for years.

Recurrence solves one problem and creates another. Once a place acquires a mystery, it attracts observers, tourists, skeptics, cameras, cars, aircraft, expectations and a growing archive of things that are not necessarily the same phenomenon. “The Hessdalen lights” becomes a noun before anyone has shown that the events share a cause. A headlight seen through a temperature inversion, a distant aircraft, a camera saturation artifact and a genuinely unusual luminous event can all enter the same folder if the folder is organized by place.

This is why the skeptical literature belongs inside the story, not in a ritual paragraph near the end. Matteo Leone’s criticism of EMBLA-era interpretations is valuable even where it does not dispose of the entire Hessdalen record, because it attacks the exact place anomalous research is most vulnerable: the jump from difficult image to exotic source. Radar has the same problem. A radar return means electromagnetic energy came back to the receiver with particular timing and signal properties. Depending on the system and environment, that return can come from an aircraft, terrain, weather, multipath, side lobes, interference, anomalous propagation, birds, insects, or an object nobody has identified. The word `radar` is not a magic solvent for ambiguity.

The deeper argument is that we have been drawing the map of the sky with the wrong border. We imagine geology below us and atmosphere above us, then treat unusual lights as if they must belong to one domain or the other. But the surface is an electrical and chemical boundary. Stress changes rock. Charge moves. Radon escapes. Water, dust, aerosols and ions enter the air. Lightning couples clouds to ground. Human infrastructure runs high voltage across the same landscape. A luminous anomaly is often a boundary problem before it is an aerospace problem.

That does not make the answer easy. It makes the experiment better.

The evidence classes used here are deliberately blunt. Documented facts include published instrument readings, dated reports, laboratory measurements and official or primary project records. Credible but incomplete material includes well-described observations whose mechanism or chain of custody is not closed. Disputed allegations remain disputed. Speculation is allowed, because a book about unexplained phenomena that forbids conjecture would be dishonest about how science starts, but speculation is labeled and given defeat conditions.

There are no secret briefings in these pages. No classified access. No recovered materials. No anonymous source whose résumé is asked to carry a physical claim. Where a project says it saw something, the project is named. Where a critic says the image may be headlights, the critic is named. Where the U.S. Geological Survey says decades of work have not produced convincing evidence for electromagnetic earthquake precursors, that boundary stays in the book even though a reliable precursor would make the narrative far more dramatic.

The point is not to protect mystery. It is to protect the measurement from the story that wants to own it.
