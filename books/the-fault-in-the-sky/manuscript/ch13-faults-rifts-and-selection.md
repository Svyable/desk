# Faults, Rifts, and Selection

A pattern in geology is more dangerous than a pattern in the sky because geology gives it a mechanism-shaped shadow.

Thériault, St-Laurent, Freund and Derr’s 2014 review of earthquake-light reports is compelling for a simple reason: the cases did not appear, in their analysis, to distribute randomly across tectonic environments. A large fraction were associated with rift settings or faults with steep geometry. The authors connected that pattern to electrical charge migration through the crust. If charge carriers move more readily toward the surface along particular structures, geology could explain why some earthquakes produce luminous reports and most do not.

This is exactly the kind of synthesis the field needs.

It is also exactly the kind that can fool us.

The first problem is case selection. The paper analyzes a curated set of reported earthquake-light events. To estimate whether rift settings truly increase the probability of light, we need to compare them with the population of earthquakes that could have produced reports but did not. That denominator has to account for magnitude, depth, time of day, population, cloud cover, electrical infrastructure, media access and camera availability. A magnitude-six earthquake at noon in a sparsely populated desert does not have the same light-detection probability as a magnitude-six earthquake at night beside a city.

The second problem is geological flexibility. Fault zones are complicated enough that after an event it is often possible to identify some steep structure, basin edge, conductive contact or lithological feature nearby. If the hypothesis is allowed to move from the mapped rupture to a secondary structure after the light is reported, it gains explanatory power by spending predictive power.

The test has to start before the light.

Select active regions based on geology alone. Some should fit the proposed favorable conditions; others should be controls. Install comparable optical and electrical instrumentation. Run long enough to capture earthquakes and non-earthquake intervals. Do not let the presence of an interesting video influence the geological coding. Then ask whether the preselected tectonic variables predict event rates.

That experiment would be slow, expensive and beautiful.

It would also answer a larger question about anomalous phenomena. Many anomaly fields are built from case-control reasoning without the controls. Researchers collect unusual events, search for shared context, and find it. Military ranges. Nuclear facilities. Faults. Bodies of water. Mineral deposits. Particular weather. Human consciousness. The shared context may be real. But because ordinary non-events were not sampled the same way, the association can be impossible to interpret.

Nuclear facilities, for example, have security cameras, trained observers, restricted airspace and cultural salience. They may generate more anomaly reports even if the underlying sky is no stranger than elsewhere. Fault zones attract geophysical instrumentation. Hessdalen attracts people looking for lights. Observation is not evenly distributed across the planet.

This does not mean patterns are artifacts. It means observation effort belongs in the model.

The rift hypothesis has a physical advantage over many pattern claims because it specifies structure that can affect transport. Near-vertical pathways could plausibly matter if charge needs to move upward. Certain igneous and metamorphic rocks may supply relevant defect chemistry. Fault damage zones change permeability and conductivity. Topography can concentrate electric fields. Those are real mechanisms. The prospective test can therefore be more than statistical: it can measure the proposed intermediate steps.

If favorable rift sites show stress-related currents reaching the near surface, atmospheric electrical changes and then luminous emission, while matched control sites do not, the chain becomes powerful. If light reports cluster at rifts but electrical measurements do not, perhaps the geological association has another cause. If neither cluster survives a prospective design, the historical pattern was likely inflated by selection.

The chain matters because correlation can be true for the wrong reason.

Suppose rift valleys have steep topography and roads that create frequent distant-headlight illusions. A retrospective catalogue might associate unusual lights with rifts without any crustal charge mechanism. Or suppose rifts host certain mineralization that affects atmospheric aerosols. The geological pattern could be real but the proposed electrical carrier wrong. The data should be allowed to preserve the association while killing the explanation.

This is another reason a single word like `geophysical` is too large.

Geophysical causes include mechanical fracture, electrical charge migration, gas release, groundwater movement, thermal effects, piezoelectric response and perhaps atmospheric coupling mediated by topography or aerosols. A genuine geological association does not tell us which one is responsible. The causal architecture has layers.

Hessdalen again sits nearby without fitting cleanly. The valley is not an active major earthquake zone. Its lights, if a residual class is genuinely local, cannot simply be imported into earthquake-light physics by saying stressed rock produces charge. The geological process would have to operate under much weaker or different forcing. Mining history, mineralization, groundwater, radon and ambient electrical conditions become more relevant. That is precisely why combining Hessdalen with earthquake lights is intellectually risky and useful.

If the same mechanism cannot plausibly operate in both places, the book has to split them.

A weaker but more durable synthesis remains: the solid Earth can participate in luminous atmospheric phenomena through several routes, so anomalous-aerial classification that ignores geology is incomplete. That claim does not require Hessdalen to be an earthquake light. It requires only that geophysical pathways exist and can occasionally mimic the visual categories assigned to aerial objects.

The distinction saves us from a common theory-building error. When two mysteries look similar, we want one hidden cause. Similarity feels like compression. But luminous balls produced by lightning, glows produced during rock fracture, transformer arcs during earthquakes and distant headlights in a valley can converge perceptually while diverging physically. The sky is a projection screen on which many processes draw with the same small palette: brightness, color, motion, duration.

A taxonomy based on appearance will therefore overmerge.

A taxonomy based on mechanism will initially over-split because the mechanism is unknown. That is acceptable. `Unresolved luminous event, class A` is a better scientific object than `orb` if the latter suggests a common cause the data have not earned.

Selection bias is not a footnote to this work. It may be the central phenomenon around every phenomenon. We notice the light because the ground moved. We notice the valley because the lights became famous. We notice the rift because the catalogue contains lights. We notice the nuclear base because the report came from a nuclear base. The observation system is coupled to the hypothesis before anyone opens a spreadsheet.

That coupling can be measured too.

Count cameras. Count observers. Count nights. Count cloud cover. Count earthquakes. Count ordinary aircraft. Count grid failures. Count every trigger that gets discarded. Put human attention into the denominator.

Then geology gets a fair trial.
