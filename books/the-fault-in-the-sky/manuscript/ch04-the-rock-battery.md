# The Rock Battery

A piece of granite on a hydraulic press is a long way from a light over a city.

That distance is where the argument lives.

Friedemann Freund’s work on stressed rock begins with defects too small to see and a claim that is easy to caricature if described from the end backward. In some common igneous and metamorphic minerals, oxygen can exist in defect structures that are electrically inactive under ordinary conditions. Under stress, according to Freund and collaborators, those structures can break in a way that releases mobile electronic charge carriers commonly described as positive holes. The term is not mystical. In solid-state physics it refers, roughly, to the absence of an electron behaving as a mobile positive charge in a lattice.

The experimental observation that matters is not a glowing rock. It is current.

In a series of laboratory studies during the 2000s, Freund, Akihiro Takeuchi, Bobby Lau and collaborators stressed igneous rock samples and measured electrical responses. Papers in *Physics and Chemistry of the Earth* and related journals reported currents flowing from stressed rock volumes, changes in conductivity and surface potentials. Their interpretation was that stress activates positive holes that can propagate through otherwise unstressed rock. The proposed geophysical consequence is that a stressed crustal volume could behave like part of an electrical battery.

The word battery is useful if we do not let it become literal too quickly.

A car battery has designed electrodes, known chemistry, controlled geometry and terminals. The crust has mineral grains, fluids, fractures, temperature gradients, variable composition and no obligation to route current toward our instruments. The laboratory result establishes that particular rocks under particular loading conditions can produce measurable charge behavior. It does not establish a continent-sized Duracell waiting to discharge into the atmosphere before an earthquake.

Still, the result is surprising enough without exaggeration. Rock is usually introduced as the passive part of geophysics. Stress accumulates, faults slip, elastic waves travel. Electrical phenomena appear later in the textbook, often as measurement techniques or secondary effects. Freund’s picture gives stressed rock an active electronic role. Mechanical deformation changes the state of defects, mobile charge emerges, and the electrical consequences can extend beyond the directly stressed region.

If that scales, several otherwise disconnected reports begin to look less disconnected. Ground potentials could change. Air near the surface could ionize. Charge could accumulate at topographic points. Electromagnetic emissions might appear. Infrared signatures might change through surface processes. Animals or instruments sensitive to electrical conditions might respond. Light could be one endpoint of a larger electrical cascade.

That sentence contains the seduction and the danger.

A mechanism that can potentially touch many anomalies can become a universal solvent. Every unexplained pre-earthquake observation gets poured into it. Radon change, temperature anomaly, radio noise, ionospheric disturbance, animal behavior, cloud pattern, luminous event: perhaps positive holes. The model gains explanatory territory faster than independent tests can secure it.

This is not unique to Freund’s work. Frontier science is full of mechanisms that begin as specific and become imperial because too many adjacent mysteries lack better owners. The right response is not to ban synthesis. It is to insist on quantities.

How much stress activates how much current in which lithology? How far can the relevant carriers propagate before recombination or trapping? What happens in rock saturated with water rather than a dry laboratory specimen? How do grain boundaries matter? What electric potential can appear at the surface over what area and for how long? What does the resulting atmospheric electric field look like in humid coastal air, dry mountain air, snow, rain or dust? At what field strengths does corona begin at real ground geometry? How much optical power results? What spectrum should the discharge have? Can it produce a diffuse glow, a compact luminous ball, a moving front, or only weak local emission?

Those questions turn a story into engineering.

The positive-hole papers do offer quantitative laboratory measurements, but the book’s present draft does not pretend that the scaling ledger is complete. That work is explicitly frozen in the research gate because it is central. A trade book can explain a mechanism beautifully and still hide six orders of magnitude inside the phrase “could scale.” This one should not.

There is another conceptual difficulty. Earthquakes are not simply moments when rock becomes stressed. Large crustal volumes live under stress for geological periods. Fault zones load unevenly. Microcracks open and close. Fluids alter effective stress. A proposed pre-earthquake electrical signal must explain why the signal becomes observable at a particular stage rather than merely why stress can create charge in principle.

Freund’s framework attempts to answer part of this by focusing on changing stress and the activation of charge carriers in stressed source volumes. But the field problem remains spatially messy. The place where stress is high need not be the place where charge reaches the surface. That could be a feature rather than a bug. It offers one way to understand reports of lights some distance from an epicentral rupture. It also makes retrospective matching easier: if the luminous region is allowed to be far from the earthquake, more lights can qualify.

Every increase in physical flexibility should therefore come with a statistical cost.

The Pisco case is a good example. Lima was far from the epicenter, yet the local crust experienced seismic waves and strong acceleration. If co-seismic light arose locally, the mechanism might involve dynamic stress from passing waves rather than the slowly loaded rupture zone. That is a different physical story from a precursor generated by pre-failure stress at the source. The same video cannot be used casually as evidence for both.

This distinction is often lost because the phrase “stress in rocks” sounds continuous enough to connect everything. But dynamic wave stress, tectonic loading and actual fracture are different histories. A mechanism sensitive to one may not respond to another.

The laboratory literature also has a rival that is less grand but harder to dismiss. Granite contains quartz. Quartz is piezoelectric: certain mechanical stresses can produce electrical polarization. In laboratory faulting experiments, Enomoto and Hashimoto observed visible luminescence and argued that piezoelectric effects played a major role. If their interpretation is correct for their setup, then at least some rock light does not require the full positive-hole pathway.

The two mechanisms are not necessarily exclusive. Real crust can contain several electrical processes. But scientific narratives dislike plurality because plurality weakens prediction. If two mechanisms can produce similar light under different conditions, observing the light alone tells us less.

This is why spectra and electrical measurements matter. A mechanism should leave fingerprints outside visible brightness. Corona in air has characteristic emissions. Hot material has a continuum and line structure related to temperature and composition. Combustion has chemistry. Piezoelectric fields have geometry. Charge migration should alter surface potentials or atmospheric conductivity in ways that can be timed against the optical event.

A photograph is where the problem enters. Correlated channels are where it becomes physics.

The rock battery is therefore best treated as a serious candidate mechanism with an unusually concrete laboratory spine. It is not a license to call every strange light geophysical. Its value is almost the opposite. Because the mechanism is physically specific, it can be made to fail.

That is a privilege many mystery explanations do not offer.

If surface electric fields predicted from realistic source volumes are too weak by orders of magnitude to produce the observed luminosity, the hypothesis loses ground. If the effect vanishes in representative wet fractured rock, it loses ground. If prospective field arrays around active faults record no electrical changes during well-characterized luminous events, it loses ground. If observed spectra are chemically or thermally inconsistent with air discharge, it loses ground.

A mechanism that survives only by moving its unmeasured parameters is not a mechanism anymore. It is a place to store hope.

The rock battery deserves better than that. The experiments are strange enough as they are: squeeze stone, and charge comes out.

Now make it cross the mountain.
