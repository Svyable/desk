# Spectra, Dust, Radon

Once a light has survived the obvious bins, explanation begins to multiply.

Hessdalen has accumulated mechanisms the way old houses accumulate keys. Plasma. Dust. Radon. Combustion. Piezoelectricity. Scandium-rich soil. Electrical coupling through mineralized ground. Ball-lightning relatives. Exotic aerospace. Some proposals are physically serious. Some are mostly nouns arranged around a photograph. The existence of many mechanisms is not evidence that the phenomenon is profound. It is evidence that the observations do not yet constrain the cause tightly enough.

Spectroscopy should help.

A spectrum asks the light what made it bright. Hot solids produce broad thermal emission shaped by temperature and emissivity. Excited atoms and molecules emit characteristic lines and bands. Ionized air carries signatures of nitrogen, oxygen and other species. Sodium streetlights advertise themselves. LEDs have their own structures. A spectrum does not reveal motive, but it is much harder for a source to lie chemically than visually.

Hessdalen researchers have made spectral observations, and some analyses have been interpreted as evidence for plasma-like luminous processes. These claims deserve attention and restraint. The crucial questions are calibration, source identity, signal-to-noise, exposure, instrument response and reproducibility. A spectrum collected from a bright unresolved event can include atmospheric transmission and nearby sources. A saturated detector can deform line ratios. If the source moves during a slitless exposure, position and wavelength smear together.

The right response is not to reject the spectra because the subject is strange. It is to make the spectra ordinary science.

Publish the raw frames. Publish the calibration lamp. Publish the response curve. Show spectra of known car headlights, aircraft lights, lamps, stars and controlled electrical discharges taken with the same instrument. Repeat until the unknown class has a stable signature. If ninety-five percent of candidate events look like known sources and five percent show something else, the five percent become interesting because the ninety-five were allowed to die.

Dust enters because luminous matter does not have to be a gas alone. Mining regions contain mineral particles. Charged aerosols can participate in electrical processes. Some hypotheses for Hessdalen have involved combustion or ionized dust, sometimes connected to unusual local chemistry. The problem is that a valley full of interesting minerals is not itself a mechanism. A useful dust model has to specify particle composition, size distribution, charge, concentration, ignition or excitation route, optical spectrum, lifetime and motion.

The same demand applies to scandium, which has enjoyed a peculiar life in popular explanations of Hessdalen. The presence of an element in regional geology does not establish that it participates in a luminous event. Trace abundance, mineral host, chemical state and aerosol availability matter. If scandium is invoked because a spectral line is claimed, that line should survive calibrated reanalysis and competing identifications. Otherwise the element is decorative geology.

Radon is more interesting because it provides a physical route from rock to atmospheric ionization.

Radon is a radioactive noble gas produced in uranium decay chains and can migrate through fractures and soil. Its alpha particles ionize air strongly over short ranges. Radon concentration can vary with geology, pressure, moisture and gas transport. Earthquake-related radon anomalies have been studied for decades, with mixed prospects as precursors. In a dusty confined environment, radioactive ionization could alter aerosol charging or local conductivity. That does not automatically generate a bright floating light, but it offers ingredients a plasma or aerosol model might use.

Again the question is quantity.

What radon concentration is measured in Hessdalen during luminous events? How much ion-pair production follows? What charge densities can be sustained before recombination? How does the field compare with breakdown thresholds? If a model requires radon levels far above measured values, it fails elegantly. If events correlate with reproducible radon spikes after weather and soil moisture are controlled, the mechanism earns attention.

This is where the 2024 VLF geophysical survey matters in a quieter way. Vargemezis, Zlotnicki, Hauge, Kjøniksen and Strand mapped very-low-frequency electromagnetic responses across the Hessdalen area to characterize subsurface conductivity structures. That kind of work can reveal faults, conductive zones and geological boundaries relevant to charge transport. It does not show that the lights are generated by those structures. A static conductive anomaly is not a dynamic luminous event.

The causal bridge needs time.

If a particular conductive fault or mineralized zone is hypothesized to feed atmospheric electrical activity, instrument it continuously. Put electrodes across it. Measure telluric currents. Monitor local magnetic variation with remote references. Track radon and soil gas. Record weather. Then see whether the electrical state changes before or during well-classified luminous events. If nothing changes across enough events, the geological-coupling hypothesis shrinks.

There is an important asymmetry here. Geology can be strongly suggestive after the fact because landscapes contain enormous structure. Almost any long-lived anomaly site will have faults, minerals, water flow or conductive contrasts worth describing. This is the geological version of seeing patterns in a star field. The proper test is predictive: use the geology to identify where events should cluster, then observe without moving the target.

Earthquake lights have the same problem. A rift association is interesting only if it predicts future luminous-event probability better than observer density and reporting bias.

Spectra can cut through some of this because they are local to the event. A spectrum can say the light contains excited atmospheric nitrogen or sodium or soil-derived silicon. But even then, mechanism is not guaranteed. Ball lightning with soil-element lines can emerge from a lightning strike because the strike supplied the energy. If Hessdalen showed similar lines without thunderstorms, something else would have to energize the material. Chemistry identifies ingredients. Energetics still pays the bill.

The bill is where speculative models should be forced to meet.

Suppose an event radiates ten kilowatts optically for thirty seconds. That is 300 kilojoules of visible output alone. The actual source energy is higher. Where is it stored? Electrical fields? Chemical combustion? Thermal material? Continuous current from the ground? A moving object? Each answer leaves different side effects. Heat. Radio emission. Ozone. Acoustic output. Magnetic change. Chemical residue. A model that supplies optical energy but predicts nothing else is suspicious.

Published estimates of Hessdalen optical power have sometimes been repeated with more confidence than the underlying distance and photometry justify. The safest approach is to recalculate from original measurements. Apparent brightness without reliable range is not power. Camera saturation without calibration is not brightness. If range comes from radar, the radar event must be securely matched to the optical one.

This is tedious. It is also how the mystery becomes smaller without becoming less interesting.

A good natural explanation should get more constrained as evidence improves. A bad one becomes more flexible. Radon plus dust plus plasma plus piezoelectricity plus an inversion can explain almost anything if none of the terms is quantified. So can `advanced technology`.

The discipline is the same on both sides: pick a mechanism, write down what it must do, and give the valley a chance to refuse.
