# Air Breakdown

Air looks like nothing until it stops behaving like nothing.

Put enough electric field across it and the gas becomes a conductor. Electrons accelerate, collide with molecules, knock loose more electrons, excite atoms and molecules, and create a cascade. The air can glow. Depending on geometry and field strength, the result may be a corona around a sharp point, a streamer, a leader, a spark or a full lightning channel. The names matter because “ionized air” is not a single state any more than “hot metal” is a single machine.

Earthquake-light explanations often arrive at this boundary too quickly. Stress activates charge in rock; charge reaches the surface; the atmosphere ionizes; light appears. The sequence is physically intelligible. The difficult word is `enough`.

Air has to be driven beyond thresholds that depend on pressure, gap, humidity, geometry and the history of the discharge. A smooth broad surface and a sharp ridge do not behave the same. A metal fence post, wet tree, fractured outcrop or snow-covered peak can concentrate fields. A field too weak to break down a meter of open air may produce corona at a point. Once ionization starts, the local conductivity changes, which changes the field again. Atmospheric electricity is full of feedback.

This is useful for a geophysical model because landscapes are made of edges.

Mountain ridges and rock tips offer places where a modest regional field can become locally intense. If subsurface charge accumulates unevenly, the visible emission may occur at topography rather than directly above the stressed source. That offers one possible route from underground stress to lights offset from an epicenter. But it also means human structures become part of the experiment. Transmission towers, wires, fences and buildings are excellent field concentrators. A supposed natural corona in an inhabited region can borrow geometry from infrastructure.

The distinction between source and trigger becomes slippery.

Imagine crustal processes raise the local electric field, but the first visible discharge appears on a power line. Is that an earthquake light? The photon came from human hardware, yet the environment may have made the discharge more likely. Or imagine strong shaking causes wires to touch and arc with no unusual crustal electrical state at all. Both appear in video as flashes synchronized with an earthquake. The same image category can contain a hybrid event and a purely technological one.

A useful taxonomy should be causal rather than aesthetic.

That is one reason color is not enough. Witnesses often describe blue, green, white, orange or red lights. Those colors sound diagnostic but are usually not. Camera white balance changes them. Atmospheric scattering changes them. Electrical arcs produce strong spectral features that can look blue-white. Sodium and other lamps can tint clouds. Hot particles change color as they cool. A genuine air discharge spectrum depends on emitting species and conditions. Human vision compresses all of this into a color word.

Spectroscopy is the obvious cure, but spectroscopy of rare transient phenomena is cruel. A slit spectrograph needs the event in the right place. A slitless system can capture a spectrum more opportunistically but mixes spatial and spectral information. A bright source can saturate. Calibration matters. A spectrum from an unknown light may include the source, the air, intervening dust, sensor response and contamination from nearby illumination.

Still, a spectrum can change the problem completely.

If a luminous event shows strong nitrogen and oxygen emission characteristic of air plasma, that is different from a blackbody-like hot object. If metal lines appear, their identity may point to soil, dust or infrastructure. If the spectrum resembles an aircraft lamp, the romance ends. If the source has a narrow-line laser-like component, that asks another set of questions. Photometry adds energy. Polarization may add scattering geometry. The vague light becomes a physical emitter.

The best example in this book comes from somewhere else: ball lightning.

In 2014, Cen, Yuan and Xue published an observation made with slitless spectrographs about nine hundred meters from a cloud-to-ground lightning strike. After the strike, a luminous ball moved horizontally and remained visible for a little over a second. The spectrum contained lines from elements abundant in soil, including silicon, iron and calcium. Whatever debates remain about ball-lightning mechanisms in general, this event did not require a vehicle. A natural electrical event created a compact luminous object with motion, duration and material spectral structure.

That should make every anomalous-light researcher slightly less comfortable with shape.

Humans infer objects from boundaries. A bright region with a dark exterior looks like a thing. If it moves coherently, it looks more thing-like. If radar also returns energy, the word object starts to feel inevitable. But plasma, aerosol clouds, combustion fronts, refractive structures and illuminated particles can all produce boundaries without rigid bodies. The atmosphere does not need permission to imitate objecthood.

At the same time, “plasma” has become a word people use when they have run out of mechanism. The fact that ionized gas can emit light does not explain how a particular glowing sphere confines itself, acquires energy, persists, moves or reflects radar. Plasma physics is a discipline, not a synonym for weird light.

This becomes acute at Hessdalen, where some researchers have described luminous phenomena in plasma-like terms. The valley offers recurring lights and a geological setting, which makes a geophysical-electrical story attractive. But unless measured spectra, electrical conditions and energy budgets support that story, plasma can become the naturalist’s version of `craft`: a noun placed where a causal chain should be.

The atmospheric boundary also forces us to think about scale in a useful way. A tiny corona may be visible nearby but irrelevant at ten kilometers. A large diffuse glow may have low surface brightness yet cover enough area to be conspicuous. A compact source that appears bright on a camera may have saturated the detector, erasing photometric information. Distance estimates based on apparent size are dangerous when the source size is unknown. A one-meter ball at one kilometer and a ten-meter glow at ten kilometers can subtend the same angle.

Triangulation fixes some of this. Two calibrated stations with synchronized clocks can turn an angular track into position and speed. Three are better. Once distance is known, optical power estimates stop floating. If the event is also detected by radar, the radar range can be checked against the optical triangulation rather than being accepted as an independent mystery multiplier.

Atmospheric electricity gives us another measurement: field mills. These instruments measure the local electric field near the surface. If earthquake lights are caused by strong electrical changes, a nearby field mill should see something unless the geometry is highly localized. A network is better because regional thunderstorms and ordinary weather can produce dramatic field changes. Reference stations let us distinguish a local anomaly from a sky-wide one.

The same principle applies below ground. A hypothesis that charge is arriving at the surface should not be tested with cameras alone. Measure surface potentials. Measure current where possible. Monitor conductivity, radon and moisture. Put the optical event inside a chain of simultaneous observables.

Then the phrase “air breakdown” becomes useful because it can fail.

If a light appears with no plausible electric-field change, no RF signature, no ionization-related spectrum and no atmospheric condition favorable to discharge, a simple corona model weakens. If a strong field spike precedes a localized nitrogen-rich luminous emission over a fractured outcrop during seismic loading, it strengthens. Those are different worlds from arguing over whether an eyewitness described the light as a sphere.

Air is not empty. It is the detector screen above the crust.

But first we have to wire it like one.
