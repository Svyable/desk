# Contradictions and incentive audit

## Contradictions that remain live

**Recorded light vs named mechanism.** Pisco supplies better-than-anecdotal evidence that flashes occurred during strong shaking at a camera site. It does not by itself show that charge traveled from a stressed crustal source, nor that the same process explains pre-seismic reports.

**Laboratory current vs crustal scaling.** Stress-activated currents have been measured in rocks. The contested step is scale: source volume, conductivity, charge lifetime, path to the surface, atmospheric field strength, and luminous efficiency under field conditions.

**Fracture luminescence vs long-duration lights.** Enomoto and Hashimoto recorded light during granite fracture, but their light was tied to rupture and was short-lived. Historical reports lasting tens of seconds or minutes require additional storage, transport, repeated discharge, or a different mechanism.

**Rift association vs selection bias.** The 2014 catalogue finds a striking geological pattern. The underlying reports are not a prospective sample, and the denominator — earthquakes with no light reports under comparable observing conditions — is difficult. A pattern can be real and still be overstated by preservation and selection.

**Hessdalen instrumentation vs event identity.** The 1984 project reported radar, photographic and other measurements, but not every instrument fired on every event, and some radar returns lack the modern metadata needed to exclude clutter confidently. “Multisensor” must not be used as if it means one perfectly synchronized object track.

**Spectral suggestion vs plasma proof.** Spectral lines can indicate emitting species, but a spectrum does not by itself establish the confinement, energy source, geometry or lifetime of a plasma. Poor calibration and source confusion matter.

**Unknown vs extraordinary.** A residual event can remain unidentified after analysis. That status is epistemic, not ontological. It does not become craft, intelligence, novel plasma, or geology merely because familiar labels fail.

## Incentives and distortions

**Earthquake prediction has enormous upside.** A genuine precursor would save lives and transform geophysics. That creates a powerful incentive to investigate weak signals, but also a publication and hindsight risk: after a large earthquake, many environmental time series can yield something unusual.

**UFO attention funds observation and contaminates interpretation.** Hessdalen’s anomaly status attracted volunteers, instruments and decades of persistence that conventional funding might not have supplied. The same label can pull analysis prematurely toward vehicles and agency.

**Debunking has its own compression error.** Electrical infrastructure, aircraft and headlights explain many light reports. Demonstrating one ordinary source does not prove that every event in a heterogeneous archive shares it.

**Project ownership matters.** Long-running citizen-science projects accumulate tacit expertise and unique archives, but participants can become invested in the persistence of the mystery. External skeptics can be equally invested in closure. Neither incentive decides the measurement.

**Media rewards the brightest category.** “Earthquake lights,” “UFO,” and “mystery plasma” are better headlines than “classification unresolved because the raw range/Doppler data were not preserved.” The manuscript keeps returning to the duller sentence because it is often the accurate one.

## Discipline used in manuscript

Attribution stays attached to claims: Strand’s project reports; Freund’s mechanism; Heraud and Lira’s timing analysis; USGS’s precursor skepticism; Leone’s ordinary-source critique. The narrator may compare those claims but does not fuse them into consensus.
