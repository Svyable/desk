# The Wave Weapon

## Project Seal and the Bomb That Tried to Move the Sea

**Sven Hardy Benson**

There is a category of secret weapon that becomes more plausible after you learn it was real.

This is not one of them.

Project Seal was real. That is the first difficulty. In 1944, New Zealand and American military personnel investigated whether explosives could be arranged to produce destructive waves against a coastline. They conducted experiments. They built instruments. They measured water. They wrote classified reports. The project survived the end of the war long enough to acquire postwar admirers and a final technical report.

Then comes the second difficulty: nearly every sentence in the paragraph above can be true while the phrase *tsunami bomb* remains badly misleading.

The sea is generous to metaphor and merciless to engineering. A wave seen after a blast is not yet a weapon. A wave produced on command is not yet a useful weapon. A wave that scales in a model is not yet a wave that survives the shelf, arrives at the right coast, climbs the right beach, and does more damage than the same explosive delivered by some less theatrical method. Every one of those transitions has a cost. Some costs are measured in energy, some in timing, some in geometry, some in ships and explosives and weather and enemy action. Secrecy can hide them all under the same word: promising.

I became interested in Project Seal because it sits in an awkward place between debunking and revelation. The easy revelation is that governments really did investigate a weapon that sounds like pulp fiction. The easy debunking is that the weapon did not become practical. Neither tells us why competent people pursued it, what they actually learned, or why the story became stranger after declassification.

The book is about those missing middle steps.

It begins with an observation in the Pacific: blasting around submerged coral could produce waves larger than expected. Wartime minds did what wartime minds often do. They asked whether the nuisance could be turned around. Could water itself become the payload? Could a line of charges make the ocean cross the defensive distance that shells, aircraft, and landing craft still had to cross the hard way?

That question was not stupid. This matters. Calling an abandoned weapons idea foolish after the fact is a cheap use of hindsight. Underwater explosions do move water. Large explosive eruptions can generate damaging tsunami. Nuclear tests taught generations of researchers about cavities, surge, waves, and the peculiar inefficiency of trying to turn a compact release of energy into the long-period displacement that makes the worst natural tsunamis so dangerous. Hunga Tonga, in 2022, complicated the subject again by showing how an enormous volcanic explosion could drive tsunami through several mechanisms at once, including water-cavity collapse and pressure coupling through the atmosphere.

But the existence of those mechanisms is exactly why Project Seal deserves patient treatment. It forces us to stop asking the childish binary question — can explosions make tsunamis, yes or no? — and ask the engineer’s sequence instead. What kind of wave? At what distance? With what period? Produced by what geometry? How much energy reaches the mode of motion you care about? What happens over the continental shelf? How sensitive is the result to depth? How much explosive must be emplaced, how accurately, how close to the target, and under what conditions?

A weapon can fail without its underlying physics being false.

That is the territory of this book.

The evidence is unusually treacherous in a useful way. We have a documented secret program whose popular afterlife often outruns the primary record. We have later technical literature that clarifies why explosion-generated waves behave differently from the public imagination of a wall of water summoned by a button. We have modern natural events that demonstrate extraordinary wave generation through mechanisms Project Seal did not possess. And we have the usual residue of classification: gaps that invite either excessive trust in official closure or excessive confidence that the missing material must contain the spectacular answer.

I will use neither shortcut.

When the record is firm, I will say so. When a later report reconstructs an event from declassified files, I will attribute it. When a claimed number depends on how shots and charges were counted, I will not polish the ambiguity away. When the project’s own extrapolations look stronger than later hydrodynamics can comfortably support, the extrapolation and the objection will sit beside each other. A secret project is not made more technically competent by being secret.

The larger question is what states learn from weapons that never become weapons.

Sometimes a failed project dies cleanly. Sometimes it leaves instruments, methods, reports, people, and a technical vocabulary that migrate elsewhere. Sometimes it leaves a myth whose half-life exceeds that of the program because the public encounters the terrifying premise before it encounters the denominator. Project Seal belongs to that third category, but perhaps not only that category.

The sea did move.

The argument begins there.
