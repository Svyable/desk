# Working Notes

## Central question

What, exactly, separates a real explosion-generated water wave from a practical weapon capable of producing destructive coastal inundation on demand?

Project Seal is useful because the record lets the book follow that question through several layers that are often flattened together: observation, experiment, model, extrapolation, engineering, military logistics, classification, and myth.

## Reader

A serious general reader interested in secret programmes, weapons history, frontier engineering, ocean physics, and the difference between “governments investigated it” and “it worked.” The book should be readable without equations while never hiding the physical denominator.

## Contribution beyond nearby Desk books

This is not another secrecy/UFO ecology book, not another natural-hazard book, and not another ancient-technology or nuclear-geochemistry book. Its core object is a weapons-development scaling problem with a declassified documentary spine. The closest earlier Desk material is Operation Popeye, but the empirical question is different: Popeye asks how a probabilistic atmospheric intervention crossed into operations; Project Seal asks why a real hydrodynamic effect did not cleanly cross from experiment into a practical weapon.

## Book movement

Chapters 1–4 reconstruct the wartime opportunity: observation, Nouméa, command enthusiasm, and the creation of a dedicated experimental site.

Chapters 5–10 force the reader into the physics and engineering: displacement, depth, repeated trials, charge geometry, scaling, and coastline dependence. This is the book’s technical middle and must not be reduced to “they needed a lot of explosives.”

Chapters 11–14 follow the idea into the postwar nuclear age, where much larger explosive events and later systematic wave research put the original assumptions under pressure.

Chapter 15 uses Hunga Tonga as a modern control: an enormous explosion really can produce dangerous tsunami, but by coupled mechanisms and source scales that clarify rather than rescue the Project Seal design.

Chapters 16–18 turn back to epistemology: how a failed or incomplete secret weapon survives as a conspiracy primitive, what classified dead ends contribute to knowledge, and what Project Seal actually teaches about commanding large natural systems.

## Strongest competing explanation

The strongest skeptical reading is that this is a small wartime engineering episode inflated by later journalism. Perhaps the most interesting science happened after Project Seal, and perhaps secrecy mattered little beyond delaying public curiosity. The manuscript should give this interpretation real space. If the primary records show that the senior investigators themselves treated the “weapon” as increasingly remote and the later mythology comes mostly from 1999/2013 headline language, then the thesis should narrow.

## Defeat conditions

- Primary records show no sustained military interest beyond a short exploratory phase.
- The 1950 report itself sharply rejects the practical weapon interpretation, making later “tsunami bomb” framing largely a media artifact.
- Later explosion-wave research does not materially revise or constrain the project’s own scaling claims.
- Secrecy is shown to have had little effect on the project’s cultural afterlife relative to ordinary sensational reporting.
- The experimental data are too incomplete to responsibly reconstruct the technical argument; in that case the book must become more explicitly a history of uncertainty rather than pretending to recover performance.

## High-value archival work

1. Full 1950 Leech report: digitize/page-index every experiment table, figure, scaling relation, recommendation, and uncertainty statement.
2. Gibson origin memorandum, 13 January 1944.
3. Puttick instructions and Salmon/Halsey correspondence.
4. New Zealand War Cabinet minute establishing the unit.
5. Personnel roster and site records for Whangaparāoa.
6. Experimental logs: shot number, charge mass, depth, spacing, timing, bathymetry, recorder locations, observed waves.
7. Correspondence with J. M. Snodgrass and University of California Division of War Research.
8. 1946 Compton visit and Washington/Wellington letters.
9. Leech’s postwar correspondence and CBE citation.
10. University Council records explaining why travel/research proposals were accepted or declined.
11. Classification/declassification file history.
12. Contemporary 1947 newspaper speculation to compare with later retellings.

## High-value technical work

1. Read Van Dorn/Le Méhauté/Hwang 1968 completely and build a claim ledger on coupling efficiency, characteristic periods, breaking, shelf propagation, run-up, and scale effects.
2. Recover Van Dorn’s 1961 paper on surface waves from nuclear explosions.
3. Compare Project Seal charge configurations with documented underwater nuclear-test geometry only where the comparison is physically meaningful.
4. Build a simple dimensional-analysis explainer: explosive energy is not the same as water-column displacement; source length, duration, depth, and wavelength matter.
5. Reconstruct why earthquake tsunamis get long wavelengths from broad seafloor deformation.
6. Explain cavity collapse and near-field wave generation without implying all explosive energy becomes tsunami energy.
7. Compare Hunga Tonga’s near-field cavity mechanism, atmospheric Lamb-wave coupling, and far-field tsunami to Project Seal’s intended source.
8. Investigate whether modern numerical models have explicitly revisited Project Seal configurations.
9. Quantify logistics: tonnes of explosive, emplacement distance, line length, timing tolerance, mine-laying vulnerability, and alternative military uses of the same explosive mass.
10. Treat run-up as coastline-specific. The weapon concept depends partly on the target’s bathymetry, shelf slope, harbor geometry, tide state, and topography.

## Human/history work

- Recover Leech as a person without converting him into a mad-scientist caricature.
- Recover Gibson’s actual engineering/survey background.
- Include New Zealand’s wartime scientific institutions and asymmetry with U.S. resources.
- Do not write Whangaparāoa as empty geography. Research Māori relationships to the area, wartime requisition/control, and local memory.
- Track technicians and instrument builders where the record permits; weapons programmes are usually remembered by the senior scientist even when practical knowledge sits lower in the org chart.

## Anti-slop guardrails

- Do not open every chapter with a cinematic reconstructed scene. Use documents, measurements, maps, or technical constraints as narrative objects.
- Avoid repeating “the sea is hard to control” as a chapter moral. Each chapter needs a distinct mechanism.
- Do not use the phrase “science fiction became reality” except to criticize it.
- Keep the weapon-versus-effect distinction alive without restating it mechanically.
- No invented dialogue at Nouméa, War Cabinet, Halsey meetings, or Whangaparāoa.
- Resist the temptation to turn Hunga Tonga into a dramatic proof. It is a control case, not vindication.
- Do not let the conspiracy chapter dominate the book. The physics is more interesting.

## Length expansion budget

Each chapter should ultimately land around 3,500–4,500 words. Expansion must come from original records, experiment-specific data, worked physical explanations, and sustained historical cases.

- Chs. 1–4: archival scenes and institutional history.
- Chs. 5–10: experiment tables, hydrodynamics, dimensional reasoning, site geometry, military logistics.
- Chs. 11–14: postwar correspondence, nuclear-test datasets, Scripps/Tetra Tech technical literature.
- Ch. 15: modern Hunga Tonga record and mechanism comparison.
- Chs. 16–18: declassification history, claim genealogy, epistemology of secret dead ends, and final synthesis.
