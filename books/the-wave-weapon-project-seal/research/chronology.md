# Working Chronology

## Before Project Seal

**1936–1942** — In Leech’s later account, Wing Commander E. A. Gibson’s Pacific survey experience includes observations that blasting submerged coral can occasionally produce unexpectedly large waves. The original survey/blasting records remain a recovery target.

**November 1943** — Tarawa demonstrates, among many other things, how shallow-water geometry, reefs, tides, and landing assumptions can become lethal constraints in amphibious warfare. Popular Project Seal narratives sometimes connect the weapon concept to the amphibious problem; that causal link must be documented rather than inferred.

## Project origin and wartime work

**13 January 1944** — According to the later Project Seal report, Gibson puts the artificial-inundation idea to Lieutenant General Sir Edward Puttick, New Zealand Chief of General Staff.

**February 1944** — Gibson, Professor J. M. Snodgrass of the University of California Division of War Research, and Professor Thomas D. J. Leech examine the problem around Nouméa, New Caledonia. Preliminary explosive-wave experiments follow.

**1944, after the New Caledonia work** — Admiral William F. Halsey is reported to support further investigation. Recover the original memorandum and date it precisely.

**5 May 1944** — Later archival reconstructions report New Zealand War Cabinet approval for a research unit under Leech. Verify against War Cabinet minute.

**6 June 1944** — The Whangaparāoa experimental programme is reported to begin. The site becomes the principal laboratory for controlled work on charge depth, spacing, timing, wave recording, and scaling.

**1944–early 1945** — Experimental series continues. Popular accounts describe thousands of explosive charges or tests; this manuscript will not settle on a precise number until the shot ledger is reconstructed from the final report.

**8 January 1945** — Often given as the date the principal wartime unit ceased operations. Verify against administrative records.

## Postwar persistence

**1945–1946** — The immediate offensive requirement recedes, but interest in the underlying work does not disappear.

**1946** — Dr. Karl T. Compton visits New Zealand and reportedly responds favorably to Leech’s deductions. A July 1946 Washington-to-Wellington letter is quoted in later reporting as indicating willingness to recommend relevant Bikini test data be shared for further study. Original correspondence required.

**1947** — Leech receives a CBE for wartime research. Contemporary press speculation about the secret weapon should be recovered because it provides an early mythology layer before declassification.

**1947–1948** — Leech is reportedly invited to engage with U.S. researchers / Bikini data analysis, while university responsibilities and institutional constraints complicate extended travel. Primary correspondence required.

**1948–1950** — Work becomes increasingly analytical and academic. Postgraduate students reportedly address unresolved wave problems.

**18 December 1950** — Leech dates the final report, *Project SEAL: The Generation of Waves by Means of Explosives*, issued through New Zealand’s Department of Scientific and Industrial Research.

## The physics grows beyond the project

**1950s–1960s** — Nuclear test programmes and oceanographic research generate much larger datasets on impulsively generated waves, underwater explosions, cavities, run-up, and shelf effects.

**1968** — Van Dorn, Le Méhauté, and Hwang produce *Handbook of Explosion-Generated Water Waves, Volume I — State of the Art* for U.S.-sponsored research. This later literature is central to testing the military extrapolations associated with Project Seal.

**1971** — A commonly reported declassification date for the final Project Seal report. Confirm exact classification/declassification history from the record itself.

## Public rediscovery

**1999** — New Zealand reporting based on released Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Trade material brings Project Seal to broad public attention. “Tsunami bomb” becomes the durable popular label.

**2004–2005** — The Indian Ocean tsunami produces a wave of conspiracy claims alleging artificial causation. Project Seal is frequently invoked as precedent despite no direct evidence connecting it to the disaster.

**2003–2005** — Impact-tsunami research revisits Van Dorn’s explosion-wave work, illustrating that the old weapons literature remained scientifically relevant to a different problem: how compact energetic events couple into ocean waves.

**2012–2013** — Renewed New Zealand media attention follows Ray Waru’s archival work, amplifying the project internationally.

## Modern physical control

**15 January 2022** — Hunga Tonga–Hunga Ha’apai erupts explosively and generates destructive tsunami through multiple mechanisms, including near-field water-cavity effects and atmospheric pressure coupling. The event is a powerful reminder that “explosion-generated tsunami” is not one mechanism and that source scale and geometry dominate comparisons.
