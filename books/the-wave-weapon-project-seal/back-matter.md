# Back Matter

## A note on evidence

Project Seal is a documented programme surrounded by unusually persistent compression. The project existed. Explosives were used to study artificial waves. New Zealand and American military interests intersected around the work. Thomas D. J. Leech produced a final report in 1950. Later U.S. technical work studied explosion-generated water waves in considerably greater depth.

Those facts do not establish that a practical strategic “tsunami bomb” was completed.

The manuscript therefore separates programme history from capability history. A document can establish that officials considered an idea without establishing that the idea worked. A model can support a full-scale prediction without constituting a full-scale demonstration. An underwater nuclear explosion can generate major local waves without behaving like a broad megathrust source. A modern explosive volcanic eruption can generate catastrophic tsunami without validating a wartime conventional-charge configuration.

Where the primary Project Seal record remains incomplete in the current research pass, claims are intentionally attributed or qualified. The next archival pass should replace secondary reconstructions with page-level citations to the 1950 report, War Cabinet records, Halsey/Puttick/Gibson correspondence, Karl Compton correspondence, experimental shot logs, and U.S. Navy evaluations.

## Research appendix: questions the next edition must answer

The current manuscript deliberately leaves several questions open rather than inventing closure.

The exact experiment count needs reconstruction. Popular histories repeatedly cite roughly 3,700 “bombs” or “explosions,” but a serious account must distinguish charges from firing events, experimental series, and recorded observations.

The full-scale design needs reconstruction from Leech’s own diagrams and equations. Later summaries frequently mention an enormous line of explosive and a destructive wave, but the exact relation among line length, explosive mass, offshore distance, water depth, model scale, and target geometry should not be inherited from journalism.

The criticism of Project Seal’s scaling needs named primary sources. Secondary treatments have said advisers found important errors in Leech’s calculations. That may be true and important. The manuscript should show the equations, critics, dates, and alternative estimates rather than repeating a verdict.

The programme’s postwar status needs sharper institutional history. Karl Compton’s reported interest, proposed Bikini-data access, university constraints on Leech’s travel, and later requests for the final report can indicate scientific relevance without proving continued weapons development. The original correspondence should decide how those relationships are described.

Whangaparāoa needs to be restored as a place rather than used only as a test-site noun. Māori and local histories, wartime land control, nearby communities, and the practical footprint of the research unit belong in the full account.

The later U.S. explosion-wave literature should be compared quantitatively with Project Seal, especially on source-depth scaling, characteristic periods, conversion efficiency, offshore breaking, bottom damping, and run-up. This is the key test of the book’s “scale trap” thesis.

Finally, the mythology needs a claim genealogy. When did “tsunami bomb” become the dominant phrase? Which numbers entered through the 1999 reporting, which through the 2012–2013 revival, and which are traceable to the 1950 report? A documented programme should not be allowed to authenticate later embellishments merely by existing.

## Acknowledgment of uncertainty

The public record may expand. New Zealand, U.S., university, or private archival material may change the assessment of technical maturity or postwar interest. The manuscript’s evidence classes are designed to accommodate that possibility. New documentation should move a claim between classes; it should not be forced to confirm either the debunking or the secret-superweapon version of the story.

That is the standard Project Seal itself deserves.
