# The Wave Weapon: Project Seal and the Bomb That Tried to Move the Sea

**Author:** Sven Hardy Benson
**Status:** Drafting
**Chapters:** 18

## Thesis

In 1944, while the Manhattan Project was still secret and unfinished, American and New Zealand military planners seriously investigated a different way to attack a coastline: use explosives to make the sea do the killing. Project Seal matters less because it produced a usable “tsunami bomb” — it did not — than because it exposed a recurring problem in secret weapons work. A frightening effect can be real at small scale while the weapon implied by it fails at the point where geometry, energy transfer, bathymetry, timing, logistics, and the enemy are finally allowed into the calculation.

The book follows Project Seal from a wartime observation of unexpectedly large waves after coral-blasting operations through New Caledonian trials, the Whangaparāoa research unit, Thomas Leech’s experiments, postwar interest, and the 1950 final report. It then places those claims beside later explosion-wave research and modern tsunami physics. The result is not a conspiracy story and not a debunking exercise. It is a book about what classified experimentation can genuinely establish, what extrapolation can manufacture, and why a failed weapon can remain culturally more powerful than a successful experiment.

## Reader promise

By the end, the reader should be able to distinguish four things that popular accounts usually collapse: making a measurable water wave with an explosion; scaling that wave in a tank, pond, bay, or model geometry; generating a destructive near-field inundation; and creating a militarily practical tsunami weapon against a defended coastline. Those are not the same achievement.

The stranger lesson is that secrecy preserves weak hypotheses differently from open science. A dead-end weapons program can remain half legendary because the public learns that the project existed long before it learns exactly where the physics, engineering, or military practicality failed.

## Strongest competing explanation

Project Seal may be historically interesting but scientifically unsurprising: wartime engineers explored an idea, learned that underwater explosions can generate waves, and ultimately confronted the poor efficiency and logistical difficulty of using blasts to move enough ocean to matter. On this view, secrecy adds atmosphere but little explanatory value. The book has to earn any stronger claim by showing exactly how classification, fragmented records, postwar reporting, and later nuclear-wave research altered what different audiences could know and when.

## Evidence discipline

1. **Verified / documented:** Project Seal existed; it involved New Zealand and U.S. military participation; Thomas D. J. Leech produced a 1950 final report titled *Project SEAL: The Generation of Waves by Means of Explosives*; wartime and postwar tests investigated explosion-generated waves; later U.S.-sponsored research studied explosion-generated water waves; large natural tsunamis generally require displacement of very large water volumes over large areas.
2. **Credible but incomplete / model-dependent:** exact counts of individual charges and subtests across phases; the degree to which particular model results could have scaled to a chosen real coastline; what senior officials believed at each moment; how close any operational design came to serious deployment planning.
3. **Disputed / not established:** that Project Seal produced a practical city-destroying weapon; that a small number of conventional or nuclear underwater blasts can reliably generate a far-field earthquake-like tsunami; claims linking later natural tsunamis to secret weapons without direct evidence.
4. **Speculative synthesis:** Project Seal as a case study in the “scale trap” of classified weapons science — a program can demonstrate a real effect, model an alarming extrapolation, and still fail to cross the engineering and operational thresholds that turn an effect into a weapon.
