# The Wave After the Blast

The strange part was not that an explosion moved water. Anyone who had thrown a stone into a pond had already established the general principle. The strange part was that some underwater blasting operations in the Pacific seemed to produce waves larger than the men doing the blasting expected.

Expectation is where weapons begin.

According to the later Project Seal report prepared by Thomas D. J. Leech, Wing Commander E. A. Gibson had spent years around Pacific surveys and submerged coral formations before he carried an idea to New Zealand’s Chief of General Staff in January 1944. Blasting reef and coral was practical work. You needed passages, channels, approaches. Explosives changed the underwater landscape faster than picks and dredges. Gibson’s reported observation was that the water did not always behave as the simple nuisance left over after the useful work. Occasionally, the wave was the interesting thing.

There are several ways to tell the story from here, and most of them are too clean.

One version begins with a brilliant officer noticing a hidden property of nature and imagining a secret superweapon. Another begins with desperate wartime planners wasting time on a physical impossibility. A third, favored by the later mythology, begins with the astonishing revelation that governments had already discovered how to manufacture tsunamis and then buried the technology. Each version knows too early what Project Seal was.

In January 1944, it was a question.

Could the effect be repeated? Could it be enlarged? Could the water, rather than the explosive fragments or blast pressure, be made to carry destructive energy onto a shore?

Those questions sound similar. They are not.

An underwater explosion transfers energy into several things at once. It heats and compresses gases. It drives a shock through water. It creates and collapses a cavity. It moves sediment and debris. It throws spray. It launches waves across the surface. The spectacular part seen from shore may not be the part that carries much energy to distant shorelines. A violent local disturbance can be a poor long-range wave generator. A wave with impressive height near the source can have the wrong period, wavelength, or directional structure to behave like the long, broad displacement produced by a great submarine earthquake.

The distinction is so elementary that it tends to disappear from popular accounts. Energy is treated like a liquid that can be poured from one container into another. Put enough explosive under the sea, the story goes, and eventually you get an earthquake’s worth of tsunami. But the ocean is not a universal energy converter. How energy is introduced matters. The geometry of the source matters. Depth matters. Duration matters. The area over which the water column is displaced matters. The shape of the bottom matters before the wave is born and again when it approaches land.

Project Seal would spend years discovering versions of that sentence.

The wartime timing gave the original question unusual permission. By 1944, Allied forces had learned at terrible cost that Pacific warfare was an argument with geography. Coral reefs could stop landing craft. Tides could expose mistakes. Beaches could be defended, mined, ranged by artillery, wired, and registered in advance. Amphibious assault compressed weather, hydrography, engineering, logistics, fire support, and human fear into a few hours in shallow water. A weapon that could cross the final miles without needing a landing craft had obvious imaginative appeal.

It is tempting to connect Project Seal directly to one famous amphibious disaster and write as though the weapon was born from a single battlefield lesson. The documentary chain is not yet strong enough for that. Tarawa is an unavoidable part of the strategic atmosphere, and later retellings have drawn the connection, but an atmosphere is not an origin memorandum. The surviving account we can currently stand on is narrower: Gibson had observed large waves associated with blasting and proposed investigating artificial inundation.

That is enough.

Wartime research is full of ideas that look absurd only after the hidden denominator becomes visible. Radar, proximity fuzes, airborne troops, bouncing bombs, acoustic torpedoes, bat bombs, ice aircraft carriers, incendiary attacks shaped by urban wind, and nuclear weapons all occupied, at different times, the same broad territory where engineering had to decide whether a surprising effect could survive contact with scale. Some did. Some did not. The fact that a concept sounds cinematic is almost useless as an early filter.

The useful filter is measurement.

What Gibson’s idea needed was not enthusiasm but a controlled relationship between charge, water depth, source geometry, and resulting wave. That would require instruments, repeatable shots, and a site where the sea could be treated, however imperfectly, as a laboratory.

Before that infrastructure existed, however, there was a faster experiment available. The South Pacific war had already assembled explosives, engineers, naval personnel, and a reason to ask unusual questions. In February 1944, Gibson, Leech, and J. M. Snodgrass of the University of California’s Division of War Research examined the concept around Nouméa, New Caledonia. The details matter enough that they deserve their own chapter. What matters here is the epistemic jump.

A wartime observation became an experimental programme.

That jump is easy to underestimate because later knowledge leaks backward. We know the project’s codename. We know there would be a report. We know the weapon was never fielded. We know “tsunami bomb” will become the phrase that survives. The men involved did not begin with that tidy package. They began with a wave and a hypothesis.

The word *tsunami* itself can distort the scene. Project Seal’s records often used the older language of “tidal waves,” a term that modern geophysics avoids because tides have nothing to do with the generation of a seismic sea wave. That linguistic imprecision does not mean the researchers confused tides with explosions. It means they were working in a period when the popular and technical vocabulary was still less settled. But the old phrase encouraged a mental picture that remains hard to kill: a high wall of water advancing like a moving cliff.

A real tsunami in deep ocean can be almost unremarkable to a ship. The destructive quality is often tied to extraordinary wavelength, the movement of an enormous water mass, and what happens as that long wave enters shallower water and runs onto land. The source of a major earthquake tsunami is not a point. A fault can rupture over hundreds of kilometers, lifting or dropping broad regions of seafloor and displacing the water above. The wave inherits that spatial scale.

An explosive charge is compact.

This is not an absolute prohibition. Compact energetic events can generate very large water waves. Landslides can. Impacts can. Volcanic explosions and collapses can. Underwater nuclear tests have generated dramatic local waves. The 2022 Hunga Tonga–Hunga Ha’apai eruption produced destructive tsunami through several mechanisms, including the collapse of a water cavity near the source and pressure coupling through the atmosphere. The point is not that explosions cannot make dangerous waves. The point is that the mechanism must be specified before the analogy earns anything.

Project Seal began before most of that later evidence existed.

The war therefore gave its researchers an unusually honest problem. They could not answer the modern argument by citation. They had to blow things up and watch the water.

There is something almost old-fashioned about the experimental logic. Put a charge at a chosen depth. Fire it. Record the wave. Change the depth. Fire again. Change spacing. Repeat. Compare measurements with predictions. Move from open water to controlled geometry. Ask whether several charges can cooperate. Ask whether their waves add or interfere. Ask whether the bottom helps or hurts. Ask whether a pattern that looks promising at one scale still looks promising when extrapolated to another.

A large part of twentieth-century weapons science is this repetitive and unromantic. The myth remembers the bomb. The work is calibration.

Calibration can also seduce.

If a model predicts an amplitude and the instrument records something close, confidence rises. If repeated experiments produce orderly relationships, the system begins to feel understood. The danger is not bad science. The danger is using good small-scale science to support a larger claim whose hidden variables have not yet entered the room.

This is the scale trap at the center of Project Seal.

Suppose a researcher demonstrates that there is an optimum charge depth for generating a surface wave. That is valuable knowledge. Suppose a set of charges produces a larger wave than one charge. Also valuable. Suppose model geometry suggests that a line of explosions at a particular offshore distance could create substantial run-up at a chosen shoreline. More interesting still.

None of these results alone tells you whether a military can emplace the required explosive mass in wartime, along the required geometry, at the required depth, with the required timing, without being stopped, and produce more useful destruction than simpler uses of the same resources.

Weaponization is a chain. The weakest link wins.

Secret programmes complicate this chain because information is not distributed evenly. The team doing hydrodynamics may understand one set of weaknesses. Senior officers may receive a summary emphasizing potential. Allied governments may see strategic possibility without seeing every experimental caveat. Later historians may recover the exciting correspondence before they recover the disappointing tables. The public may finally learn, fifty years later, that a “tsunami bomb” was real, when what was real was an investigation whose exact boundary between effect and weapon had taken years to define.

This is why declassification can make a failed programme appear stronger before it makes the programme clearer.

The first headline writes itself: SECRET TSUNAMI BOMB TESTED.

The second requires several hundred pages of hydrodynamics.

That asymmetry is not evidence of conspiracy. It is an information problem.

The phrase “they tested a tsunami bomb” also compresses the experiment into the object it hoped to become. Engineers know how dangerous that grammar is. A wind-tunnel model is not an aircraft. A critical assembly is not a power reactor. A bench-top thrust measurement is not a launch vehicle. A wave experiment is not a deployed inundation weapon.

Project Seal is unusually useful because the compression is so visible.

The surviving title of Leech’s final report is not *The Tsunami Bomb*. It is *Project SEAL: The Generation of Waves by Means of Explosives*. That is a better description of what was established. Waves were the object. Explosives were the source. The military interpretation sat on top.

The distinction should not be used to drain the story of its strangeness. The project was a weapons investigation. Its advocates were not measuring waves out of detached oceanographic curiosity. They wanted to know whether inundation could be made offensive. Admiral William Halsey’s reported interest in further development after preliminary work gives the programme its strategic seriousness. New Zealand established a dedicated unit. Personnel, ordnance, instrumentation, and restricted space were committed. The idea survived long enough to produce a postwar technical report and to attract interest around the era of the Bikini nuclear tests.

That is more than a thought experiment.

But seriousness is not success.

The difference matters because secret-weapons history is usually told by believers and prosecutors. Believers point to money, clearances, distinguished personnel, and classified reports as indirect evidence that the hidden claim must have had substance. Prosecutors point to cancellation as proof that the idea was nonsense. Both confuse institutional behavior with physical truth.

Governments investigate uncertain things precisely because they are uncertain.

The more consequential the possible payoff, the more rational it can be to spend modest resources testing an unlikely proposition. A one-percent chance of a new weapon may justify experiments during a total war. That does not mean the proposition was ninety-nine percent true. It means expected value and scientific confidence are different quantities.

Project Seal occupied that space.

The original hypothesis had another feature that military planners tend to like: leverage. The imagined weapon would use the environment as an amplifier. Instead of carrying destructive force all the way to the target in steel and explosive, the charge would recruit the ocean. A small initiating action would produce a larger consequence by setting a natural system in motion.

Weapons designers have chased versions of that dream repeatedly. Incendiaries recruit city fuel. Biological weapons recruit replication. Cyberattacks recruit connected systems. Weather modification hopes to recruit atmospheric instability. Antidam weapons recruit stored gravitational potential. A wave weapon would recruit water.

The difficult question in every case is where the free amplification actually comes from.

The ocean contains vast energy, but it is not waiting in a metastable state for a small trigger in the way a dry forest might be waiting for a spark. A tsunami source generally has to do the work of displacement. The water’s later motion redistributes that input; it does not donate a hidden reservoir of energy simply because the target is coastal.

This point will become central. Project Seal was not trying to trigger a tectonic plate. It was trying to manufacture the wave directly with explosives. The explosive therefore had to couple enough energy into the right kind of water motion. The dream of environmental leverage remained, but the ocean charged an entrance fee.

The fee was geometry.

How deep should the charge be? How far offshore? One large charge or many smaller ones? Simultaneous or sequenced? What line length? What seabed shape? What water depth? What target shelf? What wave period? What losses?

These questions are the real plot.

They are also why the project should not be judged by whether somebody, somewhere, once produced a ten-meter wave. A wave height without a location, period, source distance, bathymetry, and measurement method is almost propaganda. Ten meters in a confined model basin means something different from ten meters approaching a city. A dramatic transient near a blast means something different from a coherent long wave surviving kilometers of propagation.

The first discipline of this book is therefore to keep asking: measured where?

The second is: compared with what?

A weapon is not evaluated against nothing. If two million kilograms of explosive are required to produce a destructive inundation under favorable conditions, the question is not merely whether the inundation is possible. The question is what two million kilograms of explosive could do if delivered conventionally, whether it can be emplaced at sea, and what military problem the wave solves better than aircraft, artillery, mines, blockade, sabotage, or direct bombardment.

A technically possible weapon can lose to logistics.

A logistically possible weapon can lose to alternatives.

A frightening weapon can lose to weather.

The sea does not care how important the memorandum is.

That indifference is what makes Project Seal such a good story. It begins in the intoxicating zone where observation, war, and imagination overlap. The premise is vivid enough to survive for generations. The experiments are real enough to keep the premise from being dismissed. Yet every step toward scale introduces another physical demand.

The first step, however, looked promising enough.

In February 1944, around Nouméa, the question left paper and entered water.
