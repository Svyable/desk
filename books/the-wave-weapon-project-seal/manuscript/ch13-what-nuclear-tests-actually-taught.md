# What Nuclear Tests Actually Taught

There is a photograph of Baker that seems to answer Project Seal in one frame.

It does not.

The underwater detonation at Bikini in 1946 produced an enormous column of water and spray. Ships became scale bars. The scene looks less like an explosion in the sea than the sea discovering explosives. If the question is whether a large underwater detonation can move a terrifying amount of water, Baker is conclusive.

That was never the hard question.

The hard question was whether a compact underwater source could efficiently create a long, coherent, propagating wave that arrives at a distant coastline with militarily useful run-up.

Nuclear tests provided a way to separate those questions because they made the source dramatically larger without making it tectonically broad.

That distinction is the core of this chapter.

An underwater nuclear explosion creates several destructive mechanisms near the source. The initial shock can damage hulls and equipment. The gas bubble expands and contracts. A water column and spray dome can rise. Collapse creates strong local motions. Base surge carries water droplets and, in nuclear tests, radioactive contamination. Surface waves radiate outward.

From the perspective of a nearby ship, debating which part counts as “tsunami” is academic. The ship is in danger.

From the perspective of a weapon designer hoping to inundate a coast kilometers away, the distinction is everything.

The larger the explosive yield, the more dramatic the local effects become. But useful far-field wave generation depends on how much energy and momentum enter the relevant gravity-wave modes. Most of the yield does not become a clean long-period surface wave.

This is where intuition fails because nuclear energy is so large.

A small percentage of an enormous number can still be enormous. That means inefficient coupling can still create dangerous local waves. But inefficiency matters when comparing the wave weapon to simply using the nuclear device in a mode designed to maximize direct target damage.

Opportunity cost returns.

If an airburst over a city converts yield into blast and thermal effects with comparatively predictable area damage, putting the same weapon offshore must offer some unique strategic benefit to justify throwing much of the yield into water, shock, cavity, spray, and wave modes that do not all contribute to inundation.

This is not an argument against underwater nuclear weapons generally. Anti-submarine or anti-ship missions care about underwater shock. Harbor attacks may exploit local water effects. The question is narrower: does nuclear yield rescue the Project Seal vision of artificial tsunami?

The postwar technical literature says: not in the simple way the word *tsunami* suggests.

William Van Dorn’s work at Scripps is central. In the decades after the war he studied tsunami and impulsively generated waves, including waves associated with nuclear explosions. By 1968, with Bernard Le Méhauté and Li-San Hwang, he coauthored a handbook on explosion-generated water waves under U.S. sponsorship. The existence of that handbook alone is a useful correction to two opposing myths.

The first myth says the tsunami-bomb idea was so obviously absurd that no serious science followed.

The second says continued serious science proves a hidden operational weapon.

Neither follows.

Explosion-generated waves were a legitimate subject because underwater nuclear tests, impacts, coastal hazards, and naval warfare all required them to be understood. A technical field can flourish around a phenomenon even when one proposed application is inefficient.

The handbook’s value to this book is quantitative discipline.

How does characteristic wave amplitude scale with yield and depth?

What periods are generated?

How fast does the wave decay?

When does it break?

How does bottom topography change propagation?

What happens at run-up?

How do nuclear test observations compare with theory?

These questions turn Baker from icon into dataset.

The dataset undermines the seductive equivalence between vertical spectacle and horizontal delivery.

A water column rising hundreds of meters says little by itself about a wave kilometers away. The column contains water with enormous vertical velocity but much of that motion falls back near the source. A tsunami weapon needs organized horizontal propagation.

The same mistake appears in impact-tsunami discussions. Early popular calculations sometimes assumed that a large fraction of an asteroid’s kinetic energy becomes ocean wave energy and then spreads efficiently to distant coasts. Later work, including research that revisited the Van Dorn tradition, emphasized how source geometry, wave breaking, and propagation reduce the simple picture.

The analogy is useful because an asteroid is a naturally occurring compact energetic source.

If even asteroid impacts do not translate energy into basin-wide tsunami as efficiently as naive energy accounting suggests, then a conventional explosive array needs careful source engineering indeed.

The lesson is not that compact sources cannot be catastrophic.

Large impacts can be catastrophic through multiple mechanisms.

Nuclear explosions can be catastrophic through multiple mechanisms.

The lesson is that “total energy” does not specify “tsunami outcome.”

A nuclear test gives us another useful contrast with earthquakes. The 1964 Alaska earthquake generated a Pacific-wide tsunami. The earthquake’s seismic moment reflected rupture across a vast fault. Seafloor displacement created long waves whose source dimensions were already comparable to the wavelengths needed for efficient propagation.

A nuclear test has a radically different spectrum.

The word *spectrum* is worth learning because it removes much confusion. A complicated disturbance can be represented as a combination of waves with different wavelengths and periods. A compact violent source produces a broad range, including many shorter components. A broad slow source concentrates more energy into long wavelengths.

The ocean then filters that spectrum.

Shorter steep waves can disperse or break. Very long waves travel differently. Bathymetry selects and redirects. A harbor may amplify one period and ignore another.

A Project Seal array was, in part, a primitive spectrum-shaping device.

It tried to make the source longer and therefore move more energy toward longer waves.

Nuclearization increases amplitude but does not automatically make the source long.

This point should be enough to retire one recurring piece of conspiracy logic: if a wartime conventional project existed and nuclear weapons later became vastly more powerful, then modern states must possess effortless tsunami weapons.

The conclusion does not follow.

Modern states may possess capabilities not public. That is always possible in the abstract. But Project Seal plus nuclear yield is not evidence for them. The historical programme demonstrates interest and a class of experiments. Later science demonstrates both the reality of explosive-generated waves and the constraints on scaling them.

Capability claims require their own evidence.

The nuclear test record also complicates the word *success*.

If an underwater shot damages ships through shock and local wave action, it is successful for a fleet-vulnerability experiment even if it demonstrates poor far-field tsunami efficiency. The same event can support one weapon concept and weaken another.

This is why reading test objectives matters.

Operation Crossroads was not a full-scale Project Seal trial.

It did not arrange a line of devices offshore from a target coast to optimize inundation. It tested naval vulnerability to atomic weapons in a lagoon. Using Baker as a direct Project Seal test would be historically false.

Using Baker-derived science as evidence about compact underwater source behavior is appropriate.

That boundary should be kept sharp.

Later deep-water nuclear tests add other constraints. Operation Wigwam in 1955, for example, detonated a nuclear device in deep ocean to study submarine vulnerability. The objective was underwater shock, not coastline inundation. Such tests provide data on bubble and pressure phenomena at depths Project Seal could never reach with its small programme. Again, the relevance is physical, not genealogical.

A research lineage does not require a secret continuity of mission.

The nuclear tests also introduce radioactivity, which makes a tsunami weapon strategically stranger.

An underwater nuclear burst can contaminate water, spray, ships, and nearby terrain. If the goal is simply coastal destruction, fallout and contamination may add damage but also political and operational complications. Friendly forces planning to land after the wave could face contaminated zones. A weapon intended to prepare a beach for amphibious assault becomes self-defeating if the assault area is radiologically hazardous.

This matters because one plausible Project Seal mission was coastal preparation.

Nuclearization may solve explosive logistics while breaking the mission.

Conventional explosives avoid radiation but require enormous mass.

There is no free version.

This is a recurring feature of weapon substitution. A new technology solves the variable everyone complained about and introduces a constraint nobody had before.

For Project Seal:

Conventional array: logistically enormous, radiologically clean.

Nuclear source: compact, immensely energetic, contaminated, still geometrically compact unless multiplied.

Multiple nuclear sources: broader geometry, strategic absurdity grows.

The comparison forces the weapon into narrower niches.

The nuclear data also helped tsunami science itself. Instruments designed to record impulsive waves improved. Researchers compared model and field results. The distinction between local explosion waves and tectonic tsunami became clearer. This scientific maturation makes Project Seal historically valuable as an early stage in a field rather than as a suppressed finished capability.

That interpretation is less sensational and more durable.

The failed weapon contributes to understanding why it failed.

There is a subtle danger here of hindsight triumphalism. We should not write as though Leech should have possessed the 1968 handbook in 1944. The purpose of experimental research is to learn what later becomes obvious. If Project Seal’s scaling assumptions were optimistic, the fair question is whether they were reasonable given the available data and whether the team revised them when evidence improved.

Good science can be wrong at the frontier.

Bad science refuses to become less wrong.

The full documentary sequence will let us judge which one Project Seal was.

The nuclear era gives us the control experiment in principle: enormous compact sources existed. They created spectacular water effects. Yet tsunami warfare did not become a standard strategic category.

That negative historical fact is not proof of physical impossibility. Militaries discard weapons for many reasons. But when combined with the technical literature, it becomes meaningful.

If the wave mode were dramatically more efficient than direct nuclear attack for coastal targets, one would expect sustained doctrinal interest, testing, or at least an extensive declassified technical literature centered on the mission. What we find instead is a broader science of explosion-generated waves, underwater shock, and tsunami hazards.

The problem survived.

The weapon did not obviously do so.

This is exactly the kind of distinction conspiracy thinking hates because it lacks a clean reveal.

There is no moment when a general announces: the tsunami bomb is impossible.

There is a field gradually learning that compact explosions make the wrong kind of ocean motion for the grandest claim, except under highly local and target-specific conditions where other weapons may be better.

Technology often dies this way.

Not with a failed test.

With an unfavorable comparison.

The 1968 handbook would put numbers around that comparison.

By then, Project Seal’s question had become mature enough to answer without the code name.
