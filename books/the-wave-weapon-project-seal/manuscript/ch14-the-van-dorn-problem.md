# The Van Dorn Problem

By 1968, the secret wartime question had become a handbook.

That is a remarkable demotion and a scientific promotion.

*Handbook of Explosion-Generated Water Waves, Volume I — State of the Art* does not need the romance of Project Seal. Its authors — William G. Van Dorn, Bernard Le Méhauté, and Li-San Hwang — were writing after two decades of nuclear tests, coastal engineering, tsunami research, and model studies had enlarged the evidence base. The subject was no longer whether an admiral should fund a strange possibility. The subject was how impulsive sources generate waves, how those waves travel, and what happens when they approach shore.

A handbook is what a mystery becomes after enough people have been disappointed by it.

That sentence is unfair to handbooks and useful for Project Seal.

The shift in genre matters. Wartime programme reports are oriented toward possibility. Handbooks are oriented toward regularity. The first asks whether a new effect can be exploited. The second asks which equations and empirical relations survive across cases.

Project Seal’s most ambitious claims have to pass through the second genre.

Van Dorn is particularly important because he spent a career studying tsunami and impulsively generated waves. Later impact-tsunami researchers would return to his explosion work when trying to understand what happens when a localized energetic event dumps energy into the ocean. That reuse is intellectually elegant: a weapons question becomes a planetary-hazard question.

It also exposes the physical issue Project Seal could never escape.

A compact source is good at being compact.

Tsunami are good at being long.

The engineering task is to bridge the difference.

The 1968 handbook’s full quantitative content still needs page-indexed extraction for this manuscript. I do not want to turn secondary summaries of Van Dorn into a substitute for reading Van Dorn. But later technical discussions identify several recurring themes relevant to Project Seal: wave-generation efficiency, characteristic source depth, the role of cavity collapse, the distribution of energy across wave periods, propagation losses, breaking, and run-up.

One concept associated with this literature became known in later discussions as the Van Dorn effect: very large waves generated by compact sources may break offshore as they encounter shallower water, potentially dissipating energy over a broad surf zone before reaching the shoreline in the form naive calculations predict.

The concept has itself been revised and argued over.

That is exactly why it belongs here.

A simple debunking story would say Project Seal fails because explosion waves break offshore. But later researchers such as Korycansky and Asphaug revisited the breaking problem for impact-generated tsunami and noted that bottom damping and propagation can reduce wave amplitude before the point where simplistic breaking criteria would predict enormous offshore collapse. The details are more complicated than a single “Van Dorn barrier.”

Scientific maturity makes the verdict less slogan-friendly.

The robust point is not that every large compact-source wave breaks harmlessly before shore. It is that propagation and shelf interaction can strongly transform the wave, so source amplitude cannot be carried unchanged into a run-up claim.

Project Seal’s scaling had to survive an ocean in between.

This sounds obvious enough to be trivial. It was not trivial mathematically. The continental shelf is where wave behavior changes regime. As depth decreases, long waves slow. Their amplitude may grow. Nonlinearity becomes stronger. Bottom friction matters more. The wave steepens. Breaking may occur. Energy can transfer among modes. Local bathymetry can focus or spread the wave.

A weapon that relies on favorable shoaling is betting on the same region that can destroy its wave.

That is the Van Dorn problem in a broader sense: the coast giveth and the coast taketh away.

The very physics Project Seal wanted to exploit for amplification also imposes limits.

There is a pattern here worth making explicit. Environmental weapons often depend on nonlinear amplification near the target. But nonlinear amplification tends to come with sensitivity. The same threshold behavior that can produce dramatic effect can produce dramatic failure if the conditions are slightly wrong.

A rain-seeding operation needs the right cloud.

A wildfire weapon needs the right fuel and weather.

A wave weapon needs the right source and coast.

The target environment becomes a hidden guidance system.

Guidance systems are valuable when predictable.

Bathymetry is predictable in principle, but model uncertainty in the mid-twentieth century was considerable. Even today, high-resolution inundation forecasts depend on detailed elevation data and source reconstruction. Project Seal’s planners would have been trying to predict target-specific nonlinear coastal behavior with far less information and computational power.

Physical models could help.

But physical models return us to similitude.

Van Dorn’s later work gives us a way to audit whether Leech’s experiments preserved the parameters that mattered most. If the Project Seal models were dominated by Froude similarity in the propagation phase, that may have been reasonable. If explosive source scaling relied on relationships later shown to be weak, the weapon estimates would need revision.

The audit should be technical, not rhetorical.

A full version of this chapter will eventually include side-by-side cases:

one Leech experiment,

one underwater nuclear test,

one Van Dorn model relation,

one modern numerical example.

The reader should be able to see how the same quantity changes across scale.

That quantitative passage is still missing from the current draft because the primary tables have not yet been extracted. It is a real gap, not something prose should hide.

The conceptual structure, however, is clear enough to proceed.

Explosion-generated waves carry a characteristic period tied to the source. Period matters because it governs how the wave interacts with depth and target geometry. A very short-period wave may break like storm surf rather than inundate like tsunami. A long-period wave moves more water and can sustain strong currents over longer times.

This is why spectacular photographs are a poor measure of tsunami potential.

The camera records height.

The coast experiences volume and duration.

A tall short pulse can look apocalyptic and deliver less inland flooding than a lower, much longer surge.

The 2011 Tōhoku tsunami made this distinction horribly visible. Video shows water continuing to arrive, not one cinematic breaker and then calm. The destructive force comes from sustained flow carrying vehicles, buildings, debris, and sediment. A wave weapon that merely makes a tall crest has solved the wrong problem.

Project Seal’s full report may have understood this better than its later nickname suggests. Its interest in line sources and shoaling indicates at least some attention to wave structure rather than height alone. The point should therefore be used to clarify the programme, not caricature it.

Van Dorn’s mature field also forces us to confront efficiency honestly.

How much explosive energy ends up in the wave mode of interest?

The answer depends on geometry, but it is far less than one hundred percent. This is true of almost every weapon. A conventional blast loses energy in directions and forms that do not contribute to the specific target effect. Efficiency alone does not kill a design.

The issue is comparative efficiency.

If a thousand tonnes of explosive produce a wave that damages a target more effectively than a thousand tonnes of direct attack can, the weapon may be useful even at low coupling efficiency.

If not, the ocean is an expensive intermediary.

Van Dorn gives us the physical half of this calculation. Military history has to provide the other half.

There is no universal answer because target geometry matters. A harbor with resonant vulnerability may reward indirect hydraulic attack. A cliff-backed city may not. A low atoll may be extremely vulnerable. A fortified beach may suffer temporary disruption that has disproportionate tactical value.

The weapon becomes a database of target cases rather than one performance figure.

This is a key reason the Project Seal story has been easier to sensationalize than to evaluate. The phrase “tsunami bomb” implies general capability. The actual physics points toward niche applicability, if any.

Niche weapons rarely become legends.

Doomsday weapons do.

The 1968 handbook therefore acts as an anti-legend document even when it confirms the underlying phenomenon. It says, in effect, yes, explosions generate waves; here are the conditions, efficiencies, scaling relations, and failure modes.

Nothing is more destructive to mythology than a table.

But tables can create their own illusion of completeness.

The ocean remains messy. Real bathymetry varies. Three-dimensional effects matter. Harbors resonate. Sediment moves. Large waves alter the very shallow-water assumptions used to model them. Nonlinear computation has improved dramatically since 1968, and modern events continue to surprise researchers.

Hunga Tonga is the best example.

Before 2022, tsunami warning systems were optimized primarily around seismic sources because earthquakes generate most destructive tsunami. The eruption produced global waves through mechanisms including atmospheric pressure coupling that forced scientists to revisit source classification. The event did not overthrow tsunami physics. It revealed that an unusual source can excite the ocean through several channels at once.

This matters for Project Seal because it demonstrates both possibility and specificity.

An explosion can indeed be part of a tsunami source.

But the source must be described.

Hunga Tonga involved an enormous volcanic eruption, a collapsing cavity, atmospheric Lamb waves, gravity-wave coupling, and complex seafloor and caldera processes. It is not evidence that a line of conventional charges can reproduce the same far-field outcome.

The lesson is almost philosophical: mechanism beats category.

“Explosion” is too broad.

“Tsunami” is too broad.

Project Seal becomes clear only when the nouns are decomposed.

This is one reason technical history should be suspicious of code names. A code name creates identity across changing mechanisms. Project Seal in 1944, Leech’s 1950 report, nuclear wave studies in the 1950s, and Van Dorn’s 1968 handbook can be narrated as one continuous secret weapon story. In reality, the problem is migrating from a specific military programme into general science.

The code name stopped being useful before the question did.

Van Dorn’s work also gives us a standard for evaluating modern claims about tsunami weapons. Any claim should specify source geometry, yield, depth, target distance, wave period, bathymetry, and run-up model. If those quantities are absent, the claim has not yet reached the level Project Seal itself tried to reach.

This standard is valuable because conspiracy stories often use the historical programme as a credential.

Governments once tried it.

Therefore they can do it now.

The first sentence is true.

The second is unsupported unless the scaling problem is solved and evidence of the capability exists.

History supplies a question, not a secret warranty.

By 1968, the United States had spent enough money blowing things up in and near water to know that the wave was not a free extension of explosive yield. That does not prove every classified idea was abandoned. It does make the burden of evidence much higher for claims of an effortless city-destroying tsunami device.

The mature science is less dramatic and more strange.

Explosions can make large waves.

Large waves can disappear in the wrong bathymetry.

Small offshore changes can become large harbor effects.

A compact source can be catastrophic locally and inefficient globally.

A natural explosive eruption can send pressure around the planet and make oceans respond thousands of kilometers away.

The category refuses simplicity.

Project Seal’s afterlife is therefore not that the secret weapon was either proven or disproven.

Its afterlife is a field sophisticated enough to explain why both statements are too crude.

The best modern test of that sophistication arrived without military intent.

In January 2022, a volcano in Tonga exploded hard enough to make the atmosphere ring.

The ocean answered.
