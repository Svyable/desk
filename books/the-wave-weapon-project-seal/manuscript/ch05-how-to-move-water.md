# How to Move Water

The phrase *move the sea* is too large to be useful.

An explosion moves water. A ship moves water. Wind moves water. The Moon moves water. A submarine moving quietly at depth changes the pressure field around its hull. A child running into surf displaces enough water to make a visible disturbance. The military question was not whether explosives could move water. It was whether they could move the right amount of water in the right way.

This chapter needs one piece of physics to survive the rest of the book: wave height is not the same thing as displaced volume.

The distinction is easy to feel in a swimming pool. Slap the surface hard and you can make a sharp, high splash with little total water involved. Push a broad board slowly through the pool and the visible crest may be lower, but much more water participates. A major tsunami behaves more like the second case, although the actual source is far larger and the details differ. Great submarine earthquakes can raise or lower broad regions of seafloor over tens or hundreds of kilometers. The water column above is displaced over that area. The resulting wavelengths are enormous.

A compact explosion starts from the opposite geometry.

Energy density near the source can be extreme. The pressure pulse can be violent. But the source region is comparatively small. The explosion has to convert some fraction of its energy into long surface-gravity waves rather than shock, bubble motion, heat, spray, turbulence, sound, deformation of the bottom, and other forms.

This is the first reason the usual comparison between explosive yield and earthquake “energy” misleads.

Earthquake magnitude is a measure related to seismic moment and faulting, not a bucket of energy that can simply be matched with explosives. More importantly, even equal total energies introduced through different source geometries do not produce equal tsunamis. The coupling mechanism matters more than the headline number.

A broad patch of seafloor moving vertically is efficient at making a broad water-column displacement because its geometry already resembles the long-wave mode that will travel across an ocean. A point-like blast has to manufacture that large-scale organization from a compact event.

Nature does not forbid it. Nature charges for it.

Project Seal’s task was to find whether arrangement could lower the price.

The simplest arrangement is a single charge. Detonate it underwater and several events happen rapidly. A shock wave radiates outward through the water. A gas bubble expands. If the bubble is sufficiently deep it oscillates, contracting and re-expanding as hydrostatic pressure and gas pressure trade dominance. If it is shallower, the bubble can interact strongly with the free surface. A plume may form. A cavity opens. Water rushes back. Gravity waves radiate outward.

The exact sequence depends on the charge and environment. For Project Seal, the important output was the surface wave.

Surface waves are not one thing either. Very short waves behave differently from long waves. Deep-water waves behave differently from shallow-water waves. The distinction is not strictly about absolute depth but about depth relative to wavelength. A wave long enough compared with the water depth “feels” the bottom and propagates as a shallow-water wave. Tsunami in the open ocean fall into this category because their wavelengths can be hundreds of kilometers long even though the ocean is kilometers deep.

That is why a tsunami can travel fast with modest open-ocean height.

Its danger becomes visually obvious near shore, where decreasing depth changes the wave. Propagation speed falls. Wavelength shortens. Energy can be concentrated into greater amplitude and powerful currents. Local coastal geometry can amplify or reduce the effect. Harbors resonate. Bays funnel. Headlands scatter. Reefs and shelves dissipate and redirect.

A wave weapon therefore has two distinct engineering jobs.

First, create a useful wave spectrum.

Second, deliver that spectrum into a shoreline that turns it into useful destruction.

The second cannot rescue a useless first source, but a favorable coast can amplify a modest source enough to matter locally. This dependence is one reason Project Seal could never be evaluated as a universal bomb.

Every target coast would be a co-designer.

The researchers’ instinct to examine charge depth makes sense in this framework. An explosion too shallow vents energy into the atmosphere and throws water upward. Spectacular, perhaps, but not necessarily efficient for propagating wave energy. Too deep and the bubble’s interaction with the free surface weakens while other forms of energy absorption dominate. Somewhere between lies a depth regime where surface displacement is improved.

The popular account often describes this as a “critical depth.” That phrase can suggest a universal magic number. There is no reason to expect one number independent of charge size, water depth, and desired wave. The useful quantity is a relationship.

This is a recurring feature of scaling science. Engineers look for dimensionless combinations because absolute dimensions do not transfer cleanly. A ten-centimeter model wave and a ten-meter prototype wave are not dynamically similar merely because one is one hundred times taller. Time, length, gravity, viscosity, and sometimes surface tension must scale in compatible ways.

For free-surface flows dominated by gravity and inertia, Froude similarity is often central. The Froude number compares inertial effects with gravity. If a hydraulic model preserves the relevant Froude relationship, many aspects of wave behavior can scale meaningfully. But explosive bubble dynamics introduce compressibility and other physics that do not politely disappear. A model can preserve one kind of similarity while distorting another.

This does not make model testing useless. It makes the model’s domain important.

Project Seal lived at the intersection of two scales: explosive-source physics and coastal gravity-wave physics. The first is violent and compact. The second can be gentle-looking and immense. Connecting them was the project.

The line of charges was an attempt to change source geometry.

One compact explosion has a compact footprint. Several explosions arranged along a line can, in principle, make the effective source broader. If timed so their generated waves reinforce in a desired direction, the pattern may create something less point-like and more coherent. This is the intuitive heart of the weapon concept.

It resembles phased systems elsewhere in physics, but the analogy should not be overworked. Water waves are dispersive or non-dispersive depending on regime, the sources are nonlinear, the seabed matters, and explosive bubble dynamics complicate clean superposition near the source. Still, the basic engineering impulse is familiar: use multiple sources to shape the field.

The question is whether shaping saves enough energy to save the weapon.

A line source requires length. Length requires explosive mass, emplacement, survey, and timing. The very move that makes the hydrodynamic source more tsunami-like makes the military device less bomb-like.

This trade-off sits at the center of the whole programme.

A point source is easy to deliver and hydrodynamically wrong.

A broad source is hydrodynamically better and militarily awkward.

Nature’s best tsunami generators solve the geometry problem by moving the seafloor itself over huge areas. They do not need to carry explosives along the rupture because tectonic stress has already been accumulated across the fault. The earthquake releases stored geological energy through a naturally broad source.

Project Seal had to build its broad source from scratch.

This is the hidden reason “using nature as a weapon” can be deceptive. A tsunami looks like free energy because the wave later occupies an enormous area. But the tectonic system spent centuries loading the fault. The weapon designer does not inherit that stored strain merely by working near the sea.

Could an explosion trigger an earthquake and thereby recruit the fault?

That is a different class of claim and not Project Seal’s documented mechanism. The programme studied generating waves with explosives, not reliably initiating tectonic rupture. Later conspiracy theories sometimes blur the categories because triggering a natural source would solve the energy problem. But solving a narrative problem is not solving a geophysical one. There is no event-specific evidence that Project Seal acquired such capability.

The programme’s real approach was less magical and more difficult: put enough explosive into the right geometry to make the wave directly.

How much was enough?

That number became one of the legend’s favorite pieces. Later popular accounts describe a proposed line of roughly two million kilograms of explosive positioned offshore, sometimes with an eight-kilometer distance or line dimension attached. The final report needs to be read directly before those figures are turned into settled design specifications. The distinction among explosive mass, line length, offshore distance, model scale, and proposed target case matters.

But the order of magnitude itself is instructive.

Millions of kilograms is not a bomb in the ordinary sense. It is a logistics operation.

Even before asking whether the resulting wave would perform as hoped, military comparison begins. Two thousand metric tonnes of explosive represent an enormous conventional demolition resource. How is it transported? In what vessels? How long does emplacement take? What happens if ten percent of the line is misplaced or destroyed? How accurately must charges detonate together? Can a defender detect the operation? How many aircraft sorties, naval shells, mines, demolition attacks, or direct bombardment missions could the same industrial effort support?

These questions are not reasons the project should never have been attempted. They are reasons small-scale hydrodynamic success could coexist with strategic failure.

The local wave may have been real.

The weapon’s comparative advantage had to be real too.

That is a much higher standard.

There is another subtlety. Military usefulness does not require a wave to imitate the worst natural tsunami. A harbor can be vulnerable to much smaller disturbances. Ships can break moorings. Landing craft can be damaged. Mines can move. Floating debris can become dangerous. Low defensive works can flood. A tactical inundation system could therefore be useful at amplitudes far below city-erasing catastrophe.

This makes the project harder to dismiss cleanly.

If the standard is “could explosions create any militarily useful coastal wave under chosen conditions?” the answer might be yes in some settings. If the standard is “could Project Seal produce a practical general-purpose tsunami bomb able to destroy coastal cities?” the burden becomes much higher.

A serious book cannot switch between these standards whenever convenient.

The project’s own documents may have switched. That is what needs to be traced.

Weapons research often begins with a broad objective and ends with a niche capability. The reverse also happens: a tactical effect attracts strategic imagination. Understanding Project Seal means watching whether the objective narrowed or expanded as the data came in.

The hydrodynamics likely pushed toward narrowing.

The more the team learned about depth, charge geometry, shoaling, and coastline dependence, the harder it became to imagine a single standardized weapon. Instead, the concept would need target-specific planning. That can still be viable. Torpedo attacks are target-specific. Dam attacks are target-specific. Amphibious landings are intensely site-specific.

But target specificity removes another layer of leverage.

A universal bomb can be stockpiled.

A coastal hydraulic weapon has to be designed partly around the coast.

The source physics also affects warning and surprise. A natural tsunami can travel across an ocean after an earthquake whose location is far from the target. A Project Seal system based on conventional charges would likely need to operate comparatively near the target to avoid losing useful amplitude. That reduces warning time for the wave but increases the attacker’s exposure during emplacement.

Again, the trade is structural.

This is why the phrase *doomsday weapon* is such a poor guide. Project Seal’s problem was not too much destructive power. It was getting enough of the explosive’s power into the specific ocean motion desired.

Later research on explosion-generated waves would formalize this limitation. Nuclear underwater tests produced spectacular local phenomena, yet the long-range wave threat did not scale in the simple way public imagination expected. Researchers such as William Van Dorn examined wave generation, propagation, breaking, and run-up using data unavailable to the wartime New Zealand team. Those later results do not retroactively make Project Seal foolish. They tell us which of its open questions eventually became better answered.

The modern tsunami literature makes the source-geometry lesson even harder to avoid. USGS descriptions of earthquake tsunami generation emphasize broad seafloor displacement. Work on impact-generated waves, landslides, and volcanic eruptions shows that different sources create different wave spectra and propagation behavior. Hunga Tonga generated tsunami through multiple pathways at once. Source classification is not pedantry. It predicts what the water will do.

Project Seal was an attempt to engineer a source class that nature rarely provides in the exact desired form.

That is why the project needed so many experiments.

The sea was not one problem. It was a sequence of transformations:

explosive to shock,

shock and gas bubble to displacement,

displacement to wave spectrum,

wave spectrum to propagation,

propagation to shoaling,

shoaling to run-up,

run-up to military damage.

An inefficiency at any stage multiplies with the next.

The most important habit, then, is to stop asking whether explosives can make waves.

Of course they can.

The question is how much of the original explosive resource arrives at the target as the kind of water motion that matters.

That answer begins with depth.
