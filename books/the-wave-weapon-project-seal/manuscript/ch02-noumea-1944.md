# Nouméa, 1944

Nouméa was not a laboratory in the ordinary sense. It was a wartime port at the edge of an ocean the Allies were still fighting across, crowded with ships, depots, headquarters, engineers, aircraft, ordnance, and people whose work had been rearranged by distance. The South Pacific war made specialists collide. A survey observation could reach a general. An engineering curiosity could reach a fleet commander. An academic could find himself attached to a military problem whose value, if real, would be measured in beaches and casualties.

That is roughly how Project Seal entered its first experimental phase.

The later final report says that after Wing Commander E. A. Gibson proposed investigating artificial inundation in January 1944, arrangements were made for Gibson, Professor J. M. Snodgrass of the University of California’s Division of War Research, and Professor Thomas D. J. Leech, then acting in New Zealand’s wartime scientific organization, to examine the idea in New Caledonia the following month.

Three people are enough to start a myth. They are also enough to start a measurement programme.

Snodgrass matters because the University of California’s wartime research system brought American scientific resources into naval problems across the Pacific. Leech matters because he would become the project’s central New Zealand technical figure and eventually the author of the report by which much of the programme is now known. Gibson matters because the origin story belongs, in the surviving account, to his observation rather than to Leech’s imagination. Later retellings often collapse the project into a single inventor. The actual beginning is more institutional and therefore more interesting: observation, command channel, scientific evaluation, then experiment.

The first New Caledonian work needs deeper archival recovery than it usually receives. Popular histories move quickly past it because the Whangaparāoa tests are easier to package as the main event. Yet Nouméa is where the project first had to answer the only question that could justify everything that followed: was there a reproducible wave effect worth investigating?

A wartime preliminary experiment does not have to solve the weapon. It has to produce enough signal to earn the next experiment.

The distinction explains a great deal of weapons research that looks irrational in retrospect. Early tests are not built to satisfy the standards of final deployment. They are built to collapse uncertainty cheaply. If the wave from a submerged charge is trivial under every tested condition, the idea dies. If charge depth changes the effect significantly, if measured waves behave with some repeatability, if simple forecasts agree with instruments within tolerable error, then the idea acquires scientific structure.

Structure attracts resources.

The Leech report, in excerpts that have circulated publicly, describes the early work as sufficiently encouraging that further development was sought. Later accounts say Admiral Halsey believed inundation in amphibious warfare had “definite and far-reaching possibilities” and wanted New Zealand officers to continue the development. The original Halsey memorandum remains a primary-source recovery target for this manuscript. Until it is in hand, the point should be kept exact: archival reporting indicates high-level operational interest followed the preliminary work. That is evidence of seriousness. It is not evidence that a city-killing wave had been demonstrated.

What, then, might the first experiments have shown?

An explosion beneath the surface creates an expanding gas bubble. Very near the charge, the initial shock is violent. The bubble grows, rises, and can oscillate. The surface above may dome and rupture. Water is thrown upward and outward. A cavity forms and collapses. Surface gravity waves radiate away. Which part dominates depends on the charge, depth, water geometry, and timescale being measured.

A military observer does not need the full theory to notice that depth matters.

Put a charge too close to the surface and energy vents dramatically into air and spray. Put it too deep and more work is done compressing and moving the surrounding water in ways that may not create the desired surface wave. Somewhere between those extremes, for a particular charge and geometry, surface-wave generation can improve. Later Project Seal work would treat this as one of the central experimental variables.

That fact is simultaneously encouraging and discouraging.

It is encouraging because an optimum implies design. The wave is not random. A charge can be placed better or worse. Engineering can act on the effect.

It is discouraging because every optimum becomes a requirement. If depth is critical, then a weapon is no longer “put explosives in water.” It is “put a great deal of explosive at specific depth relative to local bathymetry and sea state, in a deliberate geometry, with accurate timing, near the coast you intend to attack.” The more sensitive the effect is to placement, the less magical the weapon becomes.

In Nouméa, sensitivity still looked like opportunity.

The surrounding war encouraged that interpretation. Amphibious operations were exercises in turning coastlines into equations under pressure. Hydrographic teams measured channels. Underwater demolition teams cleared obstacles. Naval gunfire officers calculated ranges. Engineers worried about reefs, gradients, surf, and landing-craft draft. A weapon based on coastal geometry belonged to that world more naturally than it belongs to ours.

The phrase “tsunami bomb” makes us imagine strategic annihilation: a distant detonation, a huge wave crossing the sea, a city erased. The original military appeal may have been both more local and more tactical. Inundation could be useful against coastal defenses even if the wave did not resemble a basin-wide natural tsunami. A sufficiently large local wave might damage fortifications, landing obstacles, harbor facilities, vessels, or low ground. The threshold for military interest was therefore lower than the threshold implied by later doomsday language.

This difference in target definition matters enormously.

If the programme’s objective is “generate a natural-tsunami analogue capable of destroying a distant metropolis,” the physics looks brutal very early. If the objective is “determine whether coordinated explosive charges can produce useful local inundation against selected coastal defenses,” the proposition is less absurd. It may still lose on logistics, efficiency, and alternatives, but it has a different burden of proof.

The surviving record needs to be read with those target definitions separated.

Weapons programmes often migrate between them without announcing the move. An experiment proves a local effect. A memorandum describes a broader possibility. A planner imagines a target class. A reporter decades later chooses the largest plausible consequence as the name of the device. By the time the story reaches the public, the tactical investigation has become an apocalypse machine.

Nouméa is where we can resist that drift.

The evidence we need from the early shots is ordinary: charge mass, depth, distance to recorder, bottom profile, water depth, measured amplitude, and the prediction made beforehand. Leech’s final report is said to note agreement between some forecast wave amplitudes and observations within experimental error. That is meaningful. It indicates the work was not simply a series of explosions followed by impressionistic excitement. The researchers were trying to predict.

Prediction changes the status of an experiment.

A one-off surprising wave can be anecdote. A model that says where and how high a wave should appear, followed by a measurement in the predicted range, starts to build a transferable relation. It does not establish the relation will survive six orders of magnitude in energy or a new coastline. But it gives the programme something more durable than spectacle.

The distinction also helps explain why Project Seal persisted after the immediate war requirement weakened. A weapon may fail while the underlying wave problem remains publishable science. The same experiment can therefore have two afterlives. In one, it is an abandoned military system. In the other, it is a set of observations about impulsively generated waves.

The second afterlife eventually became the stronger one.

We should not know that yet.

In early 1944, the experimental signal was enough to move the programme home to New Zealand. The choice made sense for security, access, and control. Whangaparāoa, north of Auckland, offered military-controlled coastal terrain where a dedicated unit could build equipment and run repeated trials without competing with active Pacific operations.

That move marks another important transition. Nouméa was opportunistic. Whangaparāoa would be systematic.

Systematic work changes what a project is able to fool itself about.

This deserves explanation, because “more data” is usually treated as an unconditional cure. More data can expose a weak hypothesis. It can also make a local model extremely convincing inside the domain being measured. If you run hundreds of controlled shots and establish clean relationships among depth, spacing, and wave amplitude, you become legitimately confident in those relationships. The danger arrives when confidence in the local relationship gets silently transferred to a larger domain.

A good small-scale model can make extrapolation feel earned.

Sometimes it is.

Engineering depends on similitude. Bridges are tested in models. Aircraft are tested in tunnels. Ships are tested in towing tanks. Hydraulic structures are studied in flumes. The question is not whether models can teach us about full-scale systems. They can. The question is which dimensionless relationships have to be preserved, which physical effects enter or disappear with scale, and whether the model includes the variables that dominate the real system.

Water-wave modeling is particularly unforgiving because gravity, inertia, depth, wavelength, viscosity, surface tension, compressibility, bubble dynamics, and bottom interaction do not all scale together under one convenient transformation. A model can be excellent for one regime and misleading for another.

Project Seal was born into that problem before it had the language to explain itself to a later public.

The early reports’ use of “tidal wave” is an example. The researchers were not necessarily making a naive equivalence between an explosion wave and a tectonic tsunami. But the name invited equivalence. A military sponsor hears “inundation.” A later journalist hears “tsunami.” A modern reader hears the 2004 Indian Ocean disaster or 2011 Tōhoku. The image grows with historical experience.

The event being compared has changed.

In 1944, the deadliest modern tsunamis were not yet part of a global real-time visual memory. There was no satellite video of coastlines disappearing under black water, no phone footage of cars floating through streets, no global replay of harbor surges. The word did not carry the same cultural payload. Project Seal’s weapon concept therefore developed before the public meaning of tsunami became what it is now.

That later cultural amplification matters because it can make the project sound more technically specific than it was. A “tsunami bomb” seems to promise an engineered version of a great subduction-zone event. “Artificial inundation by explosive-generated waves” is less cinematic and closer to the actual research question.

This book will prefer the less cinematic phrase whenever precision matters.

The first experiments also reveal something about wartime command culture. Halsey, if the later quotations are accurate, did not need proof of an operational weapon to request further study. He needed enough evidence that the possibility was worth New Zealand’s time. That is a rational threshold in war.

The Manhattan Project is a misleading comparison because its success has trained us to treat enormous wartime scientific bets as evidence of deep prior confidence. In reality, many wartime programmes were portfolios of uncertainty. Some produced decisive systems. Others produced technical knowledge. Others produced almost nothing. Decision makers could not know the final category in advance.

Project Seal’s later association with the atomic bomb — especially the repeated claim that some officials regarded it as potentially comparable in importance — should be handled in that portfolio context. The existence of two secret programmes at the same time does not make their technical maturity comparable. One does not validate the other. The atomic comparison tells us something about perceived upside and wartime appetite for unconventional weapons. It tells us very little about whether the ocean could be made to cooperate.

The ocean had its own portfolio.

Some shots would make more wave. Some less. Charge depth would prove critical. Multiple sources would interact. Shoaling and bottom shape would complicate extrapolation. The experiments would have to distinguish wave generation from wave survival.

These are ordinary technical problems now. In 1944, they were the frontier of a weapon somebody still hoped might work.

There is a habit in secret-history writing of treating every classified programme as if it contains a single hidden truth. Either the government knew the wonder weapon worked or it knew the public story was false. Real programmes are usually messier. Different people know different parts. The experimentalist knows the shot behaved strangely. The theorist knows the equation is sensitive. The commander knows the effect might solve a military problem. The logistics officer sees what it would take to deploy. The politician sees a possible strategic advantage. The archivist, decades later, sees folders.

The truth is distributed.

Nouméa produced enough distributed truth to keep the question alive.

That was the accomplishment.

Not a tsunami. Not yet a bomb.

A reason to build a better laboratory.
