# The Depth Problem

The first useful secret of an underwater explosion is that deeper is not automatically better.

This sounds obvious after somebody tells you. Before somebody tells you, depth looks like a dial labeled *more water*. Put the explosive farther below the surface and there is more ocean above it to push. A larger water mass seems available. The blast is better contained. The device is harder to see. If the objective is a large wave, depth initially feels like an ally.

Then the wave disappears.

Not literally. Energy still moves through the water. The shock still travels. The gas bubble still expands and contracts. But the fraction that emerges as the surface-gravity wave of interest can change sharply with depth. The best position for one effect is not the best position for another.

Project Seal’s experiments repeatedly returned to this problem because depth is where source physics becomes military engineering.

An underwater explosive creates a high-pressure gas bubble almost instantaneously. The bubble expands against surrounding hydrostatic pressure. Expansion consumes energy. Momentum pushes water outward. As the bubble grows, its internal pressure falls. The surrounding water eventually reverses the motion, compressing the bubble. Depending on depth and circumstances, the bubble may oscillate through several cycles. Buoyancy also pulls it upward. Near enough to the surface, the expanding bubble and displaced water can vent, dome, rupture, and create a large cavity whose collapse contributes to the outgoing surface disturbance.

The exact dynamics become complicated quickly. For this book the important point is simpler: the explosion has several ways to spend itself, and depth changes their proportions.

Too shallow, and much of the violence is spectacularly local. Water erupts upward. Gas escapes. Spray and plume dominate. A camera loves it. A distant shoreline may not.

Too deep, and the free surface participates less directly in the initial bubble motion. The explosion may couple strongly into pressure and bubble oscillation without maximizing the long surface wave planners want.

Between those regimes lies the experimental temptation: an optimum.

Find the optimum depth and the system becomes designable.

That is the kind of result a classified report can summarize in one sentence and a weapons programme can spend months earning.

The exact Project Seal relationships need to be reconstructed from Leech’s tables and figures. Until then, the important historical claim is bounded: the team treated charge depth as a critical parameter and developed forecasts of wave behavior around it. Later technical literature on underwater explosions confirms the general importance of charge depth and source geometry. Nothing mystical is required.

But the military consequence is larger than the hydrodynamic result.

A depth optimum means a field tolerance.

Suppose, only as a conceptual example, a particular charge works best when suspended at a depth proportional to some characteristic bubble or wave scale. The operation now requires every charge in a multi-charge array to be placed close enough to that depth. If the seabed varies, the suspension method varies. If tide changes absolute water depth but the charges are seabed-mounted, the relation to the free surface changes. If the charges are suspended, currents matter. If they are floating downward on timed systems, uncertainty grows. If a line extends for kilometers, the array crosses different bottom contours.

The optimum has become a logistics specification.

The story of modern technology is full of this conversion. The scientist says, “performance peaks here.” The engineer hears, “we must hold the system here.” The operator hears, “I have to hold it here in rain, darkness, damage, fatigue, and enemy action.”

Project Seal’s problem was unusually literal. The variable had to be held underwater.

Depth also complicates explosive scaling. A charge ten times larger does not simply require the same number of meters beneath the surface. Bubble size, pressure relationships, and wave geometry change. If model tests use small charges in limited water, full-scale design requires a scaling relation. The project’s confidence therefore depended on more than finding an optimum; it depended on believing the optimum could be translated.

Hydraulic modelers know the difficulty. For ordinary surface waves where gravity dominates, Froude scaling can preserve the ratio between inertial and gravitational effects. But an explosive source adds compressibility, high pressure, bubble dynamics, and potentially surface tension at small scales. No single model can preserve every dimensionless relationship unless the experiment is designed around a specific regime.

This is not a fatal objection. Engineers routinely work with partial similitude. They identify which processes dominate the outcome of interest and accept distortions elsewhere. The question is whether Project Seal identified the right dominant processes for a full-scale coastal weapon.

That question becomes uncomfortable because the programme’s objective crossed regimes.

Very close to the explosive, water is compressible enough for shock physics to matter. The gas bubble is strongly nonlinear. Near the free surface, the cavity and plume matter. Farther away, the desired output becomes a surface gravity wave where the violent source details can be compressed into an initial condition. Near shore, nonlinearity returns as the wave shoals, breaks, runs up, and interacts with topography.

One weapon therefore passes through several different kinds of physics before it damages anything.

The experiment can be excellent in one region and poorly constrained in another.

This is why a waveform measured at one recorder is not the weapon.

Imagine a trace: time on the horizontal axis, water elevation on the vertical. A crest arrives. Then a trough. Another crest. The researcher can measure amplitude and period. Repeat the shot at another depth. The curve changes. This is clean, satisfying science.

Now ask what a village eight kilometers away experiences.

The answer requires propagation, bathymetry, shoreline shape, and run-up. The recorder near the test does not contain those answers automatically. It provides an input to the next model.

The chain of inference lengthens.

Depth was therefore both a genuine discovery and the beginning of extrapolation debt.

This is a useful concept beyond Project Seal. Every time an experiment replaces direct demonstration with a validated intermediate model, it creates a debt that must be paid before final capability is established. The debt is not a flaw. Engineering would be impossible without it. But the debt accumulates.

A wave height at short range pays for source validation.

A scaled model pays for some propagation questions.

A coastline model pays for some run-up questions.

None alone pays for operational feasibility.

Classified programmes can carry large extrapolation debt because outsiders cannot audit the layers. The programme’s existence becomes a proxy for confidence. The public eventually learns that scientists found a “critical depth,” and that fact sounds like a nearly finished recipe. In reality, an optimum can be the first line of the requirements document.

There is another reason depth matters: it determines how much of the explosion remains legible to an observer.

A shallow underwater explosion advertises itself. It throws water, gas, debris, and noise into the air. A deeper one may conceal more of the visible source while creating strong underwater pressure. For a military operation, visibility is part of survivability. If Project Seal required shallow placement to maximize useful surface waves, the weapon would be easier to detect and perhaps harder to emplace. If it required deeper placement, different challenges emerge.

The hydrodynamic optimum may therefore conflict with the tactical optimum.

Weapons designers live in these conflicts. The aerodynamically ideal shape may be hard to manufacture. The best armor is too heavy. The quietest submarine sacrifices speed. The safest reactor runs below maximum output. The best wave-producing depth may be the worst place to put explosives under fire.

Project Seal’s later mythology strips away these compromises because mythology prefers one secret formula.

The real project was a collection of trade-offs.

The depth problem also helps explain why nuclear weapons did not automatically solve everything after 1945. A nuclear device offers much larger yield in a compact package. If the only Project Seal problem were insufficient explosive mass, nuclearization would seem like the obvious answer. But a larger compact charge does not become a broad tectonic source merely by being larger. It produces a larger cavity, stronger local wave, more shock, and more dramatic near-field effects. Source geometry remains compact relative to a great fault rupture.

The nuclear age changed the scale and provided invaluable data. It did not repeal the coupling problem.

Operation Crossroads at Bikini in 1946 would become especially important to postwar thinking about underwater explosions. The Baker shot, detonated underwater, produced one of the iconic images of the atomic age: a towering column and base surge swallowing ships in a white geometry of water and condensation. Visually, it seems like the ultimate confirmation of the wave weapon.

The image is deceptive for exactly the same reason as the shallow blast.

A huge vertical column is not a far-field tsunami.

The Baker test certainly generated major local water motion. Ships were violently affected. The lagoon experienced waves. But the destructive mechanisms near an underwater nuclear explosion include shock, spray, radioactive base surge, cavity effects, and local waves. The spectacle does not tell you how efficiently yield became a long-period wave useful against a distant coast.

Later explosion-wave research exists partly because that distinction had to be quantified.

This book will get there. For now, the important point is that Project Seal discovered the same conceptual boundary at conventional scale. You can make an impressive disturbance without making the desired propagating weapon.

Depth controls which disturbance you get.

The project’s attention to depth also reveals that its researchers were not merely chasing headlines. An unserious programme would have been content with dramatic splashes. Project Seal measured. It looked for a repeatable source condition. It tried to forecast amplitudes. These are the habits of legitimate engineering.

That legitimacy makes the eventual limit more interesting, not less.

When bad science fails, there is little to learn except how the bad science happened. When good local science supports a weapon that still struggles at scale, the failure exposes the gap between phenomenon and system.

Project Seal belongs, provisionally, in the second category.

Provisionally matters. The full report may reveal errors, overconfident equations, selective interpretations, or unresolved anomalies that change the judgment. A later BBC treatment, for example, has claimed that some advisers found serious flaws in Leech’s calculations and that the programme’s larger experiment never occurred. That secondary account cannot simply be imported as verdict. It is a research lead. The technical criticisms need names, documents, equations, and dates.

If Leech’s scaling was wrong, the book should show how.

If the equations were reasonable inside their assumptions but the assumptions failed at full scale, that is a different story.

If critics were themselves extrapolating from incomplete theory, different again.

“Scientists realized it wouldn’t work” is not enough.

The depth problem gives us a place to test the quality of the science because it is concrete. The report should contain predicted relationships. Later underwater-explosion literature should contain improved relationships. We can compare them. Where do the curves agree? Where do they diverge? Did the project understand bubble scaling correctly? Did it overestimate wave conversion? Did it treat shoaling too generously? Did it assume coherent addition of multiple charges under conditions where nonlinear losses would dominate?

Those are answerable questions.

They are also more revealing than the question “was the tsunami bomb real?”

Real is too weak a category.

A design sketch is real.

A laboratory effect is real.

A test programme is real.

A deployable weapon is real in a different sense.

Project Seal moved through the first three and never clearly reached the fourth.

Depth is one reason why.

There is a final irony in the search for the optimum. Military technologies usually seek robustness. A rifle should work when held slightly wrong. An aircraft should tolerate gusts. A fuse should not require laboratory temperature. If a weapon’s performance depends on a narrow depth window and exact geometry, the research team may celebrate precision while the field commander sees fragility.

The more Project Seal optimized the wave, the more it may have discovered how many conditions the ocean demanded in exchange.

That bargain would become clearer when the team stopped treating one charge as the source.

The next move was multiplication.

If one blast could not move enough sea, perhaps many could.
