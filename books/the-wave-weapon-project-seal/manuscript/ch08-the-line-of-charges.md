# The Line of Charges

A single explosion is a point in the ocean.

A tsunami source is usually not.

This mismatch is the whole reason the line of charges matters.

If Project Seal has one genuinely clever engineering move, it is the attempt to change source geometry by multiplication. One charge produces a roughly compact disturbance. Many charges arranged along a line can make the source longer. If their waves are generated with the right spacing and timing, some of the resulting motion may reinforce in a preferred direction. The system begins to resemble a deliberately constructed moving boundary rather than one isolated blast.

The idea sounds more modern than it is because we now live among phased arrays. Radar antennas combine many emitters to steer beams. Loudspeaker arrays shape sound fields. Seismic sources can be coordinated. Engineers routinely use distributed elements to make a larger effective aperture.

Water waves invite the same intuition.

The analogy is useful and dangerous.

Useful, because interference is real. Waves add. Two crests can reinforce. A crest and trough can cancel. Timing and spacing matter.

Dangerous, because an explosive water-wave source is not a neat linear antenna. Close to each blast the physics is nonlinear and violent. Bubble dynamics depend on depth. Sources can interact. The seabed alters propagation. Surface waves contain multiple wavelengths. The desired target may lie in a shallow-water regime while the source begins in a different one. A line that looks coherent in a model can become less cooperative at full scale.

Still, the line solves one conceptual problem that a bigger single charge does not.

It makes the source broader.

This is important enough to linger on. A larger point explosion gives you more energy and a larger local disturbance, but it does not change the fundamental source footprint as efficiently as spreading the energy over distance. Natural earthquake tsunamis are powerful not merely because earthquakes are energetic but because fault rupture displaces the seafloor over broad regions. The initial water deformation is born long.

Project Seal could not move a fault.

It could imitate length with explosives.

That imitation has a price per meter.

The project’s later popular summaries often describe a proposed full-scale arrangement involving enormous quantities of explosive distributed in a line offshore. Precise figures vary in retelling and need to be pinned to the actual report. What matters before the archival details are settled is the direction of travel: the weapon grows laterally.

At some point, the word *bomb* becomes misleading.

A bomb is usually thought of as an object delivered to a point. A line of thousands of charges — or even hundreds, depending on charge size — is a field. It resembles mining, demolition, or prepositioned infrastructure. Its deployment problem is spatial.

This change should alter how the weapon is judged.

Suppose a line source really can produce a stronger, more coherent shoreward wave than the same explosive mass concentrated at one point. Hydrodynamically, that is a win. Militarily, the attacker now has to construct the line.

How?

Surface ships could lay charges, but ships near an enemy coast are observable and vulnerable.

Submarines could perhaps emplace some systems, but quantity and precision become constraints.

Aircraft can drop mines, but maintaining exact depth and line geometry becomes harder.

Prewar covert emplacement is imaginable against fixed strategic targets, but now the weapon is less a wartime improvisation and more an engineering campaign.

Each method changes the original proposition.

This is the line-of-charges paradox: the more the source begins to resemble the geometry of a tsunami generator, the less the delivery system resembles a practical bomb.

The paradox can be formalized without much mathematics. Let the desired source length grow. If charge spacing stays fixed, the number of charges grows with length. Explosive mass grows. Placement operations grow. Timing channels grow. Opportunities for failure grow. The number of local depth conditions grows. Unless the destructive effect grows faster than those burdens, the system loses operational attractiveness.

That comparison is missing from most popular accounts because the wave is dramatic and the deployment is boring.

War is often decided by the boring part.

The same problem appears in mine warfare. A minefield can be strategically powerful precisely because many individually modest devices are distributed across a route. But minefields are useful when you can lay them in places the enemy must traverse and when individual mines do not have to detonate with exquisite coordination to produce one coherent physical effect. A Project Seal array would demand more organization from the field itself.

Demolition lines offer another analogy. Engineers can use multiple charges to cut structures or obstacles. The structure keeps the geometry fixed. The ocean does not.

Currents move suspended objects. Moorings stretch. Surface conditions change. Navigation errors accumulate. A kilometer-scale line is not one placement repeated. It is a distributed tolerance problem.

Timing adds another layer.

If charges are meant to detonate simultaneously, firing systems must preserve synchronization over the full line. If deliberate delays are meant to steer or focus the wave, timing becomes part of the wave-forming geometry. In 1944, remote electrical firing was well understood, but reliability over a wet, explosive, distributed maritime system under operational conditions was not free.

The target also moves in time even when the city does not.

Tide changes the shoreline. A wave arriving at high tide can run farther than the same wave at low tide. Harbor water levels vary. Weather adds or subtracts background surge. Coastal defenses may be exposed or covered differently. If the weapon’s effect depends on narrow run-up margins, detonation timing must coordinate with tide as well as the charge array.

Now the “bomb” has a firing window.

The more constraints accumulate, the easier it is to understand why an idea can remain technically plausible while becoming militarily ridiculous.

Ridiculous is too strong without the final calculations. The point is that practicality is an independent variable.

Some specialized weapons are worth extraordinary preparation. The British attack on the dry dock at Saint-Nazaire in 1942 consumed a destroyer as a delayed-action explosive delivery system because the target mattered enough. Operation Chastise used purpose-built bouncing bombs against dams because ordinary attack profiles were inadequate. Special operations accept complexity when the strategic payoff is high.

A Project Seal array could therefore be practical against a sufficiently valuable and uniquely vulnerable coast even if it was useless as a general weapon.

That possibility deserves examination.

Which target classes were actually considered?

Harbors?

Beaches ahead of amphibious assault?

Coastal cities?

Fortifications?

Anchored fleets?

The literature often jumps straight to Japanese cities because the Pacific context and later “atomic rival” framing make that imaginable. But imagining a plausible target is not evidence that planners selected it. Primary planning documents need to show the intended mission.

Mission changes the required wave.

To inconvenience beach defenses before a landing, you need one scale of inundation and timing.

To destroy harbor installations, another.

To kill a city, another entirely.

A weapon can fail one mission and remain plausible for another.

This is why Project Seal cannot be summarized with one yes/no verdict.

The line-of-charges concept also raises the question of directionality. A point disturbance radiates broadly. A line can shape the outgoing field. If the array can concentrate more energy toward the target and less away from it, efficiency improves. But the ocean’s free surface does not provide perfect directional control, especially once irregular bathymetry and multiple wavelengths enter.

The final report’s model diagrams should reveal what kind of focusing or directivity the team expected.

Did they seek a plane-like wave front moving shoreward?

Did they exploit a bay or shoaling bottom to amplify it?

Did delays create a traveling source?

Were charges simply simultaneous along a line parallel to shore?

These design details separate serious hydrodynamics from retrospective cartoon.

The weapon’s efficiency depends on them.

Efficiency here means more than energy. A military system cares about effect per resource. If a line array can double useful wave amplitude with the same total explosive mass, that may be valuable because destructive force in inundation does not scale linearly with crest height alone. Run-up, current velocity, debris transport, and structural loading can cross thresholds. A modest increase in water level can produce a large increase in damage in a low coastal area.

This nonlinear damage response is one reason even inefficient wave generation can remain militarily interesting.

The physical coupling may be poor, but the target may be brittle.

Harbors are a good example. Resonance can amplify certain wave periods. Narrow entrances and basin geometry matter. Ships and floating infrastructure are sensitive to strong currents and oscillations that would not impress a person thinking only in terms of a cinematic wall of water. A tailored wave weapon might exploit those local vulnerabilities better than a universal “tsunami bomb.”

Again, the weapon becomes target-specific.

Target specificity is not necessarily failure. It simply changes the category.

A universal strategic terror weapon belongs with atomic-bomb comparisons.

A niche coastal engineering weapon belongs with special demolition.

The surviving record may show that Project Seal drifted between these categories as sponsors and researchers interpreted the same results differently.

That drift is worth tracing because it is common in research funding. A programme survives by changing the promise without necessarily admitting that the promise changed. The original objective can shrink into a narrower technical application while retaining the prestige of the original ambition.

There is no evidence yet that Leech or his sponsors deliberately did this. It is a hypothesis to test against reports over time.

The line of charges provides the metric.

If projected explosive requirements grew while the claimed target effect stayed constant, the project was becoming less efficient.

If target assumptions became more favorable — shallower bays, steeper beaches, resonant harbors — while performance claims stayed dramatic, the concept was narrowing.

If the required line moved closer to shore, operational risk was increasing.

If timing tolerance tightened, deployment robustness was falling.

These are the variables by which optimism can be audited.

The same audit should be applied to critics. Later skeptics may take the most ambitious city-destroying version of the proposal, show that it is inefficient, and declare every local military use impossible. That is the mirror-image mistake.

The right conclusion may be less satisfying: Project Seal discovered a real method for shaping explosion-generated waves that was too logistically burdensome for the grand weapon imagined, while retaining some theoretical utility against selected coastal geometries.

Or the full data may show even the narrow version was poor.

The evidence has to decide.

There is another reason the line matters historically. It links Project Seal to a broader family of attempts to synthesize large natural processes from many small engineered actions.

Cloud seeding distributes nuclei through a weather system.

Seismic exploration uses arrays of sources and sensors.

Controlled avalanche release uses smaller triggers to release unstable snow deliberately.

Forest management uses prescribed fire to influence later fire behavior.

In each case, engineers work with a medium whose dynamics exceed the initiating device.

Project Seal was unusual because it tried to create, not merely trigger, the destructive large-scale process.

That difference is decisive.

A snowpack may already contain gravitational instability. A convective cloud may already contain supercooled water. A fault may already be stressed. If a small intervention releases stored environmental energy, leverage is possible.

Project Seal’s array could not assume the coastal ocean was waiting to erupt into a tsunami. It had to inject the organized displacement itself.

The line of charges was an attempt to buy source area with ordnance.

This is the hidden invoice behind the weapon.

The broader the desired wave, the longer the line.

The longer the line, the larger the operation.

The larger the operation, the more the attacker has to ask why it is not simply attacking the target directly.

This question becomes acute when conventional explosive mass reaches thousands of tonnes. The same resources might be used for bombardment, demolition, mining, or aerial attack. A wave weapon needs a unique advantage to justify converting so much explosive into an indirect hydraulic effect.

What advantage could that be?

Perhaps access. Water can cross obstacles that bombs cannot.

Perhaps area. Inundation can affect a broad low-lying zone.

Perhaps surprise. Defenders may not expect the sea to become the attack vector.

Perhaps deniability, though a wartime line of conventional charges is hardly subtle.

Perhaps a way to clear or disrupt coastal defenses before landing.

Each candidate advantage has to be compared with the burden of building the source.

The line therefore turns Project Seal from a physics problem into a systems problem.

Once that happens, “does it make a wave?” becomes almost irrelevant.

The system has to make the wave at a cost and risk worth paying.

That is the beginning of the scale trap.

Project Seal could make a line in a controlled test.

The next question was whether a line on paper could survive becoming a coastline-sized machine.
