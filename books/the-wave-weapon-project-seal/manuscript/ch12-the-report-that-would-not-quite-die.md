# The Report That Would Not Quite Die

A final report is supposed to end a project.

Project Seal’s did something more ambiguous. It gave the project a durable object.

On December 18, 1950, Thomas D. J. Leech dated a document titled *Project SEAL: The Generation of Waves by Means of Explosives*. The title is almost aggressively sober compared with the phrase that would later dominate public memory. No tsunami bomb. No doomsday machine. Waves, generated by explosives.

That title may be the most honest sentence in the entire history.

By 1950, the war that created Project Seal had been over for five years. Hiroshima and Nagasaki had changed strategic destruction. Bikini had put nuclear weapons into the sea. The original military urgency had evaporated. Yet Leech’s report was still worth completing.

Why?

The answer matters because secret programmes often survive in documents after they die in operations. Later readers confuse documentary survival with capability survival. A report requested in 1950 can look like evidence of an active weapon when it may instead be evidence that unfinished research was finally being organized.

Leech’s own account reportedly says that circumstances changed enough in 1950 to allow the report to be completed and that postgraduate engineering students took up smaller problems left from the earlier work. That sounds less like a clandestine deployment programme than a project being metabolized by an academic institution.

But even that interpretation should remain provisional until the report is read in full.

The final report is the book’s most important missing primary object.

A catalog record establishes its existence, authorship, title, publisher, and date. Excerpts have circulated. Secondary writers have quoted sections. Those fragments are enough to establish the programme’s broad shape. They are not enough to audit its technical conclusions.

A full reading has to answer several different questions.

What did Leech believe the experiments proved?

What did he believe they merely suggested?

What full-scale configuration did he consider plausible?

What target effects did he predict?

What assumptions supported the scaling?

What criticisms did he acknowledge?

Which experimental anomalies remained unresolved?

How did he use or discuss nuclear-test data?

Did he distinguish tactical inundation from strategic tsunami?

What next work did he recommend?

And perhaps most importantly: what did he stop claiming by 1950 that the programme had hoped for in 1944?

Subtraction is evidence.

If early memoranda use expansive language and the final report narrows the objective, the narrowing tells us what five years of work did to the original idea. If the final report remains grand, later technical literature can test whether the confidence was justified. If the report is cautious but later journalism becomes spectacular, the mythology belongs largely to the rediscovery phase rather than the programme itself.

This is why document sequence matters more than document existence.

Secret-history culture is addicted to isolated pages. One memo says “far-reaching.” Another says “top secret.” A later letter requests data. Each can be held up as proof of a hidden continuity. But a programme is a time series. The meaning lies in how claims change.

Project Seal’s report can anchor that time series.

The document’s classification history matters too. Popular accounts differ on whether material was “secret” or “top secret,” when the final report was declassified, and which New Zealand files were released in the 1990s. Those distinctions should be treated as archival questions, not atmosphere.

Classification level can tell us how an institution assessed sensitivity. It cannot tell us the capability worked.

Declassification date can tell us when a record became legally accessible. It cannot tell us when anyone actually read it.

Release in an archive can precede public discovery by decades.

These gaps create the peculiar phenomenon of delayed revelation.

Imagine a report declassified in the 1970s but largely ignored until journalists rediscover related files in 1999. To the public, the weapon appears to have been hidden until 1999 even if a technical document was technically available earlier. The cultural secrecy period is longer than the legal secrecy period.

This distinction may explain part of Project Seal’s afterlife.

Archives are full of truths nobody is looking for.

A secret can stop being secret without becoming known.

That is especially true for technically dense dead ends. The people most capable of understanding the report may have little reason to search for it. The people most interested in the sensational premise may not know it exists. Then a journalist, historian, or collector connects the file to an irresistible phrase, and the dormant document acquires a second life.

The second life is not controlled by Leech.

This is where title drift begins.

*The Generation of Waves by Means of Explosives* becomes “tsunami bomb.”

“Investigation of potentialities” becomes “developed a weapon.”

“Model extrapolation” becomes “could create a ten-meter wave.”

“Military interest” becomes “rival to the atomic bomb.”

Every step may have some documentary basis. The compression changes the implied certainty.

A researcher’s conditional becomes a headline’s noun.

This is not merely a media criticism. The report itself may invite compression if it uses confident language around full-scale possibilities. Scientists are participants in their own mythology when they write beyond the evidence.

That is another reason to recover the full text.

Leech’s position in 1950 was unusual. He had spent years with the problem. He had professional investment in making sense of the work. The programme had not produced the wartime weapon initially imagined, but that does not mean he saw it as failure. Engineers often separate a programme’s technical success from whether politicians or militaries choose to deploy it.

He may have believed the principle remained sound and the missing step was only scale.

He may have believed a specific full-scale trial was needed.

He may have thought nuclear data changed the prospects.

He may have moved on to the scientific value of wave generation.

The report should tell us.

A final report also has to justify the project to posterity inside the institution. It records what the money and labor produced. That creates a subtle incentive toward coherence. Messy years of uncertain experiments are reorganized into chapters and conclusions. Dead branches disappear. A sequence of wrong turns becomes a progression.

This is ordinary technical writing, not deception.

But historians should restore some of the mess.

Experimental notebooks, correspondence, interim memoranda, and criticism are valuable precisely because they preserve the paths the final report smooths out.

Did somebody object to Leech’s scaling early?

Were there disputes over interpretation?

Did U.S. scientists agree with the conclusions?

Did military sponsors lose interest before the technical team did?

Did postgraduate students inherit questions because the original equations were inadequate?

These are the places where a programme becomes human.

The report’s survival also created a future comparison point. By the 1960s, explosion-generated wave science had matured. Researchers could look back at earlier experiments with better theory and nuclear data. If Project Seal’s report circulated among them, it may have contributed directly. If it did not, the later field may have reinvented or independently formalized similar questions.

Citation history can answer this.

Who cited Leech?

Did Van Dorn cite Project Seal?

Did coastal-engineering literature discuss the New Zealand experiments?

Did U.S. Navy reports absorb the data without emphasizing the codename?

A dead weapons programme can disappear as a name while surviving as data.

That is a more interesting kind of secrecy than suppression.

Knowledge changes clothes.

The final report may also contain a clue to why Project Seal never fully disappeared: it framed unfinished work.

A project with a clean negative result is easy to bury. A project with partial success and unresolved scaling is harder. The phrase “not yet demonstrated” has a long half-life. Future technology may change the answer. A new explosive, better modeling, improved underwater emplacement, or different mission can reopen the question.

Nuclear weapons seemed to do exactly that.

Then nuclear-test data made the physical limitations clearer.

Later computers made target-specific modeling easier.

Modern autonomous underwater vehicles could make distributed emplacement more plausible.

These changes tempt each generation to ask whether the old concept should be revived.

That is legitimate engineering curiosity.

It is not evidence that the original project secretly continued.

The distinction between revival and continuity matters in every classified field. A public researcher can rediscover an old concept and improve it without inheriting a hidden programme. Conversely, a classified programme can continue under a different name. Evidence has to show which occurred.

Project Seal’s record after 1950 appears thin enough that continuity claims should be treated skeptically unless new documents emerge.

The technical problem, however, unquestionably continued.

Explosions in water remained militarily and scientifically important. Nuclear testing, anti-submarine warfare, underwater demolition, harbor vulnerability, impact hazards, and tsunami science all had reasons to study impulsive waves.

The original weapon question dissolved into a larger field.

This is perhaps the most dignified fate for a failed programme. It stops being a project and becomes a source of questions.

The report itself became a fossil.

Fossils are dangerous when treated as living animals.

A 1950 proposal is not a 2026 capability statement. A scale estimate based on wartime models is not a modern simulation. A military interest memo is not an operational plan. At the same time, the fossil preserves anatomy we would otherwise lose. It shows how engineers once connected observation to weapon.

That anatomy can be compared with later science.

The comparison is the next phase of the book.

Project Seal’s wartime team had limited data about very large underwater explosions.

The nuclear age produced enormous ones.

The report would not quite die because the question it asked had become easier to test at terrifying scale.

What did those tests actually teach?
