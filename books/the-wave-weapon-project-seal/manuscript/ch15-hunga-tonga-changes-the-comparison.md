# Hunga Tonga Changes the Comparison

On January 15, 2022, Hunga Tonga–Hunga Ha’apai did something Project Seal had spent years trying to make the ocean do: it produced destructive waves from an explosive event.

For anyone interested in artificial tsunami, the temptation is immediate.

There. Proof.

That temptation should be resisted for the same reason Project Seal deserves to be taken seriously in the first place. Mechanism matters.

The Tonga eruption was not a large bomb dropped into ordinary seawater. It was an enormous volcanic event involving eruptive mass, gas expansion, a submarine and emergent volcanic system, caldera processes, atmospheric pressure waves, and ocean coupling on a scale no wartime conventional array approached. Modern observations showed multiple tsunami-generation mechanisms operating together.

That complexity is not an inconvenience around the example.

It is the example.

USGS researchers and collaborators described at least three important pathways. Near the source, the explosive eruption and collapse of a large water cavity generated strong waves. The eruption also launched a powerful atmospheric shock or Lamb wave. As that pressure disturbance raced across the ocean, it coupled into the water surface, producing meteotsunami. Additional atmospheric gravity waves contributed. The result was an unusually persistent global tsunami field that did not behave like a simple earthquake source.

One event, several engines.

Project Seal had essentially one: explosives moving water.

The distinction changes what Hunga Tonga can teach us.

It confirms that explosive energy can participate in tsunami generation at catastrophic scale.

It does not establish that a practical weapon can reproduce the relevant source conditions.

This sounds like a lawyer’s distinction until the numbers and geometry are considered.

The eruption displaced and accelerated extraordinary volumes of atmosphere, water, volcanic material, and gas. Its pressure wave circled the Earth. The ocean responded not only near Tonga but at far-field locations where the atmospheric wave continued forcing the sea. In some regions, deep-ocean and trench geometry created resonance and amplification between moving atmospheric pressure and ocean wave speed.

A conventional underwater explosive array has no equivalent global atmospheric driver.

A nuclear explosion can create atmospheric pressure waves, but matching the duration, spatial scale, and coupled volcanic source is a separate problem. “Explosion” is not a sufficient similarity metric.

Hunga Tonga therefore makes Project Seal both more plausible and less plausible depending on which claim is being tested.

More plausible: the category of explosion-generated destructive tsunami is physically real.

Less plausible: the idea that total explosive energy alone predicts the result.

This is the kind of complication a good control case should create.

The eruption also exposes a problem with twentieth-century source taxonomy. Tsunami warning systems and public education often divide events neatly: earthquake, landslide, volcano. Hunga Tonga blurred the categories. The eruption generated water waves directly and through the atmosphere. A pressure pulse traveling near the speed of sound could continue forcing the ocean at great distance. The wave field was being generated while it propagated.

Project Seal imagined a source event that ended when the charges detonated.

After that, the water was on its own.

This difference is profound. A continuously or repeatedly forced system can accumulate energy differently from an impulse. Resonance can appear where forcing speed and ocean-wave speed align over suitable depth. The target may receive energy generated far from the original eruption.

Project Seal had no such free second act.

Its array had to create enough useful wave at the source to survive the journey.

The comparison also clarifies why natural events make poor weapons analogies. Nature can be wasteful at scales militaries cannot afford. A volcano does not care how much energy goes into ash, heat, sound, seismic waves, atmospheric pressure, ejecta, and water. It does not have a logistics budget. It does not need to smuggle itself near an enemy coast.

A weapon has to compete.

This is perhaps the most important economic fact in geophysical warfare. Nature’s catastrophes are proof of possibility under no resource constraint. Engineering needs possibility under a human resource constraint.

A volcanic eruption may release energy orders of magnitude beyond any conventional weapon programme. An asteroid impact can excavate an ocean cavity kilometers across. A megathrust earthquake can rupture hundreds of kilometers of fault. The fact that all can produce tsunami says almost nothing about whether a navy can reproduce the mechanism economically.

Project Seal’s mistake, if it had one, was not thinking nature could be imitated.

Engineering imitates nature constantly.

The question is what must be paid to imitate the part that matters.

Hunga Tonga also corrects a tendency to treat wave height as the final metric. The eruption’s far-field effects were shaped by atmospheric coupling, trenches, harbors, sea level, and local geometry. Some places experienced surprising amplitudes. Others did not. Researchers had to piece together different instruments and mechanisms after the event.

If a natural event observed by global satellites, pressure sensors, tide gauges, seismic networks, and eyewitnesses can still require years of reconstruction, imagine the uncertainty in a 1944 weapons estimate derived from small explosive tests.

This is not an argument that old science was useless.

It is an argument for proportion.

Project Seal’s models were trying to predict a coupled nonlinear coastal system before numerical oceanography existed in anything like its modern form. The team’s ability to produce forecast relationships at all is impressive. Their ability to predict a full-scale target should be judged against the tools available, not against a modern forecast center.

But military feasibility has no historical handicap.

A weapon either works in the world or it does not.

The enemy does not grade on 1944 computational limitations.

This creates a tension between historical sympathy and engineering judgment. We can say Leech’s reasoning was sophisticated for its time and still conclude the proposed system was too uncertain for reliable use. We can say the programme generated useful science and still say the weapon claim exceeded what had been demonstrated.

Hunga Tonga strengthens this balanced reading because it shows the ocean remains capable of surprising modern science.

The surprise was not “we did not know explosions can make waves.”

The surprise was the combination and reach of mechanisms.

The eruption’s Lamb wave traveled around the world multiple times. Tide gauges far away recorded responses synchronized with atmospheric passages. Near-source damage also depended on cavity and local processes. Researchers had to separate signals moving at different speeds.

This is what mechanism-first analysis looks like.

A conspiracy-first analysis works in the opposite direction. It starts with the outcome — a surprising tsunami — then searches for a powerful hidden source capable of causing it. Project Seal becomes supporting lore because it proves governments once investigated artificial waves. The actual event physics becomes optional.

This is how historical truth can be used to support present falsehood.

The technique is powerful because the first premise is genuinely surprising.

Yes, a U.S.–New Zealand wartime project investigated explosive-generated inundation.

Yes, nuclear tests created giant local water disturbances.

Yes, modern volcanic explosions can generate destructive tsunami.

Therefore, the argument suggests, a later natural disaster might be a secret weapon.

The conclusion still needs event-specific evidence.

Without it, the true premises are camouflage.

Hunga Tonga demonstrates what event-specific evidence looks like. Researchers used satellite observations, pressure records, tide gauges, models, seismic data, and timing relationships to identify mechanisms. Different hypotheses made different predictions about arrival time and spatial pattern. The evidence was not “somebody has technology that could make a wave.” It was the fingerprint of the actual source.

That standard should be applied to every weapon allegation.

What signal should the proposed source leave?

Where is it?

A large underwater explosion should have acoustic and seismic consequences. A nuclear source may leave radionuclide evidence depending on containment and location. A distributed line of charges has a different source signature from fault rupture. Atmospheric forcing has a characteristic propagation speed. Timing across tide gauges can discriminate mechanisms.

The ocean is a witness.

So is the Earth.

A secret weapon may hide the operator. It does not automatically hide physics.

This point is especially important in an era of global sensing. Hydroacoustic networks, seismic stations, satellites, infrasound arrays, ocean buoys, and civilian sensors make very large geophysical interventions difficult to erase completely. Classification can hide intention and design more easily than it can hide planetary-scale consequences.

That does not make covert action impossible. It raises the evidentiary standard.

Project Seal belonged to a world with far fewer witnesses.

A 1944 test could occur in controlled water with limited public awareness and leave a thin archival trace. A modern artificial tsunami large enough to affect an ocean basin would interact with a sensing environment orders of magnitude denser.

The mythology aged in the opposite direction.

As monitoring improved, claims of invisible geophysical superweapons grew easier to circulate because the public gained access to more images of disaster but not necessarily more understanding of source physics.

Hunga Tonga can correct that gap if read patiently.

The event also reveals an unsettling possibility for future weapons research that is different from Project Seal. If direct explosive coupling is inefficient, an engineer might seek a system already close to instability — a landslide, volcanic flank, ice mass, reservoir, or other stored-energy environment — and try to trigger it.

This is the true leverage model.

The weapon supplies the trigger. Nature supplies most of the energy.

Such concepts raise obvious ethical and strategic problems, and reliable triggering is highly uncertain. They should not be conflated with capabilities that are not documented. But conceptually they solve Project Seal’s most expensive problem: they recruit stored potential rather than manufacturing the entire disturbance.

Project Seal itself did not document a reliable tectonic or volcanic trigger weapon.

This line must remain bright.

The programme generated waves with explosives. It did not acquire command over earthquakes.

Hunga Tonga therefore expands the book’s question without rewriting its answer.

Could a sufficiently large explosion generate catastrophic tsunami? Yes, under the right mechanisms and scale, explosive events can.

Did Project Seal demonstrate a practical way for a military to do so with its proposed system? No full-scale demonstration established that.

Does modern science prove no future engineered system could ever do it? No.

Does historical government interest prove such a system exists now? Also no.

This is the epistemic shape of serious uncertainty. It refuses both impossibility theater and capability theater.

The distinction matters because frontier military history attracts both.

Skeptics sometimes overreach by declaring a class of effect impossible when the real argument is that a particular design is inefficient.

Believers overreach by treating physical possibility as evidence of possession.

Hunga Tonga destroys the first mistake.

Project Seal’s record destroys the second.

The sea can be moved violently by explosive processes.

The military still has to build the process.

That is where the 1944 dream keeps failing the comparison.

The volcano already had the mountain, the caldera, the gas, the atmosphere, and the ocean.

Project Seal had to bring the explosives.
