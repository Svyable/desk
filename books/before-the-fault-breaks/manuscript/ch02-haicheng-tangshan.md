# Haicheng, Tangshan, and the Birth of a Scientific Trauma

The Haicheng story has been told so many times that it behaves like a precursor itself. Depending on the teller, it predicts whatever argument is coming next.

If you believe earthquakes can be predicted, Haicheng is the proof of concept: a large earthquake, a warning, an evacuation, lives saved. If you believe prediction is mostly retrospective mythology, Haicheng becomes a story inflated after the fact around an unusually obvious foreshock sequence. If you are interested in the sociology of science, Haicheng is a demonstration that prediction is never merely a technical act. Somebody has to decide that the evidence is strong enough to interrupt a city.

The event matters because all three versions contain something true.

The February 4, 1975 Haicheng earthquake occurred after a period of elevated concern in northeastern China. Chinese earthquake workers had been monitoring the region. A pronounced sequence of smaller earthquakes developed before the mainshock. Local authorities issued warnings and many residents stayed outside. The mainshock then damaged or destroyed a large fraction of the city’s buildings. Whatever ambiguities attach to the exact chronology and causal contribution of each precursor, the result was extraordinary enough that foreign scientists traveled to China to understand what had happened.

The later U.S. assessment was admiring but careful. The foreshocks did much of the predictive work. Claims involving animals, groundwater, geoelectric changes, and other signs were harder to audit. That distinction matters because a foreshock sequence is already seismicity. It is the fault speaking in the same language as the eventual rupture, just at lower amplitude. If a cluster of earthquakes changes in a way that sharply raises the conditional probability of a larger event, the problem is not metaphysical. It is statistical seismology.

The difficulty is that most small earthquakes are not foreshocks.

The word foreshock is assigned afterward. Before the mainshock, it is simply an earthquake. The same magnitude 4 event that becomes a “foreshock” in a successful narrative is an ordinary isolated earthquake in the vastly more common case where nothing larger follows. A warning system must operate before the label is available.

This turns precursor science into a problem of denominators. How many times did the signal occur? How many were followed by the target event? How many target events arrived without the signal? What geographic radius counts? What time window? If the researcher chooses those boundaries after seeing the earthquake, the signal gets to design its own test.

Tangshan was the denominator arriving with horrifying force.

On July 28, 1976, the Tangshan earthquake struck a densely populated industrial city. The death toll remains politically and historically complicated, but the disaster was among the deadliest earthquakes of the twentieth century. More important for prediction science, Tangshan did not present the same usable precursor pattern that had made Haicheng actionable. The contrast damaged the idea that a broad bundle of observable anomalies could be promoted into a dependable national warning method.

There is an uncomfortable lesson here that extends well beyond seismology. Scientific fields often develop around memorable successes before they have learned how to count failures. Medicine did this with heroic case reports. Intelligence does it with celebrated coups. Venture capital does it with unicorns. Anomaly research does it almost by definition. A rare hit acquires a biography. The misses become background noise.

Earthquake prediction is unusually unforgiving because a false positive is not free.

Imagine a city of five million receiving a credible official warning that a destructive earthquake may strike within seventy-two hours. Schools close. Hospitals alter procedures. Factories stop hazardous processes. Rail, aviation, fuel distribution, policing, and emergency shelters change posture. People sleep outside. Some leave. Businesses lose money. Traffic accidents increase. Patients miss care. If nothing happens, the next warning is weaker even if the science is stronger. Trust is a consumable resource.

False negatives are worse.

A system that issues few warnings may preserve credibility while missing the earthquake that actually comes. A system tuned to catch nearly every event may drown the public in alarms. This is not a side problem. It is the core engineering tradeoff. A precursor is operationally meaningful only when the costs of its false positives and false negatives are understood.

The Chinese experience in the 1970s exposed this before statistical learning gave us fashionable names for it.

Prediction enthusiasts often talk as though the only question is sensitivity: can we detect something before an earthquake? Public safety needs precision too: when we detect it, how often is an earthquake actually coming? Those quantities can diverge badly in rare-event problems. Even a test that behaves impressively in laboratory terms can produce mostly false alarms when the event itself is rare.

Suppose a particular precursor appears before half of all large earthquakes. That sounds useful. Suppose it also appears one hundred times more often when no large earthquake follows. Depending on the base rate, almost every warning could still be wrong. The public would experience the system not as “50 percent sensitive” but as a machine that cries wolf.

The mathematics is ordinary. The psychology is not.

Humans do not naturally reason in base rates, especially when the event is dramatic. We remember the dog that escaped before the earthquake and do not record the thousand afternoons when the dog escaped because the gate was open. We notice the radon spike before the rupture and rarely publish the equivalent spike during a wet week with no seismic consequence. We plot the geomagnetic anomaly that preceded the mainshock and quietly discard the months of solar-driven variability in which similar deviations occurred.

Haicheng also demonstrates something more subtle: prediction systems are composite institutions.

A sensor does not evacuate a city. Data travel through people. Someone decides whether an anomaly is real, whether it is local, whether it resembles previous cases, whether political leaders should be told, whether the warning should be public, and whether the public will comply. In 1975 China, earthquake prediction existed inside a particular political and scientific culture that encouraged mass participation in observation. Reports could include wells, animals, ground changes, and local sensations alongside instrument readings. That broad observational net increased the number of potential signals. It also increased the number of ways hindsight could assemble a persuasive story.

This tension never went away. Modern precursor science has simply replaced villagers and handwritten reports with satellites, GNSS receivers, machine-learning classifiers, magnetometers, radon stations, thermal cameras, and giant databases. The architecture is more quantitative, but the temptation is the same: collect enough channels and some of them will look prophetic after the red line is drawn.

The scientific solution is prospective discipline.

Before the earthquake, freeze the model. Define the geographic cell. Define the magnitude threshold. Define the warning window. Define how missing data are handled. Define what solar storms, weather, industrial interference, and instrument failures do to the alarm state. Then run the system for years and count everything, including humiliations.

That is much harder than writing a paper after one famous earthquake.

It is also the only path by which the strange parts of precursor science can become ordinary science.

Consider animal behavior. A town reports snakes leaving burrows before an earthquake. One can ask whether the reports were true, whether the behavior was unusual for season and temperature, whether small foreshocks were already occurring, whether people were primed to notice, whether the animals responded to vibrations humans could not perceive, or whether the story grew in the retelling. None of those questions insults the witnesses. They are what respect for the claim looks like.

Now imagine the better experiment. Instrument colonies or farms continuously. Track movement, sleep, vocalization, feeding, body temperature, and location. Establish months of baseline. Record weather, predators, machinery, husbandry changes, geomagnetic conditions, and local seismicity. Keep the analysts blind to future events while algorithms produce prospective anomaly scores. Then ask whether abnormal collective behavior predicts earthquakes above chance under predeclared windows.

The folklore becomes testable.

The same is true for groundwater. Wells can respond to strain, shaking, pressure, rainfall, pumping, tides, and aquifer dynamics. Reports of pre-earthquake water-level or chemistry changes are not intrinsically absurd. Earthquakes involve enormous crustal stress changes and fluid-filled rock. But the plausible existence of a coupling mechanism does not tell us whether the effect is large, early, local, stable, and specific enough for warning.

This is the recurring architecture of the book. Plausibility is not predictability.

Haicheng taught the world that an earthquake can, under favorable circumstances, be preceded by a sequence dramatic enough to alter public action. Tangshan taught the world that nature is under no obligation to repeat the sequence. Together they should have killed the search for a universal omen.

Instead, they should have started a better search.

What kinds of earthquakes produce foreshocks? Under what fault conditions do fluids, gas, electrical charge, or deformation propagate to the surface? Which apparent precursors are actually consequences of tiny ruptures already underway? Which biological observations reduce to acute sensory detection of P waves? Which ionospheric disturbances precede earthquakes rather than follow them? Which anomalies survive correction for the Sun?

These are narrower questions, which is why they are worth asking.

A field becomes dangerous when a single spectacular success is allowed to define its expectations. It becomes equally dangerous when a spectacular failure is allowed to forbid all adjacent questions.

Haicheng and Tangshan are usually presented as yes and no.

They are more useful as a pair of brackets around uncertainty.
