# L’Aquila and the Cost of Saying Calm Down

On April 6, 2009, a magnitude 6.3 earthquake struck L’Aquila in central Italy and killed more than three hundred people.

The city had been experiencing months of smaller earthquakes. Residents were frightened. A technician named Giampaolo Giuliani had attracted attention with claims involving radon measurements and impending earthquakes. Authorities and scientists were under pressure to explain what the swarm meant.

This is the point where earthquake prediction stops being a laboratory subject and becomes governance.

A meeting of Italy’s Major Risks Commission took place in L’Aquila on March 31, six days before the mainshock. The scientific position was not that a large earthquake had been ruled out. Earthquakes could not be predicted with the required precision. But public communication around the meeting was widely understood by some residents as reassuring.

After the disaster, six scientists and one government official were prosecuted for manslaughter over the risk assessment and communication. They were convicted at trial in 2012. In 2014 an appeals court acquitted the six scientists and reduced the conviction of the government official, Bernardo De Bernardinis. The case was never accurately summarized by the slogan that Italy jailed scientists “for failing to predict an earthquake.” The prosecution focused on the adequacy and communication of risk assessment, not a legal duty to possess impossible foresight.

The distinction matters because the wrong lesson is easy.

One wrong lesson is that scientists should never reassure anyone. Another is that authorities should issue warnings whenever unusual seismicity appears. A third is that experts should retreat into technical language so nobody can accuse them of advice.

All three would make public safety worse.

The L’Aquila problem is that probabilistic information enters a human environment that demands binary action. Sleep inside or outside. Send children to school or keep them home. Close a hospital or keep it operating. Evacuate or stay. “Risk is elevated but a damaging earthquake remains unlikely” is scientifically coherent and behaviorally difficult.

This is the same base-rate problem we encountered with foreshocks, now attached to bodies.

A seismic swarm can raise the short-term probability of a larger earthquake without making the larger earthquake likely. If the background probability over a short window is tiny, multiplying it many times can still leave a small absolute probability. Scientists naturally emphasize the absolute number. Frightened residents naturally hear the relative increase.

Both are rational.

The communication failure occurs when one number is allowed to erase the other.

There is also an asymmetry between false alarm and false reassurance. An unnecessary evacuation is visible immediately: lost work, traffic, cost, discomfort, hospital disruption, political anger. A precaution not taken is invisible unless disaster arrives. Institutions therefore feel pressure to avoid overreaction during the many swarms that end quietly. Over years, that pressure can harden into a reassuring style precisely when uncertainty should remain audible.

Prediction enthusiasts exploit this asymmetry too. If an independent forecaster issues repeated warnings and one earthquake occurs, the successful warning becomes famous. The false alarms diffuse into the past. Government agencies cannot operate with that accounting. Every warning has consequences and records.

This is why operational precursor science cannot be evaluated only by hit rate.

It needs a decision model.

What action would a ten-percent three-day probability justify? Perhaps not evacuating a city, but inspecting vulnerable structures, staging emergency crews, communicating aftershock-style guidance, checking hospitals, suspending particularly hazardous operations, or encouraging residents in fragile masonry buildings to review safer options. A five-percent probability and a fifty-percent probability need not map to the same action.

Binary prediction culture makes this harder because it imagines the only useful output as “earthquake coming.”

A calibrated risk system can support graduated action.

This is where earthquake precursor research has often put the cart before the fault. Researchers ask whether an anomaly is statistically associated with earthquakes before asking what decision the association would change. A signal with weak predictive value might still justify low-cost preparedness steps. A strong but geographically broad signal may be useless for evacuation and useful for regional readiness. Warning value depends on action cost.

L’Aquila also demonstrates the danger of mixing unsupported prediction claims with legitimate uncertainty.

Giuliani’s radon-based claims became part of the public atmosphere before the earthquake. The problem with an unvalidated predictor is not only that it may be wrong. It changes the communication landscape for everybody else. Officials respond to it. Journalists frame questions around it. Residents choose between voices. Skeptical scientists may overcorrect toward reassurance because the alternative appears irresponsible.

An unvalidated warning can therefore distort the official message even when authorities reject it.

This pattern appears in other anomalous domains. A weak extraordinary claim can polarize institutions into belief and dismissal, making nuanced evidence harder to communicate. The result is a false binary in which acknowledging any uncertainty looks like endorsing the fringe.

The cure is explicit evidence classes.

What is documented? What is statistically credible but incomplete? What is disputed? What is conjecture? Public communication should preserve those categories instead of compressing them into confidence theater.

For L’Aquila on March 31, 2009, the documented facts included an ongoing seismic sequence in a vulnerable region. The credible scientific assessment was that earthquakes cannot be deterministically predicted from such a swarm, while a larger event was not impossible. Claims that radon measurements supplied a reliable countdown were not established. The open question was how elevated short-term risk should change behavior in a city with vulnerable buildings.

That last issue is partly engineering, not prediction.

If buildings are fragile enough that residents must know the exact night of rupture to survive, the risk system has already failed upstream. Earthquake safety is strongest when it does not depend on foresight: reinforced structures, secured contents, automatic gas shutoffs where appropriate, resilient utilities, drills, education, rapid alerts, and emergency capacity.

This is why mature earthquake policy shifted toward mitigation.

Prediction is attractive because it seems to let society keep vulnerable systems and dodge only the dangerous hours. Building codes are expensive every day. Retrofitting is inconvenient every year. A perfect warning would be cheaper.

Nature has not offered that bargain.

The precursor question remains worth studying, but L’Aquila imposes a moral constraint on how results are described. A statistically interesting anomaly cannot be rhetorically promoted into actionable warning until its false alarms are known. A probability shift must be communicated as a probability shift. Researchers should not borrow the language of certainty from successful retrospective cases.

The reverse is also true. Authorities should not translate “we cannot predict” into “nothing unusual is happening.”

Ignorance is not reassurance.

A swarm can matter even when it cannot tell us the date. A fault can be dangerous even while tonight’s probability remains low. A building can be unsafe independent of any precursor.

The best communication keeps those truths together.

L’Aquila’s scientific legacy should not be fear of speaking. It should be precision about what each sentence does.

“There is no validated way to predict a large earthquake from this sequence” is one statement.

“A large earthquake will not occur” is another.

The space between them is where people live.
