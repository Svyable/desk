# Zhangheng in Orbit

On June 14, 2025, a Chinese rocket carried Zhangheng 1B into orbit.

The satellite is also known as CSES-02, the second China Seismo-Electromagnetic Satellite. It joined CSES-01, launched in 2018, in a program built around measuring electromagnetic fields, waves, ionospheric plasma, energetic particles, and related geophysical phenomena. Italy is not a distant observer. The Italian Space Agency coordinates a substantial national contribution through the Limadou collaboration, involving INFN, INAF, INGV, CNR, and several universities. Italian instruments fly on the mission. The collaboration had already generated more than two hundred scientific papers from CSES-01 data by early 2025, according to ASI.

This is not how dead scientific questions behave.

The public American story of earthquake prediction is largely one of retreat. The grand prediction programs of the twentieth century lost credibility; the U.S. emphasis moved toward hazard, resilience, earthquake early warning, and probabilistic forecasting. The Chinese-Italian CSES program presents a different institutional choice: keep measuring the possible electromagnetic and ionospheric edges of earthquake preparation with dedicated spacecraft.

The contrast is more interesting than a national competition narrative.

China is not “the country that believes in earthquake precursors” while the United States is “the country that follows science.” China’s own earthquake history includes both the celebrated Haicheng evacuation and the catastrophic failure to warn Tangshan. Chinese researchers know the false-alarm problem. Italy is one of the world’s mature seismological nations, with painful earthquake experience and sophisticated geophysics. The institutions involved in CSES include mainstream space and physics organizations.

The program therefore deserves something better than ridicule.

It also deserves something better than credulity.

CSES-01 orbits at roughly five hundred kilometers and carries instruments measuring magnetic and electric fields, ionospheric plasma, GNSS occultation, radio signals, and energetic particles. The mission studies more than earthquakes. Space weather, lightning, particle precipitation, radiation-belt physics, and other phenomena share the instrument suite. This breadth is scientifically sensible because the confounders for seismo-electromagnetic signals are themselves important physics.

CSES-02 improves the program by adding a second spacecraft with updated instrumentation and different sampling. Two satellites can revisit regions more frequently and help separate temporal from spatial variability. In principle, the pair can test whether purported anomalies are coherent, repeatable, and localized.

In practice, the decisive advance will not come from hardware alone.

The program sits at the center of the precursor field’s epistemic problem. It produces enormous multidimensional data sets in which interesting anomalies are guaranteed to exist. Electric field, magnetic field, plasma density, electron temperature, particle flux, latitude, longitude, altitude, local time, orbit number, geomagnetic activity: somewhere, before some earthquake, something unusual will happen.

The scientific question is whether earthquakes improve our ability to predict which anomalies should happen—or whether the anomalies improve our ability to forecast earthquakes.

Those are opposite directions of inference.

Much of the precursor literature runs backward. Start with earthquake. Look backward in time. Search nearby measurements. Characterize anomaly. The workflow is natural for discovery. It is inadequate for validation.

Validation must run forward. Start with measurements. Produce anomaly score. Freeze it. Wait.

CSES is unusually well positioned to make that transition because it is now a long-lived program rather than a one-off mission. CSES-01 created a historical data set. CSES-02 can serve as a prospective test bed. Algorithms developed on the first spacecraft could be frozen and evaluated on the second, with carefully designed corrections for instrument differences.

This is the experiment I would most like the collaboration to run.

Select several candidate precursor families before looking at the full CSES-02 target period. For each, specify the measured variable, spatial footprint, lead-time window, magnitude threshold, local-time restrictions, solar and geomagnetic exclusions, and anomaly threshold. Generate daily or orbital alerts. Publish them or cryptographically timestamp them. Score every alert against later seismicity. Keep a parallel stream of matched control locations.

Then let the system be wrong in public.

A null result would be a success for geophysics because it would collapse a large region of hypothesis space. A modest positive result—say, a reproducible probability shift for a particular earthquake class—would be more important than fifty retrospective case studies. An enormous effect would announce itself quickly.

The issue of earthquake classes is crucial.

A shallow continental strike-slip earthquake, a deep subduction event, a volcanic earthquake, and a megathrust rupture are not physically interchangeable. If a proposed precursor depends on crustal gas release or surface electrical coupling, earthquake depth should matter. Oceanic versus continental setting should matter. Rock type should matter. Ground conductivity should matter. Weather and atmospheric structure may matter.

A physically grounded model should predict where the satellite is blind.

This is an underused test. Proponents tend to emphasize where a precursor should appear. Strong theories also specify where it should not. If a mechanism requires gas-mediated atmospheric ionization over continental crust, deep offshore earthquakes become negative controls. If the same anomaly appears equally before them, the mechanism is in trouble.

CSES also creates an international epistemology problem that is rarely discussed openly.

Data access, processing pipelines, publication incentives, language barriers, institutional trust, and geopolitical tension affect how findings are received. A Chinese-led mission reporting a precursor can trigger skepticism in Western communities for reasons that mix legitimate methodological criticism with assumptions about national science. Conversely, national prestige can make negative results politically awkward inside any large program.

The Italian collaboration is valuable partly because it complicates that dynamic. Independent institutions share instruments, analysis, and publications. But international collaboration is not the same as independent replication. Teams using the same satellite and pipeline can share hidden assumptions.

True replication would use different instruments or independent analysis code.

Europe’s Swarm satellites, global GNSS networks, ground magnetometer arrays, atmospheric data, and seismic catalogs offer opportunities for cross-platform tests. A CSES anomaly that appears simultaneously in an independent measurement system is more interesting than one that exists only after mission-specific processing.

The 2025 launch also reveals something culturally important. The precursor question survived the era that supposedly falsified it because the scientific target evolved.

Nobody serious needs to promise “an earthquake will strike Tuesday.” The mission can study lithosphere-atmosphere-ionosphere coupling as a physical system. It can measure how earthquakes disturb near-Earth plasma after rupture. It can characterize natural electromagnetic environments. It can constrain candidate pre-seismic effects even if they prove useless for warning.

A failed predictor can still be a successful sensor.

This distinction is what lets good science continue after hype collapses.

There is a parallel in neutrino astronomy, gravitational-wave physics, and exoplanet searches. Instruments are often built around ambitious targets but produce valuable side science. CSES’s value does not stand or fall on deterministic earthquake prediction. That makes it easier, not harder, to demand ruthless precursor tests.

The mission has scientific room to fail.

It should use it.

The greatest risk is not that CSES finds nothing. The greatest risk is that it produces endless suggestive anomalies, each publishable, none decisive. A field can survive indefinitely in that middle state because every event refreshes the narrative.

The way out is prospective scarcity: choose fewer hypotheses and make them face more data.

Zhangheng was an ancient Chinese polymath associated with an early seismoscope, a device that detected distant earthquakes after they happened. The modern satellite bearing his name is pointed at a harder question: whether the Earth leaves traces before failure.

The irony is useful.

The ancient instrument did not predict.

It listened well.
