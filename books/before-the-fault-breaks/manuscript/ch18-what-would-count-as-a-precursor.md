# What Would Count as a Precursor

The word precursor has been allowed to become too generous.

An observation occurs before an earthquake. A paper calls it a precursor. The word then smuggles causality and usefulness into what may only be chronology.

We need a harder definition.

A scientifically meaningful earthquake precursor is a measurable phenomenon generated by, or tightly coupled to, a preparatory state of the fault system and occurring before the target rupture. An operational precursor must do more: it must improve prospective forecasts enough to change a decision.

Those are two different standards.

The first can be satisfied by an effect that occurs only under special geological conditions. The second needs enough sensitivity, specificity, lead time, and spatial precision to be useful in the world.

This book has spent sixteen chapters examining candidates that repeatedly blur the distinction. Radon changes may reveal permeability changes without predicting rupture. Animals may detect small seismic waves without sensing a days-ahead preparation state. Earthquake lights may be genuine electrical phenomena and mostly coseismic. Ionospheric disturbances after earthquakes are established while pre-seismic versions remain contested. A satellite may measure an anomaly perfectly and have no idea what caused it.

Measurement is not meaning.

A serious precursor claim should pass at least six tests.

The first is temporal priority. The signal must precede the target event in data that were recorded and preserved before the event. This sounds trivial. It excludes more than one might expect. Post-event recollection, reconstructed baselines, and processing that uses future information violate temporal priority even if the raw measurement existed beforehand.

The second is a defined target. “Earthquake” is not specific enough. Magnitude threshold, depth range, tectonic setting, spatial relationship, and warning window must be stated. A signal that precedes shallow continental magnitude-seven earthquakes is making a different claim from one that precedes all earthquakes above magnitude five.

The third is a denominator. How often does the signal occur without the target? How often does the target occur without the signal? No denominator, no precursor.

The fourth is incremental information. The signal must outperform or improve a baseline that already includes what we know: tectonic setting, historical seismicity, recent earthquakes, and other conventional predictors relevant to the region. Rediscovering that active faults are active is not short-term prediction.

The fifth is mechanism or at least mechanistic constraint. A statistical signal can be useful before its mechanism is fully understood, but it should make physical sense and produce testable consequences. If radon changes because permeability changes, neighboring hydrological measurements should behave in constrained ways. If a magnetic anomaly comes from crustal currents, spatial arrays should locate a source distinguishable from geomagnetic forcing.

The sixth is replication. Another team, another period, ideally another instrument or region. A phenomenon that exists only inside one pipeline is not ready for the public.

These tests are not equally mandatory for discovery. They are mandatory for promotion.

The history of science contains useful effects discovered before mechanisms. Nobody should reject an earthquake signal merely because the coupling physics is incomplete. But incomplete mechanism increases the burden on prospective statistics because alternative explanations are harder to exclude.

Likewise, an elegant mechanism does not rescue weak prediction. Rocks can generate electrical signals under stress. That fact does not tell us whether a particular ULF anomaly before a particular earthquake came from the fault.

This separation between mechanism and attribution is one of the most useful habits in all anomalous research.

Microwaves can affect tissue under known exposure regimes. That does not establish the cause of a diplomat’s illness. Governments have secretly recovered and exploited foreign hardware. That does not establish non-human crash retrieval. Human subjects can produce statistically unusual results in some psi experiments. That does not establish a mechanism or operational psychic channel. The same epistemic mistake appears under different costumes: proving that a class of mechanism exists and using that existence to attribute a disputed event.

Earthquake precursors are a cleaner laboratory for learning the habit because the eventual event is not disputed. The earthquake happens. The argument concerns the lead-up.

There is also a seventh test I have avoided numbering because it is more philosophical: the precursor must contain information the future has already made available.

This sounds tautological until rupture physics enters.

Suppose final earthquake magnitude is not fixed during the early preparatory state. Suppose a rupture begins on a small patch and only later either arrests or cascades across a much larger fault area depending on stress heterogeneity encountered along the way. Then no days-ahead precursor can encode the exact final magnitude because the relevant interactions have not happened.

A signal might still indicate elevated failure probability. It might identify a nucleation process. It might reveal that a fault patch is close to instability. But demanding exact magnitude and time would be like demanding the final path of a branching crack before it reaches the branch.

This is not ignorance in the usual sense.

It is possible indeterminacy at the level of the event we care about.

Earthquake prediction debates often assume the future event exists as a hidden object, fully specified but unread. Researchers search for a code. If they fail, the sensor was too weak or the model too simple.

The alternative is that the future is only partly specified.

Stress state, friction, fluids, and fault geometry constrain possibilities. The system may become increasingly unstable. But the exact rupture may be selected through a cascade that remains sensitive to local conditions. The best possible forecast could therefore be probabilistic even with perfect measurements of many precursor channels.

Meteorology is a useful analogy with limits. Weather prediction improved enormously because atmospheric states are densely observed and equations are known, yet chaos imposes horizons on deterministic detail. Earthquakes may be worse because the relevant fault properties at depth are sparsely observed and rupture intervals are long.

This perspective makes modest precursor effects more scientifically plausible and deterministic prediction less so.

A fault could emit useful state information without emitting a schedule.

That is the thesis I would want a skeptical seismologist to attack. Not “dogs know.” Not “the ionosphere predicts earthquakes.” Not “China launched a satellite, therefore electromagnetic precursors are real.” The claim is narrower: preparatory processes may alter multiple measurable systems before some ruptures, but the field has not yet shown that those alterations carry enough prospective, incremental information for operational prediction.

This thesis can fail in both directions.

It may be too optimistic. Prospective multichannel tests could show that once environmental confounders and retrospective freedom are removed, nearly all non-seismic precursor effects vanish into noise. The remaining pre-event information may come almost entirely from foreshocks and deformation already incorporated into conventional seismology.

Or it may be too pessimistic. A carefully controlled satellite-ground network could discover a reproducible pattern with useful lead time. Radon and electromagnetic anomalies might turn out to define a particular preparatory regime. Biological sensors might identify a weak environmental signal before instruments were designed to detect it.

Both outcomes are allowed.

That is what distinguishes an open conjecture from a belief.

A mature field should be able to say in advance what result would change its mind.

For the strong electromagnetic-precursor hypothesis, repeated prospective failure across well-instrumented large earthquakes should drive probability downward. For skeptics, a frozen algorithm producing calibrated out-of-sample forecasts across independent regions should drive probability upward. No amount of disciplinary identity should be permitted to outweigh that.

The odd thing about earthquake prediction is that the public argument is far more certain than the interesting scientific middle.

One side says impossible.

Another says suppressed.

The instruments say something less dramatic: noisy, conditional, unresolved.

That is not a satisfying slogan.

It is a good place to do science.
