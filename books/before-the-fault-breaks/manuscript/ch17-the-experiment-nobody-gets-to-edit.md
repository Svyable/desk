# The Experiment Nobody Gets to Edit

If I had the budget to settle one argument in earthquake science, I would not build a machine that predicts earthquakes.

I would build a machine that prevents researchers from rewriting what they meant after one happens.

The hardware would be impressive, but the governance would matter more.

Choose several tectonically active regions with different fault types and environmental settings. Dense seismic arrays already exist in many. Add borehole strain where feasible, high-rate GNSS, groundwater pressure and chemistry, radon and thoron at selected geologically justified sites, broadband magnetometers, atmospheric electric-field sensors, weather stations, infrasound, and continuous low-light cameras. Where livestock or wildlife monitoring is practical and ethical, add instrumented animal cohorts as experimental sensors rather than anecdotes. Integrate satellite observations from CSES, Swarm, GNSS-derived ionospheric TEC, thermal remote sensing, and space-weather measurements.

Then make most of the system boring.

Every instrument has calibration records. Every station has a site survey. Every outage is logged. Nearby infrastructure is mapped. Weather and human activity are treated as first-class variables. Data flow continuously whether earthquakes occur or not.

The important layer sits above the sensors: a prospective prediction registry.

Before each evaluation year begins, participating teams submit frozen algorithms. Each algorithm must output explicit probabilities or alert states for predefined geographic cells, magnitude thresholds, and time windows. The code is sealed. The exact version is hashed. Feature lists and exclusions are recorded. Teams can develop new models during the year, but those models compete only in the next evaluation period.

No editing the exam after seeing the answer.

The system should support multiple hypothesis families rather than one grand model. A seismicity-only forecast. A deformation model. A radon-hydrology model. A ground-electromagnetic model. An ionospheric model. An animal-behavior model. A multichannel fusion model. A deliberately stupid baseline using only historical hazard and recent seismicity.

The stupid baseline is essential.

Frontier models should earn complexity by beating something simple and competent.

Evaluation would use metrics that punish the real failures. How many target earthquakes were preceded by alerts? How many alerts were followed by no target event? What fraction of space-time spent under warning? How much lead time? How precise was location? How did probability calibration perform? Did the model add information beyond recent seismicity and long-term hazard?

A model that places an entire tectonic belt under permanent elevated warning gets little credit for catching earthquakes there.

A model that generates one spectacular hit and hundreds of misses is not celebrated through a case study. The full ledger remains visible.

The experimental design should also preserve the four evidence classes used throughout this book.

Class one is documented measurement. The magnetometer recorded a field change. The well pressure shifted. The herd changed movement pattern. The satellite measured a plasma anomaly. These statements can be true without any earthquake relationship.

Class two is credible but incomplete association. A predefined anomaly occurs before target events more often than expected under matched controls, but replication, mechanism, or operational value remains uncertain.

Class three is disputed claim. A team reports performance that depends on contested processing, weak controls, unreplicated stations, or post hoc choices.

Class four is speculative synthesis. A proposed causal chain links stress, fluid, electrical, atmospheric, ionospheric, or biological effects but remains unvalidated.

The registry prevents categories from silently upgrading themselves.

A measurement does not become a precursor because an earthquake happened later. A precursor association does not become a mechanism because one exists in the laboratory. A mechanism does not become prediction because a model can fit historical data.

There should be a disinformation policy too, although the word sounds dramatic for geophysics.

Prediction attracts fraud, self-deception, publicity, and political pressure because successful forecasts are spectacular. A global experiment should require timestamped public or escrowed predictions from anyone claiming operational skill. No credit for predictions revealed only after the event. No vague geographic regions or elastic windows. No deleting failed forecasts from websites. No counting aftershocks as independent successes unless the rule declared them targets beforehand.

A simple public prediction archive would do more for the field than another decade of argument.

The experiment also needs a scientific graveyard.

Every failed hypothesis stays accessible. If a particular ULF threshold was tested prospectively for five years and performed at chance, future papers cannot resurrect it without explaining what materially changed. If a radon algorithm fails outside one cave, the locality becomes part of the theory. If animal movement shows no association after controlling for weather and microseismicity, the negative result is preserved.

Science loses time when failed ideas vanish instead of accumulating.

This is especially severe in earthquake prediction because target events are rare. A researcher can spend a career waiting for enough large earthquakes in one region. Institutional memory must outlive individual careers.

The project should therefore be designed for decades.

That sounds expensive until compared with the cost of one urban earthquake.

The sensor network would generate valuable science even if prediction fails completely: fault mechanics, hydrology, atmospheric electricity, space weather, animal behavior, ionospheric physics, rupture dynamics, and earthquake early-warning improvements. This side value is not a consolation. It is what makes long-horizon experiments rational when the main hypothesis is uncertain.

CSES already demonstrates the principle in orbit. The spacecraft study broader Earth-space physics while testing seismo-electromagnetic ideas. A terrestrial program should do the same.

There is a governance choice about whether alerts are public during the experiment.

My preference is tiered access. Raw data should be public wherever safety and infrastructure constraints allow. Scientific anomaly products should be public or at least timestamped. But experimental “earthquake alerts” should not automatically become civil-defense warnings until performance crosses a predeclared operational threshold and emergency-management agencies decide how to use them.

This separates scientific transparency from irresponsible public signaling.

The threshold could be defined in decision terms. A model becomes eligible for operational pilot only if it improves a standard forecast by a specified amount, maintains calibration over an independent period, and keeps false alarms below a level compatible with the proposed action. Different actions can have different thresholds.

Triggering enhanced sensor sampling requires little confidence.

Advising infrastructure operators to check contingency plans requires more.

Publicly recommending evacuation requires vastly more.

This hierarchy protects both science and the public from the binary prediction fantasy.

The experiment should also contain deliberate red teams.

One team tries to reproduce every positive result with non-tectonic explanations. They model solar and geomagnetic effects, weather, power-grid noise, pumping, agriculture, animal handling, instrument drift, satellite orbital biases, and data-processing artifacts. Their job is not to be “skeptical” in attitude. Their job is to build quantitative alternatives.

Another team performs blind replication without knowing which periods contain large earthquakes until outputs are frozen.

A third audits statistical multiplicity: how many variables, windows, regions, thresholds, and model versions were tried before the reported one survived?

This structure treats skepticism as instrumentation.

The history of precursor science shows why it is needed. Researchers are not uniquely gullible. They are uniquely exposed to a data structure that rewards hindsight. Rare dramatic events create red lines. Environmental signals are noisy. Mechanisms are numerous. Public attention favors hits. Journals favor anomalies. Careers are short compared with recurrence intervals.

The system is almost optimized to produce sincere overinterpretation.

Better people will not fix that.

Better architecture might.

There is one more feature I would require: predictions about non-events.

A strong theory should sometimes say that conditions are unusually quiet. If a model claims to infer fault state, it should identify periods when risk is lower than baseline as well as higher. Those low-risk states can be scored too. This prevents every anomaly channel from being interpreted only in the direction of impending disaster.

The Earth provides far more non-earthquake time than earthquake time. Use it.

At the end of twenty years, the experiment might produce the least cinematic result imaginable. Perhaps foreshock statistics improve modestly. Perhaps groundwater and radon add local value at a handful of sites. Perhaps satellite electromagnetic variables contribute nothing prospectively once space weather is controlled. Perhaps animal behavior reduces to sensitivity to small seismic waves. Perhaps earthquake lights remain rare physical curiosities. Perhaps no multichannel model achieves operational warning value.

That would be an extraordinary scientific achievement.

We would finally know which mysteries were measurements and which were mirrors.

And if a real precursor survives?

It will not need a legend.

It will have a log file.
