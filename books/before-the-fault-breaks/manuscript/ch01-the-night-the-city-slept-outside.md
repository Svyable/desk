# The Night the City Slept Outside

On February 4, 1975, people in and around Haicheng, in northeastern China, left their buildings before the ground broke. The earthquake that followed was large enough to destroy or seriously damage most of the city’s structures. The evacuation became the most famous success story in the history of earthquake prediction, a story repeated for decades whenever somebody needed to prove that the Earth whispers before it moves.

The whisper is the interesting part. The Haicheng prediction was not a single instrument blinking red. It was an accumulation: a region already considered dangerous, changes in seismicity, a conspicuous foreshock sequence, reports of unusual animal behavior, groundwater observations, and the judgment of officials operating inside a political system willing to order or encourage people into the cold. A U.S. scientific delegation that visited China in 1976 came away impressed by the achievement but less impressed by the mythology already forming around it. The strongest part of the case was the foreshock sequence. Many of the more exotic precursors were harder to evaluate after the fact.

Then Tangshan happened.

On July 28, 1976, a magnitude 7.6 earthquake struck the industrial city without comparable warning. Hundreds of thousands died. The famous Chinese precursor system had not failed in some technical, arguable way. It had encountered the problem that ruins prediction schemes: sometimes the Earth behaves differently before doing the same thing.

That is where this book begins, not because Haicheng was fraudulent or Tangshan proves prediction impossible, but because the pair exposes the central mistake in how earthquake precursors are usually discussed. We keep asking whether a phenomenon is “real.” Did radon rise? Did dogs behave strangely? Did the ionosphere change? Did lights appear over the fault? Did the magnetic field twitch? Those are scientific questions, but they are not yet prediction questions. A precursor can be real and useless. It can be causally related to tectonic stress and still occur before too few earthquakes, or occur too often without earthquakes, or arrive too late, or be measurable only after enough statistical grooming that nobody could have recognized it prospectively.

Prediction requires specificity.

For a warning system, the burden is ugly. It must say where, when, and how large. It must tolerate a planet already full of magnetic storms, thunderstorms, groundwater fluctuations, industrial noise, animal migrations, temperature changes, solar activity, instrument drift, ordinary small earthquakes, quarry blasts, and human observers who remember hits better than misses. An anomaly is not a forecast until somebody has defined in advance what counts as an anomaly, what area it applies to, how long the warning remains active, what magnitude threshold matters, and what happens when the signal appears and nothing breaks.

The history of earthquake prediction is therefore partly a history of beautiful retrospective plots.

Put a vertical red line at the time of an earthquake and almost any noisy environmental time series becomes narratively suggestive. One week before the red line, a bump. Three days before, a dip. Twelve hours before, a cluster. The temptation is almost physical. Human vision is an anomaly detector with no built-in penalty for multiple comparisons. Give us twenty sensors and one disaster and we will find a premonition.

This is not an argument for ignoring anomalies. It is an argument for making them pay rent.

The strange thing is that the field never completely died. In public American seismology, deterministic earthquake prediction became disreputable after repeated failures and overconfident claims. The U.S. Geological Survey now says plainly that neither it nor any other scientists have predicted a major earthquake in the strict sense, and that decades of work have not produced convincing electromagnetic precursors. The center of gravity moved toward probabilistic hazard, building codes, rapid detection, ShakeAlert-style early warning after rupture begins, and aftershock forecasting. This was not surrender. It was engineering honesty.

But elsewhere, and sometimes quietly within mainstream institutions, the precursor question remained alive.

China launched the first China Seismo-Electromagnetic Satellite, CSES-01, in 2018. Italy became a scientific partner through the Limadou collaboration. The spacecraft carries instruments for electric and magnetic fields, ionospheric plasma, energetic particles, and related measurements. Then, on June 14, 2025, China and Italy launched CSES-02, a second-generation mission intended to improve temporal and spatial coverage. The Italian Space Agency does not describe the project as folklore. It describes a serious Earth-and-space-science program studying possible electromagnetic, ionospheric, and magnetospheric phenomena associated with seismic activity.

That fact is worth sitting with.

Half a century after Haicheng and Tangshan, after Parkfield, after generations of false alarms, national space agencies are still putting exquisite instruments into orbit partly to investigate whether the solid Earth leaks advance information into the atmosphere and ionosphere.

The correct response is neither belief nor ridicule. It is curiosity with bookkeeping.

If the entire precursor enterprise is wrong, why is it so persistent? One answer is institutional inertia. Another is that noisy data can keep weak hypotheses alive indefinitely. A third is that the physical system genuinely does produce pre-failure phenomena, but not in the clean universal form prediction enthusiasts once wanted. Brittle materials crack before catastrophic failure. Fluids migrate. Faults creep. Rocks deform, fracture, change permeability, and alter local stress fields. Foreshocks sometimes accelerate. Radon can move through newly opened pathways. Charges may redistribute in stressed rock under some conditions. Animals can sense signals humans miss, though what they sense may be nothing more mysterious than early seismic waves or local environmental changes. The ionosphere undeniably responds to phenomena originating below it, including earthquakes after rupture through acoustic and gravity waves. The hard question is whether any of these channels reliably carry information before rupture.

Notice the difference between “nothing happens” and “nothing sufficiently diagnostic happens.”

Earthquakes are not idealized blocks snapping on a classroom table. They are failures in heterogeneous rock at depth, inside fault systems with long histories, fluids, roughness, changing effective stress, and geometries we cannot observe directly at the relevant scale. Some ruptures begin with foreshocks; many do not. Some faults creep. Some lock. Some large ruptures appear to grow from starts that, at least initially, resemble much smaller earthquakes. If nature itself does not commit early to the eventual size of a rupture, then the dream of a universal days-ahead signature may be asking for information that does not yet exist.

That possibility changes the philosophical shape of the problem. The failure of prediction would not mean we failed to notice the message. It could mean there is no message until the event is already becoming itself.

Yet the planet keeps offering odd facts.

People have reported luminous glows around earthquakes for centuries. A 2014 study in Seismological Research Letters assembled a set of better-documented earthquake-light cases and found an apparent association with certain rift environments and steep faults. Mechanisms remain debated. One family of models involves electrical charge activation and migration in stressed rocks; other work asks whether crustal electric fields can couple into the lower atmosphere strongly enough to produce visible discharge. These are not established forecasting tools. They are attempts to explain a recurring class of observations without asking folklore to do physics.

Radon presents a similar problem. The gas is produced by radioactive decay in rocks and soils. Its concentration can change with pressure, moisture, ventilation, groundwater movement, and countless local factors. Studies have reported anomalies before some earthquakes. Other work shows exactly why those anomalies are treacherous: a “spike” can be meteorology wearing a tectonic costume. Recent researchers have begun throwing machine learning at the problem, not because AI is magic, but because separating environmental covariates from genuinely unusual residual behavior is precisely the sort of messy multivariable task that defeated simpler threshold rules. The danger is obvious. A sufficiently flexible model can also learn the earthquake catalog backward and call the fit foresight.

Then there are animals.

The stories are ancient. Snakes leaving holes. Dogs barking. Toads disappearing. Cattle becoming restless. Fish behaving strangely. The U.S. Geological Survey’s position is appropriately skeptical: consistent, reliable behavior before earthquakes has not been established. Animals can detect P waves seconds before people feel strong shaking, which explains some apparently prophetic behavior without requiring any pre-rupture phenomenon at all. Yet even the skeptical literature leaves a narrow door open. If faults generate physical precursors—ground tilt, groundwater changes, electrical or magnetic variations—animals with relevant sensory systems could in principle detect them.

That conditional is more interesting than the legend.

Biology has repeatedly embarrassed human assumptions about what counts as a sense. Birds, turtles, bacteria, fish, and insects use magnetic information. Elephants communicate partly through low-frequency sound and seismic vibrations. Sharks detect weak electric fields. Pit vipers sense infrared radiation. A dog does not need a sixth sense to know something before us. It needs one ordinary sense we underappreciate, connected to a physical signal we have failed to characterize.

The question becomes experimental rather than mystical: what signal, at what amplitude, over what distance, with what latency, and with what false-positive rate?

This book follows that question across geology, biology, atmospheric science, satellite engineering, Cold War-era prediction programs, modern machine learning, and the sociology of a scientific embarrassment. It will treat earthquake lights seriously without declaring them warnings, animal behavior seriously without granting animals prophecy, radon seriously without turning every spike into tectonics, and Chinese-Italian satellite missions seriously without confusing an expensive instrument with a validated theory.

The evidence will stay in four bins.

There are documented facts: an evacuation occurred at Haicheng; Tangshan arrived without comparable warning; Parkfield’s famous forecast window expired before the expected earthquake came; CSES satellites exist; instruments have measured real ionospheric, electromagnetic, chemical, hydrological, and biological variations around earthquakes.

There is credible but incomplete evidence: specific precursor classes that show statistical or mechanistic promise in some data sets but lack adequate prospective validation across regions and events.

There are disputed claims: individual spectacular predictions, retrospective anomaly catalogs, and causal interpretations that do not survive independent controls or cannot be separated from confounders.

And there is conjecture: the possibility that a multichannel system, designed prospectively and punished for false alarms, could extract a narrow class of useful pre-rupture information even if universal prediction remains impossible.

The last category is where I am willing to take a risk, but not a shortcut.

The wager of this book is not that earthquakes can be predicted. It is that the phrase “earthquake prediction failed” has become too blunt to describe what we actually learned. The old dream—one precursor, one clock, one coming rupture—probably deserved to die. The more interesting question is whether some faults, some of the time, enter measurable states that slightly alter the odds before failure.

That sounds smaller. It is.

Small is what survives contact with the Earth.
