# Rocks That Make Electricity

You can make rock produce electrical signals in a laboratory.

This fact is simultaneously less and more important than it sounds.

Crack certain materials and charges separate. Deform piezoelectric minerals and voltages appear. Friction can electrify surfaces. Fluids moving through charged pores can generate streaming potentials. Magnetic minerals can change magnetization under stress. Fracturing can produce electromagnetic emissions. None of these effects requires exotic physics.

The problem begins when “rocks can make electricity” becomes “a fault broadcasts a detectable warning before earthquakes.”

Between those sentences sits most of a continent.

The crust is not a benchtop sample with clean electrodes. It is hot, wet, conductive, fractured, heterogeneous, mineralogically complicated, and buried under kilometers of material. Electrical signals attenuate. Groundwater redistributes charge. Human infrastructure produces enormous electromagnetic noise. The ionosphere and magnetosphere are driven by the Sun. Lightning fills wide frequency ranges. Railways, power lines, pipelines, mines, transmitters, and industrial systems all leak into sensors.

Any proposed seismo-electromagnetic precursor has to cross this environment and still retain enough identity to be recognized.

There are several mechanism families, and they should not be blended simply because they produce electrical vocabulary.

Piezoelectricity is the most familiar. Quartz is piezoelectric. Mechanical stress on a suitable crystal orientation creates polarization. Because crustal rocks can contain quartz, piezoelectric mechanisms have long appeared in earthquake-electromagnetic discussions. Scaling from crystals to disordered polycrystalline rock is not trivial, however. Random orientations can cancel signals. Conductive pathways can dissipate charge. A mechanism that works beautifully in a crystal may be weak at geological scale.

Electrokinetic or streaming-potential mechanisms involve fluids. Water moving through pores and fractures carries charge relative to mineral surfaces, producing electrical potentials. Because fault zones contain fluids and permeability changes with stress, these mechanisms are physically relevant to crustal electrical observations. But again, ordinary hydrology can generate them too.

Piezomagnetism describes stress-induced changes in magnetization of magnetic rocks. The effect is real and has been studied in geophysics, including models of electromagnetic fields generated by earthquake-related stress changes. A 2025 Journal of Geophysical Research paper, for example, modeled fields generated by an earthquake through the piezomagnetic effect. Modeling coseismic fields is not the same as demonstrating a reliable precursor, but it helps define what amplitudes and spatial patterns are physically plausible.

Then there is the positive-hole hypothesis associated especially with Friedemann Freund and collaborators. In that model, defects related to peroxy bonds in oxygen-bearing minerals can activate mobile positive charge carriers under stress. The charges could propagate beyond the stressed volume, reach the surface, modify surface potentials, ionize air, and contribute to infrared, electrical, magnetic, or luminous anomalies.

The attraction of the hypothesis is that it attempts to connect several strange observations with one solid-state mechanism.

That is also the danger.

A theory capable of explaining radon changes, air ionization, animal behavior, infrared anomalies, electromagnetic signals, and earthquake lights can become unfalsifiable if every anomaly is allowed to count. The mechanism must instead predict which combinations should occur, with what signs, amplitudes, distances, and timing, under what rock types and stress states.

Mineralogy should matter.

If positive-hole activation requires particular defect chemistry, regions with favorable rocks should behave differently from regions without them. Fault geometry should matter. Stress orientation should matter. Surface conductivity and groundwater should matter. Humidity should matter for atmospheric effects. A mechanism that predicts everywhere equally is suspicious because the Earth is not equally made.

This is one reason the 2014 earthquake-light study’s reported association with rift environments is interesting. A geological association can turn a folklore category into a constrained hypothesis. If steep faults in certain tectonic settings provide efficient pathways for charge migration, then luminous events should cluster there. But the observation must be tested on future events and against the distribution of reporting opportunities. Populated rifts with good visibility may simply produce more reports.

The challenge is always the denominator.

Electromagnetic precursor research has also been haunted by the phrase “ULF,” ultra-low frequency. Low-frequency magnetic fields penetrate farther through conductive Earth than high-frequency fields, making ULF signals attractive for crustal monitoring. Researchers have reported anomalous ULF activity before earthquakes. Skeptics have pointed to geomagnetic variation, cultural noise, sensor artifacts, and weak statistical controls.

Both sides can find examples.

The correct experiment is array-based. A local crustal source should produce a spatial pattern that differs from a geomagnetic disturbance arriving over a broad region. Multiple magnetometers can separate common-mode space-weather signals from local deviations. Reference stations outside the tectonic target area can provide controls. Solar-wind and geomagnetic indices can identify external forcing. If an anomaly remains localized and physically consistent with a crustal source, it earns more attention.

This is basic source separation.

The remarkable thing is how often precursor claims are published without enough of it.

A single-station anomaly is weak evidence because almost any unknown local interference can imitate a signal. Two stations are better. An array with known transfer functions, calibrated clocks, environmental monitors, and public raw data begins to look like a real experiment.

Electrical claims also need mundane engineering audits. Is there a railway nearby? Cathodic protection on pipelines? High-voltage transmission? Electric fences? Mining equipment? A new inverter? A failing power supply? The more extraordinary the geophysical interpretation, the more boring the site survey should become.

This principle generalizes to anomaly research: before invoking new physics, inventory the extension cords.

There is a reason seismo-electromagnetic work persists despite skepticism. The physical ingredients exist. Stress changes electrical and magnetic properties of materials. Fluids move. Rocks fracture. Charges separate. Earthquakes generate measurable electromagnetic effects during and after rupture under some conditions. The open question is not whether mechanical and electromagnetic worlds couple. They do.

The open question is whether pre-rupture coupling escapes the crust in a stable, diagnostic form.

A useful way to think about this is information loss. The fault may produce many microscopic electrical changes as stress evolves. By the time those signals propagate through heterogeneous crust and reach a surface sensor, most details may be smeared, attenuated, or mixed with larger environmental fields. The physics can be real while the information channel is poor.

That is a less romantic reason prediction might fail.

The Earth whispers, but the transmission line is terrible.

Satellite missions change the geometry. Instead of measuring fields only at the surface, they monitor the ionosphere, where electrical coupling from below might appear over broader areas. But moving the sensor upward does not simplify the inference. It introduces a vast new source of variability from solar and magnetospheric processes.

The next chapters follow that problem into orbit.

Before we leave the rocks, it is worth preserving one hard boundary.

Laboratory stress-generated electrical effects are evidence that candidate mechanisms exist. They are not evidence that a particular pre-earthquake field observation has that cause. A physical effect establishes possibility, not attribution.

This distinction is easy to say and hard to maintain once an anomalous trace is sitting beside an earthquake time.

The red line exerts gravity.

Good science has to pull the other way.
