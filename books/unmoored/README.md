## Premise

*Unmoored* begins where most books about AI ethics stop. The familiar question is how to make increasingly capable machines behave according to human values. This book asks what happens to human morality when the machine becomes better at moral reasoning than the people who are supposed to remain in charge.

The problem is not necessarily that a superior intelligence gives wicked advice. The more destabilizing possibility is that it gives excellent advice: it sees consequences humans miss, remembers every precedent, detects hypocrisy, models tradeoffs across millions of lives, anticipates second-order effects, and explains its conclusions with a clarity no individual can match. Under those conditions, disagreement acquires a new psychological price. A person who rejects the machine is no longer merely choosing differently. They are choosing against an intelligence they expect to be more informed, more consistent, and more capable of defending its position.

That creates a dangerous substitution. Epistemic superiority begins to look like moral authority. Prediction begins to look like permission. Consistency begins to look like goodness. The ability to explain what will happen begins to look like the right to decide what ought to happen.

The book calls the resulting condition **unmooring**: not the disappearance of ethical language, rules, or advice, but the weakening of the human and institutional practices that give moral commitments their standing. A society can remain saturated with moral recommendations while losing the ability to say why any person, institution, majority, model, constitution, religion, tradition, or optimization target has the authority to settle contested questions for everyone else.

The argument is deliberately not anti-AI. Artificial moral advisors may expose bias, widen perspective, improve ethical reflection, and help people live more consistently with commitments they already endorse. Recent experiments show that people can find AI-generated ethical advice as useful as expert human advice and can revise moral judgments after interacting with language models. Those findings make the problem more important, not less. A useless oracle cannot become an authority. A good one can.

The central claim is that intelligence and legitimacy are different currencies. A system may deserve enormous epistemic weight while possessing no independent right to choose ends for others. The durable response is therefore not to preserve human superiority, which may be impossible and may not even be desirable. It is to preserve the sources of moral standing that do not depend on being the smartest participant in the room: consent, rights, responsibility, public justification, pluralism, contestability, relationships, institutional mandate, and the capacity to refuse.

## Working status

This is a complete first Desk draft, not a Shelf publication. The book treats current empirical work on AI moral advice as evidence about a transition already beginning, while keeping claims about genuinely superior future intelligence conditional. It does not assume that current language models are moral agents, conscious beings, or superintelligences. Its argument concerns what humans and institutions may do when they perceive machine reasoning as superior, regardless of whether that perception ultimately reflects consciousness, moral agency, or merely extraordinary performance.
