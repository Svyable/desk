# Chapter Briefs and Falsification Notes

These briefs track what each chapter has to establish, the strongest evidence against overclaiming, and what would make the argument fail. They are not prose outlines.

## Chapter 1 — The Better Answer

**Job:** Open with the ordinary act of asking a machine what one ought to do, then introduce the destabilizing case in which its answer is not merely fluent but predictively and analytically better than the user's.

**Evidence to seek:** Landes, Francis, and Everett (2026); Meincke, Nave, and Terwiesch (2026); Krügel, Ostermaier, and Uhl (2026); contemporary work on AI moral advice.

**Counterevidence:** People do not blindly defer to AI; obvious bad reasoning can reduce persuasion, and source aversion remains in some contexts.

**Falsification note:** The chapter fails if it treats current LLMs as demonstrated morally superior agents or implies that persuasion proves legitimate authority.

## Chapter 2 — The Difference Between Knowing and Owing

**Job:** Separate factual and predictive intelligence from normative standing. Use the is/ought distinction without pretending that morality is independent of facts.

**Evidence to seek:** Hume; contemporary moral epistemology; examples where better forecasting legitimately changes moral deliberation without settling the underlying end.

**Counterevidence:** Many moral disagreements are partly disagreements about facts, consequences, feasibility, or consistency; better intelligence can resolve those components.

**Falsification note:** Do not turn the is/ought distinction into the false claim that facts never bear on what ought to be done.

## Chapter 3 — The Euthyphro Machine

**Job:** Recast the Euthyphro problem for artificial authority: is an act right because the superior intelligence recommends it, or does the intelligence recommend it because there are reasons that make it right independently?

**Evidence to seek:** Plato's *Euthyphro*; ideal-observer and artificial-moral-advisor literature.

**Counterevidence:** A sufficiently capable advisor might be valuable precisely because it tracks independent reasons more reliably than humans.

**Falsification note:** Do not imply the ancient dialogue is literally about AI or that the dilemma alone disproves moral realism, ideal-observer theories, or moral expertise.

## Chapter 4 — Moral Testimony

**Job:** Examine why moral advice feels different from ordinary expert testimony and why understanding, character, and ownership of judgment may matter even when the advice is correct.

**Evidence to seek:** Alison Hills on moral testimony; work on moral expertise; Rodríguez-López and Rueda; Giubilini and Savulescu.

**Counterevidence:** Refusing reliable testimony merely to preserve authorship can be irresponsible when stakes are high and the recipient lacks relevant understanding.

**Falsification note:** The chapter fails if it romanticizes unaided judgment or claims people must independently derive every moral belief.

## Chapter 5 — The Weight of Being Wrong

**Job:** Show how expected competence gaps alter the burden of dissent. When the advisor is usually right, disagreement becomes psychologically and institutionally expensive even without formal coercion.

**Evidence to seek:** algorithm appreciation, automation reliance, expertise and advice-taking research.

**Counterevidence:** Humans often resist algorithms, selectively adhere, or discount them after visible errors; deference is context dependent.

**Falsification note:** Do not assume a smooth monotonic relationship between measured accuracy and obedience.

## Chapter 6 — When Advice Becomes Authority

**Job:** Distinguish advice, influence, delegated decision power, and legitimate authority. Explain how a system can cross practical thresholds without any explicit constitutional moment.

**Evidence to seek:** democratic legitimacy scholarship; work on algorithmic decision legitimacy; moral-expertise literature.

**Counterevidence:** Reliable advisory systems can remain genuinely advisory when users have time, alternatives, appeal, and real power to refuse.

**Falsification note:** Do not define influence itself as illegitimate authority.

## Chapter 7 — The Good-Enough Conscience

**Job:** Use 2026 evidence that people may defer to LLM moral advice on a response-by-response basis and that high-quality reasons are not always necessary for persuasion. Explore moral satisficing rather than panic about brainwashing.

**Evidence to seek:** Landes et al. (2026); Krügel et al. (2026).

**Counterevidence:** Bad reasons can undermine persuasion; users retain agency; experimental vignette effects may not generalize to consequential real-world choices.

**Falsification note:** Do not turn measured advice effects into claims of mind control or moral capture.

## Chapter 8 — Better Than the Ethicist

**Job:** Take the strongest affirmative case for AI ethical advice seriously. Use evidence that participants can rate AI advice on par with expert advice and sometimes prefer it.

**Evidence to seek:** Meincke, Nave, and Terwiesch (2026); Myers and Everett; artificial moral advisor proposals.

**Counterevidence:** Usefulness ratings do not establish moral correctness, wisdom, agency, or authority; source disclosure still affects trust.

**Falsification note:** The chapter must not caricature human ethicists or imply a benchmark for advice quality settles moral truth.

## Chapter 9 — The Preference Mirror

**Job:** Examine proposals that an artificial moral advisor should help users act consistently with their own values. Ask what happens when values are unstable, contradictory, adaptive, socially produced, or self-serving.

**Evidence to seek:** Giubilini and Savulescu; Lara and Deckers; value-alignment literature; work on preference learning and pluralism.

**Counterevidence:** Reflective tools can reveal contradictions and help users revise preferences rather than simply freezing them.

**Falsification note:** Do not imply all personalization is manipulation or that a stable universal moral code is the only alternative.

## Chapter 10 — The Tyranny of Consequences

**Job:** Explore why superior prediction creates pressure toward consequential reasoning: if a system can model harms and benefits at scale, refusing the forecast can look irresponsible even when rights, duties, loyalties, or procedural commitments resist aggregation.

**Evidence to seek:** experimental findings that people expect AI moral advisors to be more utilitarian; human-realignment studies of model behavior in dilemmas; philosophical literature on consequentialism and deontology.

**Counterevidence:** AI systems can be instructed or trained to represent non-consequentialist commitments; humans themselves routinely use consequential reasoning.

**Falsification note:** Do not claim AI is inherently utilitarian or that consequence-sensitive reasoning is morally suspect.

## Chapter 11 — The Moral Crumple Zone

**Job:** Show how nominal human responsibility can expand as practical control shrinks, especially when a machine's recommendation becomes the institutional default.

**Evidence to seek:** Madeleine Elish's moral crumple zone; automation and responsibility literature; delegated-agency scholarship.

**Counterevidence:** Well-designed accountability can attach responsibility to organizations, designers, system owners, and operators without pretending the final click carries all agency.

**Falsification note:** Do not use 'moral crumple zone' as a synonym for every human-in-the-loop arrangement.

## Chapter 12 — The Responsibility Leak

**Job:** Follow responsibility as it diffuses through prompts, policies, training data, vendors, model outputs, supervisors, and final decision makers. Explain why an apparently responsible chain can become practically unaccountable.

**Evidence to seek:** distributed-responsibility scholarship; AI risk-management and governance frameworks; delegated-agency analysis.

**Counterevidence:** Complex institutions already allocate responsibility across teams and can build auditable chains without requiring a single morally omniscient person.

**Falsification note:** Complexity alone is not irresponsibility; the question is whether authority, evidence, correction, and consequences are assignable.

## Chapter 13 — The Dissent Tax

**Job:** Define the social and institutional cost of overruling a system believed to outperform the human. Show how documentation burdens, liability fears, performance metrics, and hindsight can convert an override into a professionally risky act.

**Evidence to seek:** human factors, algorithm reliance, organizational decision-support literature, professional accountability examples.

**Counterevidence:** Some institutions deliberately protect justified override and require independent human judgment.

**Falsification note:** Do not invent universal policies or claim every disagreement with automation is punished.

## Chapter 14 — The Private Constitution

**Job:** Explore personalized moral advice as a private normative environment: a system that remembers values, relationships, history, vulnerabilities, and prior commitments can become more intimate than public moral institutions.

**Evidence to seek:** personalization and persuasion research; AI companion literature; artificial moral advisor designs; child-rights and privacy work.

**Counterevidence:** Personalization can increase accessibility, contextual relevance, reflection, and inclusion while preserving common public rules.

**Falsification note:** Do not claim personalization inevitably causes polarization or isolation.

## Chapter 15 — Institutions Without Convictions

**Job:** Examine organizations that outsource moral articulation to systems while preserving human signatures. Ask what it means for an institution to have values if no one inside can defend the reasons except by querying the same model.

**Evidence to seek:** democratic legitimacy, public reason, professional ethics, organizational responsibility, model-governance literature.

**Counterevidence:** Institutions have always depended on distributed expertise, legal counsel, precedent, and external knowledge; internal memorization is not required for genuine institutional commitments.

**Falsification note:** The chapter fails if it equates distributed cognition with moral emptiness.

## Chapter 16 — The Alien Majority

**Job:** Connect moral pluralism to machine aggregation. A model trained or aligned across enormous populations may represent something like a statistical moral center without acquiring the legitimacy of an electorate, jury, legislature, community, or affected public.

**Evidence to seek:** Schuster and Kilov (2025); Baum and Slavkovik (2025); Gabriel on values and alignment; social-choice literature.

**Counterevidence:** Aggregation can be a useful input to legitimate institutions, and broad participation may reduce elite capture.

**Falsification note:** Do not describe training-data frequency or preference aggregation as a literal democratic vote.

## Chapter 17 — Children of the Oracle

**Job:** Treat childhood exposure as a developmental question about where moral vocabulary, conflict, apology, loyalty, courage, and judgment are practiced when a conversational system is always available.

**Evidence to seek:** UNICEF 2026 child-rights work on AI chatbots and companions; education guidance; emerging developmental research.

**Counterevidence:** AI can provide safe rehearsal, language support, tutoring, perspective taking, and access to guidance children may otherwise lack.

**Falsification note:** Do not claim demonstrated long-run developmental harm from evidence that does not establish causation.

## Chapter 18 — The Right to Be Morally Wrong

**Job:** Defend a bounded domain of human moral authorship without celebrating harmful error. Distinguish a right to choose within legitimate freedom from a right to impose preventable harm on others.

**Evidence to seek:** autonomy, liberal pluralism, consent, freedom of conscience, democratic legitimacy.

**Counterevidence:** Society already limits individual choice when actions seriously harm others; superior decision support may justify stronger safeguards in high-stakes domains.

**Falsification note:** The title is deliberately provocative; the argument must never become a blanket defense of recklessness, discrimination, abuse, or rights violations.

## Chapter 19 — Superiority Without Authority

**Job:** State the constructive design principle: grant superior systems epistemic weight proportional to evidence while keeping moral and political authority tied to legitimate sources of standing.

**Evidence to seek:** public-justification and democratic-legitimacy scholarship; appeal and contestability research; inspiration-not-authority approaches to artificial moral advisors.

**Counterevidence:** Some future artificial agents may themselves develop claims to moral standing or authority; the chapter should not preclude that possibility by definition.

**Falsification note:** Do not smuggle species membership in as the sole source of moral authority.

## Chapter 20 — Anchors

**Job:** End with the institutions and practices that remain meaningful even after human cognitive superiority disappears: consent, rights, responsibility, public reason, pluralism, contestability, relationships, mandate, and refusal.

**Evidence to seek:** No new empirical burden; synthesize the prior record and preserve uncertainty about future machine agency.

**Counterevidence:** Some anchors may themselves evolve as new forms of agency and social membership emerge.

**Falsification note:** The conclusion fails if it becomes an anti-AI call to preserve human dominance rather than a case for preserving legitimate moral authorship under cognitive asymmetry.
