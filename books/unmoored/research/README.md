# Research Brief — Unmoored

## Research question

What happens to human moral agency and institutional legitimacy when an artificial system is perceived to reason better than the humans who remain formally responsible for choosing what ought to be done?

The manuscript distinguishes four questions that are often collapsed:

1. **Epistemic performance:** can a system identify facts, consequences, inconsistencies, arguments, or options better than a person?
2. **Moral understanding:** can a system grasp morally relevant reasons rather than merely produce persuasive moral language?
3. **Moral agency:** can a system be the kind of entity to which responsibility, blame, obligation, or virtue appropriately attaches?
4. **Moral or political authority:** even if a system is extraordinarily capable, what gives it standing to settle contested ends for other people?

The book's core thesis concerns the fourth question. It does not require a final answer to the second or third. Human beings can treat a system as authoritative before philosophers agree that it possesses understanding, consciousness, agency, or rights.

## Novel synthesis

There is an established literature on artificial moral advisors, moral testimony, AI alignment, algorithmic authority, automation bias, moral deskilling, machine ethics, moral responsibility, democratic legitimacy, and value pluralism. *Unmoored* does not claim those literatures do not exist.

Its distinct synthesis is the **legitimacy problem created by cognitive asymmetry**. As machine reasoning becomes more capable, a person can have increasingly strong epistemic reasons to take its analysis seriously while having no corresponding reason to surrender the authority to choose ends. The psychological and institutional danger is that the first kind of reason can impersonate the second.

The manuscript calls that substitution **unmooring**. It is a condition in which ethical recommendations become abundant while the sources of moral standing become obscure. The result need not be nihilism. It may look like the opposite: more consistent moral language, more quantified consequences, more explicit principles, more reasons, and less clarity about who is entitled to decide.

## Evidence boundaries

- Current LLMs are not treated as established superintelligences.
- Current experiments on AI moral advice establish patterns of trust, persuasion, aversion, deference, and source effects under particular experimental conditions; they do not establish universal human behavior.
- The manuscript does not claim that intelligence is irrelevant to morality. Better information, forecasting, consistency checking, and perspective taking can improve moral decisions.
- The manuscript does not claim that only humans can possess moral agency in principle. It argues that demonstrated task performance does not by itself settle agency or authority.
- The manuscript does not claim that human moral judgment is reliable merely because it is human. Human beings are biased, self-serving, inconsistent, tribal, and often badly informed.
- The manuscript treats reasonable moral disagreement as persistent rather than assuming that enough intelligence necessarily converges on one uncontested moral answer.
- Legal rights and political legitimacy are kept distinct from philosophical claims about moral authority.

## Research method

The draft draws on:

- experimental work on AI-generated ethical advice and moral persuasion;
- philosophy of moral testimony, moral expertise, autonomy, and understanding;
- work on artificial moral advisors and moral deskilling;
- human-factors research on automation bias and reliance;
- scholarship on moral crumple zones, responsibility gaps, and delegated agency;
- AI value-alignment and moral-pluralism literature;
- democratic-legitimacy and public-justification scholarship;
- child-rights guidance on AI companions and developmental exposure;
- classic philosophical problems used as conceptual tests, including Hume's is/ought distinction and Plato's *Euthyphro*.

The chapter briefs record counterevidence and falsification conditions so later revision can strengthen, narrow, or abandon claims rather than forcing new evidence into the thesis.

## Drafting rule

The prose may use hypothetical future systems to make the asymmetry vivid, but factual claims about current systems, studies, institutions, laws, and historical events should remain traceable to the source ledger. Hypotheticals should be recognizable as hypotheticals rather than narrated as reported events.
