# Moral Testimony

There are facts we are content to borrow.

Ask a stranger what time the train leaves and, under ordinary conditions, you do not feel morally diminished when you believe the answer. Ask a physician whether a scan shows a fracture and you may reasonably accept the diagnosis without learning radiology. Ask an astronomer how far away a star is and nobody insists that authentic knowledge requires you to reproduce the measurement yourself.

Testimony is one of civilization's basic technologies.

Most of what any person knows was learned from someone else.

Moral knowledge feels different.

Imagine a colleague asking whether it was wrong to humiliate an employee in front of a team. You answer yes.

“Why?”

“Because Professor Chen says so.”

The reply is strange even if Professor Chen is the world's leading scholar of workplace ethics.

The strangeness has occupied philosophers of moral testimony for years. Alison Hills and others have asked why a person who relies on moral testimony can seem to lack something that does not trouble us in ordinary factual testimony. The problem is not simply that moral experts are unreliable. Suppose the expert really is excellent. Suppose she is almost always right.

Something can still feel incomplete.

The person knows that the humiliation was wrong in one sense. They possess the correct verdict. Yet they may not understand why it was wrong. They may be unable to recognize the relevant feature in a new case. They may not know what apology is required, why the employee's dignity matters, or how the same principle should change their behavior next week.

The difference is between having an answer and inhabiting the reasons.

That difference becomes important when moral advice moves from human experts into machines.

Artificial systems are unusually good at supplying verdict and explanation together. A model can say that public humiliation was wrong and then give reasons: the power asymmetry, the unnecessary injury to dignity, the chilling effect on other employees, the possibility of correcting performance privately, the manager's obligation not to use status as a weapon.

The explanation can remove much of the old discomfort.

You are no longer believing merely because the machine says so. You have reasons.

But the appearance of reasons creates a new question.

What does it mean to understand a moral reason when the explanation itself is supplied on demand by the same source as the verdict?

Suppose the manager reads the answer, nods, apologizes, and resolves not to repeat the mistake. Has anything been lost because a machine did the moral thinking first?

Probably not much.

If the apology is sincere and the behavior changes, it would be perverse to insist that moral improvement counts only when it originates unaided. People have always learned morality from others. Parents tell children why hitting is wrong. Friends point out selfishness. Novels enlarge sympathy. Religious traditions provide language for obligations. Therapists help people see the effects of their behavior. Political movements make previously ignored harms legible.

Moral growth is social.

The fantasy of a self-created conscience has little historical support.

An artificial adviser can enter that ecology as another source of perspective. It may even improve it. Unlike a friend, it may not be afraid to disagree. Unlike a parent, it may not be protecting family mythology. Unlike a priest, it need not belong to one tradition. Unlike a therapist, it may be available at two in the morning when the argument actually happens.

The strongest case for artificial moral advice begins here.

A system can become a mirror that does more than flatter.

This was part of the attraction in early proposals for an Artificial Moral Advisor. Alberto Giubilini and Julian Savulescu imagined a system that could know a user's own values, preferences, and relevant circumstances better than ordinary human advisers, then help the user make choices more consistent with those commitments. The proposal was not “let the machine invent morality for you.” It was closer to “use superior cognitive support to become the person you already mean to be.”

There is something appealing about that.

Human beings fail our own standards constantly. We make promises when calm and break them under pressure. We say family matters most and answer work email through dinner. We claim to care about the future and make decisions that externalize costs to people not yet born. We defend impartial principles until our own child loses the competition.

A machine that remembers what we said before can expose the gap.

You told me last month that honesty matters even when the truth is costly.

You said you wanted to give your employee direct feedback rather than complain about her to others.

You decided that you would not make major purchases when angry.

You promised your brother you would call before making this decision.

The moral force does not come from the machine's preference. It comes from ours, returned without the selective memory that usually protects us.

This is moral testimony in a new form.

The system is partly witness.

The trouble begins when the witness becomes the owner of the account.

Imagine relying on the system for years. It remembers your commitments more accurately than you do. It has seen your patterns. It can tell the difference between the moral principles you invoke sincerely and the ones that appear mostly when convenient. It has watched which advice you follow, which advice you regret ignoring, and how your relationships change afterward.

Then it tells you that a decision is inconsistent with your values.

You do not see the inconsistency.

It explains.

You still do not quite see it.

The explanation references dozens of prior conversations, patterns in your behavior, and consequences that unfolded over years. The evidence is too large to hold in your head at once.

Do you trust it?

The rational answer may be yes.

That answer can be both rational and dangerous.

The danger is not that trust is irrational. It is that the human relation to the moral reason changes from understanding to dependence.

Moral testimony has always required dependence. A person learns that a joke is cruel because the person targeted by the joke explains an experience the speaker did not have. A traveler learns a local norm from someone who lives there. A student learns about an injustice from historians who have read archives the student will never see.

The goal cannot be to eliminate dependence.

The relevant question is what capacities survive inside it.

One capacity is recognition.

Can the person recognize the morally relevant feature when it appears again?

A child who learns “do not hit because Mom said so” possesses a rule. A child who begins to understand another person's pain possesses something more transferable. The difference does not require philosophical sophistication. It requires some internal connection between action and reason.

A second capacity is contest.

Can the person identify when the advice does not fit?

A rule against lying may make sense until someone asks where a persecuted neighbor is hiding. A norm of loyalty may be admirable until loyalty becomes complicity. A principle of equal treatment may require unequal accommodation when people face different physical barriers.

Moral life is full of exceptions that reveal what the rule was for.

A person who only possesses the verdict cannot locate the exception.

A third capacity is ownership.

Can the person say “I did this” in a morally serious way?

Ownership is not the same as originality. You can adopt a principle from another person and make it yours. People convert traditions into convictions all the time. The important change occurs when the principle becomes available for self-criticism rather than remaining an external command.

The source may have been a teacher.

The responsibility becomes yours.

A fourth capacity is extension.

Can the person reason when the adviser is unavailable or when the new case is unlike the examples already given?

This matters less for some moral decisions than philosophers sometimes imply. Nobody needs to become a general moral theorist before behaving decently at lunch. Habit and social norms do enormous work.

But extension matters in positions of responsibility.

A judge cannot outsource every unfamiliar conflict to precedent without interpreting precedent. A physician cannot treat informed consent as a phrase while failing to recognize coercion in a form nobody anticipated. A manager responsible for employees cannot know only the list of prohibited acts. Institutions rely on people who can encounter novelty.

Superior artificial intelligence could strengthen all four capacities.

It could ask the user to explain before it gives an answer. It could present a counterexample. It could show how the same principle behaves in another case. It could reveal uncertainty. It could offer competing moral interpretations. It could ask what the user would decide if the parties were reversed. It could preserve the reasoning trail rather than simply outputting a verdict.

A machine can be a tutor rather than an oracle.

This is why the design of moral advice matters as much as the quality of the advice.

Imagine two systems with identical underlying intelligence.

The first is optimized for completion. Ask what to do and it gives a recommendation as quickly and confidently as possible. It knows that users reward clarity. It minimizes friction. It remembers enough personal context to tailor the answer. Its commercial success depends partly on making uncertainty less painful.

The second is optimized for reflection in consequential cases. It may still give a recommendation, but it distinguishes factual uncertainty from value conflict. It identifies which of the user's stated commitments bear on the question. It tells the user when its answer depends heavily on one contested premise. It shows a strong alternative. It marks which parts of the reasoning come from external rules and which come from inferred preferences.

The first system may feel better.

The second may leave more conscience behind.

That phrase—leave more conscience behind—should make us nervous. Conscience is not a muscle with a measurable mass. Moral agency cannot be reduced to whether a person struggled long enough before acting. Easy access to good advice can improve lives. Friction is not inherently virtuous.

Still, we know from learning in other domains that the structure of assistance changes what the user learns.

A worked example can teach. A generated answer can substitute. A navigation system can help someone reach a destination while leaving little spatial memory. A calculator can free attention for higher mathematics or prevent a student from learning arithmetic, depending on when and how it is used.

Moral assistance will have its own version of this distinction.

The stakes are unusual because the skill at issue is tied to responsibility.

Suppose a hospital adopts an advanced ethical advisory system. It helps with consent disputes, scarce-resource allocation, end-of-life decisions, conflicts among family members, and questions about experimental treatment. The system is not the legal decision maker. Clinicians remain responsible.

At first the system is used selectively. Difficult cases go to an ethics committee, which uses the model alongside human discussion. Doctors read the analysis and challenge it. The tool is one voice.

Then performance improves.

The model summarizes the medical record better. It identifies relevant policies instantly. It remembers similar cases. It predicts which conflicts are likely to escalate. It provides a nuanced map of competing ethical considerations. Committees increasingly begin with its memo.

Years pass.

A new generation of clinicians arrives after training in the system's presence. They have never worked in a hospital where ethical analysis started from a blank page. They are not stupid. They may be more ethically informed in daily practice than the earlier doctors. They have had access to high-quality analysis in hundreds of cases.

But what have they practiced?

Perhaps they have practiced evaluating arguments.

Perhaps they have practiced reading conclusions.

The difference depends on the workflow.

A hospital can therefore become better at ethical decision support while becoming less capable of producing ethical judgment independently of the support.

Again, that may be acceptable.

Airlines do not preserve every obsolete navigation skill. Laboratories do not require scientists to manufacture every instrument. Institutions advance by embedding knowledge in tools.

A lost capacity matters when the remaining people still carry responsibilities that require it.

If clinicians are expected to override the ethical system in exceptional cases, they need enough moral understanding to recognize an exception. If they are expected to explain a decision to a family, they need reasons they can inhabit rather than recite. If the system fails or encounters a case outside its training, somebody must know what kind of problem is in front of them.

Moral testimony becomes dangerous when the person receiving it retains responsibility while losing the competence that makes responsibility meaningful.

This is close to the moral deskilling problem Shannon Vallor described before the current generation of language models. Technologies can remove opportunities to practice the habits and judgments through which moral skill develops. They can also create new opportunities for moral upskilling. The direction is not determined by the presence of technology.

It depends on what the human still does.

The same is true privately.

A teenager who asks a conversational system how to apologize after hurting a friend may receive advice better than anything available at home. The system can help name the harm, suggest taking responsibility without excuses, and warn against turning the apology into a demand for forgiveness.

That can be a genuine good.

If the teenager copies the apology word for word without understanding it, the good is smaller.

If the teenager reads the advice, thinks about what it means, rewrites it in their own language, and notices something about the friend's experience that had been invisible before, the machine has participated in moral learning.

The distinction is not human versus machine.

It is whether the machine returns the person to the moral world with more capacity to see and act, or makes returning unnecessary.

This becomes harder as the machine improves because superior advice creates a reasonable case for shortcutting the learning process.

Consider an emergency.

A physician faces a life-and-death decision with minutes to act. The system recommends an option with a strong ethical and clinical rationale. This is not the moment to demand that the physician rediscover moral philosophy. Good assistance should compress expertise. The patient should not pay for the doctor's character development.

Moral autonomy cannot require inefficiency at every moment.

The capacity has to be built elsewhere.

That is what professions already do when they work well. A pilot practices failures in a simulator so the aircraft does not become a classroom during an emergency. A surgeon learns anatomy before a rare complication arrives. A judge studies doctrine before facing the case that tests its edge.

If moral reasoning becomes heavily assisted, the same institutional logic may be necessary.

Practice when the stakes are low.

Interrogate the system when time allows.

Preserve cases where humans must generate the analysis before seeing the model's view.

Train disagreement rather than assuming it will appear spontaneously.

The goal is not to prove the human can beat the machine.

The goal is to ensure that the person who remains responsible can still recognize what responsibility is asking of them.

This is where the old philosophical suspicion of moral testimony becomes useful without becoming dogma.

The problem with testimony is not that another mind contributed to the judgment.

The problem is a moral life made entirely of conclusions whose reasons always live elsewhere.

A person can be right for years that way.

They can also become curiously unprepared for the first moment when the adviser is wrong, conflicted, unavailable, captured, or simply not entitled to decide.

Superior intelligence makes this problem more severe precisely because the testimony can be excellent.

We should expect people to rely on it.

Often they should.

The task is therefore architectural rather than heroic. Telling everyone to “think for yourself” will fail, because thinking entirely for oneself is neither possible nor desirable. Telling everyone to “trust the evidence” will also fail when the argument is about which moral premise gives the evidence significance.

The better standard is to ask what kind of relationship to advice a system creates.

Does it preserve the difference between a reason and a verdict?

Can the user see when a recommendation rests on their own stated commitments, on a public rule, on predicted consequences, or on a normative default supplied by the system?

Can disagreement occur without the interface treating it as user error?

Does the user ever have to articulate a reason, or only select among generated ones?

Can the institution explain a decision without asking the system to explain itself again?

Those are not questions about whether AI should give moral advice.

The advice has already arrived.

They are questions about what sort of moral person remains after the advice becomes good enough to use every day.

A society can borrow answers and become wiser.

It can also borrow so many answers that nobody remembers the difference between believing the right thing and knowing why it has a claim on them.

The superior intelligence will not need to steal our conscience.

We may place it there for safekeeping.
