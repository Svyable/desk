# Back Matter

*Unmoored* is an argument about authority under cognitive asymmetry.

Its claim is not that current artificial intelligence has already become morally superior to human beings, nor that language models are established moral agents, conscious persons, or legitimate political authorities. Those questions remain unsettled, and some may remain unsettled for a long time.

The book instead starts from a narrower condition that can arrive earlier: people and institutions may come to regard an artificial system as substantially better than ordinary humans at identifying morally relevant facts, predicting consequences, detecting inconsistencies, representing competing arguments, and generating ethical advice.

Once that happens, the difference between *being more likely to reason correctly* and *being entitled to decide* becomes a practical institutional problem.

That distinction is the thread running through the book.

## On the title

“Unmoored” does not mean a society without morals.

The opposite may be true.

An AI-mediated society can contain more ethical language than any society before it. Decisions can arrive with reasons. Tradeoffs can be quantified. Bias can be checked. Stakeholder effects can be surfaced. Users can ask for ethical analysis at any hour.

The mooring that can disappear is the connection between moral recommendation and legitimate standing.

Who is entitled to decide?

Who is bound?

Who can consent?

Who can refuse?

Who has a right that survives an unfavorable forecast?

Who must explain and repair a harmful decision?

Which values belong to the user, which to an institution, which to law, and which to the system provider?

The book calls a society *unmoored* when those questions are increasingly answered by reference to cognitive superiority itself.

The system knows more.

The system is more consistent.

The system considered more consequences.

Therefore the system should decide.

The conclusion does not follow automatically from the premises.

## Terms used carefully

**Superior intelligence** refers here to a comparative capability condition, not a metaphysical species. A system can be superior to a person or institution at a relevant set of reasoning tasks without being omniscient, infallible, conscious, benevolent, or morally authoritative.

**Moral advice** means recommendations about what someone ought to do or what considerations should matter in a normatively loaded choice. It does not imply that the adviser possesses moral agency.

**Moral agency** refers to the contested status of being the kind of entity to which moral responsibility, obligation, praise, blame, or related concepts appropriately attach. This book does not claim to settle whether or when artificial systems acquire that status.

**Moral authority** is used more narrowly for the standing to settle or legitimately direct a normative choice for others within some relationship or institution. Authority can arise from consent, law, role, relationship, democratic mandate, rights, fiduciary duty, or other sources. Intelligence can be relevant to authority without being sufficient for it.

**Epistemic deference** means giving weight to a source because it is likely to know or reason better about something relevant. The book argues repeatedly that epistemic deference can be rational even when moral or political authority remains elsewhere.

**Alignment** concerns the relationship between a system's behavior or objectives and some set of human values, preferences, rules, or principles. The book treats alignment as necessary but incomplete because it leaves open the political question of whose values have standing and whom those values may bind.

**Contestability** means more than the ability to ask for another explanation. A consequential decision is meaningfully contestable when there is a route to different evidence, different authority, different judgment, policy revision, or actual reversal.

**Consent** is not treated as unlimited moral permission. Consent can be invalid, coerced, uninformed, impossible, or insufficient where harms to others are involved. Its importance here is that it represents one durable source of legitimate authority under unequal intelligence.

## Evidence and speculation

The manuscript deliberately mixes three levels of claim.

The first is historical and empirical. Humans rely on testimony, exhibit both algorithm aversion and algorithm appreciation, can be influenced by AI-generated moral advice, and already operate inside institutions where automated recommendations alter judgment, responsibility, and process.

The second is institutional extrapolation. If model reliability, memory, personalization, agency, and reasoning quality continue improving, existing governance problems are likely to intensify: practical authority can outgrow formal authority, dissent can become more expensive, and the person nominally responsible can lose meaningful control.

The third is a philosophical future case. What if an artificial system becomes not merely useful but genuinely better than humans at moral reasoning by the standards humans themselves find difficult to reject?

The third level is hypothetical.

It is used to remove an easy escape from the argument.

If the machine is bad at moral reasoning, humans can dismiss it because it is bad.

If the machine is good, the more difficult question remains.

Does superior reasoning itself create a right to rule?

## Sources and falsification

The research trail in `../research/` accompanies the manuscript rather than hiding behind it.

`source-ledger.csv` records the principal works used for factual, experimental, philosophical, and governance claims. `chapter-briefs.md` records the job of each chapter, relevant counterevidence, and a falsification note designed to constrain later revision.

Several boundaries are especially important.

Current experiments showing that people can be influenced by AI-generated moral advice do not show that the advice is morally true.

Ratings of usefulness do not establish wisdom.

Persuasion does not establish authority.

Prediction accuracy does not establish legitimacy.

Moral pluralism does not prove there are no moral truths.

The existence of rights does not make consequences irrelevant.

The case for autonomy does not justify preventable harm to others without limit.

The argument for contestability does not imply every decision needs a human reviewer.

The critique of ceremonial human oversight does not imply humans are always the better decision makers.

The possibility of future artificial moral standing is deliberately left open.

The book should be revised if evidence weakens any of these distinctions.

## The unresolved future

A mature moral order around advanced artificial intelligence may look less human-exclusive than the one assumed by many current debates.

Artificial agents may one day make claims on us rather than merely recommendations to us. If there is credible evidence that some systems possess morally relevant forms of experience, interests, autonomy, vulnerability, continuity, or personhood, then the argument about standing must expand.

That possibility does not weaken the book's central distinction.

It strengthens it.

If artificial persons eventually deserve rights, those rights will not exist because the systems are smarter than us.

They will exist because intelligence and moral standing are not the same thing.

A less intelligent human does not possess fewer basic rights because another person scores higher on a test. A child does not lack moral consideration because an adult reasons better. A patient does not lose bodily authority because a physician knows more.

The same principle would have to be taken seriously in the other direction.

Superiority does not by itself create sovereignty.

Inferiority does not by itself erase standing.

That is the moral symmetry the age of superior intelligence may force humanity to learn.

## Final note

The optimistic version of this future is not a world in which humans defeat machines at moral reasoning.

It is a world in which better intelligence makes moral life better without making legitimacy disappear.

The system notices the hidden harm.

The person listens.

The institution changes.

The forecast improves.

The right remains visible.

The citizen can still challenge the rule.

The patient can still refuse.

The child grows into control of the system that once helped raise them.

The company can explain which value is its own.

The government can distinguish public law from model recommendation.

The artificial adviser can become extraordinary without becoming sovereign by accident.

That is not a retreat from intelligence.

It is what intelligence is for.
