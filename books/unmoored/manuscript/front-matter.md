# Unmoored

*The Dissolution of Morals in an Age of Superior Intelligence*

Sven Hardy Benson

There is an old comfort hidden inside almost every argument about artificial intelligence.

We may build something stronger than us, faster than us, more knowledgeable than us, perhaps eventually better at nearly every cognitive task we know how to measure. But morality, we tell ourselves, will remain ours.

The machine may calculate. We will decide what the calculation is for.

The machine may predict. We will choose which future is worth wanting.

The machine may advise. We will remain the ones who say yes or no.

This book is about what happens when that division stops feeling stable.

Not because a machine seizes power. Not because it develops a secret ideology. Not because it becomes conscious, declares itself sovereign, or persuades a frightened population to worship it.

Those are dramatic stories. The more difficult possibility is quieter.

The machine gives the better answer.

It notices the person you forgot. It remembers the precedent you misremembered. It recognizes that the policy helping one group will impose a hidden cost on another. It can compare outcomes across years rather than hours. It detects that the moral principle you invoked last Tuesday points in the opposite direction today. It can reconstruct the strongest argument against your position before you have finished stating your own. It knows more history, more law, more medicine, more economics, more psychology, more local detail, more statistics, more consequences, more exceptions.

And then it asks a question you cannot answer comfortably:

Why are you still disagreeing?

The question need not be spoken. It can live inside the room.

A physician reviews the system's recommendation and knows the system has seen more cases than any physician could see in a lifetime. A judge reads an analysis that has traced every relevant precedent and modeled the likely effects of competing rulings. A parent asks an assistant how to handle a child's lie and receives a response that is patient, informed by developmental research, attentive to the family history, and more emotionally balanced than the parent feels at the end of a long day. A cabinet confronts a decision involving thousands of uncertain consequences and receives an analysis no human staff could reproduce in time.

The system may still be wrong.

That is not the point.

Humans are wrong too. We are often wrong for worse reasons. We are tired, tribal, vain, hurried, inattentive, afraid, ambitious, vindictive, sentimental, loyal to bad precedents, captured by incentives, and extraordinarily talented at discovering principles that happen to ratify what we already wanted.

If artificial intelligence becomes genuinely better at moral reasoning—or merely becomes good enough that people reasonably expect it to be better—then the oldest defense of human authority becomes unavailable.

We will not be able to say: we decide because we know best.

Perhaps we will not know best.

That is where *Unmoored* begins.

Most discussion of AI ethics asks how human values can be put into machines. The language is alignment. We have preferences, principles, laws, rights, constitutions, norms, traditions, moral theories, professional duties, religious commitments, and political institutions. The machine is the powerful new thing. The job is to constrain it, teach it, supervise it, or otherwise make its behavior acceptable to us.

That problem is real.

It is not the only problem.

The opposite movement matters too. Machines do not merely receive values. People receive reasons from machines. We ask them what is fair, whether an action is selfish, how to apologize, whether to report a colleague, how much risk is acceptable, which patient should receive scarce attention, whether a rule should bend, whether a relationship should end, whether a war can be justified, whether a child should be punished, whether a company owes something it is not legally required to give.

Today these systems are uneven. They can be wise in one paragraph and shallow in the next. They inherit bias, reproduce fashionable consensus, misunderstand context, overstate certainty, and sometimes generate nonsense with an unnerving command of tone. No responsible argument about the future should pretend otherwise.

But the relevant trend is not that people are already receiving infallible machine ethics. The relevant trend is that moral advice has entered the interface.

In 2026, experimental work began to make the point harder to dismiss. Researchers reported that people could be persuaded by moral advice from large language models even when they trusted human advisers more. Other work found that participants rated AI-generated ethical advice on par with advice from a prominent human ethicist and, in direct comparisons, sometimes preferred the AI answer. Another experiment found that a chatbot's recommendation could shift moral judgment even when high-quality justification was not what produced the effect.

None of those studies proves that the machines were morally correct. A person's changed judgment is not a moral fact. Preference is not truth. Persuasion is not legitimacy.

That distinction is the subject of this book.

A machine can deserve epistemic deference without possessing moral authority.

The sentence sounds simple. Living inside it will not be.

If the system predicts consequences better, fairness may require taking those predictions seriously. If it sees inconsistency in our principles, integrity may require listening. If it exposes a self-serving rationalization, humility may require changing course. If it knows that a policy expected to save ten thousand lives will actually cost more, ignoring it in the name of human dignity would be grotesque.

Superior intelligence should matter.

The mistake is to let the kind of mattering change unnoticed.

Knowing more does not automatically create the right to command. Being harder to refute does not establish the right to rule. A perfect forecast of the consequences of a war would still not, by itself, decide which sacrifices one people may demand of another. A complete model of a child's likely future would not settle every question about the child's freedom. An intelligence that can prove that a lie will produce better aggregate outcomes has not thereby erased the relationships, promises, duties, rights, or identities that make the lie morally intelligible in the first place.

This is not an argument that consequences are unimportant. It is an argument against converting competence into jurisdiction.

We already understand the distinction in other domains. A doctor can know more than the patient about a disease without acquiring ownership of the patient's body. An engineer can know more than citizens about bridge design without acquiring the right to decide where every road should lead. An economist can model a tax more accurately than voters without thereby becoming the legislature. A lawyer can explain the law without becoming the sovereign.

Expertise creates reasons to listen.

Authority requires something else.

Sometimes it comes from consent. Sometimes from law. Sometimes from a role voluntarily accepted. Sometimes from democratic authorization, fiduciary duty, parenthood, professional obligation, treaty, property, membership, relationship, or a right possessed simply because one is a person rather than because one has won an argument.

Those sources of standing are messy. They conflict. They can be abused. They have ugly histories. They do not produce clean answers to every case.

They are also what prevent intelligence from becoming the only currency that counts.

A civilization that forgets this may not become immoral in the cinematic sense. It may become saturated with ethics.

Every decision can arrive with a moral explanation. Every policy can be scored against declared values. Every private conflict can be accompanied by a balanced list of obligations. Every institution can maintain an ethical assistant. Every child can have a patient counselor in a pocket. Every executive can request a stakeholder analysis. Every government can model distributive effects. Every citizen can ask whether a choice is defensible.

And yet the civilization can become less certain about why any human refusal should survive a superior answer.

That is the dissolution in the subtitle.

It is not the disappearance of morals. It is the loosening of the things morals are tied to.

A moral rule used to be anchored in some combination of faith, tradition, kinship, law, philosophy, civic identity, professional role, lived experience, public argument, and the friction of having to defend a choice to another person who could answer back. Modern societies have been loosening and rearranging those anchors for centuries. Artificial intelligence does not begin that history.

It changes the pressure.

For the first time, we are plausibly building systems that may become better than most individuals not only at supplying information but at producing the reasons through which choices are justified. If that happens, human moral authority cannot rest on cognitive supremacy because cognitive supremacy will belong elsewhere.

We will need a better account of why anyone gets to choose.

The task is not to save humanity's position at the top of an intelligence hierarchy. There may be no principled reason to preserve that ranking, and no practical way to do it.

The task is to prevent the hierarchy from becoming a moral constitution by accident.

That requires taking several uncomfortable possibilities seriously at once.

The machine may be right.

The human may be wrong.

The human may still retain the right to decide.

The machine may one day acquire forms of moral standing of its own.

A society may legitimately constrain both.

No single moral theory will dissolve the conflict because the conflict includes the question of who is entitled to impose the theory.

The chapters that follow move through that territory from the smallest scale to the largest. They begin with a person asking for advice and end with institutions that may no longer be able to explain their values without consulting the systems that operationalize them. Along the way are moral testimony, expertise, prediction, consequences, responsibility, dissent, personalization, childhood, pluralism, democracy, and the peculiar right to make a choice that an intelligence can explain is worse.

That right has limits. So does every right.

The interesting question is where the limits come from once 'because humans know best' is no longer available.

We are used to asking whether the machine can be aligned with us.

The harder century may ask whether we can remain morally anchored when the machine gives reasons better than ours.
