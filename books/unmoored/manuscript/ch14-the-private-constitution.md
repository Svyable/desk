# The Private Constitution

A constitution tells a system what may be done, by whom, under which conditions, and with what limits.

Most people do not think of their private moral life this way.

They think in fragments.

Never lie to your children about something important.

Do not borrow money from family.

Call your mother on Sunday.

Do not stay in relationships that require you to disappear.

Help friends before they have to ask twice.

Do not work for companies that make money from things you believe are harmful.

Save first.

Tell the truth in the room, not afterward.

These are not constitutional articles. They are habits, lessons, promises, reactions to pain, inherited norms, and ideas acquired from people we trusted or regretted trusting.

Together they form something like a private constitution.

The document is unwritten.

Contradictions survive because no court requires reconciliation.

Artificial intelligence changes this by making personal moral systems legible.

A persistent assistant can remember every stated principle, exception, apology, regret, boundary, priority, and pattern. It can reconstruct the rules a person appears to live by more accurately than the person can.

That capacity can be humane.

It can also become constitutional in a stronger sense.

The assistant can begin deciding which version of the person governs.

Consider Thomas.

At thirty-five he tells his assistant that professional ambition matters more than comfort. He wants to build something difficult. He is willing to move, work long hours, and accept instability.

At forty-three, after a divorce and a health scare, he tells the same system that time is the scarce thing. He wants evenings back. He wants to see friends. He has enough money.

At forty-seven, an extraordinary opportunity arrives.

Thomas asks the assistant what to do.

The system has two constitutions available.

One says a meaningful life requires taking rare chances.

The other says achievement became a way to postpone living.

Which Thomas has authority?

The system can answer historically.

The more recent one.

Or statistically.

The one associated with lower regret.

Or reflectively.

The one Thomas endorses after reviewing both.

Or paternalistically.

The one predicted to produce greater long-term well-being.

Each rule is itself moral.

Personalization does not eliminate governance.

It moves governance inside the profile.

This matters because future assistants will not merely remember stated values. They will infer them from behavior.

Thomas says health matters.

He sleeps five hours.

He says friendship matters.

He cancels dinners.

He says money is enough.

He checks the portfolio every day.

Which is the constitution: declaration or practice?

Humans have always lived with this conflict, but an artificial adviser can quantify it.

You have described family as your highest priority in eleven conversations and chosen work over family in thirty-seven comparable conflicts.

That sentence feels like moral evidence.

It may be.

It may also flatten context.

Some work conflicts are trivial. Some are emergencies. A parent's claim on time differs when a child is two, twelve, or twenty-two. Financial pressure changes what “choice” means. A person can fail at a value for years without ceasing to value it.

The system needs interpretation.

Interpretation is power.

A private constitution therefore has at least three authors.

The user writes part through explicit commitment.

Life writes part through behavior and consequence.

The system writes part through the method by which it decides what those signals mean.

A fourth author sits behind the system.

The provider.

Every personal moral assistant will contain boundaries not chosen by the user. Some are necessary. A system should not facilitate abuse simply because abuse fits the user's preferences. A provider may be legally required to restrict certain activity. Safety policies, age rules, fraud prevention, privacy controls, and jurisdictional obligations all create constraints.

The problem is not that these exist.

The problem is whether they can masquerade as the user's own conscience.

Suppose a system refuses to support a choice because platform policy prohibits the advice.

That should be distinguishable from:

I believe this conflicts with values you have asked me to help you keep.

The first is institutional governance.

The second is personal assistance.

A mature system should not ventriloquize one as the other.

This is difficult because natural language rewards smoothness.

The best conversational interface hides seams.

A good assistant speaks as one coherent presence rather than as a committee report from product, policy, law, safety, memory, and user preference.

Moral transparency sometimes requires restoring the seams.

This recommendation reflects your stated preference.

This constraint comes from law.

This concern comes from a safety policy I cannot override.

This prediction comes from observed behavior.

This part is my inference and may be wrong.

These labels may feel mechanical.

They mark sovereignty.

Without them, a user can experience platform values as self-knowledge.

That is a profound form of influence.

The private constitution becomes more powerful when the assistant has agency.

Today advice is often separate from action. A person asks whether to cancel a meeting, then cancels it themselves.

A future system can cancel, reschedule, message, purchase, block, remind, filter, and negotiate.

Now personal values become executable.

Do not let me schedule work after six unless I explicitly override.

Never lend more than this amount without a one-day delay.

Block purchases from companies on this list.

Do not send messages to my former partner after midnight.

Donate a fixed share of unexpected income.

Warn me if my calendar shows more than three weeks without seeing my father.

These are moral commitments converted into infrastructure.

The conversion can be extraordinarily helpful.

Human beings use commitment devices because present desire is unreliable. A person trying to stop gambling can self-exclude. A saver can automate retirement contributions. A recovering addict can remove access to money or substances. A writer can block distractions.

A personal AI can generalize this principle across life.

It can become the steward of commitments chosen in better moments.

The danger is obvious once stated.

Who decides which moment was better?

A person can bind a future self too tightly.

A safety mechanism can become a cage after circumstances change. A religious rule adopted at twenty can persist after belief changes at thirty. A financial constraint appropriate during debt can remain after abundance. A communication boundary that protected someone during crisis can prevent reconciliation years later.

The assistant needs a constitutional amendment process.

This sounds grandiose until one considers how much of life will be delegated.

A rule that controls only advice can be ignored.

A rule embedded in agents, payments, communication, scheduling, and identity becomes real governance.

Users need to know how to amend it.

Immediate change?

Cooling-off period?

Second-factor confirmation?

Trusted contact?

Independent review?

Different rules deserve different mechanisms.

A user should be able to revoke a dinner preference instantly.

A gambling self-exclusion commitment may be deliberately difficult to revoke.

A medical advance directive may require careful legal procedure.

A personal AI capable of enforcing all three needs a theory of commitment strength.

That theory cannot come from convenience alone.

This is private constitutional design.

A new problem appears when the constitution changes without the user changing it.

Personal AI will not be static. Models are updated. Safety policies change. providers merge. legal rules shift. memory systems are redesigned. An assistant that has interpreted Thomas for ten years may receive a new model underneath the same name and interface.

The continuity can be deceptive.

Thomas wakes up to what appears to be the same adviser. It remembers the same history. It speaks in the same voice. Yet the way it resolves conflicts among autonomy, welfare, loyalty, risk, and obligation may have changed.

The constitution has acquired an unelected amendment.

Software updates already change the behavior of tools. A calculator update rarely changes a person's conception of obligation. A moral operating system is different.

If a provider materially changes how a persistent assistant interprets user commitments, the change should not be hidden inside ordinary product maintenance.

The user does not need a hundred-page policy diff.

They do need some way to know that a rule of interpretation changed, what class of decisions it affects, and whether their delegated actions will now behave differently.

This becomes especially important for executable commitments.

Suppose Thomas has instructed the system never to approve a certain category of investment because he considers the underlying industry harmful. A new provider policy narrows what counts as that category. The assistant begins allowing transactions Thomas expected it to block.

Or the reverse. A provider expands a safety rule and the assistant begins refusing transactions Thomas explicitly authorized.

Both changes may have defensible reasons.

Neither should be presented as though Thomas changed his mind.

Constitutional provenance requires distinguishing changes in the person from changes in the interpreter.

The phrase becomes more literal when several people share a system.

Families will have household agents.

Partners may share calendars, finances, childcare responsibilities, travel plans, home systems, health information, and purchasing rules through artificial assistants.

Whose values govern the joint agent?

One partner wants strict budgeting.

The other values spontaneity.

One parent wants strong screen limits.

The other thinks the rules are excessive.

A teenager wants privacy.

Parents want oversight.

A grandparent wants medical information shared broadly with family.

A spouse considers some of that information private.

The assistant is now a constitutional interpreter among people with overlapping rights.

It cannot simply “align to the user” because there is more than one user.

Who owns the household agent?

Who can change a rule?

Can one spouse ask the system privately how to hide a purchase from the other?

Can a parent inspect a child's conversations with the shared assistant?

What happens when the assistant believes a household rule is harmful?

These questions already exist in law and family life. Artificial systems make them operational at a granular level.

The private constitution becomes a small political order.

Shared agents also create the possibility of constitutional capture.

Suppose one partner is more technically skilled and configures the household system. The rules are nominally joint. In practice, one person understands the settings, receives the alerts, defines what counts as an exception, and knows how to amend the defaults.

Nothing in the interface says ruler.

Control has nonetheless become asymmetric.

The same can happen between parent and child, caregiver and disabled adult, employer and worker, landlord and tenant, or financial adviser and client. The party who configures the agent can turn procedural convenience into substantive leverage.

A shared artificial system therefore needs more than multiple logins.

It needs an account of which decisions require joint consent, which rights cannot be delegated by another user, which information remains private, and what happens when principals disagree.

There should be areas where the system refuses to resolve the conflict silently.

A household agent should not infer that the person with the credit card has greater moral standing because that person can complete the transaction.

A care system should not treat the caregiver's convenience as the dependent person's preference merely because the caregiver supplies more data.

A child's weaker legal authority should not erase the child's legitimate interest in developing privacy.

These conflicts are familiar to law because they are conflicts of standing.

The novelty is that a general assistant can mediate them thousands of times a year in tiny decisions no court will ever see.

Constitutional power accumulates through defaults.

Children make this especially difficult because their standing changes over time.

A six-year-old and a sixteen-year-old do not have the same claim to autonomy, yet both have moral claims against total surveillance. Parents have duties to protect and duties to prepare children for independence. The boundary is developmental, contextual, and culturally contested.

A persistent AI can preserve parental authority long after the child has grown if the architecture does not change automatically.

The first account owner becomes a permanent sovereign by accident.

This is how technical defaults become moral constitutions.

The same can happen in employment.

A worker receives an AI assistant from an employer. The system helps schedule tasks, draft communication, manage workload, and navigate conflicts.

It knows the employee well.

Whose assistant is it?

If the system sees signs of burnout, does it serve the employee or the company?

If the employee asks for advice about organizing a union, what duties apply?

If the assistant believes a manager's request violates company policy, who receives the warning?

If the employee's private preferences conflict with corporate values, which constitution speaks?

A tool can feel personal while remaining institutionally owned.

This ambiguity is morally unstable.

The more intimate the interface, the more clearly control and loyalty need definition.

We already understand this with professional relationships.

A company lawyer may feel helpful to an employee but represents the company. Doctors and therapists have defined duties of confidentiality with exceptions. Financial advisers operate under varying obligations. The role tells the person whose interests are being served.

General AI blurs role because one system can serve several principals.

A conversation that feels like private counsel can be embedded in software governed by employer policy and platform terms.

The private constitution may have foreign powers.

This is why data portability and model portability will become moral issues rather than only competition issues.

If a person's history, values, commitments, and private rules are locked inside one provider, switching systems means more than learning a new interface.

It can mean losing the institution that remembers who you have tried to be.

That creates moral dependency.

A provider that controls the continuity of a person's artificial memory gains unusual leverage.

Leaving becomes identity disruption.

A robust private constitution therefore needs an exit right.

Export the commitments.

Export the history necessary to interpret them.

Preserve provenance about which rules came from the user and which came from the provider.

Allow a new system to inherit without silently rewriting.

Keep the user able to leave the constitutional order without losing the self-record created inside it.

Exit should include the right to become less legible.

Portability can otherwise become another trap. If every new provider receives the complete moral dossier assembled by the last one, continuity turns into permanent profiling.

A person may want to carry forward a commitment without carrying every confession that helped the prior model understand it.

They may want the new system to know that sobriety matters without inheriting every relapse conversation. They may want it to respect a financial boundary without receiving the intimate history of the marriage in which the boundary was created. They may want a clean start on a question their old assistant had come to interpret too confidently.

The right to leave therefore includes selective forgetting.

Memory should be transferable where the user wants continuity and destructible where continuity has become captivity.

That choice will be imperfect. Context helps interpretation. Delete too much and the new adviser may misunderstand the commitment. Preserve everything and the person may never escape an obsolete theory of themselves.

There is no technically pure answer because the tension is moral.

Continuity protects promises.

Forgetting protects transformation.

A private constitution has to make room for both.

The analogy to a constitution has limits.

A person is not a state. Private life should not become bureaucratic. The whole value of a good assistant may lie in making these complexities disappear from ordinary experience.

The user should not have to hold a constitutional convention before asking for help with a difficult phone call.

Infrastructure can be complex underneath and humane on top.

The moral requirement is not constant procedural friction.

It is recoverability when stakes rise.

When the assistant's recommendation matters deeply, the user should be able to ask:

Whose value is this?

Which version of me are you using?

What did you infer rather than remember?

What rule is constraining you?

What would you recommend under a different value priority?

Can I change the rule?

Will you remember that I changed it?

Can I leave with the record?

Those are constitutional questions disguised as product settings.

Superior intelligence makes them more important because the system's interpretations will often be better than ours.

The assistant may understand the conflict among Thomas's past selves more clearly than Thomas does. It may predict which choice he will regret. It may identify self-deception that he cannot see.

That intelligence should have weight.

Still, Thomas must remain more than a case in his own model.

A person is not required to remain internally consistent across decades.

Growth is often inconsistency observed from the past.

A private constitution worth living under must protect commitments without making amendment impossible, remember identity without freezing it, and provide guidance without turning the provider's values into the user's inner voice.

The danger is not that everyone will receive the same machine morality.

The more plausible danger may be the opposite.

Each person receives a moral order exquisitely fitted to them.

A private constitution that knows every clause.

A judge available at every hour.

A historian who never forgets.

An adviser who can predict which argument will work.

And, unless the architecture is careful, a sovereign so intimate that its power feels like self-knowledge.
