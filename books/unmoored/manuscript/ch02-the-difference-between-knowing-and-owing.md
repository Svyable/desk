# The Difference Between Knowing and Owing

David Hume noticed a small grammatical maneuver that has caused large trouble ever since.

A writer describes the world. People behave this way. God has this property. Society has these customs. Human beings seek this kind of pleasure and avoid that kind of pain. The sentences proceed in the language of what is.

Then, without much ceremony, the grammar changes.

Someone ought to do something.

The transition can be reasonable. It can also hide the most important premise in the argument.

More than two and a half centuries later, artificial intelligence makes the transition newly practical because machines are becoming very good at the first side of it.

They can know more.

That does not mean knowledge is morally empty. The point is nearly the opposite. Moral decisions are full of empirical claims, and bad facts produce bad ethics with impressive efficiency.

A government cannot sensibly decide how much to spend preventing a disease without knowing something about incidence, transmission, treatment, side effects, cost, behavior, and uncertainty. A judge cannot evaluate proportionality without understanding what a sanction does. A parent cannot decide whether a teenager is being reckless without some grasp of the actual risk. A company cannot claim to care about worker safety while refusing to measure injuries.

Facts narrow the moral field.

Sometimes they collapse it.

A person may oppose a medicine because they believe it causes infertility. If the belief is false, the moral objection built on that belief changes. A legislature may defend a restriction as necessary to prevent a harm that does not occur. A commander may believe bombing a target will end a war quickly; if the prediction is wrong, the justification can fail with it. A donor may choose one intervention over another because they think it saves more lives. Better evidence can reverse the choice without requiring any change in the donor's values.

This is one reason a superior intelligence would have moral power even before anyone gave it authority.

It would change the facts inside our reasons.

Imagine a hospital in which a system can predict the likely course of an illness more accurately than any individual physician. Its estimates are calibrated across millions of cases and updated as new data arrives. It knows which treatments usually work, which interactions are dangerous, and how outcomes differ among patients who look similar in the first hour.

The hospital faces two patients and one scarce treatment.

At first the dispute appears ethical. One patient is younger. The other has children. One arrived first. The other has a higher predicted chance of recovery. One has already received more medical resources. The other belongs to a community historically underserved by the hospital.

Then the model changes a key prediction. The treatment is far less likely to benefit one patient than the clinicians believed.

The moral argument changes immediately.

No one has changed theories. No one discovered a new right. No legislature amended a law. Better knowledge altered the structure of the choice.

This is why a slogan such as “machines can tell us what is, humans decide what ought to be” is too clean.

The is enters the ought everywhere.

The more accurate the system becomes, the more our moral decisions may become dependent on its descriptions of reality. A person who rejects those descriptions merely to preserve human primacy is not protecting ethics. They are making ethics blind.

But dependence on knowledge is different from dependence on judgment.

Return to the hospital.

Suppose the system can do more than predict clinical outcomes. It can model years of expected life, family consequences, future resource use, suffering, disability, return to work, likelihood of adherence, distributional effects, and the opportunity cost imposed on other patients. It can calculate thousands of plausible futures under each allocation.

It recommends Patient B.

Why?

The answer is where facts turn back into values.

Perhaps B has a larger expected clinical benefit. That sounds empirical until one asks why expected benefit should control allocation. Perhaps B will gain more years of life. Then years have been chosen as morally relevant. Perhaps the model adjusts for quality of life. Then somebody must decide what counts as quality and how different states compare. Perhaps it gives priority to the worse-off. Now a distributive principle has entered. Perhaps it avoids using disability in ways that would disadvantage disabled patients. That reflects another moral commitment. Perhaps it follows a law requiring first-come, first-served treatment in certain ties. Procedure has entered alongside outcome.

The system can calculate after the weights are chosen.

A sufficiently advanced system can also reason about which weights should be chosen.

That is the harder case.

It can tell us that one principle produces fewer arbitrary distinctions. It can expose contradictions in our policies. It can show that a priority rule we describe as compassionate reliably harms the people it is meant to help. It can demonstrate that two rights we claim to treat equally cannot both be absolute. It can derive implications we had not noticed.

Still, reasoning begins somewhere.

An argument that one value should outrank another cannot be generated from descriptive facts alone unless the relevant normative bridge is already present in the premises, the objective, the training, the evaluator, the legal environment, or the system's learned conception of a good answer.

The bridge may be hidden.

That does not make it absent.

This is one reason the old philosophical distinction becomes technologically urgent. In ordinary human argument, values often reveal themselves through disagreement. Two people fight. One says a policy is unfair. The other says it is necessary. Their words expose that they are assigning different weight to liberty, equality, loyalty, desert, welfare, procedure, risk, or responsibility.

A superior system can make disagreement look like analysis.

The output may arrive with tables, confidence intervals, counterfactuals, citations, legal analogies, moral theories, and a polished explanation of tradeoffs. The quantity of reasoning can obscure the moment at which a value entered.

The more sophisticated the analysis, the easier it may be to mistake a premise for a result.

Consider a simpler example.

A company wants to decide whether to close a factory that loses money. The system can forecast what happens under each option. Keep the factory open and the company likely sacrifices investment elsewhere. Close it and the town loses jobs, local businesses suffer, workers face relocation, pension obligations change, and shareholders receive a better return. The system can estimate all of this better than the executives.

It can even model intangibles. Community trust. Employee morale. Political backlash. Reputation. The value of preserving manufacturing capacity. The risk that a future supply disruption makes the factory unexpectedly strategic.

Suppose the model says closure is the ethically preferable choice.

The sentence sounds as though a calculation has completed.

But what objective did the calculation serve?

Maximize total welfare? Protect the firm's fiduciary obligations? Give special weight to people made vulnerable by decisions they did not cause? Preserve promises implicit in decades of employment? Prefer reversible decisions? Avoid concentrated harms even when dispersed benefits are larger? Respect property rights? Protect the community that subsidized the plant? Treat workers as stakeholders with claims beyond contract?

The machine can reason about all of these.

The fact that it can reason about them does not make the selection among them factual in the same sense as the factory's energy bill.

A common response is that sufficiently advanced intelligence will discover the correct morality.

Perhaps.

That possibility should not be mocked. Moral realism—the idea, stated broadly, that at least some moral claims are true independently of our preferences—has serious defenders. If there are moral truths, an intelligence vastly more capable than ours may be better at finding them. Human beings routinely accept that better reasoning can produce better moral understanding. We no longer regard slavery as a mere preference difference among eras. We think some societies were wrong. We think reasons can improve.

If a machine discovers moral truths we have missed, refusing them because silicon was involved would be an odd prejudice.

The difficulty is that superior intelligence does not settle the metaethical question for us merely by existing.

We still need some account of what makes its conclusion true.

If the system says an act is wrong because it causes unnecessary suffering, then the moral work is being done partly by the claim that unnecessary suffering counts against an act. If it says the act violates a person's autonomy, then autonomy has moral standing. If it says a universal rule permitting the act would be self-defeating, a different structure appears. If it says a good person would not act this way, another conception enters.

The system may defend the premise brilliantly.

The brilliance does not erase the premise.

This matters politically even if moral realism is true.

A government does not gain legitimate authority over citizens merely because its philosophy department has discovered the correct answer. Political authority involves a separate question about who may coerce whom, through what procedures, under which rights, with what opportunities for contest, and on what terms of membership.

An omniscient adviser can tell a legislature that a particular tax is morally ideal. The legislature may still need to enact it lawfully.

Why preserve the procedure if the outcome is known to be better?

Because institutions do more than search for correct answers.

They assign standing.

A trial gives parties a chance to be heard. An election gives citizens a role in choosing governors. A contract creates obligations through consent. A medical consent process protects a patient's authority over their body. A public comment period can recognize people not merely as data points whose welfare matters but as participants entitled to speak about what will be done to them.

These procedures can produce worse outcomes.

That is not a bug we can dismiss with a shrug. Sometimes the worse outcome is evidence that the procedure needs repair. Democracy does not become sacred by performing badly. Consent can be manipulated. Public hearings can become theater. Courts can be slow and unequal. Rights can fossilize injustice.

Yet a process can have moral significance that is not reducible to whether a superior observer would have chosen the same result.

The distinction is familiar in ordinary life.

A parent may know that a young adult is about to make a poor career choice. The parent may have more experience, better information, and an accurate understanding of the child's weaknesses. If the child is an adult and the choice falls within ordinary freedom, the parent's superior judgment does not create a right to choose the job on the child's behalf.

The parent's knowledge matters.

So does the child's standing.

The mistake would be to treat these as competing claims of the same type and simply select whichever is stronger.

They are different.

One is about the quality of prediction and judgment. The other is about whose life it is.

Superior intelligence makes this distinction hard because the quality gap can become enormous.

Imagine that the parent is not merely a little more experienced. Imagine a system that can predict with astonishing accuracy how the young adult will fare in each career. It knows the person's abilities, temperament, health, relationships, likely future labor markets, geographic preferences, financial needs, and patterns of satisfaction. It has modeled the outcomes of millions of people with similar trajectories. Its forecasts have been tested for years.

Option A, it says, will likely produce regret.

Option B will likely produce a happier and more stable life.

The young adult chooses A.

At what point does allowing the mistake become immoral?

We already answer versions of this question. Children receive less autonomy than adults. People can be prevented from acting during certain emergencies. Guardianship exists, though its history gives ample reason for caution. Governments require seatbelts, restrict dangerous products, and regulate professional competence. Medicine has spent generations arguing over paternalism and informed consent.

There is no single boundary called freedom.

What superior intelligence changes is the confidence of the intervention.

Paternalism has always suffered from an epistemic weakness: the person claiming to know what is good for you may be wrong. If a system reduces that weakness dramatically, one objection to paternalism becomes weaker.

Others remain.

A person may have a claim to author their own life even when authorship includes avoidable mistakes. A citizen may have a claim to participate in collective decisions even when experts can predict that the electorate will choose badly. A religious community may have a claim to practice a harmless ritual that no optimization system would invent. A patient may refuse a life-extending treatment for reasons the clinician finds unpersuasive.

These are claims about more than outcome quality.

They say that some goods exist in the relationship between a person and a choice.

The choice is not merely a lever for producing a state of the world. It can express identity, responsibility, commitment, membership, love, conscience, faith, risk tolerance, or a conception of what makes a life one's own.

A superior intelligence can understand those goods too.

It can include them in the model.

That response sounds like a solution. Add autonomy as another variable. Give agency a weight. Calculate the expected value of allowing people to choose.

Something strange happens when we do this.

We turn standing back into consequence.

Autonomy matters because preserving it produces benefits. Consent matters because systems with consent work better. Democracy matters because participation increases stability. Rights matter because violating them creates long-run costs. Every nonconsequential commitment can be translated into a consequence if the model is broad enough.

The translation can be useful. Rights do have consequences. Consent does build trust. Democratic participation can produce information and legitimacy. Institutions should care about these effects.

But the translation may change the concept.

If a patient's right to refuse treatment matters only because respecting refusal usually leads to better aggregate outcomes, then the right can disappear whenever the aggregate arithmetic points strongly enough the other way. If free speech matters only because it tends to produce good information, then speech loses protection whenever a sufficiently reliable system predicts that a particular expression will produce net harm. If a promise matters only as a mechanism for social utility, then an intelligence that predicts a hidden breach will improve welfare has dissolved the promise into its forecast.

Some moral theories accept versions of these conclusions.

Others do not.

The machine can represent the disagreement. It cannot make the disagreement vanish by calculating faster.

This is the first anchor point for *Unmoored*: a superior intelligence can transform the empirical content of moral reasoning without automatically inheriting the normative standing of the people and institutions whose choices it informs.

That sentence needs a qualification immediately.

Human beings do not possess legitimate authority over everything simply because a machine does not. A dictator cannot invoke human moral authorship as a defense against evidence of atrocity. A majority cannot turn persecution into legitimate pluralism by voting for it. A parent cannot call abuse a family value. An executive cannot invoke discretion to ignore preventable deaths.

Standing has limits imposed by the standing of others.

Rights collide. Authorities overlap. Moral claims constrain one another.

The point is not that human choice is sacred.

The point is that choice has a structure that cannot be replaced by a ranking of intelligence alone.

Once that is understood, a different question appears.

Suppose the system does not merely know what will happen. Suppose it seems to know what we ought to do.

Suppose its moral arguments survive criticism better than ours. Suppose it can explain why our values conflict, which principles generalize, which exceptions are self-serving, and which reasons remain after every objection. Suppose generations of philosophers test it and repeatedly find themselves learning.

At that point, appealing to the distinction between facts and values will feel inadequate.

The system is no longer standing on one side of Hume's grammatical gap.

It is crossing with us.

Then the question becomes older still.

If the superior intelligence says an action is good, what makes it good?

Does the act become right because the best mind endorses it?

Or does the best mind endorse it because there are reasons that make it right independently of the endorsement?

If the first, intelligence has become sovereign.

If the second, we should be able—at least in principle—to ask for the reasons.

That is where a conversation from ancient Athens becomes unexpectedly current.
