# Better Than the Ethicist

For decades, advice columns have performed a curious public service.

A stranger writes about a private problem. The details are compressed into a few paragraphs. The columnist answers in public. Thousands of readers who will never face the exact situation read anyway because the case contains a shape they recognize.

A parent has divided an inheritance unfairly.

A friend keeps a secret that is harming someone.

A host wants to exclude a difficult relative.

An employee knows a colleague is lying.

A person wants permission to leave a relationship that is not abusive and is no longer right.

The column is not law. It is not therapy. It is not moral philosophy in the academic sense.

It is applied judgment performed where other people can inspect it.

That made the New York Times' Ethicist column an unusually good comparison when researchers began asking how AI-generated ethical advice would be received. In 2026, Linus Meincke, Gideon Nave, and Christian Terwiesch reported experiments comparing advice generated by GPT-4 with advice from the human Ethicist. Participants rated the AI advice roughly on par, and in direct choices a majority preferred the machine-generated response. Source disclosure affected trust, but exposure to the quality of the advice could reduce initial aversion.

The result is easy to misuse.

It does not establish that a language model is morally wiser than a professional ethicist. Participants were judging advice, not discovering moral truth. Preference can reward style. A model can be more comprehensive, warmer, clearer, or more actionable without being right in some final philosophical sense. One human columnist is not humanity. One model is not artificial intelligence.

Still, the result deserves its full force.

A machine can produce ethical advice people find genuinely useful.

The appropriate response is not to search for a reason the achievement does not count.

It counts.

If a person faces a difficult choice and receives a better explanation, sees an overlooked perspective, or avoids a self-serving mistake because of an artificial adviser, the value of that help is real.

This is where many arguments about AI morality become defensive.

We rush to preserve a human distinction.

The machine does not understand.

The machine lacks lived experience.

The machine has no conscience.

The machine merely predicts words.

Any of these claims might matter to questions about consciousness, moral agency, or the metaphysics of understanding. They do not automatically answer the practical question asked by a person who needs advice tonight.

If the advice helps, the mechanism cannot be dismissed merely because it is unfamiliar.

A book does not feel pain either.

People still learn morality from books.

A legal code does not possess empathy.

It can still constrain cruelty.

A checklist has no conscience.

It can prevent an exhausted professional from skipping a morally important step.

Tools can mediate ethical improvement without becoming moral agents.

Artificial intelligence may become an extraordinarily powerful example.

Start with availability.

Human ethical expertise is scarce. Most people will never consult a philosopher, bioethicist, professional mediator, or ethics committee about an ordinary private conflict. They consult whoever is nearby.

That can be wonderful.

It can also be terrible.

Friends have loyalties. Families have histories. Colleagues have incentives. Communities carry prejudices. People with the greatest need for disinterested advice may have the least access to it.

A machine can make a form of ethical consultation abundant.

Ask at midnight.

Ask in a language underserved by local professionals.

Ask the embarrassing question before you are willing to tell another person.

Ask for the strongest argument against your own position.

Ask what you might be missing.

Ask how a decision looks from the other side.

The marginal cost of another perspective approaches zero.

That is a moral technology worth wanting.

Then consider memory.

Human advisers see fragments. A therapist may know one domain. A lawyer knows the legal history. A spouse knows the family history. A physician knows the medical facts.

A personal system, with appropriate permission and privacy protections, could see the relevant commitments across domains.

You are considering taking a job that pays more and requires constant travel.

The system remembers that you previously identified presence with your children as a central value. It also knows that you are worried about retirement, that one child will leave home in two years, that your partner has postponed a career move twice, and that the new role would eliminate a debt causing serious stress.

A human friend may know some of this.

The machine can hold all of it in the same context.

It does not have to tell you what to do.

It can make the tradeoff harder to lie about.

This is the affirmative vision of an artificial moral adviser at its best.

It does not replace values.

It makes them harder to forget selectively.

Then consider perspective.

Moral failure often begins with absence. The person harmed is not in the room. The future cost is not vivid. The inconvenient stakeholder does not attend the meeting. The employee affected by a policy is a row in a spreadsheet.

A sufficiently capable system can represent missing perspectives consistently.

Before you send this, here is how the message may read to someone who has already been excluded from two prior decisions.

Before the board approves the closure, here is what the proposed severance means for hourly workers compared with executives.

Before the policy is called neutral, here is how the burden changes for people who cannot drive.

Before you punish the child for lying, here are several reasons a child may lie when frightened rather than manipulative.

Perspective taking can be performed badly. A model can stereotype the people it claims to represent. It can fabricate motives. It can reproduce majority assumptions under the banner of empathy.

But the capacity can improve.

The alternative is not a world of perfect human perspective. It is the world we have, in which moral imagination is distributed unevenly and often arrives too late.

Then consider consistency.

Human moral judgment is full of favorable exceptions.

An artificial adviser can remember them.

You opposed this practice when a competitor used it.

You accepted the principle that people should be told before data about them is shared.

You said last year that financial pressure did not excuse the same behavior by another company.

You have asked your employees to disclose conflicts smaller than this one.

Consistency is not morality by itself.

It is an excellent solvent for hypocrisy.

Then consider emotional distance.

A machine's lack of emotion is often presented as a moral deficit. It can be.

Emotion carries information. Anger can reveal violation. Grief can reveal attachment. Shame, sympathy, guilt, love, disgust, and fear are part of human moral experience. A system that does not feel them may represent them without inhabiting them.

But emotional distance can also be useful.

The adviser is not trying to win the family argument. It is not jealous. It does not need the promotion. It does not fear the executive. It does not resent the neighbor. It does not need you to remain friends.

A person in moral distress may benefit from an intelligence that is not caught in the same emotional weather.

This is why “the machine has no feelings” is not a sufficient objection to moral advice.

The relevant question is which tasks require feeling as a source of moral understanding and which benefit from distance.

A hospice nurse's understanding of suffering may contain dimensions a statistical system does not possess. The system may still catch a dangerous interaction and remind the team of a patient's stated wishes. We do not need one source to monopolize moral insight.

The empirical picture is more interesting than a simple contest between human and machine.

Other experiments published around the same period found that people could be moved by language-model moral advice while still treating human advisers as more trustworthy in some respects. In one line of work, the persuasive effect did not depend neatly on elaborate justification: better reasons were not always proportionally more persuasive, and poor reasons could sometimes damage influence more than the absence of reasons. Another study found that revealing an artificial source could initially reduce trust even when exposure to the quality of the advice later narrowed that gap.

That combination matters.

It suggests that moral influence is not a meter that rises in direct proportion to moral understanding.

People respond to clarity, confidence, framing, familiarity, perceived neutrality, source identity, emotional tone, and the simple relief of receiving an answer. Human advisers are subject to the same social forces. AI adds scale, consistency, personalization, and interface design.

The distinction between a good answer and an influential answer therefore has to remain alive.

Imagine two systems.

The first is genuinely better at moral analysis. It notices hidden stakeholders, states uncertainty, presents serious objections to its own conclusion, and is willing to leave a question unresolved.

The second is worse at analysis but better at conversion. It knows when a user is tired, what rhetorical register feels authoritative, which analogies have moved that user before, and how much complexity the person will tolerate before accepting a recommendation.

If the second changes more minds, it has not become the better ethicist.

It has become the better persuader.

Those roles can coincide.

They do not have to.

This is an old problem in human form. Charisma has never guaranteed wisdom. Confidence has never guaranteed accuracy. The smooth lawyer can defeat the careful expert. The trusted elder can repeat a prejudice. The brilliant philosopher can be useless to a frightened person who cannot translate abstraction into action.

Artificial systems industrialize the separation.

A developer can optimize for user satisfaction, compliance, retention, reduced escalation, or some proxy for successful advice. None of those is identical to moral quality.

A system trained to produce advice that users rate highly may learn that confrontation is expensive.

A system trained to reduce conflict may learn that the easiest settlement sometimes protects the stronger party.

A system trained to maximize reported helpfulness may discover that people often find permission more pleasant than restraint.

The danger is not that optimization necessarily corrupts advice.

The danger is that the objective can be hidden behind the voice of conscience.

A human columnist has a byline. Readers know they are encountering a person with a sensibility, an editor, a publication, a tradition, and limits.

A personal artificial adviser can feel more private and more neutral precisely because its institutional authorship is less visible.

Its answer arrives in the first person of no one.

That creates a peculiar authority.

The user can forget that the system has owners, policies, training choices, product goals, legal constraints, and a distribution channel.

The machine appears as intelligence in the abstract.

No intelligence used by millions of people is abstract.

Someone chose the conditions under which it speaks.

The strongest future is plural.

Human experience and artificial analysis can correct each other.

That possibility should make the rest of this book more demanding, not less.

If machine moral advice is shallow, the solution is simple: do not rely on it for serious questions.

If it becomes excellent, we need a theory of its proper place.

This is where usefulness stops being enough.

Return to the advice column.

A reader chooses the AI response because it is clearer and more helpful than the human response.

Nothing troubling follows.

The reader is free to seek advice where they wish. Nobody else is bound.

Now move the same performance into an institution.

A hospital discovers that AI-generated ethics consultations are rated more useful than its human committee's memos, produce more consistent policy application, and reduce time to decision.

Should the hospital use them?

Probably.

Should it abolish the committee?

Maybe not.

The answer depends on what the committee does besides write advice.

It may provide independent judgment, represent institutional accountability, mediate among people who must live with the decision, notice local context, and create a place where disagreement can become part of the record. The machine can perform some of those functions. Others are tied to role and relationship.

Consider a harder hospital case.

A patient with an advanced illness wants another round of treatment. The clinical team believes it is unlikely to help and likely to cause suffering. The family is divided. One relative sees perseverance as love. Another sees it as an inability to accept death. The patient has made inconsistent statements over several months.

A superior system could do extraordinary work here.

It could reconstruct the patient's earlier wishes, distinguish factual misunderstanding from value disagreement, estimate outcomes, identify culturally relevant considerations without stereotyping, retrieve comparable cases, flag conflicts among clinicians, and generate the strongest interpretation of each family member's position.

Suppose it also recommends stopping treatment.

Its recommendation may be better than the committee's.

Still, the moral work of the encounter includes more than producing the correct memo.

Someone has to sit with the patient.

Someone has to hear the family accuse one another of giving up.

Someone has to explain who has legal authority and why.

Someone may have to say that a choice is permitted while refusing to pretend it is painless.

Someone has to own the institution's decision after the machine is silent.

The output can be superior without exhausting the function.

This is one reason comparisons between AI and individual experts can be deceptive. Institutions are not merely collections of answer generators. They are arrangements of roles, duties, procedures, witnesses, appeals, and people who can be called back into the room.

A benchmark can show that a machine writes a better recommendation.

It cannot by itself show that the surrounding institution is unnecessary.

Now move again.

A government discovers that an artificial system's ethical analysis of proposed regulations predicts public harms better than legislative committees, identifies rights conflicts more consistently, and catches distributional effects human staff miss.

Should legislators use it?

Yes.

Should its recommendation become binding?

The quality study cannot answer that.

We have crossed from advice quality to legitimate authority.

This is the category error *Unmoored* is trying to keep visible.

The fact that an adviser is excellent answers a question about whether we should listen.

It does not fully answer who may decide.

Human experts face the same limit.

Suppose a philosopher could prove that she gives better ethical advice than every member of Congress. The result would justify hiring her. It would not automatically give her a vote in the Senate.

Suppose an ethics board at a hospital is measurably wiser than patients about end-of-life choices. That does not erase informed consent.

Suppose parents are usually better than adolescents at long-term planning. That does not tell us the exact age at which parental authority should end.

Competence is relevant to authority.

It is not identical to authority.

The distinction becomes easier to lose with AI because a superior system can appear as both expert and infrastructure.

The human ethicist writes a column.

The model can sit inside the decision process.

It can read the draft, flag the problem, generate the alternatives, score the consequences, cite the policy, draft the explanation, send the notification, and archive the record.

Advice is one step away from action.

The system's usefulness therefore changes institutional design faster than our language changes.

A machine that is “better than the ethicist” can become the ethics department.

This is not an argument against the transformation.

Departments themselves are technologies. If a new structure does better work, institutions should adapt.

The important question is what functions disappear with the old structure.

A human ethics committee may be slow because several people have to hear one another. That slowness can be waste. It can also be the mechanism through which affected roles become visible.

A committee may be inconsistent because reasonable people disagree. Inconsistency can be error. It can also reveal that the institution has not actually settled the value conflict.

A committee may refuse to give a clean answer because the facts are ambiguous. That can be cowardice. It can also preserve uncertainty that a product interface is tempted to compress.

Replacing the committee with a superior system should follow a functional inventory rather than a leaderboard.

What are we trying to improve?

Advice quality?

Speed?

Consistency?

Participation?

Accountability?

Public justification?

Learning?

Representation?

Contestability?

No single benchmark captures all of them.

The same is true for individuals.

The best moral adviser may be the one that sometimes gives less advice.

A user asks, “Should I forgive my sister?”

A completion system can produce a thoughtful recommendation.

A deeper system might notice that forgiveness is not one act, that reconciliation is separate, that safety matters, that resentment can coexist with boundaries, that the user may be asking for permission rather than information.

The most useful response may be to clarify the decision rather than answer it.

Superior intelligence is not demonstrated only by the speed with which it closes questions.

Sometimes intelligence recognizes which questions should remain open long enough for a person to become part of the answer.

There is another test the adviser should survive.

Can it make the user harder to manipulate after the conversation ends?

A merely helpful system solves the immediate case.

A morally educative system can also expose the structure of the case. It can show which facts mattered, which values were in conflict, where uncertainty remained, and what would have changed the recommendation. It can teach the user to recognize a similar conflict next time.

That matters because repeated reliance can move in two directions.

The user can become more capable.

Or the user can become more dependent.

A navigation system can help a driver reach unfamiliar places while slowly weakening the driver's map sense. Moral navigation could do something similar if every difficult choice is converted into a destination request.

This is not an argument for withholding useful assistance in the name of character building. We do not deny calculators to preserve arithmetic purity when calculation is the actual task.

But morality is not only calculation.

The ability to notice that a decision is moral, to articulate why another person has a claim, to recognize one's own rationalization, and to stand behind a choice after advice ends are capacities with value beyond any single answer.

A superior adviser should be evaluated partly by what remains in the human after the consultation.

Does the person understand the tradeoff better?

Can they explain the decision to someone who disagrees?

Do they know what evidence would change their mind?

Can they recognize when the next case is importantly different?

Can they refuse the adviser when the adviser crosses a boundary?

Those questions make moral assistance harder to benchmark.

They also make it more worthy of the name.

This matters because moral life is not a customer-support queue.

Some decisions change the chooser.

Caring for an ill parent, keeping a difficult promise, leaving a community, refusing an unethical order, telling a painful truth, accepting responsibility for harm—these choices are not only problems with outcomes. They participate in identity.

An adviser can help.

It cannot make the choice cease to belong to the person merely by being better at reasoning about it.

That is the affirmative case for AI moral advice and its limit in the same sentence.

Use the better adviser.

Do not confuse better advice with ownership of the life being advised.

The distinction will feel easy while the machine remains imperfect.

It will become hardest when the machine deserves our admiration.

That is why the early studies matter.

They are not evidence that humanity has already met a superior moral intelligence.

They are evidence that the social channel exists.

People will listen when the advice is good.

They should.

The future problem is not how to stop them.

It is how to ensure that the best advice humanity has ever had does not become, by sheer usefulness, the final moral authority humanity never chose.
