# The Right to Be Morally Wrong

Freedom includes the possibility of choosing badly.

The statement sounds trivial until the bad choice becomes predictable.

A person marries someone every close friend thinks is wrong for them.

A student chooses a degree with poor economic prospects.

An adult child moves across the country knowing an aging parent wants them nearby.

A patient refuses a treatment likely to extend life.

A voter supports a candidate experts believe will govern badly.

A founder turns down an acquisition everyone else considers rational.

A person forgives someone who may hurt them again.

These choices differ morally and legally. Some affect only the chooser. Some impose burdens on others. Some create duties. Some are protected by rights. Some may justify intervention.

What joins them is authorship.

Part of being a person is living inside decisions that do not come with a guarantee of wisdom.

Superior intelligence threatens to make this condition look obsolete.

If a system can predict which marriage is likely to fail, which education produces regret, which medical choice shortens life, which political decision produces recession, and which relationship will cause repeated harm, then allowing error can begin to look like negligence.

Why let people choose badly when a better answer is available?

The question has old roots.

Paternalism has always defended restrictions by appealing to welfare. Seatbelt laws, drug regulation, compulsory education, guardianship, medical intervention, workplace safety, and restrictions on dangerous products all contain judgments that people should not be entirely free to impose certain risks, even on themselves.

Liberal traditions respond by drawing boundaries around personal autonomy. John Stuart Mill's harm principle is one famous attempt: coercion is easier to justify to prevent harm to others than to protect a competent adult from their own choices.

The principle has never been simple in practice.

What counts as harm to others?

What counts as competence?

What about addiction?

What about children?

What about social costs?

What about manipulation?

What about choices made under severe informational inequality?

Superior intelligence makes every ambiguity harder because it strengthens the paternalist's factual case.

One historical objection to paternalism is that the authority claiming to know what is best may be wrong.

A future system may be wrong less often.

Suppose a personal health model can predict with ninety-nine percent confidence that you will regret declining a treatment.

The predicted regret is based on millions of similar cases and your own values over time.

You decline anyway.

Should the system merely inform you?

Should it make refusal harder?

Should the physician be required to challenge you repeatedly?

Should an insurer refuse to cover consequences judged avoidable?

Should a family member be notified?

At what probability does advice become intervention?

The temptation is to answer with a threshold.

Ninety percent.

Ninety-five.

Ninety-nine.

The number cannot do the moral work by itself.

Even perfect prediction does not necessarily create jurisdiction.

Suppose the system knows with certainty that you will regret a tattoo.

The certainty does not make the tattoo shop owner your guardian.

Suppose it knows with certainty that you will dislike a career.

The knowledge does not give the labor department authority to assign you another one.

Suppose it knows that a romantic relationship will end painfully.

The state still does not get to prohibit the date.

These examples feel obvious because the domain of personal choice is strong.

Now move toward the boundary.

A parent makes a choice predicted to harm a child.

An intoxicated driver chooses to drive.

A business owner ignores a safety recommendation affecting workers.

A patient with a contagious disease refuses isolation.

A politician rejects a policy whose consequences will fall on people who did not choose the risk.

The right to be wrong narrows as the standing of others enters.

This is why the title of the chapter needs precision.

There is no universal right to make morally bad choices.

A person does not gain permission to abuse, discriminate, defraud, endanger, or coerce simply because autonomy matters.

The defensible claim is narrower and more important: legitimate human freedom includes domains in which people retain authority to choose among options even when superior intelligence predicts that one option is morally or prudentially worse.

The existence of the domain is itself a moral commitment.

Without it, autonomy becomes permission granted only when the machine agrees.

That is not autonomy.

It is supervised optimization.

This can emerge gradually through defaults rather than law.

Imagine a financial assistant that knows you are prone to impulsive purchases when stressed. You asked it to help.

At first it warns you.

Then it suggests a cooling-off period.

Then you authorize it to delay transactions above a threshold.

Years later, your financial behavior is excellent.

You try to make a purchase the system considers inconsistent with your long-term goals.

It asks you to reconsider.

You insist.

Should it comply immediately?

The answer depends on the mandate you gave it.

If you deliberately authorized a binding rule, the system may be respecting autonomy by enforcing an earlier choice against a later impulse.

But what if the assistant infers that the purchase is irrational and creates friction without explicit authorization?

Now paternalism has entered through design.

The system may be right.

The user may even be grateful tomorrow.

The moral problem remains because the user did not necessarily grant the assistant standing to convert prediction into constraint.

This distinction between help and guardianship will become central to personal AI.

A system can recommend.

A system can remind.

A system can warn.

A system can ask for reconsideration.

A system can enforce a commitment the user previously authorized.

Each step changes the relationship.

The mistake is to move between steps solely because prediction improves.

Superior accuracy is evidence that the advice deserves weight.

It is not consent.

Regret itself is also a complicated target.

A person can regret a choice and still believe they had the right to make it. Someone may regret leaving a secure job to start a failed company while remaining glad that the choice was theirs. A parent may regret moving a family and still reject the idea that a predictive system should have blocked the move. A patient may later wish they had accepted treatment without concluding that refusal should have been impossible.

Regret is evidence about welfare.

It is not a retroactive transfer of authority.

The distinction matters because artificial systems will be able to learn from retrospective judgments at scale. They can identify decisions people later describe as mistakes and use those patterns to steer future users away from similar choices.

That can be helpful.

It can also create a moral trap if every later disappointment is treated as proof that earlier freedom was poorly designed.

A life contains losses that are not policy failures.

Love can end.

A vocation can disappoint.

A risk can fail for reasons no one could control.

A commitment can become costly and still have been worth making.

The absence of regret cannot be the objective of moral life unless moral life is reduced to successful prediction management.

This is another reason superior intelligence needs more than a welfare model.

It needs an account of which bad outcomes are grounds for intervention and which are among the costs a free person is entitled to bear.

Medical ethics offers a useful analogy because modern practice has spent decades moving away from strong paternalism toward informed consent while preserving exceptions.

A physician can know more than the patient.

The physician is expected to explain.

The patient can refuse.

That refusal can be tragic.

The structure expresses a judgment that bodily authority belongs to the competent patient in a way expertise alone cannot override.

This is one of the clearest examples of superiority without sovereignty.

A future AI system may know more medicine than any physician who ever lived.

The patient does not become its property.

The same principle can extend beyond the body, though less cleanly.

A person's conception of a worthwhile life is not simply a clinical outcome.

Some people choose risk.

Some choose sacrifice.

Some choose religion.

Some choose family obligations outsiders would not accept.

Some choose art over income.

Some choose solitude.

Some choose communities with demanding rules.

A superior intelligence can predict which choices produce happiness by a measurable standard.

Happiness is not the only candidate good.

Even if the system learns each person's own values, people retain the possibility of changing them through choices that the prior value model would classify as errors.

A life can become itself by disappointing its forecast.

This is not romantic nonsense.

Prediction is based on patterns.

Moral transformation often begins with breaking one.

A person raised in a prejudiced family befriends someone they were taught to distrust.

A careerist walks away from status.

A timid person takes a public stand.

A wealthy heir gives away money.

An addict seeks help.

A loyal employee becomes a whistleblower.

Before the action, the new self may be statistically unlikely.

Afterward, the action becomes evidence about who the person is.

A system optimized to prevent regret or inconsistency can accidentally defend the prior self against moral growth.

This is why human unpredictability has moral value in bounded domains.

Not because randomness is good.

Because authorship includes revision.

A person needs space to choose something their model does not yet understand.

The concept of competence will therefore become more important, not less.

Autonomy depends partly on whether a person can understand the choice, appreciate consequences, reason about alternatives, and express a decision without coercion. Superior intelligence can help assess and support these capacities.

It can also undermine them if competence is defined as agreement with the superior recommendation.

A patient who chooses the option the model dislikes is not necessarily incompetent.

A citizen who gives greater weight to a value the model ranks lower is not necessarily irrational.

A teenager who rejects a parent's AI-generated plan is not necessarily incapable of judgment.

Competence is the capacity to decide, not the capacity to reach the expert's answer.

This distinction is already difficult in medicine and law. It will become harder when the expert can demonstrate that dissent predicts worse outcomes.

Institutions will need to resist outcome-based hindsight.

If a competent person chooses freely and the bad outcome occurs, the occurrence does not prove the original decision process was defective. Otherwise every exercised freedom becomes conditionally valid only if it ends well.

That principle would swallow autonomy after the fact.

A more mature approach separates the quality of the process from the quality of the outcome.

Was the person informed?

Was manipulation limited?

Did they understand the material consequences?

Were they free to choose?

Were the rights of others adequately protected?

Did the institution have authority to impose additional constraints?

These questions can be answered before the future reveals which option worked.

They give freedom something stronger than luck as a foundation.

This becomes politically explosive when collective choices are involved.

Suppose an AI system predicts election outcomes and policy consequences with extraordinary accuracy. It recommends Candidate A because Candidate A's platform will likely produce higher growth, lower crime, better health, and greater public satisfaction.

Citizens elect Candidate B.

The model's forecast proves correct. Conditions worsen.

Was democracy wrong?

In one sense, yes. The electorate made a choice with bad consequences.

Does that imply the electorate should lose the authority to choose?

Not automatically.

Democracy is not justified solely by an empirical claim that crowds always outperform experts. Sometimes they do not.

Its defense includes political equality, participation, peaceful transfer, contestability, and the idea that citizens are not merely subjects whose welfare should be administered by whoever predicts best.

Superior intelligence will force democracies to confront this foundation honestly.

If we defend elections only because voters collectively know best, a system that knows better defeats the argument.

If political authority rests partly on equal standing among citizens, the performance gap does not erase the standing.

This does not mean every democratic decision deserves deference forever.

Constitutions limit majorities. Courts review laws. Expertise receives delegated authority. International obligations constrain domestic choice. Democratic systems already mix participation with competence and rights.

The future question is how much artificial competence can be added without turning the citizen's vote into a ceremonial override of the responsible answer.

There is a moral cost to letting people choose badly collectively.

The costs are imposed on others.

This is where the right to be wrong is weakest and most contested.

A voter can help produce a war, recession, discriminatory law, or failed public-health response that harms people who voted differently.

Why should standing protect the majority's error?

Because no alternative authority is neutral.

If a superior intelligence receives the right to overrule democratic choices whenever expected welfare is higher, then the intelligence has acquired political sovereignty.

Perhaps a future society could legitimately grant such power in defined domains.

The grant itself requires a constitutional source.

Otherwise optimization has replaced consent by inference.

The tension cannot be eliminated.

A free political order tolerates some bad collective choices.

A responsible political order constrains others.

Superior intelligence can help locate the consequences with unprecedented accuracy.

It cannot tell us, by accuracy alone, where self-government should end.

The issue becomes even more difficult with children and people whose competence is impaired.

A child's right to be wrong expands with development.

Parents routinely prevent choices that an adult would be free to make.

The moral justification is partly protective and partly educational. Children are becoming agents.

A good parent does not simply optimize every outcome. They allow age-appropriate mistakes because learning requires ownership.

A child chooses the wrong friend, spends money foolishly, loses a game, forgets an assignment, or takes a manageable risk.

Preventing every mistake can produce dependence.

A superior AI parent proxy could remove much more error than any human parent.

Would that be good parenting?

Not if the goal is to produce an adult capable of agency.

Development requires a zone of survivable wrongness.

The same logic applies to organizations that claim to preserve professional judgment.

A junior engineer will sometimes make a less efficient design choice than the optimization system would. A young physician will sometimes ask for a test the model correctly predicts will be unnecessary. A new manager will sometimes mishandle a conversation that a coaching system could have scripted better.

Institutions should not tolerate unlimited error merely for educational romance.

But if every meaningful judgment is preempted because the model can do it better, the institution eventually stops producing people capable of carrying responsibility when the model fails, conflicts with another authority, or enters a domain where its mandate ends.

Some forms of supervised error are part of succession planning.

This is familiar in aviation, medicine, engineering, and the military. Trainees gain authority gradually. They make decisions under supervision because future competence cannot be downloaded as a policy document.

Superior intelligence may make training feel inefficient.

Why let the apprentice reason when the system already knows?

Because an institution that never allows developing agents to own bounded decisions may discover that it has preserved human titles while eliminating human capacity.

The same principle can apply to citizens. Self-government is not only a mechanism for selecting optimal policy. It is a practice through which people learn to argue, compromise, organize, lose, revise, and accept responsibility for common decisions.

A perfectly benevolent optimizer could produce better outputs while weakening that practice.

Whether the trade is worth making cannot be settled by the optimizer's output score because the practice itself is one of the things being valued.

This may become an important principle beyond childhood.

Institutions can preserve bounded zones where people remain entitled to choose without optimization, provided the external harms are limited.

A personal sphere.

A creative sphere.

A religious sphere.

A political sphere constrained by rights.

A professional sphere where judgment has real authority under defined conditions.

The boundaries will be contested.

That is healthy.

The important thing is that the existence of such zones is defended on grounds other than machine error.

If the only reason to let humans decide is that AI might be wrong, then improved AI will logically shrink human authority toward zero.

That is an unstable foundation.

We need reasons for human and institutional authorship that survive machine superiority.

Consent is one.

Rights are another.

Political equality.

Responsibility.

Relationship.

Development.

Identity.

Legitimate mandate.

These reasons do not make every choice good.

They identify who is entitled to make some choices despite the possibility of error.

There is a final uncomfortable implication.

Sometimes respecting autonomy will mean watching a person make the choice the machine correctly predicts will hurt them.

The system can warn.

Friends can plead.

Professionals can explain.

The person may still choose.

If the domain is genuinely theirs, respect requires allowing the outcome.

This will feel barbaric in an age of superior intelligence.

Why permit preventable regret?

Because eliminating all preventable regret would require eliminating the authority to become someone the predictor did not authorize.

A life without avoidable error may be safer.

It may also be less fully owned.

The right to be morally wrong is not a celebration of wrongness.

It is a limit on the proposition that superior judgment creates superior jurisdiction.

The person can listen.

The person can learn.

The person can be warned with extraordinary precision.

And sometimes, within a legitimate domain of freedom, the person can still say:

I understand.

I choose differently.
