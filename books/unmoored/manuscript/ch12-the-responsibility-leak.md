# The Responsibility Leak

Responsibility leaks when everyone can explain why the decision was not quite theirs.

The engineer followed the specification.

The specification came from policy.

Policy relied on the model.

The model used approved data.

The data came from the source system.

The source system was configured by another team.

The manager approved the deployment because legal signed off.

Legal reviewed the vendor's representations.

The vendor complied with the contract.

The contract assigned final use to the customer.

The user clicked confirm.

No sentence is necessarily false.

Together they can describe an institution in which an action clearly happened and authorship has become difficult to locate.

This problem did not begin with artificial intelligence. Modern organizations have always distributed agency across people, procedures, technologies, departments, and rules. A bank loan is not made by one mind. A hospital discharge is not the product of one judgment. A criminal sentence can reflect statute, plea bargaining, prosecutorial discretion, sentencing guidelines, judicial interpretation, institutional habit, and resource constraint.

Complexity diffuses action.

Artificial intelligence adds something unusual: a component that can participate in the reasoning itself.

The system is not merely the pipe through which a decision travels. It can generate the recommendation, construct the rationale, compare alternatives, predict consequences, draft the explanation, and tell the human reviewer which considerations matter.

That changes the shape of responsibility because reasons have entered the machinery.

A conventional database can contain a wrong number. A recommendation model can tell you what the number means.

A form can omit a field. A language model can explain why the omission should not change the conclusion.

A workflow can route an application. An agent can decide that the application belongs in one category, invoke a rule, prepare the denial, and answer the applicant's first appeal.

The more of the normative sequence a system performs, the easier it becomes for every human participant to experience themselves as downstream.

This is the responsibility leak.

The phrase should not imply that responsibility is a liquid with a fixed volume. Institutional responsibility can be shared. Several people can each be genuinely responsible for the same outcome in different ways. A board can be responsible for governance while an executive is responsible for execution and an engineer responsible for a specific design choice.

The leak occurs when distribution becomes disappearance.

Consider a hiring system.

A company decides that it wants to reduce arbitrary screening and make hiring more consistent. It adopts an AI system that reads applications, evaluates work samples, structures interview notes, and recommends candidates. The company has good reasons. Human hiring is noisy. Interviewers favor people who resemble themselves. Confidence can masquerade as competence. An evidence-based tool may improve fairness.

The organization does not ask the model to “choose the best person” in one unconstrained step. That would be reckless.

Instead, multiple layers are built.

Industrial-organizational psychologists define competencies.

The legal team reviews protected characteristics.

The vendor trains and evaluates models.

Recruiters administer the process.

Managers conduct interviews.

The system synthesizes the record and recommends a rank.

A hiring manager approves one candidate.

Later the company discovers a pattern: applicants with a particular type of disability are systematically disadvantaged because the work-sample interface penalizes a form of interaction unrelated to actual job performance.

Who owns the discrimination?

The manager says she never knew the interface produced that effect.

The recruiter says the assessment was validated by the vendor.

The vendor says the customer selected the competencies and deployment context.

The psychologists say they did not design the interface.

Legal says it reviewed the documented variables and prohibited criteria, not every interaction effect.

Executives say the process was implemented to reduce bias, not create it.

Every participant can point to a legitimate boundary around their role.

The boundary can also become an escape hatch.

Organizations need a principle stronger than “someone should have noticed.”

They need responsibility attached to powers.

Who chose the objective?

Who selected the system?

Who defined what evidence counted?

Who had authority to suspend use?

Who was required to monitor outcomes?

Who could see complaints across cases?

Who could change the interface?

Who controlled the data?

Who was entitled to decide that the observed disparity was acceptable, remediable, or disqualifying?

Responsibility becomes more intelligible when traced through verbs rather than job titles.

Choose.

Configure.

Authorize.

Observe.

Override.

Appeal.

Repair.

Stop.

These are powers.

Moral responsibility follows power imperfectly but persistently.

A person cannot be responsible for everything they do not control. A person also cannot erase responsibility by dividing control into slices small enough that each slice appears morally insignificant.

Artificial intelligence encourages slice-making because systems are assembled through supply chains.

A foundation model may be built by one company, fine-tuned by another, integrated by a vendor, configured by a customer, used by employees, and governed under laws written for categories that did not anticipate the arrangement. Each layer can plausibly say that the harmful outcome depended on decisions elsewhere.

This is ordinary in technology.

The moral novelty comes when the system itself can supply persuasive reasons for the outcome.

The explanation makes the decision feel authored even when no human author exists.

An applicant asks why they were denied.

The system responds: the role requires sustained client-facing conflict resolution, and your work sample demonstrated weaker performance on the relevant dimensions than the selected candidate.

The explanation is coherent.

Who believes it?

Who stands behind it?

If the applicant challenges the premise, who can change the decision?

If the explanation was generated after the outcome, does it describe the causal basis of the decision or merely a plausible rationale?

If nobody inside the organization can distinguish those possibilities, the explanation has become a moral prop.

This is why “explainability” can fail as a substitute for responsibility.

A reason is useful only if it connects to an accountable process.

Otherwise the system can explain without anyone answering.

The difference between explanation and answerability matters.

Explanation gives an account.

Answerability creates a relationship in which one party is entitled to demand an account from another who has standing and power to respond.

A citizen asks an agency why a benefit was denied.

If the agency replies with a model-generated explanation that nobody can revise, the citizen has received information without an accountable interlocutor.

A patient asks why a treatment was refused.

If the clinician can only repeat the system's rationale, the patient has not reached the person who owns the decision.

An employee asks why they were flagged for investigation.

If every manager says the model triggered the process and every technical team says policy defined the threshold, responsibility has leaked between the two.

This is one reason due process depends on more than transparency.

A transparent wall is still a wall.

People need somewhere to go.

The future moral architecture of AI-heavy institutions will therefore depend on appeal more than most early discussions admitted.

Not every decision needs a human appeal. Low-stakes reversible systems can be corrected automatically. Some automated processes may be more fair, fast, and consistent than human review.

But consequential decisions need a route to a different authority when the issue is not merely whether the system followed its own rules correctly.

The difference is crucial.

An automated appeal that asks the same model to reconsider its output can catch errors.

It is not independent review if the dispute concerns the assumptions built into the model.

A second model trained on the same data and policy can provide redundancy.

It may not provide pluralism.

An internal human reviewer reading only the model's summary can appear independent while inheriting the same frame.

Real appeal changes at least one source of judgment.

Different evidence.

Different mandate.

Different model family.

Different human role.

Different policy authority.

Different institutional interest.

The exact form varies by domain.

The principle is that challenge should not be forced to travel through the same reasoning channel that produced the contested answer.

This is how responsibility becomes visible again.

An appellate layer has to own the possibility of reversal.

Ownership creates pressure to understand what is being reviewed.

Superior intelligence complicates this because independent review may be weaker than the original system.

Suppose the first model is vastly better than the human appellate officer.

The officer receives a challenge and sees no technical reason to disagree. The model's record is excellent. The appellant's argument sounds emotional and particular.

Why should the weaker reviewer have power?

Because the appeal may concern something other than task competence.

The first system may be excellent at applying policy.

The appellant may be arguing that the policy was not authorized to decide this kind of case.

The model may be accurate about predicted risk.

The appellant may be asserting a right not to be classified by that risk.

The system may have interpreted the law consistently.

The appeal may contend that the law itself requires a hearing before this consequence can be imposed.

The reviewer is not present to beat the model at prediction.

The reviewer represents a different source of authority.

This distinction is one of the book's recurring anchors.

Superior intelligence should dominate questions where superior intelligence is the legitimate criterion.

It should not automatically dominate questions about who is permitted to decide.

A chess engine has authority over the best chess move only because the game defines performance in a way that makes move quality central. It does not acquire authority over whether the tournament rules are fair because it calculates chess better.

Institutional life is full of hidden game definitions.

A credit system can predict default.

Who decided that default risk may be used in this decision?

A school system can predict dropout.

Who decided that predicted dropout should affect admission?

A policing system can predict where crime will be reported.

Who decided which police presence is legitimate and which behaviors should generate enforcement?

A medical model can predict survival.

Who decided how survival should interact with disability, age, waiting time, prior access, or patient preference?

The model can answer every downstream question more intelligently while remaining downstream of the upstream authority choice.

Responsibility leaks when institutions forget the upstream choice existed.

This can happen gradually because objectives become technical objects.

A board approves a policy in ordinary language.

A product team turns it into requirements.

An engineering team turns requirements into thresholds.

A vendor maps thresholds to model behavior.

Users experience outputs.

Months later, nobody remembers which phrase in the original policy became the decisive variable.

The technical implementation acquires inevitability.

“This is how the system works.”

The sentence is one of the great refuges from responsibility.

Systems work as they do because people and institutions made choices, inherited choices, or failed to revisit choices.

Some choices become difficult to reverse. Difficulty does not convert history into nature.

A superior intelligence may make this problem worse by optimizing the translation from policy to implementation so thoroughly that humans interact less with the intermediate steps.

It may also make the history easier to preserve.

A responsible AI system can carry provenance alongside decisions.

This recommendation implements Policy 7.3.

The relevant threshold was changed on this date by this authority.

The recommendation depends heavily on these variables.

These protected factors were excluded.

The model is operating outside the range in which validation is strongest.

This user requested an override.

This reviewer denied it.

This harm signal increased after deployment.

This information does not solve the moral problem.

It prevents amnesia from pretending to be complexity.

The next step is more demanding.

Responsibility needs a duty to monitor consequences.

Organizations often assign responsibility only at the moment of decision. Deploy the system. Approve the policy. Sign the contract.

Many moral failures emerge after deployment.

A model changes user behavior. A classification becomes a target. Workers adapt to the metric. Feedback loops intensify disparities. People learn how to game the system. A policy that was defensible under one distribution becomes harmful under another.

Who is responsible for noticing?

If the answer is nobody in particular, the institution has designed a leak.

A robust governance structure names the observer.

Not merely an auditor who arrives once a year.

Someone with authority to see patterns across cases and power to intervene.

This is a moral function because many harms are invisible at the single-decision level.

One denial looks reasonable.

Ten thousand denials reveal a pattern.

One override looks idiosyncratic.

A cluster of overrides reveals a category the model misunderstands.

One complaint sounds anecdotal.

A hundred similar complaints are data.

Superior intelligence can help detect these patterns.

Again, the machine can strengthen responsibility if the institution decides that detection creates obligation.

Otherwise the system produces a report nobody owns.

The same problem appears privately in a quieter form.

A personal assistant recommends a series of choices over years.

Take this job.

Move here.

Do not contact that person tonight.

Invest in this training.

Have this difficult conversation.

The user chooses. The assistant advises.

Who is responsible for the shape of the life?

Legally, mostly the user.

Morally, the question is more interesting.

If the assistant has become deeply influential, if the system knows which framing persuades, if its optimization target includes engagement or retention, and if the user cannot inspect how advice has shifted over time, then the company providing the adviser has acquired a form of power without ordinary fiduciary duties.

This is not identical to a friend giving bad advice.

The scale, memory, asymmetry, and commercial structure are different.

A friend does not A/B test your conscience.

A platform can.

A future moral economy will need sharper distinctions between products that influence and relationships that owe duties.

A lawyer owes a client duties.

A doctor owes a patient duties.

A financial adviser may have duties depending on the relationship and jurisdiction.

A close friend has moral duties that are not contractual.

What does a persistent artificial adviser owe?

The machine itself may or may not be a moral agent.

The institution deploying it unquestionably makes choices.

It can choose to optimize for the user's expressed goals.

It can choose to promote platform-defined welfare.

It can choose to maximize engagement.

It can choose to retain data.

It can choose to make the advice portable.

It can choose to expose conflicts of interest.

Those are governance choices with moral weight.

Responsibility attaches to them whether or not the model has consciousness.

This matters because debates about machine moral agency can distract from ordinary institutional responsibility.

We do not need to decide whether the model deserves blame before deciding whether a company was negligent, manipulative, reckless, or accountable.

The possibility of artificial agency may become morally profound later.

It is not a permission slip for human institutions now.

A final form of responsibility leakage deserves attention because it will become seductive in an age of superior intelligence.

The machine can become the reason.

A leader makes a controversial decision and says, “The analysis is clear.”

A board closes a facility and says, “The model showed the alternative was unsustainable.”

A government restricts a benefit and says, “The evidence does not support continuation.”

Each statement can be accurate.

It can also conceal who decided what counted as sustainability, what evidence threshold mattered, which harms could be traded, and whose interests defined success.

The intelligence supplies epistemic cover.

The human supplies the hidden norm.

This is the inverse of the moral crumple zone.

There the human absorbs responsibility for machine-shaped decisions.

Here the machine absorbs responsibility for human-shaped values.

Both are forms of leakage.

The cure is the same: make the architecture of authority visible.

Who chose the objective?

Who supplied the facts?

Who had power to decide?

Who was required to explain?

Who could appeal?

Who could stop the system?

Who monitored what happened next?

Who had something to lose if the process harmed people?

Responsibility becomes real when an answer to each question names more than a screen.

A society that cannot answer them will not become morally neutral.

It will become morally foggy.

Decisions will still be made.

People will still suffer and benefit.

Reasons will still appear.

The superior intelligence may produce the most sophisticated ethical analysis civilization has ever seen.

And every participant may still be able to say, with perfect sincerity:

It wasn't really my decision.
