# Chapter 6 evidence notes — The Review Budget

## Chapter job

Make review capacity observable rather than treating attention as an unlimited managerial virtue. The chapter should show that consequential review is budgeted through staffing, allotted hours, queues, and output incentives — and that institutions often make those tradeoffs explicitly even when the resulting quality cost is hard to measure.

The strongest usable case here is patent examination because the review budget is unusually literal: the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office assigns examiners a finite amount of time per application, varies that allowance by technology and seniority, tracks output through a production-count system, and carries a large incoming queue.

## Primary case — a review budget measured in hours

### GAO-25-107218, April 30 / May 7, 2025

GAO reported that USPTO employed **8,944 patent examiners at the end of fiscal year 2024**, received about **527,000 new patent applications** that year, and granted about **365,000 patents**. Examiners search patents and scientific literature and evaluate statutory requirements including novelty, nonobviousness, eligible subject matter, and clear disclosure.

Source: U.S. Government Accountability Office, *Intellectual Property: Patent Office Should Strengthen Its Efforts to Address Persistent Examination and Quality Challenges*, GAO-25-107218, published April 30, 2025 and publicly released May 7, 2025: https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-25-107218

The useful detail is the hour allocation. GAO explains that USPTO sets an expected examination time according to examiner seniority and technological complexity. In GAO's example, the same senior examiner might be allotted **16.6 hours** for an application involving fishing lures and **27.7 hours** for one involving satellite communications. A junior examiner could receive **30.2 hours** for the fishing-lure application because less-experienced examiners receive more time.

This makes the chapter's abstraction concrete. “Review budget” is not a metaphor here. The institution has to decide how many hours of qualified attention one application can consume while hundreds of thousands of other applications wait.

## Output incentives change what the budget feels like

USPTO measures examiner production using “counts” awarded for specified examination actions. GAO reports that a first office action on the merits is worth **1.25 counts**, while later examination activity can be worth up to **0.5 counts**. GAO's interpretation is that the system gives relatively more production credit to initial examination than to later stages, even when later applicant responses create substantial material to review.

GAO's 2025 focus groups with nearly 50 randomly selected examiners found a recurring complaint that time pressure and output goals can crowd thoroughness. Examiners in all six focus groups identified time pressure as a challenge to thorough examination; GAO says roughly 40% of examiner time is spent searching prior art, and some examiners said they could likely find more directly relevant prior art with additional time.

Use carefully:

- These are GAO findings built partly from focus groups and agency survey material, not a controlled estimate that an additional hour causes a specific increase in patent validity.
- The chapter should not convert examiner complaints into a claim that USPTO patents are generally poor quality.
- “Counts” are a production-management mechanism, not evidence that individual examiners neglect quality.

The sharper point is institutional: once review is measured as throughput, a reviewer can be simultaneously responsible for quality and rewarded under a clock that makes additional inquiry expensive.

## Long-running evidence, not a one-year complaint

GAO found the time-pressure concern in earlier work as well. In 2016 it estimated from its patent-examiner survey that **70% of examiners said they did not have enough time to complete a thorough examination given a typical workload**.

Source: GAO-16-490, *Intellectual Property: Patent Office Should Define Quality, Reassess Incentives, and Improve Clarity*, June 30, 2016: https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-16-490

The 2025 report says USPTO increased allotted examination time by an average of about **9% in 2019**, with the amount varying by application complexity and other factors. USPTO reported that office actions found noncompliant with statutory patentability requirements fell from 2,708 in FY2020 to 1,984 in FY2023 — about a **27% decline**.

That observation belongs in the chapter because it pushes against a fatalistic story. Review capacity is not fixed. An institution can buy more time, redesign allocation, improve search, and change the error rate it observes.

But GAO explicitly cautions that USPTO had **not rigorously evaluated the effect of increased examination time on statutory compliance**, so the decline should not be narrated as proof that the 9% time increase caused the 27% reduction.

## Strongest counterargument — more time is not the only way to buy review

USPTO disputes the implication that examination quality can be reduced to allotted hours. In its May 8, 2025 response to GAO, the agency described the problem as a tradeoff among time, quality, and cost and argued that it regularly recalibrates that balance.

Source: USPTO, “USPTO response to GAO report on addressing persistent examination and quality challenges,” May 8, 2025: https://www.uspto.gov/about-us/news-updates/uspto-response-gao-report-addressing-persistent-examination-and-quality

GAO also notes that USPTO has incorporated AI-assisted prior-art search features, including similarity-search tools intended to help examiners identify relevant domestic and foreign patent documents.

This is the strongest disconfirming evidence for a simple “attention only gets scarcer” thesis. Better tooling can make one hour of examination more productive. The right empirical question is therefore not whether review hours are finite — they obviously are — but whether instrumentation, search, triage, and evidence presentation improve *decision-relevant coverage* as fast as incoming complexity and volume grow.

A separate 2026 USPTO process-improvement case makes the same point in a lower-stakes administrative step. The agency reported that it redesigned the Notice of Allowance mailing workflow after a time study, automating some manual steps and reducing average processing time from **25 minutes to 6 minutes per application**, while increasing weekly throughput from roughly 6,000–7,000 to about 15,000 and virtually eliminating that mailing backlog.

Source: USPTO, “USPTO achieves significant progress in reducing patent application Notice of Allowance mailing pendency,” May 27, 2026: https://www.uspto.gov/subscription-center/2026/uspto-achieves-significant-progress-reducing-patent-application-notice

Use this only as a process-design counterexample. Mailing a Notice of Allowance is not the substantive examination that determines patentability. Its value is narrower: queues that look like fixed labor constraints can sometimes collapse when the workflow is redesigned rather than merely staffed up.

## A second institutional comparison — selective review is normal

The SEC's Division of Corporation Finance openly describes its filing-review system as selective. It reviews each reporting company at some level at least once every three years, reviews some filings more frequently, and concentrates staff resources on critical disclosures or apparent accounting and disclosure problems. The SEC also states that its review is **not a guarantee that a filing is complete and accurate**; responsibility remains with the company and others preparing the filing.

Source: SEC, “Filing Review Process,” September 27, 2019: https://www.sec.gov/about/divisions-offices/division-corporation-finance/filing-review-process-corp-fin

This provides a useful contrast with vague “human in the loop” language. Mature review systems do not pretend that every artifact receives exhaustive attention. They specify which classes are reviewed, how deeply, and what the review does *not* certify.

## Mechanism to carry into prose

The useful mechanism is not “workers are overwhelmed.” It is more specific:

1. consequential review consumes a measurable quantity of scarce, qualified time;
2. institutions turn that scarcity into queues, time allowances, sampling rules, and output targets;
3. those rules affect what reviewers can investigate, even when everyone involved wants high quality;
4. better tooling can increase effective review capacity, but only when it improves access to independent, decision-relevant evidence rather than merely increasing the speed of producing another summary;
5. the residual uncertainty should remain visible rather than being hidden behind an undifferentiated “reviewed” state.

For Chapter 6, USPTO is therefore better than a generic burnout anecdote. The institution literally prices attention in hours and counts, and its own record shows both sides of the argument: constrained time can compromise thoroughness, while changed time allocations and better tools can improve the review budget.

## Falsification / what would weaken the chapter

The chapter would be too strong if it implied that more reviewer time always produces better outcomes, that examiner-reported time pressure proves poor patent quality, or that queues are inherently signs of institutional failure.

The strongest countercase is efficient triage and tooling. If an institution can make evidence easier to retrieve, automate low-judgment checks, improve assignment, and reserve expert attention for the parts that require discretion, it can increase effective review capacity without matching production growth hour for hour.

The chapter should therefore argue for **observable allocation**, not maximal review. The important questions are where qualified attention is spent, what evidence it can reach, what work is intentionally sampled or automated, and what consequence the resulting review is authorized to approve.
