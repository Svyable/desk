# Chapter 2 Evidence — Nobody Read the Contract

## Chapter claim

Contracts have long operated without proof that every signer read every term. The law substitutes notice and manifested assent for literal comprehension. Generative drafting does not create that gap, but it can widen it by making tailored clauses, redlines, and versions cheaper to produce than to compare. The useful design response is not universal line-by-line reading. It is to make provenance, deviations, authority, and consequence visible at the point of assent.

## Central case trail

In August 2011, Barnes & Noble advertised discontinued HP TouchPads at a steep discount. Kevin Nguyen ordered two through the retailer's website and received confirmation. The company cancelled the order the next day after unexpectedly high demand. Its terms of use included an arbitration clause and class-action waiver. A link to those terms appeared near the bottom of pages in the transaction, but Nguyen neither clicked it nor affirmatively indicated agreement.

The Ninth Circuit's 2014 opinion treated this as a question of notice and assent, not a test of whether Nguyen had actually read the terms. The court held that the placement of the link, without a prompt or other affirmative act tied to agreement, did not provide sufficient constructive notice. It affirmed the refusal to compel arbitration. The opinion is useful because it shows both sides of the institutional compromise: contracts do not require proof of reading, but a business cannot always turn mere use of a site into agreement to inconspicuous terms.

The Second Circuit reached the other side of that line in *Meyer v. Uber Technologies* in 2017. It found Uber's registration screen reasonably conspicuous and held that pressing the registration button manifested assent. The user did not need to click the linked terms. Together, the cases show that interface design can determine which unread language acquires legal force.

## Scale evidence

Bakos, Marotta-Wurgler, and Trossen observed 48,154 monthly visitors to the websites of ninety software companies. Only one or two shoppers in a thousand accessed the end-user license agreement, and those who did generally spent too little time to read much of it. The result predates current generative systems and blocks the lazy claim that AI invented contractual unreadness.

The American Bar Association's Formal Opinion 512 says lawyers using generative AI retain duties of competence, confidentiality, communication, candor, supervision, and reasonable fees. Its billing example distinguishes a short prompting interval from the professional time reasonably needed to review the output for accuracy and completeness. That distinction matters here: generation time is not review time.

Current legal-drafting products advertise the ability to turn term sheets into first drafts, compare language with playbooks, flag omissions, and suggest replacements. This establishes available capability, not independent proof of accuracy or net time savings.

## Strongest counterclaim

Machine assistance can make contracts more readable and review more discriminating. A system can start from approved language, expose deviations, compare a draft with a playbook, and translate legalese into plainer prose. Martínez, Mollica, and Gibson found that lawyers understood and recalled simplified contract language better and did not regard it as less enforceable. If automation reduces needless variation and directs attention toward changed or consequential terms, it can narrow the comprehension gap.

The chapter must therefore oppose unbounded variation, not automated drafting as such.

## Falsification and kill tests

- If generative drafting is consistently paired with reliable provenance, clause-level comparison, consequence flags, and accountable review, the predicted inspection deficit weakens.
- If evidence shows that bespoke generation replaces rather than adds to contractual volume and materially improves comprehension, revise the chapter's account of scale.
- Do not infer that an unread contract is invalid, deceptive, or inaccurate. Those are separate questions.
- Do not treat product descriptions as performance evaluations.
- Do not generalize from consumer assent cases to every negotiated commercial agreement.

## Draft guardrails

- Say what the court record establishes and keep imagined details out of the TouchPad purchase.
- Preserve the difference between authorship, review, notice, and authority.
- Treat the signature as an allocation of consequence, not a cognitive scan receipt.
- End at the merge button: software review makes the same production-inspection problem visible in a more instrumented environment.
