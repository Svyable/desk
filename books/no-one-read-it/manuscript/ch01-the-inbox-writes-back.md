# The Inbox Writes Back

The first machine-written email most people sent did not feel like machine authorship.

It appeared beneath a message as three short possibilities. A friend confirmed dinner. A colleague answered a question. The software offered a few replies that required no keyboard and very little thought. Tap one and the conversation could move.

When Google researchers described Smart Reply in 2016, the system was already assisting with roughly one in ten mobile replies sent through Inbox by Gmail. It processed hundreds of millions of messages each day, decided which ones seemed to require an answer, generated possible responses, and tried to present alternatives that did not all say the same thing. By the following year, Google reported that the feature accounted for about 12 percent of replies in Inbox on mobile. Smart Reply moved into Gmail.

The achievement was easy to miss because the result was so small.

The machine did not write a legal opinion. It did not prepare a board memorandum or produce a hundred-page report. It saved somebody from typing a sentence whose meaning was already settled. The user remained in control of the send button. The recipient received an ordinary email. Nothing in the exchange announced that a production system had predicted the reply.

That modesty was part of the design. For a short, obvious response, authorship can feel like an unnecessarily grand word. If the honest answer is yes, the useful work is choosing yes. Moving a thumb across eight letters does not add much wisdom.

Smart Reply nevertheless changed the economics of a message. It separated the act of answering from the act of composing an answer. Until then, even the most routine reply demanded a small toll: open the message, summon the words, type them, check them, send. The toll was not large, but it existed every time. The suggestion removed much of it.

This was a gift to the person facing the inbox. It was also an early look at the problem this book is about.

Institutions often mistake a transmitted answer for completed work. The two overlap often enough that the mistake survives. An acknowledgment can close a social loop. A confirmed time can settle a schedule. A support agent can give a customer the instruction that fixes the problem. In those cases, making the reply cheaper makes the work cheaper.

Other messages move the work without finishing it.

A reply can accept an obligation, ask for another document, widen the list of recipients, introduce a new condition, move responsibility to a colleague, or postpone the decision with language polished enough to resemble progress. The inbox records all of these as activity. It does not know which one resolved the reason the thread existed.

That difference was present long before artificial intelligence. Offices have always circulated memoranda that avoided decisions. Managers have always copied people who did not need to be involved. Anyone who has watched a scheduling exchange survive its seventh reply knows that human beings do not require machine assistance to turn communication into weather.

The machine changes the price.

Researchers have been able to observe one part of this change in controlled conversations. In a study published in 2023, Jess Hohenstein and colleagues paired participants in a messaging application and randomly varied who had access to algorithmic reply suggestions. The pairs discussed a policy issue. Smart replies made up 14.3 percent of the messages sent, and making the suggestions available increased the rate of messaging by 10.2 percent.

The conversations did not simply become faster versions of themselves. The suggested language altered their emotional tone. Actual use of smart replies could make partners seem more cooperative and increase feelings of affiliation. Yet participants tended to judge a partner more harshly when they suspected that person of using automated replies, whether or not the suspicion was accurate.

The software entered the relationship in two different ways. Its words affected the exchange. The idea of its presence affected how people interpreted the person on the other side.

These were short experimental conversations, not months of workplace email. Messages per minute are not profit, insight, or a decision correctly made. Still, the result catches something that a time-saving demonstration cannot. A suggested reply does not only reduce effort inside an unchanged conversation. It can change the conversation's speed, language, and social meaning.

Once the cost of a response falls, more responses fit inside the same time.

Whether that is useful depends on what the responses do.

The modern office entered this experiment with no shortage of messages. In a 2025 report based on aggregated Microsoft 365 activity, Microsoft said the average worker in its data received 117 emails a day, most skimmed in less than a minute. Mass emails to at least twenty recipients had risen while one-to-one threads declined. Teams messages added another stream. At the busiest end of the distribution, meetings, email, and notifications arrived often enough to fracture the day into intervals too small for much thought.

The report came from a company selling tools meant to relieve this pressure, including generative tools. Its data excluded education customers and European Union tenants, and some of its most dramatic interruption figures described the fifth of users receiving the most pings. It should not be mistaken for a census of every office worker on Earth.

The scale is still useful because it shows the environment into which automated composition arrived. The inbox was not empty, waiting for artificial intelligence to make it productive. It was already a crowded allocation system for attention.

Volume does not describe the whole burden.

In 2016, Gloria Mark and colleagues reported a workplace study that followed forty information workers for twelve workdays. The researchers combined computer logs with daily surveys and physiological measures. Longer daily stretches spent on email were associated with lower perceived productivity and higher measured stress. The details resisted the easy advice usually attached to research about inboxes. People who interrupted themselves to check email differed from people pulled in by notifications. Batching was associated with higher perceived productivity when email took longer, but the researchers did not find evidence that batching lowered stress.

The study was small and observational. It could not prove that email caused every change it measured. Its value here lies partly in its refusal to produce one magic rule. An inbox exerts pressure through duration, interruption, habit, anticipation, and the meaning of what waits inside it. Two workers can receive the same number of messages and inhabit different workdays.

One has ten notices that can be archived without thought. Another has ten short requests from people who can change a budget, a deadline, or a career. One checks a queue at chosen intervals. Another sees a subject line appear during every difficult paragraph. Counting messages treats these inboxes as equal.

Generative systems can reduce several parts of the burden. They can group routine notices, summarize threads, identify requests, and help the worker remain inside the task that mattered before the notification arrived. They can also make a consequential message resemble the routine notices around it. A confident summary and a polished draft response may reduce the felt difference between classifying a request and deciding it.

Attention is not consumed only while words are on the screen. An unresolved message can survive as an intention: reply after the meeting, check the figure, ask legal, find the attachment, decide whether silence will be interpreted as consent. The inbox stores these intentions in rows. The worker stores some of them elsewhere.

Every message proposes a small transfer.

The sender spends time producing it. The recipient spends time deciding what it means. Sometimes the exchange is fair. The sender has done the hard work of making a question precise, and the recipient can answer quickly. Sometimes the sender exports the hard work. A vague request takes seconds to send and leaves the recipient to discover the question, assemble the missing context, and guess what would count as done.

Email hides this transfer because each side sees a different object. The sender sees a completed draft. The recipient sees an interruption with an unknown interior.

The imbalance grows with the recipient list. One carefully written note sent to twenty people may be a good use of time. One careless note sent to twenty people creates twenty occasions to open, classify, ignore, forward, answer, or wonder whether somebody else will answer. The cost of adding the twentieth recipient is almost invisible to the sender. It is not invisible to the twentieth recipient.

Generative tools can improve either side of the exchange. They can help a sender clarify the request, shorten the note, and include the context recipients would otherwise have to hunt down. They can help a recipient summarize the thread, find an attachment, draft a response, and recognize that no response is required. Because the same machine can increase and decrease the burden, the button itself tells us almost nothing about the system's effect.

The most important early evidence does not support a simple story of growing overload.

Between 2023 and 2024, Microsoft worked with 66 large firms on a six-month randomized experiment involving 7,137 knowledge workers. Some workers received access to Microsoft 365 Copilot, integrated into the applications they already used for email, meetings, and documents. Others continued with their existing tools. The researchers could observe product activity but not the contents of the work.

In the later months of the experiment, workers assigned access to Copilot spent about 1.4 fewer hours each week in Outlook, a reduction of 12 percent from the earlier average. The estimate for workers whose use was induced by receiving access was about two hours. They also spent less time in work applications outside regular hours.

They did not reply to more email threads.

That finding deserves more weight than a prediction about what automated offices might someday do. The workers saved time in email without a measurable increase in the number of threads they answered. Their close colleagues did not appear to inherit a compensating increase in email work. At least in this experiment, the tool made an existing workload less consuming.

There are limits. The firms were large, interested in early adoption, and heavy users of Microsoft products. The researchers could not see work outside the applications they tracked, read the messages, or directly measure output quality. Copilot use stabilized at a modest level rather than becoming continuous. The experiment captured individual access during an early period, not an organization rebuilt around communicating agents.

Yet those limits do not erase the result. They locate it.

At the level of the individual inbox, generative assistance can give time back. It can do so without provoking a visible burst of replies. Any account of the future of communication that ignores this is not caution. It is nostalgia with a technical vocabulary.

The concern begins one level above the individual.

Saved time has no permanent owner.

A worker can take it home. The Microsoft experiment found some reduction in after-hours activity, suggesting that at least part of the gain remained with workers. A team can spend it on work that had been neglected. A company can treat it as capacity for more customers, more analysis, more documentation, or more frequent updates. A manager can raise the expected response rate because responding is now easier. A software product can turn the saving into a new feature that generates still more material for somebody elsewhere to inspect.

Each choice can be reasonable. Together they determine whether automation empties the queue or enlarges the institution that feeds it.

The arithmetic of a queue depends on both sides. How quickly can work be handled? How quickly is new work admitted? A tool can improve the first number while a company changes the second. If writing a proposal once took a day and now takes an hour, the immediate gain is real. If the organization responds by requesting eight proposals where it once requested one, the person choosing among them may receive no gain at all.

This does not mean the organization was wrong to ask for more options. One of the additional proposals may be much better. Cheaper exploration can improve decisions. It means productivity cannot be inferred from the speed of the producing step alone. The relevant result sits farther downstream, after selection, review, coordination, and action.

Email makes the accounting unusually slippery because a message can be both an output and an input. My completed reply becomes your new arrival. Your efficient response becomes my next item. Each dashboard can show productive activity while the shared thread remains open.

The difference becomes clearer in customer support. Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li, and Lindsey Raymond studied the introduction of a generative assistant used by more than 5,000 support agents. In the published study, access to the system increased productivity by roughly 15 percent. The largest gains went to less experienced and lower-skilled workers. Customers' sentiment improved, and the evidence suggested that the tool helped spread practices associated with stronger agents.

The researchers measured issues resolved per hour.

That choice of outcome matters more than it first appears. A support conversation has a recognizable reason for existing. The customer cannot log in, a charge looks wrong, a device will not work, or an order has not arrived. The case may be difficult and the resolution may be imperfect, but the operation has a concept of closure. A fast reply that leaves the customer confused does not count the same way as an issue resolved.

The assistant helped move work toward the thing the institution actually wanted.

Many office messages lack that discipline. A strategy thread can continue because nobody has authority to choose. A request for comments can gather more comments than the author can reconcile. A status update can prompt requests for another status update. A document can circulate among people who each add language while assuming somebody else is responsible for deciding what the document is for.

In such systems, measuring messages sent rewards motion. Measuring response time rewards speed. Measuring drafts completed rewards production. The unresolved obligation remains off the dashboard because it is harder to count.

The inbox is a user interface laid over those obligations. It makes unlike things look alike.

A note from a friend and a notice from a regulator arrive in rectangles of similar size. A machine-generated sales approach sits beside a message from a person whose job depends on the answer. A receipt, a threat, an invitation, a promise, and a request for judgment all present the same small set of controls: reply, forward, archive, delete.

The recipient supplies the missing classification.

This is why reducing the time required to write is not the same as reducing the time required to communicate. Composition is only one stage. A person still has to decide whether the message deserves an answer, what information it changes, which promise can be made, who possesses authority, and whether sending words will close the issue or merely move it.

For routine exchanges, the machine can often help with all of this. A scheduling assistant can compare calendars and settle a time without forcing two people to perform clerical diplomacy. A system can recognize a receipt, update a record, and suppress a reply nobody needs. A support tool can retrieve the relevant procedure and help an agent solve the case. Good automation removes the conversation when the conversation is only overhead.

Trouble appears when the words are easy and the decision is not.

Consider a request to approve a project. A generative system can turn sparse notes into a complete memo. It can add a market summary, enumerate risks, anticipate objections, and prepare an executive version. Another system can summarize the memo for each recipient. A third can draft the questions those recipients might ask. The project has acquired the documentary surface of mature consideration.

The underlying decisions have not necessarily become easier. Someone must still judge the demand, the evidence, the competing use of money, the organization's ability to deliver, and the consequences of being wrong. More complete language can help that judgment. It can also make the distance between a thin premise and a consequential approval harder to feel.

The sender experiences leverage. The recipient experiences a claim on attention.

Before generative systems, producing a long, polished request usually imposed some cost on the person making it. That cost did not guarantee quality. People produced dreadful documents slowly. But the effort discouraged at least some marginal documents and forced the writer to encounter choices along the way. A writer had to decide which facts belonged, how sections connected, and where the argument weakened.

Now the marginal version is cheap. The sender can ask for a longer draft, another tone, five alternatives, a comparison table, a summary, and a tailored note to each stakeholder. The artifacts may all be useful. They also create more surfaces at which somebody can misunderstand, disagree, or feel obliged to respond.

The cost has not vanished. It has moved.

It may move into computation, which is inexpensive and getting less expensive, or into the recipient's reading time. It can reappear during verification, when a settled-looking claim must be traced to its source, and during coordination, when several plausible versions need to be reconciled. In the worst case it moves all the way to consequence, where a fluent document becomes expensive only after the institution acts on it.

The larger problem waits inside that movement.

An easy response can also preserve ambiguity that a harder response would have forced into the open. Human beings have long used polite language for this purpose. A person can acknowledge a request without accepting it, praise an idea without funding it, or thank a colleague for a document without revealing that the attachment remains unopened. Suggested language makes this easier, but it did not invent the instinct.

There is even a social reason to welcome the help. The Hohenstein experiments found that smart replies could make conversation more positive and cooperative. Routine warmth has value. A quick acknowledgment can reassure a colleague that a message arrived. A kind response can prevent avoidable friction. Insisting that every small courtesy be handcrafted would preserve effort, not authenticity.

The difficult question is what the courtesy appears to certify.

If a reply says that a document looks good, did the sender read it? If it says the proposed approach makes sense, which part was evaluated? If it says the sender will handle the issue, has responsibility actually moved? Office language survives by leaving these boundaries soft. Automation can multiply the number of soft assurances a person can issue before noticing how much has been promised.

The send button remains human-sized.

That fact supplies comfort. A person still approves the draft. A person can edit it. A person owns the account and appears in the From line. The system has not removed the human; it has placed the human at the end of a production process that may have taken seconds.

But pressing Send proves very little about what happened before it.

The person may have written every word, edited a generated draft carefully, skimmed it for tone, checked only the names, or accepted it because it looked ordinary. The recipient usually cannot tell which. More important, the sender's own organization may not know. A familiar ritual—human approval—now covers a widening range of cognitive involvement.

For a dinner confirmation, the distinction is comic. For a promise about price, delivery, safety, employment, compliance, or legal rights, it becomes the beginning of a different problem.

The first generation of suggested replies kept close to exchanges whose answer could fit on a button. Contemporary assistants can summarize long threads, retrieve context, compose full messages, and prepare responses in a requested style. This is real progress. It is also a movement from helping express a decision toward helping construct the document in which the decision appears.

As the machine does more, the moment of human approval becomes thinner unless the surrounding process changes with it.

That process may change. Organizations can distinguish drafts from commitments. They can require evidence for material claims, route financial promises through systems with explicit authority, and let machines close low-risk loops without pretending a person reviewed each word. They can measure cases closed instead of messages sent. They can protect time for the decisions that remain.

They can also do what institutions often do with a productivity gain: keep the old approval ritual, increase the expected volume, and call the result efficiency.

The next stage is easy to imagine because parts of it already exist. A sender asks an assistant to prepare a request. A recipient's assistant summarizes it and drafts a response. The first assistant reads the response, notices an unresolved condition, and produces a follow-up. The exchange continues at machine speed until it reaches a choice the systems cannot make, a permission they do not possess, or a conflict their instructions conceal.

This could be excellent automation. If the assistants negotiate a meeting time, reconcile a routine invoice, or close a support case, the humans have been spared an exchange that never required them. The machines have not flooded the organization. They have removed clerical weather.

If the exchange generates six polished descriptions of an unresolved disagreement, the humans inherit a larger documentary record of the fact that nobody decided. The exact count matters less than the direction of the system.

When both sides can write, writing ceases to ration the conversation. Authority, evidence, attention, and the willingness to commit become the limits. The inbox can carry more traffic than the institution can convert into decisions.

At that point, the familiar productivity question—how much time did the tool save while drafting a reply?—is too local. The institution must ask what happened to the work after the reply left.

Did the message close a loop?

Did it transfer a fact?

Did it create an obligation?

Did anybody understand the obligation well enough to accept it?

These questions lead away from email. The inbox is only the first place the new ratio becomes visible because messages are small, frequent, and socially easy to send. Contracts, software changes, scientific papers, financial analyses, and policy memoranda carry the same imbalance with heavier consequences. Their production is becoming cheaper. Their acceptance still depends on a person, committee, court, market, or institution that can be bound by what the artifact says.

The earliest Smart Reply asked the user to choose among three short answers. The user knew the question and could see the whole proposed response.

The next artifact will not fit on a button.

It will end with a signature.
