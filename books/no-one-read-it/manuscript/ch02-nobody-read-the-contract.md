# Nobody Read the Contract

In August 2011, Barnes & Noble put discontinued HP TouchPads on sale. Kevin Nguyen ordered two through the retailer's website. The order went through far enough for him to receive confirmation. The next day, Barnes & Noble cancelled it, citing unexpectedly high demand.

The dispute that followed turned on language far removed from the tablets themselves. Near the bottom of the website was a link to the company's terms of use. Inside those terms were an arbitration clause and a waiver of the right to bring a class action. Barnes & Noble argued that by using the site, Nguyen had accepted them.

He had not clicked the link. He had not read the terms. He had not checked a box saying that he agreed.

The factual record gave the court unusually little cognitive activity to interpret. There was no question about whether Nguyen had understood the arbitration clause, because he had not seen it. There was only the interface and the action: a link on the page, an order placed, and a claim that one meant assent to the other.

The Ninth Circuit declined to make that claim. In its 2014 opinion, the court held that the placement of the link, without a conspicuous prompt or an affirmative act tied to agreement, did not provide sufficient notice. It affirmed the district court's refusal to compel arbitration. The governing ideas were old ones—notice and mutual assent—applied to a website that made buying a tablet much easier than discovering the rules attached to the purchase.

The opinion did not establish a right to remain untouched by unread terms. Three years later, the Second Circuit considered the registration screen in Uber's mobile app. The notice about terms was close to the button that completed registration. The terms themselves were linked rather than displayed. The court found the screen reasonably conspicuous and held that pressing the button manifested assent.

The user did not have to open the link. The user did not have to read the contract. The difference was that the interface made the legal meaning of the next action visible enough.

This is one of the quiet accommodations on which modern commerce depends. A contract can be binding without becoming a memory in either signer's mind. A court can ask whether a person had notice and acted in a way that expressed agreement. It ordinarily cannot demand proof that each sentence was read, reconstructed, compared with alternatives, and understood. If it did, much ordinary contracting would stop.

The signature has never been a receipt issued by the brain.

It performs another function. It allows an institution to proceed. The document fixes language, identifies parties, records an action, and allocates what happens next. It makes commitment observable even when comprehension is not. The gap is tolerable because other arrangements narrow the range of surprise: law constrains some terms, custom makes others predictable, lawyers reuse known language, regulators require disclosures, negotiators focus on material changes, and courts sometimes refuse to enforce what was too well hidden.

None of these arrangements guarantees reading. They make complete reading less necessary.

The evidence from online shopping is blunt. In a study of 48,154 monthly visitors to ninety software-company websites, Yannis Bakos, Florencia Marotta-Wurgler, and David Trossen found that only one or two shoppers in a thousand accessed the end-user license agreement. Those who did usually did not remain long enough to read much of it. This was not a study of language models or automated negotiation. It described the human bargain years before generative drafting became an ordinary product feature: transact now, leave the possible dispute to a document almost nobody opens.

It is tempting to treat this as a story about lazy consumers and legal prose designed to repel them. Both may matter, but neither is the whole mechanism. Reading every agreement is often a poor use of the reader's time. A person who installs an application, joins a wireless network, rents a car, buys a train ticket, creates a bank account, and updates a phone could spend hours inspecting terms while gaining little ability to negotiate them. Attention is costly. The likely influence of that attention on the offered deal is close to zero.

The rational response is a rough form of sampling. People notice the price, the duration, the cancellation policy, perhaps the permissions. They use prior experience to assume that the rest belongs to a familiar class. They read more when the stakes are high or something looks strange. Most of the time, the arrangement works well enough that the unread language stays inert.

Commercial lawyers use a more elaborate version of the same method. They begin with templates, playbooks, precedent agreements, checklists, and the reputations of the parties. They focus on deviations. They may divide a document among specialists. Tax reads tax. Privacy reads data rights. Employment reads the provisions that could change who counts as an employee. The partner does not prove authorship by retyping the clauses. The value of the review lies in knowing which language is ordinary, which is altered, which risks interact, and who has authority to accept them.

This is why a redline is such an important legal artifact. It is not merely a document with colored marks. It is an attempt to convert an unbounded reading problem into a bounded comparison. What changed? Was the change intended? What else does it affect? A good redline does not make the old language true or the new language fair. It gives scarce attention somewhere to land.

Generative drafting enters a system already built around selective comprehension. Its immediate attraction is easy to see. A lawyer can describe a transaction and receive a first draft. A term sheet can become provisions. A clause can be shortened, made more favorable, adapted to a jurisdiction, or compared with a playbook. The work product can arrive with tracked changes and annotations. Drafting tools can flag missing language or propose replacements.

Some of this is a genuine improvement. There is no virtue in spending an afternoon hunting for a familiar clause or manually carrying defined terms from one version to another. If a machine starts with approved language, exposes every departure, and reliably points a reviewer toward unusual consequences, it may reduce both drafting time and review time. It may make the document easier to understand.

That last possibility deserves more credit than it usually receives. Legal language is often defended as if complexity were the price of precision. Sometimes it is. But complexity can also be inherited, copied, or performed. Eric Martínez, Francis Mollica, and Edward Gibson found that lawyers, not only lay readers, had more difficulty understanding and recalling contracts written in legalese. The lawyers in their studies rated simplified versions more highly and did not regard them as less enforceable. Expertise did not turn dense syntax into a preferred native language.

A tool that helps a lawyer say the same thing more plainly can increase the number of real readers. A tool that detects conflicting definitions can prevent a mistake no human wants to make. A tool that compares a draft with an approved playbook can make variation visible before it becomes obligation. Faster production and better comprehension are not opposites.

But the same capability can be used to produce the opposite shape of work. It can make every agreement look individually considered. It can generate five tailored alternatives where one standard clause would have done. It can incorporate a term-sheet change across a hundred pages, revise cross-references, and return a document whose surface gives no hint about the number of new decisions inside it. The draft can become longer, more specific, and more responsive without becoming easier for anyone to hold in mind.

Before generative systems, bespoke language carried a weak but useful signal. It cost something to create. A counterparty receiving a heavily revised agreement could infer that someone had spent time making those changes, though not necessarily that the changes were wise. When the marginal cost of plausible variation falls, that signal fades. A clause may look negotiated because it is unusually specific. In fact, the specificity may be the cheapest part.

The production event and the comprehension event separate.

This separation is easy to miss because legal work records activity so thoroughly. The document has an author. Changes have timestamps. Comments have initials. The file goes through statuses—draft, under review, approved, executed. A matter-management system can show that the document visited everyone it was meant to visit. The finished agreement bears names with authority.

Yet each record answers a different question. Who caused this language to appear? Who had access to it? Who was asked for advice? Who was allowed to commit the organization? None necessarily answers: who formed an adequate model of the agreement as a whole?

The distinction matters when a draft is assembled from outputs produced in several places. One system proposes the liability language. Another summarizes data obligations. A comparison tool says the document is mostly consistent with a playbook. A lawyer checks the redline. A business executive reads a one-page summary. The board approves the transaction described in a presentation. Each step can be competently performed. Still, the whole can acquire authority without becoming the object of any one person's comprehension.

That does not make the agreement fictitious. It makes comprehension distributed and conditional. The privacy lawyer may understand the data provisions and assume finance has examined the payment mechanics. Finance may understand the price and assume outside counsel has checked termination. Outside counsel may identify a departure but accept a business instruction not to reopen it. The executive may rely on the absence of an escalated warning. The signature closes the chain of reliance.

This is how consequential institutions normally work. No surgeon personally manufactures the instruments. No pilot audits the source code in the aircraft. No chief executive recomputes every number in an acquisition model. Authority depends on trusted divisions of labor.

The new risk is not division itself. It is a change in the ratio between the work generated inside the chain and the independent attention available to test it. If each participant can produce more analysis, more variants, and more explanations for every other participant, the chain can become saturated with apparently helpful material. Everyone receives more evidence of activity and less evidence that somebody checked the important thing.

Professional duties do not disappear because a tool made the draft. The American Bar Association's guidance on generative AI makes this plain. A lawyer remains responsible for competence, confidentiality, communication, candor, supervision, and reasonable fees. In discussing fees, the guidance offers a revealing distinction: a lawyer may spend only a short time prompting a system, but may still need to spend professional time reviewing the result for accuracy and completeness.

The prompt can take fifteen minutes. The obligation cannot be reduced to those fifteen minutes merely because the language arrived quickly.

This is a difficult fact for any business model built around visible output. Drafting creates a file that can be delivered. Review often creates an absence: no inconsistency, no unacceptable deviation, no hidden consequence. When a machine makes the file arrive sooner, the client can reasonably expect some savings. But if the document still requires sustained inspection, the expensive portion may simply move. The bill no longer records the labor of forming sentences. It records the labor of deciding whether those sentences should govern anyone.

There will be pressure to disguise that movement. A reviewer may be given the same deadline for a larger document. A tool may summarize its own draft. A workflow may count an automated comparison as a completed control without testing what the comparison misses. A lawyer may receive a confidence-inducing explanation for language that was itself generated from an uncertain source. The visible stack of assistance can grow while the independent base stays thin.

The dangerous contract is not necessarily the one containing a bizarre hallucination. Strange language is often easier to notice. It invites a question. More difficult is the clause that is grammatical, locally plausible, and subtly wrong for the transaction: a definition that includes the wrong affiliate, an obligation triggered by notice rather than receipt, a limitation that fails to cover the claim most likely to arise, a governing rule borrowed from a neighboring jurisdiction. It resembles the language around it. It survives a quick read precisely because it reads well.

Then there is interaction. A provision may be acceptable alone and damaging when combined with another provision fifty pages away. A summary can describe both accurately and fail to surface the interaction. A clause-level reviewer can approve each component while nobody simulates the agreement as a system.

Contracts have always had this problem. Generative systems change how much plausible interacting language can be created before the reviewer gets tired.

The practical answer cannot be that every signer must read every word. That standard would be both dishonest and wasteful. Nor can the answer be a mandatory human glance, as if eyesight transferred responsibility into knowledge. A person can move through a document and retain less than a well-designed comparison system exposes in a second.

The better controls describe what the institution needs to know before consequence attaches.

Where did the language come from? Which parts depart from approved precedent? Which facts supplied to the drafting system remain unverified? Which clauses changed after specialist review? Which obligations are novel, irreversible, unusually expensive, or coupled to another provision? Which automated checks are independent of the generator, and what classes of error can they detect? Who owns the unresolved questions? What event should stop execution?

These are not demands for total comprehension. They are ways to preserve the path from authority back to examination.

Standardization is especially valuable here. If an organization has confidence in a well-tested template, a machine should not casually improve it. The approved clause may be inelegant but known. Its interpretation may be supported by experience. Variation should spend review budget. The more consequential the departure, the stronger the evidence it should carry.

This reverses a common expectation about intelligent tools. The impressive system produces a beautiful new clause on request. The useful system sometimes refuses to produce one. It retrieves the approved language, explains that the requested outcome conflicts with the playbook, and makes the person with authority choose. It treats novelty not as a demonstration of capability but as a claim on somebody else's time.

Plain language should work the same way. Simplification is not a cosmetic pass applied after decisions are made. If a provision cannot be restated without disagreement about what it means, that disagreement is information. The attempt to make the contract readable has exposed a decision the formal wording concealed.

The same is true of summaries. A summary can be an excellent routing device. It can tell the tax lawyer which sections changed and the executive which obligations cross an agreed threshold. It becomes dangerous when it replaces the ability to return to the underlying text. Every important assertion in the summary should remain connected to the clause from which it came, every clause to its provenance, and every material change to the person who accepted it.

This is how a signature can remain meaningful without pretending to certify impossible mental work. The signer need not know every sentence. The signer needs reason to trust the process that selected what required attention, evidence that the selection occurred, and visibility into the exceptions that survived it. Authority can rest on a chain of review, but the chain must be more than a sequence of approvals.

The Barnes & Noble case was about a consumer and a link. Its lesson is not that a conspicuous button solves comprehension. A better interface can make assent legally effective while the agreement stays unread. But the cases reveal where institutions already place the burden: not on proving an internal state, but on designing the moment at which action gains meaning.

Generative work makes that design problem urgent. When language is scarce, the document itself commands a certain attention. When language is abundant, consequence must command it instead.

Software engineers have a button for this moment. A proposed change can be generated, explained, tested, discussed, and revised. Eventually someone with authority chooses whether it joins the code on which other work depends. The button does not say that every line was understood. It says the change is allowed to become part of the system.

The button says merge.
