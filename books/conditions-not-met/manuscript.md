# CONDITIONS NOT MET

## How an impossible gravity experiment became a twenty-year lesson in replication

### Chapter 1 — The Pipe Smoke

The story begins with smoke, which is exactly the kind of detail that should make you suspicious.

Not equations. Not a torsion balance sealed behind three layers of thermal isolation. Smoke from a pipe, according to Eugene Podkletnov's later account, rising strangely above a cold ceramic disk in Tampere.

There are facts in this story. There are recollections. There are papers. There are papers that disappeared. There are machines that were built. There are machines that were almost built. There are null results. There are null results obtained from machines that were not the machine. There is NASA.

And somewhere inside that mess is a question much better than *Did somebody invent antigravity in Finland?*

The better question is: **What counts as having checked?**

That question sounds bureaucratic until the claim is impossible.

In 1992 E. E. Podkletnov and R. Nieminen published a short paper in *Physica C* titled “A possibility of gravitational force shielding by bulk YBa2Cu3O7-x superconductor.” That sentence contains enough explosive material for an airport novel. Gravity. Shielding. A high-temperature superconductor. The suggestion that an object above the apparatus might weigh slightly less.

General relativity does not contain a handy knob marked GRAVITY OFF.

So the prior probability should be brutal.

Good.

Brutal priors are useful. They stop us wasting decades on perpetual-motion machines and badly grounded bathroom scales. They do not, however, alter what happened in an experiment. A prior tells you how much evidence you should demand. It does not grant permission to replace an experiment you dislike with one that is easier to build.

That distinction became the whole story.

NASA Marshall Space Flight Center and collaborators later tested superconductors with precision gravimetry. One published configuration put large bulk YBCO ceramics inside a three-phase AC motor stator producing a rotating magnetic field. The investigators reported no effect attributable to the rotating field, thermal environment or disk rotation within their observational precision. That is evidence. Serious evidence.

It is not identical to saying: *Podkletnov's apparatus was reproduced and the reported effect vanished.*

Those sentences are different.

The difference is where this book lives.

A 2001 experiment by Clive Woods and colleagues also reported a null result. But the replication literature itself records a maddening qualifier: several conditions specified for the claimed effect were not fulfilled, including aspects of the superconducting material and the levitating/rotating system.

This does not mean Podkletnov was right.

Read that sentence twice because an entire cottage industry of fringe science depends on people skipping it.

A failed imperfect replication does not turn an extraordinary claim into a true one. It leaves an extraordinary claim inadequately confirmed while shrinking some portion of its available parameter space. That is less satisfying than either side wants.

The believer wants vindication.

The skeptic wants burial.

The experiment owes neither of them a funeral.

What makes this case unusually valuable is that superconducting ceramics are not conceptual abstractions. They crack. They have grain boundaries. Their fabrication history matters. Magnetic fields have geometry. Rotors have speeds. Cryostats boil things. Gravimeters respond to environments. A thirty-centimeter disk is not a three-inch disk merely because both contain YBCO. A static superconductor is not a rotating composite superconductor. A rotating magnetic field is not necessarily a rotating material body. And the sentence “we could not reproduce all the claimed conditions” has an experimental meaning whether or not the original claim is nonsense.

This is where frontier science gets socially weird.

Extraordinary claims attract two forms of laziness that look like opposites and behave almost identically. Believers lower the evidentiary bar because the claim is exciting. Skeptics sometimes lower the replication bar because the claim is embarrassing.

Both are ways of deciding before measuring.

The correct response to an alleged two-percent change in weight above a superconducting disk is not reverence. It is cruelty.

Build the damn thing.

Instrument everything.

Blind the operators.

Measure vibration, helium flow, pressure, temperature gradients, electromagnetic coupling, buoyancy, acoustic force, rotor state, field harmonics and gravimeter drift. Put sham disks through the same choreography. Change one variable at a time. Pre-register the analysis. Let hostile laboratories run the protocol. Publish the raw data.

And if the effect disappears, kill it properly.

There is dignity in a well-conducted execution.

Instead the historical record became stranger. NASA records show not only null measurements but continuing attempts to understand and fabricate the relevant superconducting hardware. A 2002 NASA fellowship report described the earlier Podkletnov work, noted that the experiments had not been successfully repeated, discussed the difficulty of producing stable fired disks, and treated fabrication as an unresolved engineering problem. Later reviews describe Podkletnov consulting on replication efforts and emphasize that published tests did not reproduce every feature of the original setup.

Again: none of this is positive evidence for gravity modification.

It is evidence that the phrase *NASA failed to replicate it* contains less information than it appears to.

Then the story gets worse.

Podkletnov moved from shielding claims to an alleged impulse device: high-voltage discharges through superconducting ceramic, a reported narrow repulsive effect on distant masses, composition-independent within stated error. The claim is so violent against ordinary expectation that it should trigger an almost comical level of experimental suspicion. Mechanical impulse. Electromagnetic coupling. Acoustic shock. Electrostatic effects. Instrument response. Selection. Analysis. Fraud. Everything goes on the table before gravity does.

But this later claim also created the perfect epistemic trap. The apparatus became more specialized, the voltages nastier, the ceramics harder, independent access poorer, and the gulf between “nobody reproduced it” and “nobody reproduced the apparatus” wider.

A phenomenon that requires a secret violin is indistinguishable, for practical science, from a violin that does not play.

That is not a philosophical joke. Reproducibility is part of what turns a private event into public knowledge.

So this book will not rescue antigravity from the wastebasket.

It will inspect the wastebasket.

Because sometimes the history of science hides its most useful lessons not among the discoveries but among the things everyone agrees are dead without agreeing on which experiment killed them.

Podkletnov may simply have been wrong.

The scales may have moved because cold machines are treacherous machines.

The stronger later effects may be artifacts, errors or worse.

But if we are going to say that, we should earn the sentence.

The price is a machine.

Not a mood.

Not a consensus.

A machine.

## Architecture

### Part I — The Claim
2. **A Ceramic That Should Not Matter** — YBCO, Meissner physics, flux pinning and why none of established superconductivity predicts gravitational screening.
3. **0.3 Percent** — reconstruct the 1992 Physica C experiment claim by claim, apparatus by apparatus.
4. **Two Percent** — the stronger withdrawn/unpublished-follow-up ecology, authorship controversy and what can actually be established.

### Part II — The Replications
5. **NASA Builds a Disk** — Marshall/UAH, Koczor, Noever, ceramic fabrication and the institutional reason to test ridiculous-looking propulsion claims.
6. **The Null Result** — what NASA actually measured, precision limits and environmental confounds.
7. **Conditions Not Met** — Woods/Sheffield and the difference between a failed replication and an experiment that closes only part of parameter space.
8. **The Missing Machine** — a parameter-by-parameter replication matrix: composition, microstructure, diameter, temperature, levitation, rotation, AC field, RPM, vacuum, sensor geometry.

### Part III — When a Claim Becomes Hard to Kill
9. **The Secret Violin Problem** — tacit knowledge, bespoke materials and when irreproducibility becomes fatal regardless of truth.
10. **NASA Did Not Discover Antigravity** — how institutional interest mutates into folklore.
11. **The Impulse** — Podkletnov/Modanese's later high-voltage claim, separated ruthlessly from the earlier shielding experiment.
12. **Every Ordinary Force First** — electrostatic, electromagnetic, acoustic, mechanical, thermal and instrumentation alternatives.

### Part IV — The Larger Disease
13. **Replication Is Not a Verb** — fidelity, conceptual replication, direct replication and parameter closure.
14. **Extraordinary Claims Need Better Skeptics** — why high priors against a result justify stronger experiments, not weaker ones.
15. **The Classified Escape Hatch** — how secrecy and alleged aerospace interest can make an evidentiary ecology impossible to falsify.
16. **Foreign Materiel for Physics** — borrow the logic of technical exploitation: preserve provenance, reproduce hardware, characterize before theorizing.
17. **How to Kill Antigravity** — the definitive modern adversarial experiment, designed so the claimant cannot move the goalposts afterward.
18. **What Counts as Checked** — the book's real subject: how civilization decides an anomalous result has been given enough reality to fail.
