# The Missing Uranium

A scientific mystery can begin with a deficit too small to make a good photograph.

Uranium ore from the Oklo region of Gabon entered the nuclear fuel system in 1972 and failed a boring expectation. The proportion of uranium-235 was lower than the normal terrestrial value. Uranium chemistry varies. Ore grade varies wildly. Isotopic abundance, absent a nuclear process, is supposed to be much more stubborn. The missing fraction mattered because uranium-235 is the isotope that readily sustains the kind of thermal-neutron chain reaction used in ordinary power reactors.

There were explanations available that did not require a Precambrian nuclear plant. Measurement error always gets first rights. Contamination is never glamorous but pays rent. Isotope fractionation can occur in nature. Yet the investigation did not stop at the initial anomaly. The ore carried fission-product patterns that made the bookkeeping increasingly difficult to explain without actual fission.

This is the first discipline the Oklo story imposes on every strange claim that follows in this book. An anomaly is not an explanation. It is an invitation to make the accounting harder.

The natural reactors were possible because uranium two billion years ago was not isotopically identical to uranium now. Uranium-235 has a shorter half-life than uranium-238. Run the clock backward and the fissile isotope becomes a larger fraction of natural uranium. Around the time the Oklo deposits went critical, the fraction was in the neighborhood of the enrichment used in some modern reactor fuels. That did not make every uranium deposit a reactor. Geometry, concentration, neutron economy and moderation still mattered. Oklo was a conjunction, not an inevitability.

The water is what makes the story feel engineered even though it was not. Groundwater moving through sufficiently rich uranium ore could slow neutrons. Slow neutrons were more effective at sustaining fission in uranium-235. Fission heated the system. Heat could remove the water as steam. Lose the moderator and the chain reaction weakened. Cool the rock, let water return, and the reaction could resume.

Later isotopic work made this dynamic more than a tidy cartoon. In 2004 Alexander Meshik, Charles Hohenberg and Olga Pravdivtseva reported in *Physical Review Letters* that xenon isotope patterns preserved in mineral phases were consistent with cycling: roughly thirty minutes active, about two and a half hours dormant. Their paper is unusually satisfying because it turns two-billion-year-old nuclear waste into a clock. The evidence did not merely say something happened. It retained timing information.

That timing matters beyond Oklo. Long-lived geological processes are usually inferred from products smeared across time. Here, noble-gas isotopes behaved like an ancient strip-chart recorder. The apparent pulse sequence supports a feedback mechanism in which water participates both in enabling and eventually interrupting criticality.

There is an easy mistake to make at this point. Because Oklo sounds impossible until it is explained, people use it as a coupon redeemable for other impossible-sounding ideas. It is not. Oklo proves exactly what its evidence supports: under past isotopic conditions and a particular geological arrangement, uranium ore and groundwater sustained natural fission reactions. It does not prove reactors commonly form inside planets. It does not prove one sits at Earth's center. It does not prove nuclear fission drives the geomagnetic field.

The better lesson is almost the opposite. Oklo became real because multiple traces converged: depleted uranium-235, fission products, mineral context, isotope systematics, reactor-physics plausibility. Once the anomaly appeared, investigators did not protect its mystery. They tried to eliminate it by ordinary nuclear accounting.

The missing uranium therefore gives us a standard for the rest of the book. If somebody proposes a reactor we cannot drill to, the reactor must still balance books somewhere else. It should produce heat. It should change isotopes. It should consume and breed fuel in ways consistent with nuclear physics. Most usefully now, it should emit antineutrinos.

A hidden reactor is hidden only from eyes and drill bits. It is not hidden from conservation laws.
