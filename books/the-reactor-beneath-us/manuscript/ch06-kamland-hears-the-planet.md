# KamLAND Hears the Planet

KamLAND was surrounded by reactors humans had built. That made it an awkward place to listen for a reactor nobody knew existed.

The Kamioka Liquid Scintillator Anti-Neutrino Detector sits under a mountain in Japan. Its original fame came from reactor antineutrino oscillation: Japanese nuclear power stations provided a distributed set of sources, and the detector watched electron antineutrinos disappear and reappear according to quantum mixing. The experiment helped establish neutrino oscillation parameters with extraordinary precision.

Inside the same data was another population.

In 2005 the KamLAND Collaboration reported an experimental investigation of antineutrinos produced by natural radioactivity in Earth. The statistics were limited. The significance was conceptual. Geologists had spent a century reading rocks brought to the surface. Here was a detector reading nuclear decays through the planet.

The first result did not settle Earth's heat budget. Its confidence interval was broad. The collaboration explicitly described the data as consistent with geophysical models and used the measurement to set an upper limit on radiogenic power from uranium and thorium. That restraint is part of why the paper aged well. A first detection is useful even when it does not answer the grand question attached to it.

Later KamLAND data made the signal harder to dismiss. The collaboration's geoneutrino analyses increasingly constrained the uranium-plus-thorium contribution to Earth's heat and provided upper limits on a hypothetical georeactor. Review literature summarized a 90% confidence upper limit of about 5.2 terawatts for a core georeactor from a KamLAND-era analysis. The exact bound evolved with data and assumptions, but the important fact was that the proposed reactor had become numerically cornered.

There is an irony in using KamLAND for this job. Commercial reactors create the same broad class of fission antineutrinos that a georeactor would produce. At Kamioka, the human reactor background was large enough that the history of Japan's nuclear fleet materially changed the experimental environment. Reactor shutdowns after the Fukushima disaster reduced backgrounds for some neutrino measurements. Human energy policy altered the acoustic—or rather antineutrino—landscape in which Earth could be heard.

That entanglement is a useful warning against treating data as if it arrive from nature alone. Background is history. A detector's ability to test a planetary hypothesis depends on the geographic distribution and operating schedules of power stations, the depth of the laboratory, radioactivity in materials, cosmogenic isotopes and analysis cuts.

The georeactor argument therefore cannot be evaluated by looking at a plot and asking whether there is “room” for more events. The fit must include known reactor contributions and their uncertainties. A central reactor would produce a steady or slowly varying baseline source at a distance close to Earth's radius from the detector. Its energy spectrum would be modified by oscillation. A source at the core-mantle boundary would have a different distance depending on location. These distinctions matter once the data become good enough.

KamLAND also exposes an important difference between detecting distributed radioactive decay and detecting concentrated fission. Uranium and thorium geoneutrinos below roughly 3.3 MeV prompt-energy equivalents occupy a restricted energy region. Reactor fission antineutrinos extend higher. That extra spectral territory provides leverage. A deep fission source cannot hide by calling itself mantle uranium if its events appear where uranium-thorium decay chains cannot produce them.

No single detector can make the case perfectly. But KamLAND broke the philosophical stalemate. Before direct geoneutrino detection, Earth's nuclear inventory was inferred through chemistry and heat. After it, nuclear processes inside the planet had an observable messenger.

A hypothesis at the center of Earth had acquired an external auditor.
