# The Helium Argument

Helium is where the georeactor hypothesis becomes seductive.

Some ocean-island basalts and mantle-derived materials carry helium isotope ratios that differ from typical atmosphere and many mid-ocean-ridge basalts. Helium-3 is primordial in origin on Earth to a significant degree; helium-4 is continually produced by alpha decay of uranium and thorium. Ratios between them have therefore become tracers of mantle reservoirs and degassing history.

Herndon argued that fission and associated nuclear processes in a deep reactor could produce helium isotope ratios spanning observed values. In a 2003 *PNAS* paper he compared numerical georeactor production to helium ratios in oceanic basalts and proposed a nuclear origin for at least some high ratios.

The calculation is interesting. The inference is not unique.

This is a recurring epistemic trap: a model produces an observed number, and the fit begins to feel causal. But a match between output and observation is strong only when competing models do not also produce the observation without special pleading. High helium-3/helium-4 ratios are generally interpreted within mantle geochemistry through preservation of primordial helium in relatively undegassed reservoirs, mixing, mantle convection and crustal contributions. The georeactor is an alternative source model, not the only known way to obtain the ratio.

Transport adds another layer. Even if a central reactor produces helium, the helium must travel from the deepest Earth into mantle plumes or other sampled reservoirs without its isotopic signal being erased beyond recognition. That is a geodynamic story involving core-mantle exchange, diffusion, advection and reservoir mixing. Each step is plausible in some broad sense; the chain as a whole needs evidence.

There is also a source-budget question. How much helium does a reactor of allowed power produce? How does that compare to observed fluxes? Does the spatial pattern of high helium ratios align with a transport model from the core? Are there correlated fission products or isotope signatures that would be harder for primordial-reservoir models to imitate?

This is where the helium argument can be improved rather than merely debated. Instead of asking whether georeactor simulations can reproduce a ratio, ask what joint observations differ between the models.

A primordial-reservoir model and a fission-source model make different statements about associated neon, argon, xenon and lead isotopes; about spatial distribution; about time evolution; about correlation with mantle plume geochemistry; and, crucially, about antineutrinos. The fission model has a messenger the primordial-helium model does not.

Suppose an ocean-island basalt contains unusually high helium-3/helium-4. By itself, that is evidence of a helium source history. If, independently, a persistent fission antineutrino signal were localized to the deep Earth at a power sufficient to generate relevant helium, the helium observation would become more interesting for the georeactor model. If increasingly sensitive antineutrino detectors exclude that power, the helium fit becomes less persuasive even if the numerical ratio still matches.

This is what independent evidence streams are for. They prevent one flexible observable from carrying an entire theory.

Helium also reveals why scientific disputes can persist after both sides possess real data. The measurements may be good while the source attribution is underdetermined. A geochemist can reasonably trust the isotope ratio and reject the reactor interpretation. A reactor physicist can reasonably show that fission produces the ratio and still not establish geological reality.

The temptation is to call one side closed-minded or the other speculative. The better move is to identify the measurement neither side controls.

In this case the particles are already passing through our feet.
