# Herndon's Machine

The strange thing about the georeactor literature is how ordinary parts of it look.

There are burnup calculations. Neutron spectra. Fuel breeding. Fission products. Helium yields. The papers do not read like an occult map of Earth's interior. They read like someone has taken reactor engineering and moved the boundary conditions six thousand kilometers down.

In 2001 D. F. Hollenbach and J. Marvin Herndon published “Deep-Earth reactor: nuclear fission, helium, and the geomagnetic field” in *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*. They used the SCALE reactor-physics code system to simulate a planetary reactor. Their abstract states that the numerical results demonstrate that such a reactor could, under suitable conditions, function as a fast-neutron breeder and operate over geologic time.

“Could” is doing legitimate work there.

The paper belongs in evidence class one for the fact that the calculations were performed and published. Its central physical configuration remains a hypothesis. The distinction sounds bureaucratic until one sees how often literature summaries erase it.

A fast reactor is important to the concept because there is no Oklo-style water moderator at the center. Fast neutrons can support breeding cycles in which fertile uranium-238 captures neutrons and, through beta decays, becomes fissile plutonium-239. Over long periods, maintaining criticality requires an evolving inventory of actinides, not merely burning an initial stock of uranium-235 until it is gone.

A later 2015 study by Kao-Ping Lin, Rong-Jiun Sheu and Shiang-Huei Jiang revisited the actinide inventory using updated SCALE/TRITON depletion calculations. They found that, under their assumed conditions, a hypothetical georeactor could operate at constant power around 3 TW for billions of years through a conversion chain involving uranium-238, plutonium-239 and uranium-235. Again, this is a neutronics result. It strengthens the statement “a long-lived fast reactor is not automatically impossible under the model.” It does not solve the question “did Earth assemble one?”

This is an important kind of partial support that science communication handles badly. Skeptics sometimes treat a hypothesis as worthless if any major premise is unproven. Advocates sometimes treat success in one subproblem as confirmation of the whole. The reactor simulations deserve neither dismissal nor promotion.

They tell us where to attack next.

Fuel availability is one target. Partitioning of uranium under core-formation conditions is another. Reactor stability is another. Fission-product behavior under core pressure and temperature is another. Heat transport is another. Then comes the external signature.

The external signature is the strongest because it bypasses several uncertain internal details. A fission power of 3 TW means a certain order of fission rate. Fissions produce neutron-rich fragments. Their beta decays produce antineutrinos. The resulting flux at Earth's surface can be calculated with uncertainties in fuel composition and spectrum. Neutrino oscillations can be included. The detector either allows that flux or increasingly does not.

A 3 TW reactor is especially interesting because it sits below some historical central-georeactor upper limits while being large enough to matter in proposed geophysical explanations. This places the hypothesis in the uncomfortable middle ground where it is neither already excluded at every plausible parameter nor free to explain anything.

That is exactly where experiments are valuable.

Herndon's machine is therefore best treated as a deliberately overconstrained proposal. If it is to do several jobs—supply helium signatures, contribute to geomagnetism, persist for billions of years, remain compatible with core chemistry, fit the heat budget and evade antineutrino limits—then every additional job creates another opportunity to fail.

This is not unfair. Explanatory ambition should increase evidentiary debt.
