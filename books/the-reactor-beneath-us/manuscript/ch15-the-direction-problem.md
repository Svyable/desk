# The Direction Problem

Counting is easier than pointing.

Liquid scintillator detectors are excellent calorimeters for inverse beta decay and poor telescopes for individual reactor antineutrinos. The positron's direction is only weakly related to the incoming antineutrino, and the neutron's displacement before capture carries limited directional information.

For a central georeactor, directionality would be transformative. Every detector on Earth's surface would see the source coming from approximately nadir. Distributed uranium and thorium in crust and mantle would generate a broader angular field. Commercial reactors would sit near the horizon or at known geographic bearings.

A detector that could reconstruct antineutrino direction with useful resolution would turn the problem from spectral decomposition into something closer to imaging.

Several concepts aim in that direction: segmented scintillator, delayed-neutron displacement methods, water-based liquid scintillator, and hybrid detectors that separate Cherenkov and scintillation light. None should be promoted as a solved georeactor camera. The point is that directionality is an identifiable technology gap.

There is also a network solution. Even detectors with weak per-event direction can gain localization from differing fluxes at separated sites. A source at Earth's center has equal radius to all surface detectors. A source on the core-mantle boundary does not. Known reactor backgrounds differ dramatically by site. Crustal contributions differ with local geology.

With enough detectors, geography becomes a measurement dimension.

The ideal network would include continental and oceanic sites. Oceanic crust is thinner and contains less uranium and thorium than continental crust, reducing one major geoneutrino background. A deep-ocean movable detector has long been attractive for mantle science for exactly this reason.

This is where the georeactor question connects to a broader planetary-neutrino program. Instruments built to measure mantle composition, monitor reactors, improve oscillation physics and study supernova neutrinos can also test the exotic hypothesis. The marginal cost of asking the strange question may be small once the detector exists.

That is a good model for anomalous science. Do not build a temple to the anomaly if a general-purpose instrument can test it as a side effect.

Direction is not merely a technical refinement. It is what converts “extra events” into “events from there.” For a hypothesis defined by a location humans cannot reach, that distinction is nearly the whole game.
