# A Spectrum Is Harder to Romanticize

Mysteries like nouns. Experiments prefer distributions.

A “georeactor” sounds like a thing. A detector does not see the thing. It sees candidate interactions distributed in energy and time, mixed with known reactor antineutrinos, geoneutrinos, radioactive backgrounds and cosmogenic events.

That is good news because spectra are harder to mythologize.

Uranium and thorium decay-chain geoneutrinos occupy a limited energy range in inverse-beta-decay detectors. Fission antineutrinos extend to higher energies. Commercial reactors produce the same general fission-antineutrino family expected from a natural reactor, which means their measured spectra provide both a nuisance and a template.

A deep source also propagates over a long baseline. Neutrino oscillations reshape the electron-antineutrino survival probability as a function of energy. At thousands of kilometers, many oscillatory features average, but the expected detected spectrum can still be calculated using measured mixing parameters and detector response.

This converts a broad geological claim into a likelihood problem. Assume a source location and thermal power. Choose a plausible fission isotope mixture. Propagate the spectrum through oscillation. Fold it through detector efficiency and energy resolution. Add known reactors and geological backgrounds. Compare with data.

There are uncertainties at every step, but they are explicit uncertainties.

The isotope mixture deserves attention. A hypothetical breeder reactor in Earth's center would not necessarily have the same fuel evolution as a pressurized-water power reactor. Uranium-235, plutonium-239 and other fissile contributions alter the emitted spectrum. A serious search should therefore not use one commercial-reactor spectrum as a universal georeactor fingerprint. It should test a physically motivated family of spectra.

Likewise, reactor-antineutrino physics has its own unresolved details, including the historical “reactor antineutrino anomaly” and spectral shape discrepancies such as the several-MeV bump observed in commercial reactor experiments. Those issues do not erase the ability to bound a huge hidden source, but they matter as sensitivity improves.

This is another place where anomalous research can learn from ordinary particle physics. Background disagreement is not permission to declare an extraordinary residual. It is a term in the error budget.

The strongest future analysis would be deliberately adversarial. Give one team the task of fitting all data without a georeactor using flexible but justified reactor and geological nuisance models. Give another the task of searching for a deep fission component across source locations and fuel states. Blind the signal region or inject synthetic sources. Require both teams to publish false-positive behavior before opening the real result.

The object is not to make belief impossible. It is to make surprise expensive.

A real central reactor should pay that price easily. It has all of Earth in which to hide physically and almost nowhere to hide spectrally once the detector is sensitive enough.
