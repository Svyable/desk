# Ghosts from Rock

Neutrinos are an excellent way to learn humility about matter. Trillions pass through you while you read this. Most will cross the entire planet without noticing it.

That indifference is why they are useful.

A photon from Earth's deep interior is absorbed, scattered, re-emitted and thermalized until its history is gone. A seismic wave carries information about elastic structure but does not announce whether a uranium nucleus decayed nearby. An antineutrino created in beta decay can leave the source and travel through the planet almost untouched. The weakness of its interaction is exactly what preserves the message.

The cost is detection. To see a useful number of events, experimenters build enormous targets, bury them under rock to suppress cosmic-ray backgrounds, wrap them in photodetectors and spend years understanding every nuisance process that can imitate the signal.

For geoneutrino work, the standard channel in liquid scintillator is inverse beta decay. An electron antineutrino interacts with a proton, producing a positron and a neutron. The positron gives a prompt flash whose energy carries information about the incoming antineutrino. The neutron wanders briefly before capture, giving a delayed signal. The coincidence between prompt and delayed light is a powerful signature.

But it is not a universal ear. Inverse beta decay has an energy threshold around 1.8 MeV. Antineutrinos from portions of the uranium and thorium decay chains clear that threshold. Potassium-40 antineutrinos do not. This means the familiar phrase “geoneutrino detector” can mislead. The detector is selectively deaf.

A fission reactor is loud in this channel. Human power reactors emit enormous numbers of electron antineutrinos from beta decays of neutron-rich fission products. The spectrum extends above the geoneutrino window. For particle physicists, those reactor antineutrinos are signals used to measure oscillation. For geophysicists, they can be background. For the georeactor question, they are both calibration and nuisance.

This overlap is fortunate. A hypothetical deep-Earth fission reactor does not require invented detector physics. Its basic antineutrino signature belongs to the same family already measured from commercial reactors. The dispute moves to flux, spectrum, source distance, fuel evolution and location.

Oscillation complicates the trip. Electron antineutrinos do not remain in a fixed flavor state as they propagate. The survival probability depends on energy, distance and neutrino mixing parameters. That sounds like an obstacle until one realizes that reactor experiments have spent decades measuring exactly these effects. The georeactor hypothesis is therefore forced to live inside a mature experimental framework.

There is a second humility lesson. “Detected from inside Earth” does not mean “located inside Earth.” KamLAND and Borexino can count geoneutrino events and infer spectral contributions, but event-by-event directionality in these scintillator detectors is weak. Much of the geological interpretation therefore relies on models of the nearby crust, because the local crust can contribute strongly to the observed flux. The detector sees through rock but does not automatically perform tomography.

This asymmetry explains why geoneutrino science has advanced as a negotiation between particle physics and geology. The particle physicists know the detector response, oscillation and backgrounds. The geologists build crust and mantle models. Neither can simply inherit the other's uncertainty as a footnote.

The same must apply to a georeactor search. A claimed excess cannot be declared a central reactor until crustal uranium and thorium, human reactors, detector backgrounds and spectral systematics are accounted for. Conversely, a null result can constrain a fission source even if we remain uncertain about some mantle composition, because a fission spectrum is not identical to the uranium-thorium geoneutrino spectrum.

This is the peculiar beauty of ghost particles. They make an inaccessible place measurable while refusing to make it easy.
