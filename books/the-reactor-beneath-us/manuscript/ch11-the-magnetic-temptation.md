# The Magnetic Temptation

Earth's magnetic field is a gift to speculative mechanism because it is both essential and visibly restless.

The field reverses. Its intensity changes. The poles wander. The geodynamo operates in liquid metal more than two thousand kilometers below our feet, where direct observation is impossible. A theory that places a variable energy source near the center can therefore sound immediately relevant.

Herndon proposed that an operating georeactor could participate in geomagnetic generation and variation. The attraction is obvious. Fission produces heat. Heat can drive motion. Conducting fluids plus motion can make magnetic fields. The trouble is that every arrow in that chain is shared with conventional dynamo physics.

Mainstream geodynamo models already obtain time-dependent fields from convection in the electrically conducting outer core, powered by secular cooling, compositional buoyancy associated with inner-core growth, and related energy sources. Reversals do not require a periodically failing reactor. Numerical dynamos reverse without one.

This does not make a georeactor irrelevant by decree. An extra heat source could alter buoyancy and flow. But to become explanatory it needs a prediction that differs from an ordinary dynamo with uncertain boundary conditions. Does reactor power correlate with reversal frequency? Is there a spectral or temporal signature? Could a reactor small enough to satisfy antineutrino limits still provide the required energy? What delay would exist between changes at the center and observable field behavior?

Those questions are harder than attaching a reactor to a mystery. They are also more useful.

Geomagnetic data are rich enough to punish flexible stories. Reversal chronology extends deep into geologic time. Paleointensity is imperfect but informative. Secular variation is observed directly in the historical era and now by satellites. Core-flow inversions can be attempted from field changes. A georeactor theory that invokes magnetism should engage this structure rather than point to reversals as if their existence were unexplained.

There is also a timescale problem. A nuclear reactor can vary rapidly in engineering terms. The core and mantle impose thermal and dynamical filtering. A pulse at the center does not necessarily become a pulse in the magnetic field. The transfer function matters.

This chapter is therefore less about whether fission can contribute energy than about the discipline of causal distance. The farther an explanation travels from source to observation, the more competing mechanisms enter. A fission antineutrino is one step from the nuclear process. A basalt helium ratio is several transport steps away. A geomagnetic reversal is farther still.

Evidence should be weighted accordingly.

If future antineutrino measurements were to reveal a central fission source at, say, a fraction of a terawatt, the magnetic question would still remain. One would then ask whether that power is dynamically significant. Detection of the source would not validate every proposed consequence.

If future measurements push the allowable central power far below the level needed by a particular magnetic model, the model can be rejected without solving every detail of the geodynamo.

This is the virtue of independent constraints. They shorten arguments that would otherwise last forever.
