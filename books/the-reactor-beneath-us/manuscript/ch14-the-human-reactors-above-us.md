# The Human Reactors Above Us

The biggest obstacle to hearing a hidden reactor inside Earth may be reactors we can locate on a map.

Commercial nuclear stations emit vast antineutrino fluxes. Their thermal powers are monitored. Their baselines to a detector can be calculated. Their operating schedules vary. To neutrino physicists these sources are precious. To geoneutrino work they are structured background.

This creates a strange inversion: political decisions about reactor operations can improve or worsen our ability to test a geological hypothesis.

KamLAND's Japanese environment historically carried a large reactor contribution. Borexino, in central Italy, benefited from lower nearby reactor flux. Future oceanic detectors could reduce crust and reactor backgrounds simultaneously by moving far from continents.

The background is not merely an inconvenience. It is a calibration opportunity. Known reactor spectra test energy response, oscillation models and analysis pipelines. A georeactor search should exploit periods of known reactor shutdown, refueling and power variation. A real deep source should remain when a human source turns off.

This suggests a time-domain strategy. Construct a global database of commercial reactor power histories with uncertainty. Predict the detector rate from the known fleet. Look for residual components that are steady over political timescales. Then test whether any residual fits a deep-source geometry across detectors.

Safeguards research has already considered antineutrino monitoring because reactors cannot turn off their fission-product neutrino emissions by closing a door. The same physics that makes clandestine reactor monitoring conceivable makes a planetary reactor testable.

There are complications. Reactor spectra are not perfectly modeled. Spent fuel contributes. Detector calibration drifts. Earth matter effects are small at these energies but oscillations matter. Background radionuclides imitate portions of the signal.

Still, commercial reactors give the analysis something rare in frontier science: a known class of sources with comparable physics.

If a proposed detection algorithm cannot correctly recover the human reactors, it has no business announcing the natural one.

That principle can be operationalized. Blind analyses could hide the identities of known reactor sites, ask teams to localize them statistically, and measure false source rates. Only after the pipeline demonstrates competence on the known world should it be turned toward the center.

A georeactor search does not need a special epistemology. It needs the same humiliating calibration every instrument deserves.
