# JUNO Turns On

On August 26, 2025, the Jiangmen Underground Neutrino Observatory began physics data taking in Guangdong, China.

JUNO is enormous by the standards of liquid-scintillator neutrino detectors: roughly twenty thousand tonnes of scintillator in a giant acrylic sphere watched by tens of thousands of photomultiplier tubes, under about seven hundred meters of rock. Its main mission is neutrino mass ordering and precision oscillation physics using antineutrinos from nearby nuclear power complexes.

In June 2026 the collaboration published its first physics result in *Nature*. The analysis used only 59 days of early data, from August 26 to November 2, 2025, and already improved precision on key oscillation parameters compared with prior combined measurements. The result is important here less for what it says about Earth's interior than for what it says about the instrument: JUNO works at the precision scale its designers promised.

The collaboration has explicitly listed geoneutrinos among its extended physics program. That makes the present moment unusually good for this book. The georeactor hypothesis is not waiting for a fictional future detector. A new detector with unprecedented statistics and energy resolution is operating now.

JUNO's geography is complicated for deep-Earth work because the nearby Taishan and Yangjiang reactor complexes are intentionally bright sources. That background is central to the oscillation mission. But high statistics and excellent energy resolution also improve control of reactor spectra. Over time, geoneutrino analyses can benefit from the same calibration machinery.

One should not claim that JUNO's first 2026 result already constrains the georeactor beyond Borexino or KamLAND unless the collaboration publishes such an analysis. It did not. The correct statement is narrower: by June 2026 JUNO had demonstrated high-precision reactor-neutrino performance, and collaboration planning included geoneutrino science.

That evidence classification matters because new instruments attract speculative claims before their analyses exist. Capability is not result.

Still, capability changes what questions are worth preserving. A central-reactor search can be designed prospectively rather than retrofitted. Source models, nuisance priors and reporting thresholds can be registered before the full exposure accumulates. Synthetic signal injections can test sensitivity. Multiple fuel compositions can be considered. The analysis can be structured to publish the exclusion curve even if nothing is found.

JUNO also makes the global-network idea more realistic. KamLAND in Japan, Borexino's completed legacy data in Italy, JUNO in China, and future large scintillator or hybrid detectors provide different baselines to known power reactors and different crustal environments.

The next decade of geoneutrino science may therefore be less about a single heroic detection and more about triangulating Earth's radioactivity with instruments built for other reasons.

That is often how frontier questions become ordinary. Nobody approves a billion-dollar project called Find the Reactor at the Center of the Earth. They build a precision neutrino observatory, and eventually the strange question has nowhere left to hide.
