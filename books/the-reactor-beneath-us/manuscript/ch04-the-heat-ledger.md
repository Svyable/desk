# The Heat Ledger

Earth is cooling, but it is not simply cooling.

The distinction sounds grammatical until you try to account for roughly forty-something terawatts of heat escaping from the planet. Some is leftover energy from accretion, differentiation and the long violence of making a rocky world. Some is continually supplied by radioactive decay. Tidal and chemical terms appear in more detailed ledgers. The fractions matter because they change how we reconstruct mantle convection and planetary history.

For decades the radiogenic share had a peculiar status: obviously real in mechanism, uncertain in total. Uranium, thorium and potassium decay. Rocks contain them. The question is how much of these heat-producing elements occupy reservoirs we cannot directly sample, especially the mantle.

Surface samples are biased by accessibility. Meteorites provide compositional clues but require assumptions about how Earth formed and differentiated. Seismology can infer structure and elastic properties, not directly count uranium atoms. Heat flow measures the total leaking out, not the source label attached to each joule.

This is the setting in which a georeactor can sneak into conversation. If the global heat budget has slack and the deep composition is uncertain, a concentrated nuclear source can be proposed to occupy some of the unknown. But unknown budget is not positive evidence. A gap in a ledger is not an invoice from a reactor.

Geoneutrino measurements changed the character of the ledger because uranium-238 and thorium-232 decay chains emit electron antineutrinos. Those particles escape the interior. Detect enough of them, understand the detector and geological contributions, and one can constrain radiogenic heat more directly.

KamLAND's 2005 result was still statistically limited, but it was conceptually enormous. The detector saw a signal consistent with antineutrinos from uranium and thorium decay in Earth. The collaboration wrote that the measurement could provide direct geophysical information and placed an upper limit on radiogenic power from those chains. Later KamLAND analyses improved the constraint. Borexino, with low reactor background at Gran Sasso, accumulated another geoneutrino data set and sharpened the mantle discussion.

There is a beautiful asymmetry here. Heat itself forgets its source. Once thermalized, a joule from uranium decay looks like a joule inherited from accretion. An antineutrino remembers the nuclear process that created it through its spectrum and production channel. The particles are bad calorimeters individually and excellent witnesses collectively.

Potassium-40 complicates the story because the antineutrinos relevant to its dominant beta decay are below the inverse-beta-decay threshold used by large scintillator detectors such as KamLAND and Borexino. The instruments therefore hear uranium and thorium much better than potassium. Even the new ear has a frequency range.

A fission georeactor would add another kind of antineutrino signal. Its spectrum would resemble reactor antineutrinos from human power stations, shifted by propagation and oscillation but generated by fission rather than long decay chains. That means the heat ledger and the reactor hypothesis intersect in a particularly testable way. A multi-terawatt source at great depth is not merely a thermal contribution. It should make fission antineutrinos.

This is where a good strange hypothesis starts paying rent. Herndon's deep-reactor proposal is often discussed through geochemistry or magnetic speculation, but antineutrino detectors impose a direct upper bound on how powerful such a source could be. Borexino analyses, depending on assumed source location, have excluded central georeactors above the several-terawatt scale; its 2020-era analysis gives a 95% confidence upper limit around 5.7 TW for a source at Earth's center, with tighter limits for hypothetical sources closer to Gran Sasso.

The exact number belongs to a model and data set. The epistemic move is more important: a hypothesis that once lived mainly in inaccessible geology acquires a wattage ceiling from particles passing through thousands of kilometers of rock.

Earth's heat ledger will never be a single clean column. But the accounting is becoming less permissive.
