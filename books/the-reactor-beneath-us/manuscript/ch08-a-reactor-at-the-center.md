# A Reactor at the Center

The center of Earth is an irresistible place to put an explanation.

Nobody can visit it. Pressure is immense. Temperature is high. Seismology gives us density and elastic structure at scale, not a laboratory sample. The core is chemically dominated by iron with nickel and lighter elements, but the detailed inventory and differentiation history remain active subjects. Inaccessible places invite both serious inference and metaphysical squatting.

J. Marvin Herndon's georeactor proposal belongs in neither the folklore bin nor the established-fact bin. It was developed in peer-reviewed papers with explicit reactor-physics calculations. That earns it analysis. It does not earn it existence.

One version places a compact actinide-rich subcore at Earth's center surrounded by a shell of fission products and decay products. Later work with D. F. Hollenbach used numerical reactor simulations and argued that, under appropriate assumptions, a deep-Earth reactor could operate as a fast-neutron breeder over geologic time. Herndon connected the model to helium isotope ratios and geomagnetic behavior.

The most important phrase is “under appropriate assumptions.”

Reactor physics can answer a conditional question: given this composition, geometry, neutron spectrum and fuel inventory, can a chain reaction persist and breed enough fissile material? It cannot by itself answer the geochemical question of whether early Earth actually delivered those actinides to the center in the required amount and state.

This separation is often lost. A successful SCALE calculation is evidence that an assumed reactor configuration has a viable neutron economy. It is not evidence that Earth's core contains that configuration. The model crosses one bridge and leaves another intact.

The geochemical bridge is difficult because uranium is ordinarily treated as lithophile: it prefers silicate phases rather than metallic iron under many relevant conditions. If most uranium remained in the silicate Earth during differentiation, starving the core of actinides, a central reactor becomes hard to assemble. Advocates have argued that strongly reducing conditions could change partitioning. The question therefore moves into high-pressure experiments, accretion chemistry and early-Earth oxidation state.

This is exactly the kind of collision I like: nuclear engineering meets planetary differentiation. Each field can make the other's hidden assumption visible.

There is also a geometry problem. At the exact gravitational center, dense material has no direction “down” to settle. Fission products, actinides and surrounding iron would interact under conditions unlike any terrestrial reactor. A proposed subcore and waste shell must be dynamically plausible, not just neutronically plausible. How are neutron poisons removed or segregated? How is the reactor stabilized? How does heat escape? If breeding is required, does the fuel cycle remain critical as isotopic inventories evolve?

Later independent reactor calculations have explored some of these questions and found that a hypothetical fast-spectrum georeactor can be made to persist for billions of years under chosen conditions. That is interesting because it means simple assertions of nuclear impossibility are too strong. Yet every such calculation inherits the initial inventory problem.

The best reason to keep discussing the idea is no longer the model. It is that the model emits.

A central fission reactor should generate antineutrinos with a reactor-like spectrum. Unlike helium, those particles do not need to survive mantle transport or be assigned to a reservoir. Unlike magnetic reversals, they do not require a chain of causal interpretation through fluid dynamics. They come from fission products.

This creates an unusually clean situation for an unconventional geophysical hypothesis. We do not need consensus on every piece of deep-Earth chemistry to impose an observational power limit. Borexino and KamLAND already do. Future detectors can tighten it.

If the allowed power falls below what the proposed helium or geomagnetic roles require, those roles fail even if a weaker reactor remains physically possible.

That is the difference between an exotic hypothesis and an evasive one. An exotic hypothesis can survive ridicule. It should not survive its own missing antineutrinos.
