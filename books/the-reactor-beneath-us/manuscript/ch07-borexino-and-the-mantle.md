# Borexino and the Mantle

Gran Sasso is quieter than Japan in one respect that matters here: fewer nearby commercial reactor antineutrinos.

Borexino was designed as an exquisitely clean solar-neutrino experiment. Its liquid scintillator had to be purified to levels that sound unreasonable until one understands that a handful of radioactive contaminants can impersonate the particles you want. The discipline required to hear neutrinos from the Sun turned out to be useful for hearing antineutrinos from Earth.

By 2020 the Borexino Collaboration had accumulated a geoneutrino sample large enough to make a stronger statement about the mantle contribution. Their comprehensive analysis, published in *Physical Review D*, used data from 2007 through 2019 and reported a geoneutrino signal inconsistent with zero. Interpretation still depended on subtracting the crustal contribution, especially the local crust surrounding Gran Sasso, but the analysis supported a nonzero mantle component.

This is one of those moments when “direct measurement” deserves careful punctuation. Borexino directly detects antineutrino interactions. It does not directly label individual events MANTLE. The mantle signal is extracted statistically after modeling crust and backgrounds. The distinction does not make the result weak. It tells us where the uncertainty lives.

The same paper is valuable for the georeactor question because Borexino's low reactor background lets a fission component be constrained. In later summaries of the analysis, the collaboration's limits depend on assumed source position. A hypothetical georeactor directly beneath Gran Sasso at the core-mantle boundary would be closer and therefore more tightly constrained than one at Earth's center; a source on the opposite side of the core-mantle boundary would be farther and less constrained. For a source at the center, the published limit is in the several-terawatt range, around 5.7 TW at 95% confidence in the 2020 analysis framework.

That is not a proof of absence. It is the opposite of the rhetorical style that surrounds many anomalous claims. It is a statement of how much anomaly remains physically allowed under specified assumptions.

The number is also devastating to versions of the hypothesis that casually require large powers. If a proposed mechanism needs a ten- or twenty-terawatt fission source at the center to drive a geophysical phenomenon, and antineutrino data permit only a smaller source, the mechanism has a problem. One cannot solve it by saying the detector did not see the reactor because the reactor is underground. Underground is where neutrino detectors shine.

At lower powers, ambiguity remains. A few terawatts is not trivial, but it can sit inside Earth's total heat budget without dominating it. Detecting such a source requires enough exposure and sufficiently controlled reactor backgrounds. If the source is not central, geography becomes relevant. A detector network gains value because flux changes with distance.

Borexino therefore turns the georeactor debate into an engineering argument about sensitivity rather than a philosophical argument about plausibility. That is progress even before a final answer.

It also reveals a recurring problem in science communication. A popular account may say Borexino found that radioactive decay supplies about half of Earth's heat. The collaboration's actual inference is more conditional: measured uranium-thorium geoneutrino rates, geological crust models, assumptions about thorium-to-uranium ratio and conversion from particle flux to radiogenic heat all enter. The result favors some bulk-silicate-Earth models over others but does not weigh the mantle directly on a scale.

The restraint matters because the georeactor hypothesis often grows in gaps between models. If those gaps are exaggerated, the strange hypothesis looks artificially spacious. If conventional models are presented as perfectly settled, the strange hypothesis looks artificially impossible. Neither is useful.

The Borexino era gives us a better posture. There is a measured signal. There is a model-dependent mantle extraction. There is a quantitative georeactor ceiling. There is still room for better instruments.

That is what a mature unknown looks like.
