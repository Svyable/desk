# The Reactor Beneath Us

In 1972 somebody noticed that uranium was wrong.

Not dramatically wrong. No crater, no melted shaft, no instrument screaming in a control room. The ore arriving from Gabon simply carried a slightly odd isotopic ratio. Natural uranium is supposed to be boring in this respect. Uranium-235 occurs at essentially the same proportion wherever you dig it from the crust. This batch had less.

That small accounting error turned into one of the strangest established facts in geology: roughly two billion years ago, in what is now Gabon, groundwater entered uranium-rich rock and helped sustain nuclear chain reactions. Nature had built reactors before engineers existed. The fission products survived in the geology long enough for us to reconstruct not merely that reactions occurred, but something about how the reactor breathed. Modern work on xenon isotopes has supported a pulsed cycle in which fission ran for about half an hour and then paused for roughly two and a half hours as water boiled away and later returned.

That story is not controversial in the useful sense. The details are studied; the existence of the Oklo natural reactors is not a fringe proposition.

The next story is stranger. Earth loses heat. Some of that heat is primordial: the cooling of a planet assembled violently from smaller things, differentiated, compressed, struck, churned. Some comes from radioactive decay. The amount matters because the heat budget shapes mantle convection, volcanism, tectonics and the long cooling history of the planet. For most of geology, the radioactive contribution had to be inferred indirectly from rocks, chemistry and models of a deep interior we cannot sample cleanly.

Then physicists learned to hear radioactive decay through stone.

Electron antineutrinos produced in uranium and thorium decay chains pass through Earth with insulting ease. A tiny fraction interact in large underground detectors. KamLAND reported the first direct experimental investigation of geologically produced antineutrinos in 2005. Borexino later accumulated a cleaner and larger sample. The detectors were built for particle physics, but they became instruments of planetary autopsy.

And inside that new capability sat an old, audacious claim: perhaps there is another source of nuclear energy deep below us, not diffuse decay distributed through mantle and crust, but fission concentrated in a reactor near Earth's center.

J. Marvin Herndon and collaborators developed versions of that argument in the peer-reviewed literature. They used reactor-physics calculations to ask whether an actinide-rich region could sustain fission over geologic time, and argued that such a reactor might connect to helium isotope observations or even geomagnetic behavior. Those papers are real. Their appearance in respectable journals is also not proof that the georeactor exists. A calculation showing that a reactor can be made to run under assumed conditions is different from evidence that the required material actually segregated, remained configured, bred fuel and operated in Earth's center. A model can cross the threshold from impossible to conceivable without crossing from conceivable to true.

Fortunately, this is no longer a claim that has to live entirely inside geochemical argument. A fission reactor produces an antineutrino spectrum. A sufficiently powerful deep reactor cannot merely explain things; it must also emit.

That fact changes the epistemology.

This book keeps four evidence classes separate. Class one is documented or directly measured: Oklo fission, the measured planetary heat flow, antineutrino detection, reactor-neutrino physics, the published detector limits. Class two is credible but incomplete inference: estimates of mantle radiogenic heat, compositional models of the inaccessible deep Earth, some interpretations of isotope reservoirs. Class three contains disputed claims: a persistent central georeactor, its proposed relation to helium, its proposed role in geomagnetism. Class four is synthesis and conjecture: what better directional antineutrino instruments could reveal, how a planetary reactor might be distinguished from distributed radioactivity, and what the larger scientific lesson is when an idea once treated as extravagant acquires a measurable defeat condition.

The point is not to rescue a georeactor. The point is to subject it to the indignity every interesting hypothesis eventually deserves: instrumentation.

Nature has already demonstrated that a planet can accidentally assemble a nuclear reactor. The remaining question is not whether that sentence sounds strange. It is where, how, and with how much power such a machine could still be hiding.
