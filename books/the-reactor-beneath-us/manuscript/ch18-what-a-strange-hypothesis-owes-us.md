# What a Strange Hypothesis Owes Us

Oklo is dangerous to both skeptics and believers because it rewards the wrong instinct if read lazily.

The believer says: nature did something astonishing once, therefore another astonishing thing may be true. The skeptic says: Oklo was documented, therefore undocumented claims remain irrelevant until equally obvious proof appears.

Both miss the useful middle.

Nature can produce arrangements that look engineered. That expands the hypothesis space. But Oklo became science because isotope ratios, fission products, mineralogy and reactor physics converged. That raises the evidentiary standard.

The georeactor hypothesis is unusually valuable as a case study because it has traveled farther than most speculative ideas toward a clean test. It began with arguments about composition, segregation and planetary energy. Reactor simulations showed that certain assumed configurations can remain critical over long periods. Helium and geomagnetic claims broadened the proposed consequences. Then antineutrino detectors arrived and began removing parameter space.

This is how an unconventional claim should mature. Not through accumulation of anecdotes, but through loss of freedom.

We already know several things. Natural sustained fission occurred at Oklo. Earth emits geoneutrinos from uranium and thorium decay. KamLAND and Borexino measured them. A powerful fission reactor deep inside Earth would emit a distinguishable antineutrino component. Existing data put terawatt-scale ceilings on simple georeactor models. JUNO is now operating at a new precision frontier and has geoneutrinos in its broader science program.

We do not know that uranium concentrated at Earth's center in the required inventory. We do not know that a central breeder reactor exists. Helium isotope fits do not establish it. Magnetic reversals do not establish it. Numerical criticality does not establish it.

That separation is not a compromise. It is the argument.

The deepest lesson is about the word impossible. Before Oklo, natural reactor criticality might have sounded like a category error. It was not. But the correction was not to become more permissive about extraordinary claims. It was to become more specific about physical conditions.

A strange hypothesis owes us those conditions. It owes a mechanism. It owes outputs. It owes a way for those outputs to differ from conventional explanations. It owes a failure mode.

The reactor beneath us, if it exists, cannot negotiate with that debt. Fission products beta-decay whether or not geologists agree about the core. Antineutrinos leave.

That is why I am fond of this problem even if the final answer is no.

A no here would not be the triumph of normal science over a foolish outsider. It would be the successful conversion of a difficult planetary speculation into a measured exclusion. The hypothesis would have improved by becoming killable.

And if the answer is yes, the first convincing evidence will probably not look like a revelation. It will look like too many events in the right energy range, seen by more than one detector, surviving reactor-background corrections, fitting one geometry better than another, and refusing to go away.

The most extraordinary part may be how boring the proof becomes.
