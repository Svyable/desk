# Chapter 7 — expert fixation boundary

## Why this note exists

Chapter 7 argues that expertise can become adaptation debt when learned patterns keep directing attention after the environment changes. That claim needs a tighter empirical boundary than the familiar slogan that experts become rigid.

## Direct evidence

Bilalić, McLeod, and Gobet tested chess players with problems that contained both a familiar but non-optimal solution and a better, less familiar solution. The familiar solution produced an Einstellung, or mental-set, effect even among expert players: prior knowledge could pull attention toward the known pattern strongly enough to interfere with finding the better move. The authors reported that the familiar non-optimal solution could reduce observed problem-solving performance dramatically.

This is unusually useful evidence for Chapter 7 because the experimental mechanism is narrower than the manuscript's organizational cases. It shows prior knowledge affecting search inside a domain where expertise can be measured rather than inferred from job title or tenure.

Source already in the book ledger: `AD044` — Merim Bilalić, Peter McLeod, and Fernand Gobet, “Inflexibility of Experts—Reality or Myth? Quantifying the Einstellung Effect in Chess Masters,” *Cognitive Psychology* 56 (2008), 73–102. DOI: 10.1016/j.cogpsych.2007.02.001. The bibliographic record and abstract were rechecked against PubMed and the Oxford University Research Archive on 2026-09-05.

## Strongest counterevidence

The same experiment cuts against the lazy version of the expert-handicap argument. Greater expertise did **not** monotonically produce greater rigidity. The authors found that stronger experts were less prone to the Einstellung effect. Their conclusion was deliberately double-sided: expertise can create fixation, but deeper expertise can also help a person escape it.

That matters for the book. Chapter 7 should not imply that expertise itself is adaptation debt, that novices are naturally more adaptive, or that the cure for stale expertise is replacing experts with outsiders. The defensible claim is about the interaction between a learned pattern and a changed problem: a familiar solution can bias search toward itself, while sufficiently rich expertise may provide more ways out.

## Editorial use

If this evidence is promoted into manuscript prose, use it to sharpen the chapter's existing distinction between expertise and the architecture in which expertise was learned. A compact chess example can do more work than another general warning about experts “seeing what they expect to see.”

The useful sequence is:

1. show a skilled player encountering a problem with a tempting familiar solution;
2. explain that the familiar solution can block search for the better one;
3. immediately preserve the reversal that stronger experts were less vulnerable;
4. return to the organizational argument: the danger is not knowledge but a once-useful pattern monopolizing attention after the problem changes.

Do not generalize the chess result into a universal effect size for medicine, engineering, management, or organizational change. The experiment demonstrates a mechanism under controlled chess problems; the manuscript's Polaroid, Nokia, Leonard-Barton, and Ocasio material is what carries the organizational application.
