# New Coke (1985): research note for Chapter 14

## Why this case belongs

Chapter 14 uses New Coke as a counterweight to the book's dominant failure mode. Coca-Cola did not refuse to adapt. It changed its flagship product after extensive consumer testing, then discovered that the tested attribute did not exhaust what customers believed they were buying.

The case is strongest when stated narrowly: **a transition can be evidence-based and still be premature or mis-scoped when the experiment measures one component of value while the change removes another.** New Coke does not prove that consumer research is useless, that incumbents should protect tradition, or that the original formula was objectively superior.

## Primary / first-party record checked

### The Coca-Cola Company, “New Coke: The Most Memorable Marketing Blunder Ever?”

https://www.coca-colacompany.com/about-us/history/new-coke-the-most-memorable-marketing-blunder-ever

Current company history says:

- Coca-Cola announced the reformulated flagship on April 23, 1985, the first formula change in 99 years.
- The company's U.S. flagship share lead had been slipping for 15 consecutive years; consumer preference and awareness were also declining.
- The replacement formula had been preferred in taste tests involving nearly 200,000 consumers.
- The original formula returned on July 11, 1985, after 79 days, as Coca-Cola classic.
- By June 1985 the company says its consumer hotline was receiving about 1,500 calls per day, versus about 400 before the taste change.
- The company itself frames the research failure as an inability to capture consumers' attachment to Coca-Cola, not as evidence that the new liquid lost blind taste tests.

This supports the chapter's existing distinction between **taste** and **continuity**, with one important qualification: the evidence comes from Coca-Cola's retrospective corporate history and should not be treated as a neutral causal evaluation.

### The Coca-Cola Company, “Veteran Employees Remember Infamous 1985 Launch of New Coke” (April 23, 2015)

https://www.coca-colacompany.com/about-us/history/the-infamous-1985-launch-of-new-coke

This company oral-history feature supplies useful operational evidence about the backlash. The manager of consumer affairs recalled the consumer-care staff expanding from roughly a dozen representatives to 180 to handle calls and letters. Former chief archivist Phil Mooney described the 79-day period as unusually intense and said employees learned that consumers treated Coca-Cola as more than a product.

Use these recollections as first-party testimony about the company's response, not as independent proof of consumer psychology.

### The Coca-Cola Company, *A Short History of Coca-Cola*

https://www.coca-colacompany.com/content/dam/journey/us/en/our-company/history/coca-cola-a-short-history-125-years-booklet.pdf

The company's historical chronology independently preserves the basic sequence: formula changed in 1985 for the first time in 99 years; nationwide protest followed; original-formula Coca-Cola returned 79 days later.

## Independent institutional check

### Smithsonian National Museum of American History, Coca-Cola Classic can

https://americanhistory.si.edu/collections/object/nmah_880980

The Smithsonian collection record describes Coca-Cola Classic as introduced in 1985, eleven weeks after New Coke, in response to fierce media and consumer backlash and fear that the new formula would replace the familiar taste. This corroborates the broad chronology without relying only on Coca-Cola's retrospective narrative.

## Strongest counterargument

New Coke is often narrated too neatly as “market research measured taste but forgot the brand.” That explanation is plausible and is now part of Coca-Cola's own institutional memory, but the observed reversal does not isolate one causal mechanism.

Several alternatives remain live:

1. **Replacement versus choice.** Consumers may have objected less to the new formula itself than to losing access to the old one. The relevant failure may therefore have been forced substitution, not premature product innovation.
2. **Launch architecture.** A parallel launch could have produced a different result. Indeed, after July 11 the new and classic formulas were sold alongside one another. The episode can support Chapter 12's parallel-systems argument as much as Chapter 14's timing argument.
3. **Retrospective corporate storytelling.** Coca-Cola has strong incentives to turn a famous mistake into a lesson about brand love and intelligent risk. Company histories are evidence for dates, stated test scale, and the company's interpretation; they are not a randomized causal study.
4. **The change may have generated compensating value.** Coca-Cola later argued that the episode renewed consumer attachment and changed category dynamics. Even a failed transition can produce information or attention. Chapter 14 should not imply that all 79 days were pure economic waste without financial evidence.

## Editorial implication for Chapter 14

The manuscript currently says the company “changed a variable before understanding the full asset attached to that variable.” That is the right level of claim and is better than calling New Coke proof that Coca-Cola “moved too early.” The deeper mechanism is **scope mismatch**: an extensively tested product attribute was changed through a launch that also altered availability, continuity, identity, and consumer choice.

A future manuscript pass could sharpen the scene with two verified numbers—the nearly 200,000 taste-test participants and the hotline increase to roughly 1,500 calls per day—then immediately preserve the countercase: blind preference for the new formula was not refuted by the backlash. What failed was the inference from preference on a tested dimension to acceptance of the whole transition.

That distinction fits *Adaptation Debt* better than the familiar “New Coke was a marketing disaster” anecdote. It shows why an organization can possess real evidence, act decisively, and still misunderstand which commitments customers value.

## Falsification / boundary

Do not use this case to claim:

- that the new formula tasted worse;
- that taste tests were fraudulent or meaningless;
- that Coca-Cola should never have changed its flagship product;
- that consumer attachment alone caused the reversal;
- that the episode proves early adaptation is generally worse than late adaptation.

The case should be dropped or substantially reframed if stronger contemporary evidence shows that the nearly-200,000 testing figure is materially misleading, that the launch decision was driven by a different mechanism than the company's stated competitive and preference concerns, or that consumer backlash did not materially contribute to restoring the original formula.
