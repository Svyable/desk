# Chapter 6 research note — metric gaming is not the same as metric obsolescence

## Why this note exists

Chapter 6, “Metrics Remember the Old Game,” is strongest when it distinguishes two different measurement failures instead of letting them collapse into a generic Goodhart’s-law story.

The chapter’s Adobe example is primarily about **metric obsolescence**: a familiar measure can remain honestly calculated while becoming incomplete because the business model, timing, or causal mechanism has changed.

Wells Fargo supplies a different failure mode. There, the problem was not that sales goals had become stale measures of an old business. The goals remained current, salient, and operationally powerful. The failure was that pressure, incentives, and weak controls allowed a chosen measure to reorganize behavior in destructive ways.

That distinction can sharpen the chapter without broadening its thesis.

## Primary record

The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency’s September 8, 2016 enforcement action found unsafe or unsound sales practices at Wells Fargo, including unauthorized deposit and credit-card accounts, and cited the bank’s failure to implement effective enterprise risk management capable of detecting and preventing the practices.

Source: OCC, “OCC Assesses Penalty Against Wells Fargo, Orders Restitution for Unsafe or Unsound Sales Practices,” September 8, 2016.

https://www.occ.gov/news-issuances/news-releases/2016/nr-occ-2016-106.html

The OCC’s April 2017 supervisory review is useful because it shows that incentive compensation and cross-sell practices were visible governance questions before the scandal fully broke. The report describes examinations that touched cross-sell strategy and incentive compensation but did not adequately surface the severity of the risk.

Source: OCC, “Lessons Learned: Review of Supervision of Sales Practices at Wells Fargo,” April 19, 2017.

https://www.occ.gov/publications-and-resources/publications/banker-education/files/pub-wells-fargo-supervision-lessons-learned.pdf

A later OCC enforcement record makes the causal boundary more explicit. In a January 2020 notice of charges, the OCC quoted senior officials describing the Community Bank’s problem as rooted in unreasonable or unattainable sales goals and severe pressure to meet them. The notice also summarizes the Wells Fargo independent directors’ 2017 conclusion that addressing the sales-practices abuses required abandoning the pressure created by sales goals and sales-driven incentive programs.

Source: OCC, Notice of Charges, January 23, 2020.

https://www.occ.gov/static/enforcement-actions/ean20-001.pdf

The strongest current authoritative update is the OCC’s January 14, 2025 decision concerning three former Wells Fargo executives. The agency said its decisions found failures to challenge the incentive-compensation program, install effective controls, escalate risks, and preserve audit independence. The OCC summarized the underlying misconduct this way: under pressure to meet unreasonable sales goals, thousands of employees engaged in widespread sales-practices misconduct.

Source: OCC, “OCC Imposes Civil Money Penalties Against Three Former Executives of Wells Fargo,” January 14, 2025.

https://www.occ.gov/news-issuances/news-releases/2025/nr-occ-2025-3.html

## What the case supports

A careful version of the argument is:

> A measurement system can fail in at least two different ways. The measure can become stale because the world changes while the formula does not. Or the measure can remain perfectly current and become too behaviorally powerful because incentives, status, supervision, and control systems have been built around moving it.

Adobe illustrates the first problem better than the second. Wells Fargo illustrates the second better than the first.

That distinction matters for *Adaptation Debt* because it prevents Chapter 6 from treating every measurement problem as “the metric became outdated.” Sometimes the measurement architecture is carrying yesterday’s assumptions. Sometimes it is carrying today’s objective so aggressively that the organization optimizes the proxy past the point of legitimacy.

## Strongest counterargument

The Wells Fargo record does **not** establish that sales targets, cross-selling, or incentive compensation are inherently corrupting. Most organizations use targets without producing fraud, and even the OCC’s record points to governance, escalation, audit, risk-management, and control failures alongside the goals themselves.

The defensible lesson is therefore narrower than “targets cause gaming.” A target becomes dangerous when the rewards for moving it outrun the institution’s ability or willingness to observe *how* it is being moved and whether the measured behavior still serves the underlying objective.

That also means the case should not be used as evidence that subjective judgment is safer than measurement. Weak controls plus vague objectives can be just as dangerous. The problem is not quantification itself; it is the coupling among a proxy, incentives, authority, and oversight.

## Suggested manuscript use

If Chapter 6 is revised later, the Wells Fargo case would work best as a short concrete interruption inside the section that currently moves from “metric opportunism” into Goodhart-style problems.

It should not become a second opening anecdote or a long scandal recap. One or two compact paragraphs are enough to give the abstract distinction a body:

- Adobe: the old metric tells the truth about a question that has become incomplete.
- Wells Fargo: the metric remains current but becomes a behavioral command.

The resulting line of argument is stronger than a generic warning about gaming because it preserves two different causal mechanisms and therefore two different remedies. Stale metrics require re-examining the question. Overpowered metrics require incentive, control, and governance design.

## Boundary for future edits

Do not write that Wells Fargo employees opened a fixed number of fraudulent accounts unless the exact period, regulator, and definition are specified. Different enforcement records use different estimates and scopes.

Do not describe the scandal as proof that cross-selling itself was fraudulent. The misconduct concerned unauthorized or otherwise improper practices under a broader sales system.

Do not reduce the failure to “bad employees responding to bad quotas.” The primary record repeatedly implicates management systems, incentive design, risk governance, escalation, and oversight.

And do not let the example swallow the chapter. The point is not Wells Fargo. The point is that **obsolete metrics and overpowered metrics are different kinds of measurement debt**.
