# Chapter 7 research audit — expertise and flexibility

Checked: 2026-09-13

## Why this note exists

Chapter 7, “The Expert’s Handicap,” is careful to reject the easy claim that novices are generally more adaptive than experts, but most of its support currently comes from organizational cases and conceptual literature. The strongest version of the chapter needs a direct empirical boundary: prior knowledge can bias search toward a familiar solution, yet greater expertise can also reduce that bias and improve selection of diagnostic information. The handicap is therefore conditional, not an inevitable tax on expertise.

## Direct evidence: familiar solutions can capture expert attention

Bilalić, McLeod, and Gobet used chess problems that contained a familiar but suboptimal solution as well as a better, less familiar solution. Expert players were vulnerable to an Einstellung effect: the familiar solution could block discovery of the better one. Their eye-movement work is especially useful for this chapter because it gives “trained attention” a measurable mechanism rather than leaving it as metaphor. Players continued to attend to features associated with the first solution even while trying to find alternatives.

Sources:

- Bilalić, McLeod & Gobet, “Inflexibility of Experts—Reality or Myth? Quantifying the Einstellung Effect in Chess Masters,” *Cognitive Psychology* 56(2), 73–102. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogpsych.2007.02.001
- Bilalić, McLeod & Gobet, “The Mechanism of the Einstellung (Set) Effect: A Pervasive Source of Cognitive Bias,” *Current Directions in Psychological Science* 19(2), 111–115. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1177/0963721410363571

## Counterevidence that should change the claim

The same chess work cuts against the simplistic “experts are trapped by expertise” story. Although experts showed the Einstellung effect, greater skill reduced susceptibility. The paper’s result is not that accumulated expertise monotonically destroys flexibility. It is that a highly accessible familiar solution can capture search, while stronger domain knowledge can also provide resources for escaping that capture.

A different controlled study by Spence and Brucks found that experts confronting a complex, moderately ill-structured judgment problem selected fewer but more diagnostic information inputs than novices and evaluated nonquantified inputs more consistently. That is a useful counterweight to any prose implying that selective attention is inherently dangerous. Selectivity is often exactly what expertise buys.

Source:

- Spence & Brucks, “The Moderating Effects of Problem Characteristics on Experts’ and Novices’ Judgments,” *Journal of Marketing Research* 34(2), 233–247. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1177/002224379703400204

## Theoretical boundary

Erik Dane’s cognitive-entrenchment account is useful as synthesis rather than proof. It proposes that stable domain schemas can reduce flexibility, but explicitly treats the tradeoff as conditional on task environment and attentional focus and argues that expertise-related inflexibility can be circumvented. The chapter should therefore avoid converting “cognitive entrenchment” into a universal empirical law.

Source:

- Dane, “Reconsidering the Trade-off Between Expertise and Flexibility: A Cognitive Entrenchment Perspective,” *Academy of Management Review* 35(4), 579–603. DOI: https://doi.org/10.5465/amr.35.4.zok579

## Strongest surviving proposition

The chapter can make a narrower and more interesting claim:

> Expertise earns its value by compressing experience into selective attention. The risk appears when a once-diagnostic pattern becomes highly accessible after the problem’s structure has changed. That same expertise may also supply the knowledge needed to notice the mismatch and escape it.

This fits the book’s adaptation-debt thesis better than a generic expert-versus-novice contrast. The debt is not expertise itself. It is the persistence of a learned allocation of attention after the environment has changed enough that the old allocation no longer earns its keep.

## Implications for a later prose pass

- The current opening claim that “the expert usually sees more than the novice” survives.
- The lines about expertise deciding “what they look for” gain direct experimental support from the chess eye-movement work.
- The manuscript should add the uncomfortable counterresult that the strongest chess experts were *less*, not more, vulnerable to the familiar-solution trap than weaker experts.
- The chapter should avoid implying that broad experience necessarily narrows search. Spence and Brucks show a case where experts’ narrower information search was more diagnostic.
- Polaroid and Nokia should remain organizational cases rather than being presented as demonstrations of a general cognitive law; their outcomes include business-model, organizational, emotional, and communication mechanisms beyond individual expertise.

## What would weaken the chapter further

The thesis should narrow again if cross-domain evidence shows that expert schemas generally update quickly when diagnostic feedback changes, or that apparent “entrenchment” effects mostly disappear under ecologically valid tasks with strong feedback. It should also narrow if organizational transition failures attributed to expertise are better explained by incentives, resource allocation, hierarchy, financing, or coordination than by expert attention.

The empirical burden is therefore comparative: identify when expertise’s compression of experience becomes stale faster than experts or institutions update it, while preserving the much larger set of situations in which that compression is an advantage.
