# Research Brief

## Thesis under test

*Adaptation Debt* asks why successful systems can become expensive to change even when the people inside them understand that the environment has moved.

The easy version of the argument is not worth writing. It says that large organizations become complacent, experts become arrogant, managers protect their bonuses, bureaucracies hate change, and newcomers win because they are brave. Every one of those things can happen. None is a sufficient theory.

The stronger question is why competent, attentive, rational people can continue reproducing a path that has become increasingly costly to leave.

The working claim is:

> **Success creates commitments. Commitments attract complementary investments, expectations, routines, identities, incentives, and obligations. Those complements can raise the full cost of changing course faster than the organization recognizes. The accumulated difference is adaptation debt.**

The concept is intentionally narrower than path dependence, organizational inertia, technical debt, sunk cost, or the exploration/exploitation problem. It is not offered as a replacement for those literatures. It is a trade-nonfiction lens on one recurring economic feature that runs through them: **the transition cost created when other parts of a system learn to depend on a once-reasonable choice.**

The debt metaphor must remain disciplined. Debt is not age. Debt is not tradition. Debt is not the existence of switching costs. A commitment counts as adaptation debt only when three conditions are present:

1. the commitment was initially rational or at least understandable in its original setting;
2. complementary investments or expectations have made departure materially more expensive than the original choice itself; and
3. evidence now suggests that the cost of continuing the path is rising relative to the cost of changing it.

That third condition is essential. Without it, the manuscript would simply rename durable capability as debt.

## What would make the book wrong?

The argument should weaken if the evidence shows any of the following consistently:

- Established firms facing discontinuity change core technologies, business models, or organizational identities at roughly the same cost and speed as entrants once size and resources are controlled for.
- Complementary investments—installed capital, customer workflows, trained labor, standards, contracts, incentives, and regulation—do not meaningfully affect transition cost.
- Deep expertise does not create domain-specific search or interpretation biases, or such biases are reliably dominated by expertise's informational advantages.
- Customer dependence does not materially influence resource allocation toward sustaining existing products or markets.
- Organizations that preserve exploratory capacity, modularity, reversible commitments, or separate transition units do not adapt more effectively than otherwise similar organizations.
- Supposed adaptation failures are better explained by bad forecasting, fraud, weak execution, financing constraints, or simple product inferiority than by accumulated commitments.
- Successful incumbents routinely capture major discontinuities precisely because their scale, capital, customers, and capabilities make transition cheaper rather than more expensive.

The manuscript must seek those counterexamples actively. Microsoft, Adobe, Netflix, IBM, and other organizations that navigated important transitions are not awkward exceptions to be minimized. They are necessary evidence about the conditions under which adaptation debt can be serviced rather than merely diagnosed.

## Terminology boundaries

### Adaptation debt is not technical debt

Technical debt is a software-engineering metaphor with its own history and use. Some technical debt is deliberately incurred and some is accidental; the cost usually appears in future modification, maintenance, reliability, or developer effort.

Adaptation debt can include technical debt but is broader. A technically elegant system can still be expensive to change because customers, contracts, capital, incentives, or regulation have formed around it.

Do not imply that Ward Cunningham or the technical-debt literature made claims about the broader concept used here unless a source explicitly does so.

### Adaptation debt is not sunk cost

A sunk cost is already incurred and should not, in standard economic reasoning, determine a forward-looking choice merely because it was spent.

Many adaptation costs are not sunk. They are future costs of transition: retraining, parallel operation, customer migration, contract renegotiation, data conversion, stranded but still productive assets, political conflict, and temporary loss of competence.

The book should be careful not to explain every incumbent decision as a sunk-cost fallacy when the old system continues producing cash or service.

### Adaptation debt is not path dependence

Path-dependence research asks how historical sequences and self-reinforcing mechanisms can narrow later choices. The book relies heavily on that literature.

Adaptation debt focuses on the **cost structure of leaving** the path after complementary commitments have formed.

### Adaptation debt is not structural inertia

Organizational ecology has treated inertia as connected to the reliability and accountability expected of organizations, not merely managerial laziness. That distinction supports the book: some slowness is produced by features society values.

The manuscript should preserve that insight and avoid turning inertia into a personality flaw.

## Opening-act evidence map

### Chapter 1 — The Factory for Yesterday

The opening chapter uses Eastman Kodak as a case where the common popular summary is too neat.

Facts that may be used with primary support:

- Eastman Kodak and its U.S. subsidiaries filed for Chapter 11 protection on January 19, 2012.
- Kodak's 2011 Form 10-K describes significant industry-related declines in film-related businesses, digital-camera pricing pressure, investment in consumer and commercial inkjet, legacy liabilities, and the Chapter 11 reorganization.
- Kodak's January 2012 filing announcement stated that approximately 75% of 2011 revenue came from digital businesses.
- In 2012 Kodak announced plans to phase out dedicated capture devices, including digital cameras, pocket video cameras, and digital picture frames.

Important boundary:

Do **not** write the familiar fable that Kodak simply invented the digital camera and refused to go digital. Kodak invested heavily in digital imaging and by 2011 described most of its revenue as digital. The useful question is why replacing the economics, assets, channels, organizational knowledge, and cash generation of film proved so difficult even when digital technology was plainly visible.

Mary Tripsas and Giovanni Gavetti's Polaroid study is useful as adjacent evidence because it explicitly connects capabilities and managerial cognition during the analog-to-digital transition. It should not be treated as a direct causal study of Kodak.

What would weaken the chapter:

- evidence that Kodak's collapse can be explained primarily by financing, governance, or execution failures unrelated to accumulated film-era commitments;
- evidence that its film assets and complementary capabilities imposed little meaningful cost on digital transition;
- stronger evidence that Kodak's digital businesses failed mainly because its digital products were inferior rather than because of business-model and organizational transition.

### Chapter 2 — Success Leaves Furniture Behind

This chapter moves from one company to the mechanism.

Core research:

- Levitt and March describe organizational learning as routine-based, history-dependent, and target-oriented, with lessons encoded into routines.
- March's exploration/exploitation model shows how refinement of exploitation can become effective in the short run while reducing exploration.
- Leonard-Barton describes core capabilities becoming core rigidities when the same technical systems, skills, managerial systems, and values that support performance obstruct new product or process development.
- Hannan and Freeman connect structural inertia to organizational reliability and accountability, making clear that reproducibility is itself an organizational competency.
- Sydow, Schreyögg, and Koch model organizational path dependence through self-reinforcing mechanisms and possible lock-in.

This chapter should show why the metaphor is “furniture” rather than “baggage.” Baggage is something obviously carried. Furniture becomes the room. People route around it without noticing it as a decision.

The chapter may use federal legacy IT as a contemporary example of accumulated operational commitment. GAO reported in 2025 that the federal government spends more than $100 billion annually on IT and cyber-related investments, with agencies historically reporting about 80% devoted to operations and maintenance. GAO identified critical legacy systems whose modernization plans were incomplete. Use those figures with the report's date and context; do not generalize them to all public-sector technology.

IBM mainframes provide necessary counterevidence. IBM's 2026 lifecycle history describes average service lives above eleven years for generations since zArchitecture and emphasizes upgrade paths that protect investment. Old infrastructure can therefore be a source of value rather than proof of failure.

### Chapter 3 — Adaptation Debt

This chapter defines the term and connects existing literatures without claiming novelty for their components.

Core sources:

- March on exploration/exploitation.
- Leonard-Barton on core rigidities.
- Henderson and Clark on architectural innovation, especially the problem of reconfiguring links among components while preserving component knowledge.
- Christensen and Bower on customer power and resource allocation in disk drives.
- Tushman and O'Reilly on ambidextrous organizations.
- Benner and Tushman on process management's differing fit with stable versus exploratory contexts.
- O'Reilly and Tushman's later synthesis of organizational ambidexterity.
- dynamic-capabilities literature as a counterweight to deterministic lock-in.

The chapter's original work is conceptual synthesis. It should not imply that “adaptation debt” is an established academic variable with validated measurement unless later research establishes that.

A useful operational decomposition for future chapters is:

**Capital commitments** — plants, fleets, facilities, infrastructure, software, inventory systems.

**Complement commitments** — suppliers, tools, standards, distribution, data formats, maintenance systems.

**Customer commitments** — compatibility, support, service expectations, pricing, channel relationships.

**Human commitments** — expertise, careers, compensation, identity, status, organizational memory.

**Institutional commitments** — rules, classifications, constituencies, procurement, law, public expectations.

**Measurement commitments** — metrics, targets, dashboards, performance contracts, accounting categories.

These categories are analytical, not a named framework that every chapter needs to repeat. Use them only when they clarify a case.

### Chapter 4 — Why the Newcomer Looks Brave

The central claim is that entrants and incumbents can face different transition costs even when both see the same opportunity.

Christensen and Bower's disk-drive research is important because it does not portray leading firms as universally technologically incompetent. Their argument emphasizes how existing customers shape resource allocation: established firms often developed new technology when current customers demanded it while simpler technologies aimed at emerging markets struggled for organizational resources.

Henderson and Clark provide a related mechanism: established firms can possess strong component knowledge and still struggle when innovation changes relationships among components embedded in organizational communication channels and procedures.

The chapter should distinguish three entrant advantages:

- **absence of legacy commitments** — fewer products, customers, assets, or compatibility promises to migrate;
- **selection** — entrants are observed after they have chosen the new architecture, while failed entrants disappear from the story;
- **different incentives** — an entrant can survive on a market too small to matter to a large incumbent.

The chapter should also show the limits of the romance. Entrants lack installed distribution, cash flow, trust, data, operational routines, regulatory relationships, and scale. Newness is not itself competence.

Adobe and Netflix are useful countercases for later chapters because both accepted visible transition costs rather than waiting for old economics to disappear. Adobe's fiscal 2013 filing explicitly said Creative Cloud adoption would reduce perpetual-license revenue and total net revenue during the transition while expenses would not fall proportionally. Netflix's filings show a period in which profitable DVD economics coexisted with growing streaming, plus the very negative customer reaction to its 2011 plan changes. These cases can demonstrate that incumbents sometimes pay adaptation debt deliberately—and that doing so can be messy.

## Future evidence lanes

### Capital has a memory

Use asset-lifetime and retrofit evidence rather than treating book value as transition cost. The IEA's work on existing energy-consuming assets and U.S. Department of Energy material on building retrofits are useful because they show physical systems whose decarbonization depends on modification, early retirement, or natural turnover.

### Metrics remember the old game

Use research on performance measurement, Goodhart-type effects, incentive design, and cases where organizations changed the metric because the objective changed. Avoid turning every bad incentive into Goodhart's Law.

### The expert's handicap

Use cognitive-fixation and expertise research carefully. Expertise often improves adaptation; the claim must be about domain-specific search and interpretation constraints, not a general argument that novices see the future better.

### The customer you cannot fire

Use backward compatibility, installed-base economics, enterprise migration, support commitments, and customer-power research. IBM and Microsoft provide possible cases in which preserving compatibility supported adaptation rather than preventing it.

### The constituency around the rule

Use policy-feedback and institutional-change research. Distinguish legitimate beneficiary dependence from rent-seeking. A program's constituency may exist because the program solves a real problem.

### Cannibalize what, exactly?

Use Adobe, Netflix, Microsoft, and other documented transitions where management accepted deterioration in a legacy revenue stream while financing a new model. Preserve failures and periods of ambiguity.

### Parallel systems

Use software modernization, infrastructure transitions, and industrial change. Running old and new together may reduce cutover risk while increasing cost and complexity. GAO legacy-modernization reports provide public-sector evidence; cloud and enterprise migrations can provide private-sector evidence.

### The identity write-down

Use professional-identity and occupational-transition research. Avoid psychologizing named executives without direct evidence.

### Too early is expensive too

This chapter is a required counter-thesis. Find cases where firms abandoned useful assets too soon, overbuilt for immature markets, reorganized repeatedly, or confused fashion with discontinuity. If the book cannot show the cost of premature adaptation, its main concept is too easy to misuse.

### Small write-downs and option value

Use modularity, real options, staged investment, deprecation policy, cross-training, and ambidexterity. The goal is not to propose a universal management framework but to identify recurring ways organizations reduce the cost of future change.

## Source discipline

- Prefer primary filings, official reports, original research, and contemporary records over retrospective business folklore.
- Do not invent boardroom dialogue, executive motives, employee thoughts, or sensory detail.
- For famous business cases, distinguish what the company knew at the time from what hindsight makes obvious.
- Do not use bankruptcy as proof that a particular causal theory was correct.
- Do not describe every legacy asset as “stranded.” An asset is stranded only under a specified economic or policy scenario in which expected value falls materially before its anticipated useful life.
- Separate technological capability from business-model viability.
- Preserve counterevidence showing that compatibility, scale, customer relationships, and organizational memory can make incumbents *more* adaptable.
- Re-check contemporary company examples against primary filings before Shelf updates.

## What would make the whole book false?

The book's strongest conclusion would weaken substantially if the best evidence showed that prior success does not systematically alter the cost of changing strategy once resources and scale are accounted for, or that complementary commitments are usually offset by incumbents' greater capital, information, talent, and customer access. It would also weaken if organizations that deliberately preserve flexibility consistently pay more for optionality than they gain during discontinuity.

That possibility should remain alive through the final chapter.
