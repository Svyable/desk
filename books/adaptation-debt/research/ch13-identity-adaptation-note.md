# Chapter 13 — identity and adaptation evidence note

## Dutton and Dukerich (1991)

Jane E. Dutton and Janet M. Dukerich, “Keeping an Eye on the Mirror: Image and Identity in Organizational Adaptation,” *Academy of Management Journal* 34(3), 517–554. DOI: https://doi.org/10.5465/256405

This is unusually direct evidence for Chapter 13 because the study is about organizational adaptation rather than identity in the abstract. Dutton and Dukerich trace how the Port Authority of New York and New Jersey responded over time to homelessness in its facilities. Their central mechanism is that organizational identity and perceived external image shaped how members interpreted the issue, what responses felt appropriate, and the pattern of action that followed.

For *Adaptation Debt*, the useful claim is narrower than “identity causes resistance.” Identity helps members decide what a problem means and what kinds of action are compatible with the organization they believe themselves to belong to. That supports the chapter’s argument that identity is coordination technology: it can make action more coherent while also narrowing which responses initially feel legitimate.

The case also improves the chapter’s causal discipline. Identity was not a static obstacle that simply had to be discarded. Members’ interpretations and the organization’s responses changed over time as identity interacted with how outsiders were perceived to see the Port Authority. This is empirical support for the manuscript’s existing distinction between preserving continuity and freezing one historical implementation.

## Strongest counterargument

The same evidence cuts against a deterministic reading of “identity write-down.” Dutton and Dukerich show identity participating in adaptation, not merely blocking it. A strong identity can supply motives for action when a new issue threatens the organization’s image or clashes with what members believe the organization should be. The manuscript should therefore avoid implying that weaker identification is generally more adaptive.

A better boundary is: identity becomes adaptation debt when a once-useful self-conception makes relevant evidence or viable responses harder to recognize, legitimate, or resource—even after the environment has changed enough that the old interpretation is becoming costly. Identity that can be reinterpreted may instead be an adaptation asset.

## Editorial use

If Chapter 13 receives a later prose pass, this study can replace some of the chapter’s abstract statements about identity shaping attention with a concrete institutional case. It should not be used to claim that all identity transitions resemble the Port Authority case, nor to psychologize particular executives. The value of the source is the observed process: identity and external image shaped interpretation and action while both remained capable of change.
