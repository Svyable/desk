# Chapter 13 — identity evidence boundary

## Purpose

This note tightens the evidentiary boundary for Chapter 13, “The Identity Write-Down.” It does not add a new framework. It records what the existing sources can support, what they cannot, and the countercase the prose should keep alive in any later revision.

## Gioia, Schultz, and Corley: continuity can coexist with change

The chapter’s use of Dennis Gioia, Majken Schultz, and Kevin Corley (AD036) should stay narrower than the claim that a broad purpose statement makes an organization adaptive. Their 2000 *Academy of Management Review* article challenges the assumption that organizational identity must be literally stable to provide a sense of continuity. The useful implication for this book is that identity can be durable at one level while meanings attached to it change through interaction with organizational image.

That supports a specific argument: adaptation need not require an institution to declare its old identity false. A firm may be able to preserve continuity while renegotiating which products, practices, or capabilities count as expressions of that identity.

It does **not** establish that firms with broader self-descriptions adapt better, that identity change causes superior performance, or that executives can engineer identity simply by choosing a more abstract sentence. Chapter 13 should treat those as managerial hypotheses, not findings from AD036.

## Coca-Cola: a measured preference was not the whole product

The Coca-Cola company history already ledgered as AD039 is useful because it supplies a concrete limit to purely attribute-level testing. Coca-Cola says the reformulation that became New Coke followed nearly 200,000 consumer taste tests and that the new formula performed well in those tests. The company also records the intensity of the backlash and the return of the original formula as Coca-Cola Classic after 79 days.

The chapter can safely use the episode to show that a test can measure one dimension of value while missing another. In this case, blind taste preference did not exhaust what consumers valued in Coca-Cola. Continuity, memory, symbolism, and the existing relationship with the product mattered to the market response.

The case should not be made to prove that “identity” alone caused the reversal. Coca-Cola’s own retrospective is a company-authored history, not an independent causal study; the episode also involved launch strategy, communication, competitive conditions, and the unusual economics of replacing an iconic flagship product. The strongest formulation is therefore the one already latent in the chapter: management tested the product variable, while customers were also pricing continuity.

## Strongest counterargument

Identity is not merely adaptation debt. Stable identity can reduce coordination costs, sustain trust, protect long-horizon investments, and tell employees or customers which commitments will survive a change in leadership or technology. An organization that repeatedly rewrites what it is can destroy the very capabilities it hopes to make more flexible.

This is the disconfirming pressure Chapter 13 should preserve. The relevant question is not whether identity constrains change; useful identity necessarily does. The question is whether the organization can distinguish a constitutive commitment from a historical implementation before a crisis makes that distinction for it.

A later prose pass should therefore resist three easy moves:

- treating every attachment to an old product, profession, place, or ritual as irrational resistance;
- assuming that a sufficiently broad purpose statement solves the transition problem;
- psychologizing named executives or employees without direct evidence about how they understood the organization.

The chapter is strongest when identity remains both an asset and a possible source of debt. That symmetry should be carried by evidence and consequence rather than by repeated “not X but Y” rhetoric.
