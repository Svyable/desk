# Chapter 6 research note — Wells Fargo sales targets

Wells Fargo provides a useful countercase to Adobe for Chapter 6 because it shows a different failure mode of measurement: not a truthful metric becoming strategically incomplete, but a target and incentive system reorganizing behavior around the measured output.

On September 8, 2016, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau announced an enforcement action against Wells Fargo over unauthorized deposit and credit-card accounts. The CFPB said sales targets and compensation incentives spurred employees to boost sales figures by opening accounts without customers' knowledge or consent and, in some cases, transferring funds from authorized accounts to fund the new ones. According to the bank's own analysis cited by the CFPB, employees opened more than two million deposit and credit-card accounts that may not have been authorized. The CFPB imposed a $100 million civil money penalty; the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency separately assessed $35 million and required corrective action around sales-practices risk management.

This evidence sharpens Chapter 6 in two ways. First, it distinguishes **metric obsolescence** from **metric corruption**. Adobe's revenue measures still described real economic activity but became insufficient during a business-model transition. Wells Fargo's problem was different: the institutional emphasis on sales goals and incentives helped make the measured output itself unsafe as a proxy for legitimate customer value. Second, it gives the chapter a concrete example of why "the measure becomes a target" is not merely a slogan. Compensation, employment pressure, and risk oversight can turn a target into an operating system.

The strongest counterargument should remain explicit. Sales targets are not inherently abusive, and the 2016 enforcement record does not establish that any numerical goal inevitably produces misconduct. The CFPB's own later guidance framed the risk conditionally: incentives can produce consumer harm when poorly designed or managed. The useful claim for the manuscript is therefore narrower. A metric becomes dangerous when people can improve it through behavior that breaks the causal link between the number and the objective, especially when compensation and status are attached to the number faster than governance can detect the divergence.

This case should be used sparingly if moved into manuscript prose. It is strongest as a contrast immediately after the chapter's distinction between an honestly measured but stale metric and a target that begins to reorganize behavior around itself. Avoid treating Wells Fargo as evidence that all sales measurement is corrupt; the enforcement record supports a governance-and-incentives lesson, not an anti-measurement one.

Primary source: Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, September 8, 2016 enforcement announcement and consent-order record. Corroborating authoritative source: Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, September 8, 2016 enforcement action on Wells Fargo sales practices.
