# Chapter 7 evidence audit — expertise, fixation, and flexibility

Checked: 2026-09-13

## Question under pressure

Chapter 7, “The Expert's Handicap,” argues that expertise can become adaptation debt when learned patterns of attention and interpretation keep governing after the environment changes. The current chapter is careful not to romanticize novices, but it mostly supports the mechanism with organization-level cases and conceptual literature. A direct experimental test makes the boundary sharper.

## The strongest direct experiment

Merim Bilalić, Peter McLeod, and Fernand Gobet tested chess players with an Einstellung design: positions contained a familiar, workable solution and a better but less familiar solution. The familiar solution could block discovery of the better one even among experts.

In their 2008 *Cognitive Psychology* paper, the presence of the familiar non-optimal solution substantially reduced expert performance. This is useful evidence for the chapter because the mechanism is not ignorance. Prior knowledge supplies a plausible answer quickly; that answer then changes subsequent search.

Source:

- Bilalić, McLeod & Gobet, “Inflexibility of experts—reality or myth? Quantifying the Einstellung effect in chess masters,” *Cognitive Psychology* 56(2), 2008, 73–102. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogpsych.2007.02.001
- PubMed record: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17418112/

## The counterevidence that changes the claim

The same experiment cuts against the easy version of “expertise makes people rigid.” More expert players were **less** prone to the blocking effect than weaker experts. The authors' conclusion is deliberately mixed: expert inflexibility is real, but increasing expertise also provides resources for escaping it.

That matters for *Adaptation Debt*. The manuscript should not imply a monotonic curve in which more expertise means more entrenchment. A better claim is conditional:

> Familiar knowledge can capture search, including expert search, when an old solution remains plausible. But expertise also improves the ability to recognize relevant structure and, at higher levels, can make escape from that capture more likely.

Independent expertise research reinforces the positive side. Charness et al. found expert chess players faster and more accurate than intermediates while using fewer fixations, evidence that learned selective attention is ordinarily an advantage rather than a defect. Connors, Burns & Campitelli's later replication of de Groot likewise supports pattern recognition as a central component of chess expertise.

Sources:

- Charness, Reingold, Pomplun & Stampe, “The perceptual aspect of skilled performance in chess: evidence from eye movements,” *Memory & Cognition* 29(8), 2001, 1146–1152. DOI: https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03206384
- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11913751/
- Connors, Burns & Campitelli, “Expertise in complex decision making: the role of search in chess 70 years after de Groot,” *Cognitive Science* 35(8), 2011, 1567–1579. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1551-6709.2011.01196.x
- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21981829/

A specialization experiment adds another useful boundary. Bilalić and colleagues found that chess players performed better on positions inside their opening specialization and changed their search strategy there, searching more deeply and less broadly. Familiarity can therefore alter the *shape* of search without establishing that the resulting search is generally inferior.

- Bilalić, McLeod & Gobet, “Specialization effect and its influence on memory and problem solving in expert chess players,” *Cognitive Science*. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1551-6709.2009.01030.x
- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21585497/

## What this means for Chapter 7

The chapter's strongest formulation is not “experts are trapped by their maps.” It is that **successful knowledge changes what becomes salient and which candidate solutions arrive first**. That compression is usually why experts outperform novices. Adaptation risk appears when the environment changes while an old pattern remains plausible enough to capture search, and when the surrounding organization makes it difficult to test alternatives.

This also improves the Polaroid/Nokia material. Organization-level inertia should not be casually attributed to a universal cognitive flaw in individual experts. Expertise can be adaptive while incentives, communication channels, customer commitments, authority, or the architecture of the organization prevents contrary expert knowledge from changing the decision.

## Strongest counterargument

The strongest counterargument to the chapter is not that fixation never occurs. It is that expertise is often the mechanism by which people notice meaningful anomalies, reject bad alternatives, search efficiently, and escape misleading first impressions. The Bilalić result itself shows less Einstellung susceptibility at higher expertise. A book about adaptation debt should therefore avoid treating expertise as debt in itself.

The relevant liability is a coupling:

1. a learned pattern produces a fast, plausible interpretation;
2. the environment has changed enough that a less familiar interpretation is now better;
3. search remains organized around the familiar interpretation; and
4. institutional incentives or information structures fail to force a serious competing model.

If one of those links is absent, “expert's handicap” may be the wrong explanation.

## Falsification / disconfirmation test

A stronger future version of the chapter should ask whether experts outperform matched nonexperts **after a structural break**, not merely whether they exhibit fixation in laboratory puzzles.

Useful evidence would compare:

- performance before and after a rule, architecture, technology, or market regime changes;
- experts with narrow specialization versus experts with varied recent experience;
- first interpretation versus final interpretation after explicit alternative-search prompts;
- individual expert judgment versus organizational decisions made from the same expert evidence;
- cases where incumbent experts recognized the discontinuity earlier than entrants but the organization still failed to act.

The chapter's mechanism should narrow substantially if experts routinely adapt faster than novices after genuine structural breaks, or if organizational failures are better explained by financing, authority, incentives, or execution than by expert attention/search.

## Editorial implication

A later prose pass can use the chess experiment as a compact physical demonstration of the chapter's mechanism: put a good move on the board and it can make the better move harder to see. The important turn is the result the slogan version leaves out: the strongest players were also better at escaping the trap.

That tension is more faithful to the book's thesis. Competence creates the pattern library that makes a system work. Adaptation debt begins only when the system cannot tell which parts of that library still deserve authority.
