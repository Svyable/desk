# Chapter 14 research note — concurrency as a timing cost

## Why this case belongs here

Chapter 14 argues that adaptation can become expensive when an organization commits at scale before uncertainty has resolved. The F-35 Joint Strike Fighter program provides unusually concrete primary-source evidence for that mechanism because the U.S. Government Accountability Office tracked the costs created by overlapping development, testing, and production.

This case should be used narrowly. It is evidence about the cost of **scaling production while design and test uncertainty remain material**, not evidence that early deployment or parallel development is always irrational.

## Primary evidence

GAO's May 12, 2020 report, *F-35 Joint Strike Fighter: Actions Needed to Address Manufacturing and Modernization Risks* (GAO-20-339), records that production began in 2007 while development was still in its early stages and testing continued. GAO reported that, according to an F-35 program official, as many as 550 aircraft delivered through 2020 would need retrofits for deficiencies and design issues discovered during testing. The program called these fixes "concurrency cost" and estimated them at $1.4 billion in its 2019 update.

The mechanism matters more than the headline number. Producing more units before the design and test process had resolved important uncertainty increased the number of already-delivered aircraft exposed to later rework. In Chapter 14's terms, scale was being used before the exploratory question had fully become an execution question.

The case also adds useful specificity to the chapter's discussion of the "valley between steady states." The cost was not simply that the new system was immature. The cost came from committing physical units before learning had finished, then having to propagate what was learned backward into the installed fleet.

## Counterargument and boundary

Do not turn this into a generic indictment of concurrency.

Defense acquisition can face reasons to accept overlap deliberately: urgent operational need, deterrence value, industrial-base continuity, learning from field use, and the possibility that waiting for complete information carries its own strategic cost. GAO's criticism was not that all early production is inherently mistaken. Its concern was that continued production while testing remained incomplete increased exposure to retrofit costs if additional deficiencies were found.

That distinction matters for the book. Chapter 14's argument should remain about **priced uncertainty**, not patience as a virtue. The relevant question is whether the value of earlier capability exceeds the expected cost of learning after scale. In some settings it can.

The F-35 case therefore strengthens the chapter's counter-thesis without making it symmetrical or absolute: moving early can be rational, but the organization should know which uncertainty it is converting into rework risk.

## Safe manuscript use

A future prose pass could use a compact version of the case after the chapter's hypothetical manufacturer example or its discussion of scaling experiments:

- production began while development and testing were still underway;
- GAO reported up to 550 delivered aircraft could require retrofits for issues found in testing;
- the program estimated concurrency fixes at $1.4 billion;
- the lesson is not "wait until everything is known" but "scale creates a larger surface on which unresolved uncertainty can become rework."

Avoid implying that the $1.4 billion represented the total cost of the F-35 program's timing choices, or that concurrency alone explains the program's broader cost and schedule history.
