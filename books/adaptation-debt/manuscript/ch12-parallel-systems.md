# Parallel Systems

The safest way to replace a system is often to keep the old one running.

That is also the expensive way.

A bank builds a new platform but keeps the legacy ledger alive while accounts migrate.

A government agency launches a modern application while old systems continue processing the cases the new one cannot yet handle.

A factory commissions a new production line but keeps the old line available until quality is stable.

A utility adds a new control system while operators retain the previous one as a fallback.

A company moves customers to subscriptions while perpetual licenses remain in the market.

A media business grows streaming while DVDs still arrive in envelopes.

The future appears beside the past before it replaces it.

This overlap is one of the most common ways organizations pay adaptation debt.

It is also one of the most common ways they create more of it.

Parallel operation solves a real problem.

The old system knows how to do things the new system has not yet proved it can do.

The old system has years of edge cases.

It has trained users.

It has integrations.

It has known defects rather than unknown defects.

It has a performance history.

The new system has promises.

Running both creates insurance.

If the replacement fails, work can return to the old path.

If a customer cannot migrate, the old product remains available.

If data conversion is wrong, records can be compared.

If a new process creates defects, the mature line can cover production.

If demand moves more slowly than forecast, the company has not abandoned its existing revenue base overnight.

This is rational.

Then the overlap begins to learn how to survive.

Two systems mean two sets of skills.

Two maintenance schedules.

Two data models.

Two control environments.

Two groups of users.

Two sets of bugs.

Two contracts.

Two architectures that need to exchange information.

The bridge becomes a system of its own.

That is the hidden cost of parallel operation.

The organization does not merely pay for old plus new.

It pays for old, new, and the machinery required to keep them coherent.

This is why modernization projects can feel as though costs rise before benefits arrive.

The organization adds the future without yet being allowed to subtract the past.

Government technology makes this visible because the overlap can last for years.

The Government Accountability Office's 2025 review of critical federal legacy systems found that many modernizations remained incomplete long after the need for replacement was recognized. Of the ten most critical systems GAO had identified in 2019, only three had completed modernization by February 2025. Some remaining efforts were expected to continue for years.

These systems support health care, tax processing, infrastructure, national security, and other essential functions. They cannot simply be turned off because a modernization program has started.

That obligation changes the economics.

The agency must preserve service while rebuilding the mechanism that delivers it.

This is closer to replacing an aircraft engine in flight than constructing a new building on an empty site.

The metaphor is dramatic, but the constraint is ordinary.

Transactions keep arriving.

Citizens still need benefits.

Employees still need records.

Taxes still have to be processed.

Security systems still have to function.

The new architecture does not receive a pause in reality while it matures.

This is why transition planning matters as much as target architecture.

A diagram of the future system can be excellent and still fail to answer the most expensive question.

How do we get there from here without losing what the old system currently does?

GAO's modernization reviews repeatedly emphasize milestones, work required for migration, and the disposition of legacy systems. That last word—disposition—is easily skipped in strategy discussions.

Everyone likes the new system.

Someone has to kill the old one.

Until the death plan is specific, parallel operation tends to become the default.

The old system remains because one dependency is not ready.

A customer still needs an interface.

A dataset has not been migrated.

A report is not available in the new platform.

A regional office has not been trained.

One downstream system still reads the old format.

A regulator has not approved the change.

A critical edge case failed testing.

Each reason can be legitimate.

Together they can postpone retirement indefinitely.

This is how a bridge becomes a neighborhood.

People begin building on it.

New features are added to the legacy system because customers still use it.

The new system integrates with the old system instead of replacing it.

Staff are hired who know both.

Budgets assume both will exist next year.

Data synchronization becomes a permanent service.

Reports reconcile differences between them.

The organization becomes proud of its ability to operate complexity.

Adaptation debt has financed a new capability: living with the debt.

That capability can reduce the urgency to finish the transition.

This is one reason modernization programs are difficult to evaluate.

A long overlap can be evidence of caution.

It can also be evidence of failure.

The distinction depends on whether the overlap is moving toward a defined exit.

A parallel system with shrinking legacy usage, explicit migration milestones, declining maintenance investment, and a credible retirement date is a transition.

A parallel system with expanding integrations, new legacy features, indefinite support promises, and no accountable retirement owner is an architecture.

The diagrams can look similar.

The direction is different.

Netflix's DVD-to-streaming transition gives a consumer version of the same problem.

For years, the company operated both models at once.

The DVD service generated value while streaming grew in capability and adoption. The overlap allowed Netflix to learn the new business without requiring customers to abandon the old one immediately.

That was useful.

It also created conflicting economics and customer expectations.

The two services had different costs, different interfaces, different content constraints, and eventually different strategic importance.

The 2011 effort to separate plans and the Qwikster branding episode exposed how difficult it was to turn a long overlap into a clean organizational separation. The company understood that the businesses were becoming different. Customers still experienced them as one relationship.

Parallel systems create ambiguity about what is one product and what is two.

That ambiguity can be strategically useful for a while.

Then it becomes expensive.

Adobe's Creative Cloud transition carried another version.

Perpetual-license products did not disappear on the day subscription strategy changed. The company had to manage customers on different purchasing models, different revenue recognition patterns, and different upgrade expectations while subscriptions grew.

The transition depressed traditional revenue measures before recurring economics became fully visible.

Parallel business models are not only technical complexity.

They create financial complexity.

Which model receives marketing?

Which one gets feature investment?

Can the old product be priced in a way that encourages migration without punishing customers who cannot move?

Do salespeople earn the same compensation on both?

Does supporting the old model slow improvements in the new one?

Does the new model make the old one look artificially expensive because shared costs are allocated differently?

Every overlap creates an internal negotiation.

This is why Chapter 11's question—cannibalize what?—has a temporal answer too.

Sometimes the organization should not kill anything yet.

It should run two systems deliberately.

But deliberate parallelism needs a theory of what the overlap is buying.

Risk reduction is one answer.

Learning is another.

Customer migration is another.

Regulatory assurance can be another.

Capacity protection can be another.

The overlap earns its cost when it reduces uncertainty or irreversible failure risk enough to justify duplication.

This is easy to say and difficult to measure.

Insurance always looks expensive when nothing goes wrong.

A parallel production line that never has to take over can look wasteful.

A fallback IT system that is never used can look like dead cost.

A period of dual staffing can look inefficient if the migration proceeds smoothly.

The organization may therefore be tempted to cut the bridge too early.

That can be disastrous.

A new system can pass tests and still fail under full production load.

Users can behave differently after launch.

Data can contain edge cases the migration sample missed.

A supplier can fail.

A new workflow can create bottlenecks in downstream teams.

The old system can contain undocumented behavior nobody realized customers depended on.

A safe transition often needs a rollback path.

This is the same logic behind reversible deployment in software.

Release gradually.

Observe.

Keep the ability to return.

Increase exposure as evidence accumulates.

The organization is purchasing information before committing fully.

That is option value.

Chapter 16 will return to the idea more directly.

Parallel systems are one practical way to buy an option.

The problem is that options expire poorly when nobody sets an expiration.

A rollback path can become a permanent path.

The organization keeps the old system “just in case.”

Users discover that if they complain enough, they can stay on it.

Teams avoid fixing hard migration problems because fallback remains possible.

Vendors continue supporting the legacy product because revenue remains.

The new system never receives the full pressure of production, so some defects remain hidden.

Optionality becomes avoidance.

This is why transition governance needs a ratchet.

The system should be capable of moving toward the new state even when individual cases pull backward.

New customers default to the new platform.

Old interfaces stop receiving new features.

Legacy usage becomes visible by owner and reason.

Exceptions require explicit renewal.

Migration milestones reduce the population remaining on the old path.

Retirement criteria are written before everyone is exhausted by the project.

Again, the exact ritual matters less than the direction.

The organization has to make inertia visible.

Without visibility, parallelism hides delay.

A project can say migration is ninety percent complete for three years because the last ten percent contains the hardest dependencies.

That last ten percent can account for most of the risk, cost, and political resistance.

Percent complete is a dangerous metric when work is not homogeneous.

Moving ten thousand simple accounts can be easier than moving one critical integration.

Migrating thousands of users can be easier than retiring one obsolete data format embedded across many systems.

A transition should therefore track remaining constraint, not only remaining volume.

Which dependencies still prevent shutdown?

Who owns them?

What evidence shows they truly require the old system?

What would it cost to remove each one?

Which can be accepted as permanent exceptions?

Which should be isolated behind a compatibility layer?

Which represent customers the organization is willing to lose?

The last question is uncomfortable and necessary.

Parallel systems sometimes continue because nobody wants to choose a loser.

Every customer can stay.

Every employee can keep the old skill.

Every application can keep the old interface.

Every business unit can keep its process.

The new system is added rather than substituted.

This feels humane and flexible.

It can be strategically incoherent.

The organization pays transition cost forever without receiving the simplification that justified transition.

There is a physical version of this in infrastructure.

A city adds a new transportation mode without reallocating street space, maintenance budgets, or operating priorities from old modes.

An industrial site adds electrified equipment while keeping fossil systems fully capable.

A utility builds new generation while maintaining old capacity for reliability.

Sometimes redundancy is appropriate.

Sometimes the old system remains because political and operational costs of retirement are harder than costs of addition.

Societies are good at adding layers.

Subtraction requires naming who loses.

That is why installed worlds thicken.

Parallel systems are politically easier than replacement.

They promise adaptation without immediate write-down.

The bill arrives later as maintenance.

This is adaptation debt's favorite refinancing mechanism.

Do not pay the principal.

Add another system beside it.

The analogy should not be pushed too far because redundancy can be genuinely valuable.

The Reliability Tax, another book in this Desk, is built around that fact. Critical systems often need backup capacity, slack, and reversible paths precisely because optimization toward one route creates fragility.

Parallelism is not inherently debt.

The distinction is purpose.

Redundancy is an intentional continuing design when two routes create resilience worth their cost.

Transition overlap is a temporary continuing design intended to end after uncertainty falls.

Confusing the two is expensive.

A legacy system can be relabeled “backup” when nobody has tested whether it can actually serve that role.

A workforce can be kept “for resilience” when the real reason is avoidance of a difficult reorganization.

A factory can remain “strategic capacity” while deteriorating because no one wants to record the write-down.

A rule can remain “just in case” long after the contingency has changed.

Resilience needs its own test.

What failure does the parallel system protect against?

How likely is it?

Can the backup actually take over?

What does readiness cost?

Would a smaller reserve provide the same protection?

If the old system is never exercised, how do we know it still works?

These questions separate real option value from sentimental retention.

The same test applies to expertise.

During a technology transition, organizations often need people who understand the old system even after most work has moved.

Keep too few and a legacy failure becomes impossible to repair.

Keep too many and the old skill structure remains politically dominant.

The transition needs a declining but deliberate knowledge base.

Documentation helps.

Cross-training helps.

Rotation helps.

Pairing old and new experts helps.

But tacit knowledge does not transfer perfectly.

Some capability will be lost.

That is part of the write-down.

A mature transition acknowledges it rather than pretending both eras can be preserved at full strength.

The Air Force's financial-system migration illustrates how operational these concerns become. GAO's work on moving from a legacy accounting system to the Defense Enterprise Accounting and Management System emphasized migration planning, data work, deployment, and training across sites. The transition was not only a software installation. Users had to be trained. Workloads had to move. Milestones had to coordinate technical and organizational change.

That is what parallel systems actually contain.

People living in two worlds.

For a while, they need to know both languages.

They reconcile both outputs.

They explain to customers why one transaction appears in one place and another somewhere else.

They absorb the exceptions strategy slides leave out.

Transition fatigue is therefore real.

A company can exhaust itself carrying old and new long enough that employees begin preferring whichever system makes the ambiguity end.

This creates a hidden deadline.

Even if money remains, attention does not.

Parallel systems consume attention because every difference becomes a question.

Which source is authoritative?

Which process should be followed?

Which system receives the update first?

Which team owns the incident?

Which number goes to the board?

Which customer promise applies?

The organization can spend years answering “both.”

Eventually, “both” stops being a strategy.

The best parallel transitions therefore begin with the ending written down.

Not an aspirational date chosen to satisfy a steering committee.

A definition of done.

All critical workloads migrated.

Rollback no longer needed after a specified stability period.

Customers below a defined exception threshold.

Data reconciled.

Required controls certified.

Legacy interfaces isolated or retired.

People transferred or trained.

Support contracts ended.

Hardware disposed.

Budget removed.

The important step is the last one.

Budget removed.

An organization has not retired a system while money remains available to keep it comfortably alive.

The budget is the strongest signal of which commitments the institution still considers real.

This is why decommissioning is a strategic act.

A server switched off is visible.

A line item deleted is final.

A team reassigned means the organization can no longer return easily.

These acts feel risky because they destroy option value.

At some point, that destruction is the point.

Adaptation requires commitment to the new path too.

A company that preserves every route never receives the efficiencies of choosing.

The same thing that made the old system powerful—complementary investment—has to begin forming around the new one.

People need to specialize.

Customers need to standardize.

Processes need to settle.

Metrics need history.

Suppliers need volume.

The new system has to become furniture.

That cannot happen fully while everyone expects to move back.

This is the irony of adaptation.

The organization escapes one path dependence by creating another.

It has to.

A system with no commitments has no capability.

The goal is not permanent fluidity.

It is the ability to commit without forgetting that commitment has a price.

Parallel systems make that price visible because, for a while, the organization pays both.

The overlap is a privilege.

It buys learning, safety, and time.

It should be used to discover what the new system still lacks.

Then it should end.

Otherwise the bridge becomes another place the institution has to maintain forever.
