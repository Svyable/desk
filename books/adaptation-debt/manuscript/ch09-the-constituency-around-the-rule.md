# The Constituency Around the Rule

A rule changes behavior before anyone learns to love the rule.

That is part of what rules are for.

A tax credit makes one investment more attractive than another. A zoning code changes what can be built on a parcel. A safety requirement changes how a machine is designed. A benefit program changes household budgets. A procurement rule changes which suppliers learn to serve government. A licensing regime changes which careers people enter. A standard changes what manufacturers produce and what customers expect to fit together.

Then time passes.

People make plans.

Companies invest.

Workers train.

Lawyers write contracts.

Agencies build administrative systems.

Vendors create products for the rule as it exists. Beneficiaries organize their lives around benefits they were told to expect. Property values absorb regulatory assumptions. Banks lend against cash flows produced inside the policy. Local governments hire people to administer it. Trade associations learn its vocabulary. Courts interpret its ambiguities. Consultants discover a business explaining it.

The rule acquires a constituency.

This phrase is often spoken with suspicion.

A constituency sounds like a pressure group defending a privilege.

Sometimes that is exactly what it is.

A narrow industry can lobby to preserve a protection that imposes diffuse costs on everyone else. A licensing rule can become a barrier to entry long after its original safety rationale weakens. A subsidy can survive because recipients are organized while taxpayers are not. An administrative process can continue because a professional class has grown around operating it.

But that version is too easy for this book.

A constituency can also exist because the rule solved a real problem and people rationally trusted it.

A retired person who planned around a public benefit is not a special interest in the same sense as a firm protecting a tariff.

A manufacturer that spent millions qualifying a product under a safety standard is not irrational for caring how the standard changes.

A family that bought a home under a zoning regime has made a long-lived decision partly on the assumption that neighboring uses will remain within some range.

A utility that financed infrastructure under a regulatory settlement cannot instantly treat the settlement as a historical curiosity.

The rule created expectations.

Expectations created investments.

The investments create switching costs.

This is institutional adaptation debt.

The policy-feedback literature gives a precise way to think about the mechanism. Policies do not merely allocate benefits and costs once. They can change resources, incentives, identities, organizations, and expectations in ways that reshape the politics surrounding later policy choices. Benefits that are concentrated, visible, and tied clearly to a program can strengthen the organizations capable of defending that program. Rules can also create losses that organize opposition. Institutional design affects who notices the policy and who has leverage over its future.

The important word is feedback.

Policy enters society.

Society adapts to policy.

The adapted society becomes the environment in which the next policy decision is made.

This makes institutional change different from choosing a rule on a blank page.

A government can decide in year one that a subsidy is poorly designed.

By year ten, factories may exist because of it.

A government can conclude that an entitlement formula should be different.

By then households may have made retirement decisions around the old formula.

A city can decide that land-use rules should permit more density.

By then owners on both sides of the change may have paid prices that reflected the previous system.

The original policy question was: what rule should we adopt?

The later question is: what rule should we adopt now that millions of decisions have been made under the old one?

The second question contains history.

That history is not automatically a veto.

If every reliance interest became permanent, no institution could change.

Bad policy would become immortal simply because it lasted long enough to create beneficiaries.

The constituency around the rule would be able to manufacture its own argument for survival.

This is one of the strongest objections to the concept of adaptation debt.

Every policy has transition costs.

If transition cost is treated as proof that change is unwise, incumbent arrangements win by default.

The point is not to obey the constituency.

It is to count the constituency honestly.

That means distinguishing several kinds of dependence that political debate often collapses.

One is **legitimate reliance**.

A person or organization made a costly decision because the rule intentionally encouraged that decision. The more explicit and long-lived the promise, the stronger the case for a managed transition rather than an abrupt reversal.

Another is **administrative dependence**.

An agency has people, software, procedures, vendors, and expertise dedicated to implementing the rule. Changing the rule can require a second organizational transformation beyond the legal change itself.

Another is **complementary investment**.

Private actors built assets, products, contracts, or careers that fit the rule. Those investments may retain value under a new regime or become partly stranded.

Another is **political rent**.

A group receives an advantage from the status quo and has organized to defend it even when the original public rationale is weak.

The same institution can contain all four.

That is what makes reform difficult.

It is easier to say that a rule has been captured than to separate the retiree who planned responsibly, the vendor who invested under a public contract, the agency employee whose expertise remains useful, and the firm whose profit depends on preventing competition.

They all appear at the hearing.

They do not all represent the same claim.

This is where institutional memory becomes morally complicated.

A private company can sometimes decide that a customer segment is no longer strategic and accept the loss.

A government has a different obligation. Citizens are not merely customers. Rules can structure rights, duties, eligibility, liberty, safety, property, and access to essential services. Change can impose harms on people who did nothing more than believe the institution.

That gives continuity real value.

It also gives continuity a cost.

The institution may remain faithful to an old promise after the conditions that made the promise affordable or useful have changed.

The constituency can become the mechanism through which yesterday keeps collecting votes from tomorrow.

Policy designers sometimes try to manage this with grandfathering.

The old rule continues for people already inside the system.

New entrants receive the new rule.

This can reduce the sense of betrayal.

It can also create two classes of people living under different terms for years or decades.

Grandfathering is a parallel system.

It services adaptation debt by spreading the write-down through time.

A pension reform can preserve accrued benefits while changing future accruals. A building code can apply new requirements to new construction while allowing existing structures to remain unless they undergo major renovation. A licensing regime can recognize existing practitioners while changing requirements for new ones. A software vendor can maintain an old version while placing new customers on a new platform.

The logic is similar across domains.

Do not force the full transition cost onto people who made irreversible decisions under the old rule.

But grandfathering can create a political trap of its own.

The old group remains organized.

The new group has no memory of the previous system.

Differences in benefits or obligations can become permanent sources of conflict. Administrators must maintain both regimes. Exceptions multiply. Every boundary case produces a fairness argument.

The transition mechanism becomes another layer of institutional history.

This is a recurring pattern in adaptation debt.

The bridge is not free.

A constituency also changes what information reaches decision makers.

Groups that receive concentrated benefits know exactly what a policy does for them. They have a reason to become expert in its details. The people paying diffuse costs may barely notice.

This asymmetry can make a status quo look more socially valued than it is.

The hearing room fills with people who can describe the harm of change in concrete terms.

The benefit of change may be spread across millions of people who each gain a little, or across future people who do not yet exist as an organized group.

The present constituency speaks in names.

The future constituency appears as a model.

That is the political version of the evidence asymmetry we saw in Chapter 5.

Existing capital can show production. New capital has a forecast.

Existing customers can show revenue. Future customers have a market estimate.

Existing beneficiaries can describe a loss. Future beneficiaries are hypothetical.

The status quo arrives with witnesses.

The alternative arrives with assumptions.

This does not make the witnesses wrong.

It changes the burden of proof.

A reform that ignores this asymmetry can underestimate political and human transition cost. A reform that treats it as decisive can become impossible.

The institution therefore needs a way to ask a harder question.

What did this rule originally buy?

This is the same question the book asks of factories and metrics.

A safety rule may have reduced a real hazard.

A benefit program may have solved a real income problem.

A procurement requirement may have improved accountability.

A zoning restriction may have separated incompatible uses.

A professional license may have protected people from low-quality or dangerous service.

A tax preference may have encouraged investment policymakers genuinely wanted.

The original rationale matters because reformers often confuse the visible cost of a rule with the absence of a reason for it.

Old institutions can look absurd after the problem they solved has become invisible.

The fire door is inconvenient until there is a fire.

The audit trail is bureaucracy until fraud is investigated.

The backup system looks wasteful until the primary fails.

The permit looks slow until construction creates an irreversible harm.

The difficult question is whether the original problem still exists, whether the rule still addresses it, and whether a better instrument is now available.

Those are separate tests.

A rule can address a real problem badly.

A rule can have worked well and become technologically obsolete.

A rule can have been captured while still performing useful functions.

A rule can create new harms larger than the old ones.

A rule can survive because nobody has measured what would happen without it.

Reform becomes more disciplined when these possibilities remain distinct.

This is where sunset clauses appear attractive.

Give the rule an expiration date.

Force the institution to reconsider it.

Chapter 17 will return to sunsets because they are one way to prevent commitments from becoming permanent by default.

For now, notice the limitation.

An expiration date does not erase the constituency.

If people have invested under the rule, sunset merely schedules the conflict.

The political economy still arrives at the deadline.

In some cases the looming expiration can intensify lobbying because every beneficiary knows exactly when the threat arrives.

A sunset changes the default.

It does not remove adaptation debt.

The same is true of review commissions, automatic triggers, pilot programs, and temporary authorities.

They help most when the organization preserves evidence about the original rationale and the conditions under which continuation should be questioned.

Without that record, periodic review becomes ritual.

The agency asks whether the program remains important.

Beneficiaries say yes.

Administrators describe activity.

Vendors describe investment.

Critics describe cost.

Everyone speaks from the system the policy already created.

The missing question is what the counterfactual looks like now.

If we designed the institution today, with today's technology, prices, population, risks, and administrative capacity, which parts would we rebuild and which would we keep?

That question can be unfair to history if asked carelessly.

Today's designers inherit assets yesterday's designers had to create.

A new program can look cheaper because the old program paid for infrastructure, data, public trust, professional knowledge, or market formation.

The new design may be free-riding on the very institution it criticizes.

Again, success can produce both the reason to change and the capacity to change.

A mature rule can create the institutions that make its successor possible.

A market that no longer needs a subsidy may exist partly because the subsidy helped it scale.

A profession that can now rely on standardized technology may have standardized partly because an older licensing system imposed common requirements.

A safety regime can become easier to simplify after decades of engineering improvement produced under that regime.

The constituency around the rule can therefore contain the seeds of reform.

Beneficiaries are often the people who know where the rule is clumsy.

Administrators know which exceptions consume time.

Regulated firms know which requirements duplicate one another.

Users know where eligibility boundaries create nonsense.

The same groups that can block change can also make change operationally possible.

This is why calling every constituency a vested interest is analytically lazy.

The institution needs knowledge from people who have lived under the rule.

It also needs a way to prevent that knowledge from becoming a veto.

One approach is to separate evidence about transition cost from authority over the endpoint.

Ask incumbents what breaks if the rule changes.

Require specifics.

Which contracts?

Which assets?

Which users?

Which legal obligations?

Which safety functions?

Which administrative systems?

How long would migration take?

What would it cost?

Which harms are temporary and which are irreversible?

Then make the policy judgment separately.

The constituency describes the debt.

It does not get to set the interest rate by itself.

This can expose exaggerated claims.

A group may say a change is impossible when it means expensive.

It may say customers cannot adapt when it means some customers prefer not to.

It may describe a familiar administrative process as essential when a simpler one exists.

It may invoke safety without showing the mechanism.

It may treat every job tied to the old rule as proof the rule should continue.

Employment is a transition consideration.

It is not a permanent argument for any institution that happens to employ people.

At the same time, reformers exaggerate in the opposite direction.

They call every dependence rent-seeking.

They describe transition assistance as political weakness.

They assume a rule can disappear on the date a statute changes.

They treat compliance capital as if it evaporates without loss.

They forget that abrupt institutional change can reduce trust in future policy because people learn that today's promise may be tomorrow's write-down.

An adaptive state has to preserve the ability to change without teaching citizens that nothing can be relied upon.

That is a difficult balance.

Private organizations face the same problem with standards and internal rules.

A company creates a review process after a serious failure.

Years later the process is slow and widely disliked.

The original people are gone.

Nobody remembers the incident clearly.

A new team proposes removing the step.

The veterans say it exists for a reason.

They may be right.

They may also be defending a procedure whose risk control could now be achieved automatically.

The organization needs the institutional equivalent of a medical history.

Why was this rule created?

What failure did it prevent?

What evidence shows the risk remains?

What evidence shows the rule works?

What cost does it impose?

What replacement control is available?

What constituency depends on it, and why?

Without answers, the rule survives as folklore.

With answers, it can be treated as a design.

That is the deeper point of this chapter.

Institutions accumulate adaptation debt when rules stop being understood as interventions and start being experienced as reality.

A regulation becomes “how the industry works.”

A benefit becomes “what people receive.”

A procurement process becomes “how government buys.”

A standard becomes “what the product is.”

A committee becomes “who has to approve.”

The historical decision disappears inside the current environment.

The constituency is part of that environment.

Its existence is not evidence that the rule is good.

It is evidence that the rule has done something powerful enough for people to organize around it.

That power should make reform more careful, not impossible.

The rule changed people.

Now changing the rule means changing a world partly built in its image.
