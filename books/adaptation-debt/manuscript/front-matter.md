# Adaptation Debt

**Sven Hardy Benson**

---

A successful system is full of evidence that it should keep doing what it is doing.

Customers return. Machines run. Employees know what to do. Suppliers understand the specification. A regulator recognizes the category. The sales forecast has history behind it. The accounting system knows where to put the revenue. The people who are good at the work become more valuable as they get better at it. The people who manage the work become more credible as they reproduce the result.

Success is not merely money arriving.

Success is a whole environment learning how to expect you.

That is why the usual stories about failure after success are unsatisfying.

A company becomes complacent. A bureaucracy resists change. An executive misses the future. An expert protects a dying specialty. An old institution fails to move as quickly as a new one. The adjectives come easily because we already know the ending.

The harder question is what the organization would have had to abandon before the ending was known.

A factory may still be productive on the day its future becomes questionable. A product may still generate most of a company's cash while a smaller technology begins to threaten it. A customer may still be worth serving even if the customer's preferences pull investment toward an aging market. A professional may still be excellent at the skill whose relative value has started to fall. A public program may still help millions of people even when its original design no longer fits the problem cleanly. A software system may still settle transactions correctly while everybody agrees it should eventually be replaced.

The old world does not usually disappear on schedule so that the new one can begin.

For a while, both are true.

The legacy business is profitable and weakening.

The new business is promising and uneconomic.

The old system is brittle and indispensable.

The new system is cleaner and unproven.

The established metric is misleading and still tied to compensation.

The old rule is obsolete in one case and protective in another.

The experienced person knows too much to be casually dismissed and has learned under conditions that are changing.

Transitions are difficult partly because reality refuses to make the choice obvious at the moment a choice has to be made.

This book is about the cost that accumulates during that ambiguity.

I call it **adaptation debt**.

The phrase is meant to name something narrower than resistance to change.

A system accumulates adaptation debt when a decision that once made sense becomes surrounded by enough complementary commitments that changing it later costs far more than choosing differently would have cost at the beginning.

A company buys equipment for a product that sells. The equipment requires maintenance specialists. The specialists create procedures. The procedures enter audits. Suppliers tool up for the specification. Customers design their own workflows around the product. Salespeople learn how to sell it. Managers build forecasts from its history. Financing assumes its cash flow. A regulator learns the category. An industry standard reflects its interfaces.

None of these steps is irrational.

Together they make the next step less free.

That is the mechanism.

The debt is not hidden because anybody falsified the books. It is hidden because the costs live on different books.

The factory sees depreciation.

The technology group sees migration work.

The sales team sees customer retention.

Human resources sees retraining.

Finance sees revenue at risk.

Legal sees contracts.

Compliance sees obligations.

Employees see careers.

Customers see disruption.

Executives see all of these as separate problems arriving at the same meeting.

The organization rarely has a line called **cost of becoming different**.

So it keeps paying the cost in fragments.

The idea has ancestors, and they matter.

Organizations have long been studied as systems that learn from experience and then become shaped by what they learned. Barbara Levitt and James March described organizational learning as routine-based, history-dependent, and target-oriented. James March later wrote about the tension between exploiting what an organization already knows and exploring possibilities whose payoff is uncertain. Dorothy Leonard-Barton showed how core capabilities can become core rigidities. Rebecca Henderson and Kim Clark examined how established firms can struggle when innovation changes the architecture connecting components they already understand. Clayton Christensen and Joseph Bower showed how powerful existing customers can shape which innovations receive resources. Michael Tushman and Charles O'Reilly developed the idea of organizational ambidexterity: the difficult task of exploiting mature businesses while exploring new ones.

This book does not replace that literature with a catchy phrase.

It borrows from it.

The reason for the new phrase is practical. The word **debt** makes one feature easier to notice: a past success can impose a future payment without having been a mistake when it was made.

That distinction changes how we think about adaptation.

If an organization failed only because leaders were stupid, the solution would be better leaders.

If it failed only because it ignored the future, the solution would be better forecasting.

If it failed only because employees resisted change, the solution would be stronger incentives or more aggressive restructuring.

Those prescriptions sometimes help. They are often incomplete.

A leader can understand the future and still face a factory whose useful life has years left.

A board can agree on the threat and still depend on the threatened product to fund the replacement.

Employees can support the new direction and still need time to become competent in it.

Customers can want the innovation and still refuse a migration that breaks compatibility with systems they already own.

A regulator can recognize a new technology and still be responsible for risks that were easier to police under the old category.

A city can want a new transport system and still have streets, property lines, utilities, debt, and neighborhoods arranged around the current one.

A person can know that a career is changing and still have a mortgage, reputation, network, salary, and sense of self tied to the existing role.

Recognition is not the same thing as mobility.

The opposite mistake is to glorify change.

This book is not an argument that old commitments should be liquidated whenever something new appears.

Commitments are why complex systems work.

A standard is valuable because people can rely on it. A bridge is useful because its design does not change with fashion. A professional becomes trustworthy by remaining with a domain long enough to acquire judgment. A company creates value by building assets and routines that competitors cannot reproduce instantly. A public institution earns legitimacy partly by behaving predictably. A contract matters because one party cannot rewrite it every time a better option appears.

A world with no adaptation debt would also be a world with very little investment.

Nothing would become specific enough to become excellent.

That is why the debt metaphor has to do more than make permanence sound suspicious.

Financial debt can be productive. Borrowing can build a factory, a home, a school, a network. The existence of debt tells us nothing by itself about whether the original decision was wise. The important questions are what the borrowing bought, how the obligation changes future choices, and whether the asset continues producing enough value to service what is owed.

Adaptation debt works the same way.

A specialized factory can outperform a flexible one for years. A deeply optimized process can make a company rich. A stable bureaucracy can administer benefits more reliably than an endlessly reorganized one. A long-lived software platform can support millions of users because its interfaces remain boring. A professional identity can motivate decades of disciplined learning.

The problem begins when the payment schedule changes and the institution does not notice.

The old commitment still appears on the asset side of the mental balance sheet. The cost of changing it is scattered elsewhere. The organization therefore compares a mature system's current performance with a new system's early weakness, rather than comparing the mature system's future path with the full cost and future value of transition.

This asymmetry is one reason newcomers often look heroic.

The entrant does not have to persuade yesterday's customers to migrate.

It does not have to maintain compatibility with every old version.

It does not have a salesforce whose compensation depends on the legacy product.

It does not have a factory to depreciate, a union agreement written around the current process, a regulatory classification shaped around an older technology, or a board accustomed to the old margin structure.

The entrant also lacks things the incumbent would be foolish to envy.

It may have no customers at all.

No distribution.

No trust.

No operating history.

No cash flow.

No institutional memory.

No proof that the new market is real.

The interesting difference is not courage.

It is the starting balance sheet of commitments.

The book begins with Kodak because the company has become one of the most abused stories in business history. The simplified version says that Kodak invented the digital camera, ignored its own invention, clung to film, and was destroyed by the future it refused to see.

The record is less convenient. Kodak invested heavily in digital technologies. In its January 2012 bankruptcy announcement, the company said roughly three quarters of its 2011 revenue came from digital businesses. Its filings describe the decline of film-related volumes, digital-camera competition, investment in inkjet, efforts to monetize intellectual property, legacy liabilities, and the difficult economics of replacing the businesses that had once made Kodak powerful.

That does not absolve management of every mistake.

It makes the case worth studying.

Seeing the future and becoming the organization that can thrive in it are different capabilities.

Later chapters will move through factories, software, metrics, expertise, customers, public rules, corporate transitions, personal identity, and the ways institutions deliberately keep options alive. We will look at companies that failed to adapt and companies that accepted ugly transition economics early. We will look at old systems that deserve retirement and old systems whose longevity is evidence of value. We will look at the danger of moving too slowly and the less fashionable danger of moving too early.

The purpose is not to produce a management checklist.

It is to improve the question asked at the moment a successful system begins to feel strangely heavy.

The usual question is whether the new thing is better.

That is rarely enough.

A better question is:

**What has learned to depend on the old thing, and what will it cost to teach the system otherwise?**

That cost is adaptation debt.

Every successful system owes some.

The question is whether it knows how much.
