# Capital Has a Memory

A machine can be obsolete and still be expensive to replace.

That sounds obvious until an institution has to act on it.

In July 2025, the U.S. Government Accountability Office published another review of federal legacy information systems. The report did not describe a government that had simply forgotten computers existed. Agencies had modernization efforts underway. Some had already replaced systems that GAO had flagged years earlier. Others had plans stretching several years into the future.

The striking part was how much of the old world remained economically alive.

GAO identified eleven critical systems, spread across ten agencies, that most needed modernization. They supported functions tied to health care, tax processing, critical infrastructure, and national security. Some relied on programming languages with shrinking pools of experienced maintainers. Some depended on unsupported hardware or software. Several carried known cybersecurity vulnerabilities. Yet they were not abandoned museum pieces. They were still doing work.

Together, those eleven systems cost about $754 million a year to operate and maintain.

The ages ranged from roughly twenty-three to sixty years.

The range matters because it weakens the easy story that legacy is simply a synonym for ancient. A twenty-three-year-old system and a sixty-year-old system can both become hard to replace for the same reason: enough mission activity has learned to expect them.

GAO's profiles also show how capital memory migrates into labor and risk. Eight of the eleven systems used legacy programming languages. The two Treasury systems in the selected group relied on COBOL and assembly language, and GAO highlighted the shrinking number of people available with those skills. The Environmental Protection Agency system in the group had obsolete hardware that was no longer supported by manufacturers and known vulnerabilities that could not be fully remediated without modernization.

The physical or technical asset is therefore only the first layer.

The second layer is the maintenance ecology.

Who still knows how to repair it?

Who still manufactures the part?

Who still supports the operating system?

Who still remembers what a strange field means?

Who can distinguish a harmless warning from the beginning of a failure?

An asset becomes strategically older when its ecology begins disappearing even if the asset itself keeps working.

The federal government’s broader IT budget made the pattern even clearer. Agencies typically reported spending most of their annual IT money not on building the next generation, but on operating and maintaining what already existed.

This is what installed capital does.

It keeps collecting reasons to survive.

The first reason is usually simple: it works.

A payroll system that is ugly but reliably pays hundreds of thousands of people is not equivalent to an ugly prototype sitting on a developer’s laptop. A refinery that has decades of maintenance history, trained operators, permits, supplier contracts, and known tolerances is not equivalent to a diagram of a cleaner replacement. A building with imperfect insulation still has tenants, plumbing, elevators, fire systems, financing, and a location in a city that already grew around it.

The investment becomes more than the object.

It becomes a bundle of solved problems.

That is why capital has memory.

Not literal memory, of course. A turbine does not remember the meeting that approved it. A data center does not remember the demand forecast that justified it. A warehouse does not remember which competitor frightened management into expanding capacity. But the past decision remains present in physical form. Steel, concrete, wiring, software, tooling, leases, debt service, tax treatment, trained labor, maintenance routines, spare parts, permits, interfaces, and customer expectations continue carrying the decision forward.

The past has acquired mass.

That mass changes what is cheap to do next.

Economics courses teach an important discipline: sunk costs should not determine future decisions. Money already spent is gone. A rational decision should compare future costs and benefits rather than throwing good money after bad because an earlier investment was painful.

That principle is correct.

It is also easy to misuse.

A sunk cost is not the same thing as a switching cost.

The purchase price of yesterday’s machine may be sunk. The cost of shutting it down is not. The money spent training the old workforce may be sunk. The cost of training people on the new system is not. The original installation bill may be sunk. The downtime required to cut over production is not. The old software license may be sunk. The migration errors, duplicated systems, temporary consultants, customer retraining, and reconciliation work needed to leave it are not.

One of the most common mistakes in adaptation is to wave away installed capital as though declaring it sunk makes the transition free.

It does not.

The relevant question is not whether the old investment should be emotionally defended. It is what still depends on it.

Energy systems make this visible because their assets are enormous and slow.

A power station or industrial plant can be built to operate for decades. The International Energy Agency has long emphasized a simple problem in energy transitions: existing assets carry future emissions with them. If a plant continues operating through its normal useful life, its physical existence constrains the path toward lower emissions unless it is retrofitted, operated differently, displaced, or retired early.

The IEA's 2020 analysis of carbon capture made the installed-base logic explicit. In its transition scenario, the early emphasis was on retrofitting existing fossil-fuel power and industrial plants rather than pretending the system would be rebuilt instantly on clean sites. The scenario was not a prediction and should not be treated as one. Its value here is structural: even a model designed around a radically different emissions future had to begin with assets already standing.

That is what capital memory does to scenarios.

It changes the first move.

The future can be different while the transition still starts inside the present.

The constraint is not ideological.

It is mechanical and financial.

Retiring early may mean writing off productive equipment before its expected economic life is over. Retrofitting may require new capital and may reduce usable space, efficiency, or uptime. Replacing the asset may require new transmission, pipelines, storage, permits, or fuel supply. The cleaner alternative may be cheaper on a new-build basis while still being expensive as a transition from what is already there.

This distinction matters because public debates often compare technologies as if every decision begins on an empty field.

Very few important systems do.

Buildings make the point without requiring a power plant.

The U.S. Department of Energy's residential building-stock work does not describe one universal retrofit. It segments the existing stock by building type, geography, technical package, and cost. That is necessary because “replace the building” is rarely the decision households, owners, utilities, or policymakers actually face.

The existing structure already embodies dozens of choices.

Climate.

Envelope.

Heating system.

Electrical service.

Floor plan.

Water heating.

Roof condition.

Local labor.

Utility rates.

Financing.

Tenant or owner incentives.

A retrofit has to encounter those choices one building at a time.

This is why a technology can be inexpensive at the equipment level and still spread slowly through a building stock.

The device is not the project.

The project includes assessment, installation, electrical or structural work, permits, disruption, financing, contractor capacity, and the question of whether another major component should be replaced at the same time.

Natural turnover therefore becomes strategically important.

A furnace that fails creates a decision window.

A roof replacement creates a different one.

A building sale can create another.

A major renovation can allow several systems to change together.

The same retrofit that looks expensive as a stand-alone intervention may become much cheaper when paired with work that had to happen anyway.

Capital memory has a calendar.

Ignore the calendar and adaptation is unnecessarily expensive.

Wait for every natural boundary and adaptation may be too slow.

That tension is more useful than the command to replace old assets faster.

The question is which turnover moments should be accelerated, which should be used when they arrive, and which assets still create enough value to justify another cycle.

This applies to industrial equipment as well.

A plant shutdown for scheduled maintenance can create a window for retrofit that does not exist during peak production. A contract renewal can create a supplier-switching window. A fleet replacement cycle can change propulsion without discarding vehicles halfway through useful life. A data-center refresh can alter architecture while avoiding an emergency migration. A lease expiration can make relocation or redesign cheaper.

The balance sheet sees depreciation schedules.

Adaptation needs to see decision windows.

A city does not choose transportation from scratch. It has streets, rights-of-way, stations, parking structures, bridges, commuting patterns, zoning rules, and neighborhoods whose property values partly reflect access to the existing network.

A hospital does not choose an information system from scratch. It has interfaces to laboratories, insurers, pharmacies, imaging devices, scheduling systems, archived records, compliance processes, and thousands of workers who know where the strange buttons are.

A bank does not choose a core ledger from scratch. It has decades of transactions, regulatory obligations, settlement connections, product logic, reconciliation processes, and customers who expect their money to remain exactly where it was during the migration.

The old asset is surrounded by complements.

Those complements are where much of adaptation debt hides.

A replacement can be technically superior and still lose on the total cost of transition.

This is one reason large organizations sometimes appear irrational from the outside. The outsider sees the inferior machine. The insider sees the machine plus the hundreds of connections that have accreted around it.

Both are seeing something real.

The outsider may still be right that change is necessary. But the burden of proof should include the surrounding system.

There is an old temptation in technology to mistake greenfield elegance for economic superiority.

A greenfield system can choose current tools, current standards, current assumptions, and current organizational structure. It does not have to preserve every behavior accumulated during the previous twenty years. It can make clean decisions because fewer people have already organized their lives around dirty ones.

Brownfield change is different.

The new system has to coexist with the old one while the old one is still carrying real traffic.

That coexistence is expensive.

You run two systems.

You reconcile them.

You train people twice.

You discover that a field labeled `customer_id` meant six slightly different things in six different departments.

You learn that a nightly batch process nobody considered strategically important is the only thing feeding a downstream report regulators expect at six every morning.

You find a piece of equipment whose replacement part is no longer manufactured but whose output is calibrated into the next three stages of production.

The transition does not fail because the future was misunderstood.

It fails because the past was undercounted.

The mistake can run the other way too.

Installed capital is not automatically debt.

Sometimes age is evidence of continuing value.

IBM’s mainframe business is useful precisely because it resists the lazy equation of old with obsolete. Mainframe systems have survived repeated waves of computing architecture not because customers were unaware of alternatives, but because compatibility, reliability, security requirements, transaction workloads, and accumulated software investment can make continuity economically rational. IBM’s lifecycle documentation is full of the thing disruptive-innovation stories often skip: upgrade paths.

An upgrade path changes the adaptation problem.

Instead of asking an institution to choose between preserving the entire past and abandoning it, the system creates a route by which some past investments survive the move.

That route can be extraordinarily valuable.

Backward compatibility is, in effect, a way of lowering adaptation debt.

A new processor that can run old workloads preserves options. A new software version that can ingest the old data format reduces migration cost. A new payment rail that can interoperate with the existing one reduces the amount of coordinated change required on day one. A building retrofit that uses the existing structure avoids the carbon, time, permitting, and financing costs of demolition and replacement.

The useful question is therefore not whether capital remembers.

It always does.

The useful question is what kind of memory it carries.

Some memory is productive.

It contains solved engineering problems, trusted interfaces, accumulated safety knowledge, hard-earned reliability, trained operators, and customer confidence. Destroying it casually is a form of organizational amnesia.

Other memory has become expensive.

It ties budgets to maintenance rather than renewal. It requires scarce expertise merely to keep old dependencies functioning. It forces new products through interfaces designed for requirements that no longer matter. It keeps capital tied up in assets whose remaining cash flows look acceptable only because the cost of changing has never been calculated honestly.

The difference cannot be determined by age alone.

That is one reason the federal legacy-system problem is harder than it looks.

A fifty-year-old system may be astonishingly dependable at a narrow job. Replacing it badly can create a worse system at enormous cost. Yet GAO’s reports also document the other side: unsupported components, shrinking skill pools, cybersecurity risk, and modernization projects so complicated that even planning the disposition of the old system is difficult.

The danger arrives when the institution treats continuing operation as evidence that continuing operation remains cheap.

Those are different claims.

A legacy asset often becomes more expensive gradually.

A vendor exits.

A spare part gets harder to find.

A specialist retires.

A security patch becomes impossible.

A workaround becomes standard procedure.

A new product has to translate itself into an old data model.

A regulatory exception is added because the system cannot support the new requirement cleanly.

Each individual accommodation may be rational. Together they can turn maintenance into a growing tax on future change.

This is adaptation debt behaving like interest.

The debt does not announce itself as a bill.

It appears as a series of locally reasonable expenses.

Another contractor.

Another interface.

Another exception.

Another year of dual operation.

Another project deferred because the migration is too risky this quarter.

The institution can remain solvent in cash terms while its strategic freedom narrows.

Capital allocation systems are often bad at seeing this because budgets separate categories that the transition recombines.

Maintenance sits in one budget.

New capital sits in another.

Training belongs to someone else.

Downtime is counted as an operating issue.

Customer churn is a commercial problem.

Cyber risk is assigned to security.

Write-downs sit with finance.

Nobody owns the total price of remaining on the path.

That makes the old system look cheaper than it is.

The reverse distortion is also possible. Transformation programs can load every conceivable future benefit onto the new system while treating transition costs as one-time annoyances. A sleek replacement is approved on a slide where migration is a narrow bar between “current state” and “future state.” Then the organization discovers that migration is the project.

The new architecture was the easy part.

The difficult work is carrying history across the bridge.

This is why robust adaptation needs a more disciplined way to think about capital.

The first number is not book value.

It is remaining usefulness.

What does the old asset still do well? Which outputs remain economically or socially important? How long could it perform those functions safely and competitively if nothing else changed?

The second number is not replacement cost.

It is transition cost.

What must operate in parallel? Which interfaces must be preserved? How much retraining is needed? What data must move? What customers, counterparties, or regulators must change behavior? What new failure modes appear during cutover?

The third number is not theoretical superiority.

It is option value.

Does the new investment make later changes easier or harder? Does it reduce dependence on a scarce skill, proprietary format, single vendor, inflexible asset, or brittle interface? Does it create modularity? Can it be upgraded without another full migration?

The fourth number is the cost of waiting.

This is usually the least visible one.

Waiting can preserve cash and avoid premature write-downs. It can also increase dependency, shrink the pool of people who understand the old system, deepen customer entanglement, and make the eventual migration more urgent. Delay is not neutral when the surrounding ecosystem is moving.

A good decision can still be to wait.

But waiting should earn its keep too.

The deeper point is that capital makes strategy path-dependent because physical and technical investments are not merely records of earlier beliefs. They shape present feasibility.

A factory built for yesterday can be upgraded, repurposed, run down, sold, mothballed, or closed.

Each path has a cost.

The existence of those costs does not prove the factory was a mistake.

Quite the opposite. The cost may exist because the factory was useful enough for an entire system to grow around it.

That is the paradox at the center of adaptation debt.

Success makes commitment rational.

Commitment makes departure expensive.

The institution gets into trouble only when it keeps accounting for the value the asset created while failing to account for the freedom the asset now consumes.

Capital has a memory because every serious investment leaves behind a preferred next move.

Adaptation begins when that preference is measured rather than obeyed.
