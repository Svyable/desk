# Success Leaves Furniture Behind

An organization that learns nothing is not adaptable.

It is incompetent.

This sounds obvious until the conversation turns to change. Then memory begins to look suspicious. Routines become bureaucracy. Standards become legacy. Experience becomes bias. The old way becomes something to escape.

But a system that never hardens any lesson into routine would spend its life rediscovering how to open the building.

A hospital cannot renegotiate sterile procedure every morning. An airline cannot ask each crew to invent its own checklist. A bank cannot improvise the meaning of a settled transaction. A factory cannot treat every unit as an experiment. A software service cannot change its interface whenever one engineer has a better idea. A government cannot make rights depend entirely on which clerk is working that afternoon.

Reliability requires repetition.

Repetition requires memory.

Memory changes the cost of change.

That is the sequence.

Barbara Levitt and James March described organizational learning as routine-based, history-dependent, and target-oriented. Organizations infer lessons from experience and encode those lessons into routines that guide later behavior. This is how knowledge survives individual turnover. The person who discovered why a process fails does not have to be present every time the process runs. The lesson moves into a checklist, procedure, design, database, training program, approval threshold, or ordinary habit.

That movement is one of civilization's great tricks.

It is also how yesterday becomes embedded in tomorrow.

A routine does not remember only the lesson.

It remembers something about the environment in which the lesson was learned.

A reorder point assumes something about lead times.

A staffing model assumes something about demand.

A security procedure assumes something about the threat.

A procurement rule assumes something about suppliers and risk.

A sales territory assumes something about geography.

A product architecture assumes something about which components change together.

A professional curriculum assumes something about what a competent beginner needs to know.

The rule can continue working after the assumption changes.

That is when the organization begins to look strange from outside.

Why are they still doing that?

Inside, the answer may be simple: because it still works often enough, because other processes expect it, because nobody owns the full cost of changing it, and because the alternative has not yet accumulated the evidence the old system has.

The old choice has acquired furniture.

Furniture is different from baggage.

Baggage announces itself as something carried.

Furniture becomes the room.

People walk around it without remembering that somebody chose where to put it.

An organization is full of this kind of furniture.

Consider software.

A new application can be designed around current tools. A twenty-year-old system must coexist with twenty years of data, user expectations, integrations, permissions, exceptions, audit history, and downstream dependencies. A line of code written long ago may be easy to replace in isolation and difficult to replace because nobody knows how many other processes learned to depend on its exact behavior.

The United States federal government provides unusually visible examples because the Government Accountability Office periodically inventories legacy information systems.

In 2025, GAO reported that the federal government spends more than $100 billion annually on information technology and cyber-related investments and that agencies have historically reported roughly four-fifths of that amount going to operations and maintenance. GAO identified eleven critical legacy systems most in need of modernization across ten agencies. Some used outdated programming languages. Some depended on unsupported hardware or software. Some operated with known cybersecurity vulnerabilities. For most of the systems GAO highlighted, modernization plans did not fully document the leading practices GAO expected.

It is easy to read this as a story about government being old.

That misses the harder fact.

The systems still supported essential missions.

A system becomes “legacy” not when it stops mattering but often when it matters so much that replacing it becomes dangerous.

Tax processing, health care, grants, infrastructure, national security, personnel, payments—these are not environments in which an institution can celebrate disruption for its own sake.

The old system may be costly and vulnerable.

It may also be carrying the country through Tuesday.

This creates a transition problem with two kinds of failure.

The first is failure to modernize.

The second is modernization that breaks the service.

The institution is judged for both.

That double responsibility produces behavior that looks conservative because, in an important sense, it is supposed to be conservative.

Michael Hannan and John Freeman made a related point in their work on structural inertia. Organizations are selected partly for their ability to reproduce structure reliably and to account for their actions. Inertia is therefore not simply a defect imposed on otherwise ideal organizations. The capacity to behave predictably can be part of what makes an organization legitimate.

A fire department that reinvents command structure at every incident would be adaptive only in the least useful meaning of the word.

A court that changes procedural rules in the middle of each case would be flexible and untrustworthy.

A payment network that abandons backward compatibility casually would be innovative and unusable.

This is why old systems require more careful language than the technology industry usually gives them.

Legacy can mean obsolete.

It can also mean inherited.

Inheritance can be burdensome, but it is usually inherited because somebody still values it.

IBM's mainframe business is a useful counterexample to the assumption that longevity itself is evidence of adaptation failure. IBM's 2026 mainframe lifecycle history notes that hardware families since the introduction of zArchitecture have historically averaged more than eleven years from general availability to service discontinuance, with upgrade paths designed to protect investment and extend asset life.

A long lifecycle can be a liability in one market and a product feature in another.

Banks, insurers, governments, airlines, and other large institutions do not necessarily want their most consequential systems to behave like consumer applications. Compatibility, predictability, service support, and the ability to move workloads without rewriting every dependency can have enormous value.

If we labeled every durable system adaptation debt, we would confuse commitment with failure.

The better question is what the durability is buying.

Sometimes it buys time.

Sometimes it buys reliability.

Sometimes it buys scale.

Sometimes it buys trust.

Sometimes it buys the ability for strangers to coordinate without renegotiating every interface.

Sometimes it merely postpones a cost.

Those cases can look identical for years.

This is one reason organizations become path dependent without anybody signing a document called Path Dependence Plan.

Jörg Sydow, Georg Schreyögg, and Jochen Koch describe organizational path dependence in terms of self-reinforcing mechanisms that can narrow later choices. A choice becomes more attractive because it has already been chosen. Coordination improves around it. Learning makes people better at it. Complementary investments increase its usefulness. Expectations settle. The path gains momentum.

This is not mysterious.

Imagine two tools that are roughly equivalent when a team chooses between them.

The team selects one.

People learn it.

Templates are created for it.

Scripts are written around it.

New employees are hired partly because they know it.

Vendors integrate with it.

Documentation accumulates.

Internal support gets good at solving its failures.

After five years, asking whether the other tool is now 20 percent better is the wrong question.

The organization no longer owns just a tool.

It owns an ecosystem of adaptations to the tool.

The replacement must beat that ecosystem, not the original product comparison.

This is where economic language helps.

The organization has made complementary investments.

A complement increases the value of something else. Roads make cars more useful. Chargers make electric vehicles more useful. Software skills make a platform more useful. A distribution network makes a product more useful. A standard makes compatible devices more useful.

Complements create value.

They also create transition cost.

The more complements a path attracts, the more expensive it becomes to leave abruptly.

This can produce a paradox of competence.

The organization improves the existing system so successfully that it raises the hurdle for the replacement.

Each incremental improvement is rational.

The cumulative effect can be a deeper commitment to the current architecture.

James March's distinction between exploration and exploitation captures part of this dynamic. Exploitation refines existing knowledge. It improves efficiency, execution, selection, implementation. Exploration searches: experimentation, variation, discovery, risk.

The returns arrive on different schedules.

Exploitation often pays sooner because the organization already understands the domain.

Exploration produces more variance. Many experiments fail. The successes may matter enormously, but the local feedback is weaker at the beginning.

An organization that learns from immediate results can therefore become very good at exploiting what already works.

That is not irrational adaptation.

It is adaptation to the evidence available now.

The problem is that the evidence is generated partly by the organization's existing commitments.

A mature product has customers because the company has spent years acquiring them.

A new product has few customers partly because it is new.

A mature process has low error rates because people have practiced it.

A new process looks unreliable partly because people have not.

A mature business can prove its margin.

A new one often begins with projections.

If both compete for resources under the same standards, the old system will frequently win on merit.

This is how an organization can make every annual allocation rational and still arrive somewhere it did not intend.

The furniture multiplies through local decisions.

A company adopts a measure to make performance visible.

Managers optimize it.

Compensation is tied to it.

A reporting system is built around it.

Investors learn to expect it.

A later strategy discovers that the metric now rewards the wrong behavior.

Changing the metric is no longer an analytics project.

It is a compensation project, an investor-relations project, a software project, a political project, and a credibility project.

The original measure has become furniture.

The same thing happens with expertise.

A company needs specialized knowledge, so it rewards specialists.

Specialists improve.

Promotion criteria reflect their expertise.

The organization recruits from the same profession.

Leaders emerge from the dominant discipline.

New problems are naturally translated into the language the organization knows how to use.

Again, none of this requires arrogance.

A person uses the tool that has repeatedly solved real problems.

Dorothy Leonard-Barton called attention to this paradox when she wrote about core capabilities and core rigidities. The technical systems, skills, managerial systems, and values that create an organization's strength can obstruct development when a new approach conflicts with what the organization has learned to prize.

A capability becomes a rigidity not because it was fake.

It becomes a rigidity because it was real enough to shape the institution around itself.

This is an important difference.

If the old capability were worthless, change would be easier.

The difficult transitions happen when the old thing remains good.

A mature company can have excellent engineers and still struggle with a new architecture.

A newspaper can have excellent reporters and still face a collapsing print distribution model.

A university can have excellent faculty and still find that the economics of credentials, research, teaching, and student demand are changing.

A public agency can have skilled administrators and still be trapped inside a statutory category that no longer maps neatly onto the world.

A worker can be highly competent and still discover that the market has changed what competence is worth.

Success leaves furniture because success deserves to be remembered.

That is why the solution cannot be permanent restlessness.

Organizations that change everything before lessons can accumulate never earn the benefits of learning. They remain generalists where specialization would help. They impose migration costs repeatedly. They exhaust employees. They confuse movement with adaptation.

The aim is not to live without furniture.

It is to remember that the room was arranged under assumptions.

Some institutions make this easier than others.

They preserve modular boundaries so one component can change without requiring a total rewrite. They document why a rule exists, not only what the rule says. They maintain migration paths. They design expiration into temporary policies. They separate exploratory work from the performance standards of the mature business. They keep enough organizational memory to distinguish an obsolete constraint from a forgotten safety lesson.

None of these practices guarantees adaptation.

They alter the price of it.

That is the thread this book will keep following.

The cost of change is not simply the price of the new thing.

It is the price of teaching everything around the old thing to stop expecting it.

By the time a system is successful, those expectations are everywhere.

The furniture is not clutter.

It is evidence of a life lived in the room.

That is what makes rearranging it expensive.
