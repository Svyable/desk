# Promote the Heretic, Keep the Historian

Every successful organization eventually hires people whose job is to know why the obvious new idea will not work.

This is usually a sign of maturity.

A young company can decide that a customer contract is too complicated and walk away.

A mature company has customers whose contracts became complicated for reasons.

A young engineering team can replace a component because the new one looks better on a benchmark.

A mature system has certification history, failure modes, maintenance procedures, supplier relationships, and years of production evidence wrapped around the old component.

A young public program can change eligibility language with a memo.

A mature program has beneficiaries, administrators, court decisions, vendors, databases, forms, appropriations, and people whose lives were organized around the rule.

Experience creates reasons.

Then the reasons become authority.

That is where adaptation becomes difficult.

The person saying no may be protecting the institution from repeating an expensive mistake.

The person saying no may also be protecting an assumption whose useful life ended years ago.

From the outside those two sentences sound different.

Inside an organization they can sound exactly the same.

“We tried that.”

“Customers will not accept it.”

“Compliance will never allow it.”

“That architecture cannot scale.”

“We already know what happens.”

Sometimes the sentence contains memory.

Sometimes it contains debt.

An adaptive institution needs a way to tell the difference.

One temptation is to solve the problem culturally.

Celebrate dissent.

Hire rebels.

Create a red team.

Ask someone to play devil’s advocate.

Put “challenge the status quo” on the values page.

These moves can help.

They can also produce a costume version of disagreement in which everybody knows the challenge is part of the performance.

Charlan Nemeth, Keith Brown, and John Rogers tested a version of this distinction experimentally. Their work compared authentic minority dissent with several forms of assigned devil’s-advocate opposition. The authentic dissent condition produced stronger divergent thinking than the role-played alternatives.

That is a useful result because it exposes a weakness in ceremonial challenge.

If everyone in the room knows the skeptic is arguing a position because the agenda assigned it, the group can absorb the objection without reconsidering much.

The objection belongs to the procedure.

It does not necessarily belong to anyone’s judgment.

Authentic dissent is different.

Someone actually believes the preferred answer may be wrong.

That belief creates friction because it cannot be dismissed merely by saying the exercise is over.

The dissenting person may continue asking.

They may produce more evidence.

They may refuse a compromise that preserves the original assumption.

They may make the room uncomfortable.

This does not mean authentic dissent is correct.

A sincere minority can be sincerely wrong.

A confident contrarian can waste enormous amounts of time.

An employee can turn opposition into identity and begin judging ideas by whether powerful people dislike them.

A leader can reward “courage” so theatrically that teams learn to perform conflict rather than improve decisions.

The point is narrower.

Organizations need disagreement that has enough institutional reality to survive the meeting in which it first appears.

That requires more than a chair asking, “Any objections?”

Chapter 10 showed why.

Objections can exist and still fail to become decision-relevant information.

An engineer can worry privately.

A manager can soften the warning.

A team can interpret repeated skepticism as lack of commitment.

A dissenting view can be documented in a technical appendix nobody with authority reads.

The meeting can end in apparent agreement even though the organization never actually agreed.

The adaptation problem is therefore partly architectural.

Where does disagreement go?

Who is allowed to carry it upward?

What happens to the person who keeps carrying it after everyone else wants closure?

Does a dissenting view require permission from the hierarchy it challenges?

Can the dissenter force a small experiment?

Can they request outside review?

Can they record the assumption under dispute and the evidence that would reopen the decision later?

These are design questions, not personality questions.

Amy Edmondson’s work on psychological safety gives another useful boundary. In her study of work teams, psychological safety was associated with learning behavior. The idea is often flattened into a claim that good teams make people feel comfortable.

That is not what adaptation needs.

A psychologically safe team can still have severe standards.

People can be told that an argument is weak.

A design can be rejected.

A forecast can be wrong.

A manager can make a decision after hearing disagreement.

Safety matters because interpersonal risk should not be confused with task rigor.

If saying “I think this assumption is failing” threatens status, belonging, promotion, or basic credibility, the organization will learn more slowly than the people inside it are capable of learning.

The information still exists.

It just stays private.

This is especially costly during discontinuity because the first evidence that the world has moved is usually ambiguous.

If the signal were obvious, the organization would not need courage to discuss it.

A new competitor is winning a small customer segment.

A new technology works in one environment but fails in another.

A formerly reliable metric begins producing strange exceptions.

A customer asks for something the product architecture makes awkward.

A junior employee notices behavior senior people have explained away for years.

A supplier says a once-rare constraint is becoming common.

None of these proves the strategy is wrong.

Each is easy to dismiss.

That is why organizations often need a heretic before they need a visionary.

The heretic’s first job is not to know the future.

It is to keep an anomaly alive long enough for the institution to examine it.

That role can be socially expensive.

A person who repeatedly points out exceptions slows meetings.

They complicate narratives.

They make plans harder to sell.

They introduce uncertainty after leaders have worked to create alignment.

They can sound disloyal because organizations naturally turn coordinated action into a moral good.

At some point action does require alignment.

Nobody can run a factory, hospital, airline, software platform, or government agency by reopening every assumption continuously.

The organization has to decide.

Then it has to act as though the decision is real.

This is why the heretic alone is not enough.

An institution that celebrates permanent challenge can become incapable of competence.

Every process is provisional.

Every expert is suspect.

Every rule is reopened by the newest person in the room.

No one gets to build accumulated knowledge because accumulated knowledge is treated as bias.

That is not adaptability.

It is organizational amnesia.

The cure for one form of adaptation debt can produce another.

This is why the second half of the chapter title matters.

Keep the historian.

The historian is not necessarily an archivist.

It may be the engineer who remembers why a strange tolerance exists.

The operations manager who lived through the outage that created a cumbersome approval step.

The lawyer who remembers the settlement that changed the contract language.

The nurse who knows why the handoff protocol contains a repetition everyone else considers bureaucratic.

The product manager who can explain which customer promise created the compatibility layer.

The civil servant who remembers the abuse that produced an awkward eligibility safeguard.

The accountant who knows why a control was introduced after a fraud case.

The maintainer who can explain what happened the last time the organization tried the clean rewrite.

This knowledge rarely looks strategic.

It becomes strategic the moment someone proposes removing the thing that carries it.

James Walsh and Gerardo Rivera Ungson described organizations as storing information from history through people, culture, transformations, structures, ecology, and external archives. The important implication here is that memory is distributed.

It is not enough to keep the old document repository.

The reason a rule exists may never have been written down cleanly.

It may survive in how experienced people respond to a certain failure.

That makes organizational memory both precious and dangerous.

Precious because some constraints were purchased with pain.

Dangerous because a remembered conclusion can survive after the conditions that justified it disappear.

The historian therefore needs a discipline too.

Do not say only, “We tried that.”

Say when.

Under what conditions?

What failed?

Which assumption did the failure support?

What has changed since then?

What evidence would make another attempt reasonable?

The best historian does not defend the past.

The best historian makes the past legible enough to be challenged.

This is a different social role from the person who says, “Trust me, I was there.”

Authority by memory is still authority.

It can be abused.

Long tenure can become a veto.

A veteran can turn undocumented experience into an argument nobody else is allowed to inspect.

“The regulator will never approve that.”

Which regulator?

Under what rule?

When was the question last asked?

“That customer will leave.”

Which customer?

What did they say?

Did anyone test a migration path?

“That system cannot be replaced.”

Which behavior cannot be replaced?

Which dependency still uses it?

The historian should be able to answer.

If not, memory has become mythology.

This is why adaptation benefits from pairing the heretic and historian rather than choosing between them.

The heretic asks why the old constraint still holds.

The historian explains why it existed.

The heretic produces evidence that the environment changed.

The historian identifies which hidden dependencies the proposed change has not yet considered.

The heretic notices that a former exception is becoming common.

The historian recalls what happened when the institution optimized for the exception before.

The heretic says the old product is constraining the new one.

The historian knows which customers would be stranded by a clean break.

Together they turn disagreement from a status contest into a testable argument.

This pairing also protects against a common transformation failure.

Organizations often create a new unit to escape the assumptions of the old one.

The new unit gets different people, different metrics, different technology, and permission to move faster.

For a while the separation helps.

Then the new system has to touch the old world.

It inherits customer data.

It needs regulatory approval.

It shares a brand.

It must reconcile accounts.

It needs distribution.

It encounters an edge case the legacy system handled badly but reliably.

If the organization staffed the new unit entirely with heretics, it may discover too late that freedom was partly ignorance.

If it staffed the transition entirely with historians, it may rebuild the old system in new technology.

The useful team has both.

This is one of the deeper lessons of organizational ambidexterity.

Exploration and exploitation often require different structures, incentives, and rhythms, yet they still belong to one institution. The difficult work is not simply separating them. It is creating enough senior integration that the exploratory unit can borrow real strengths from the incumbent without inheriting every constraint that made exploration necessary.

People embody that integration.

Some know the old business deeply enough to distinguish obligation from habit.

Some know the new environment deeply enough to distinguish opportunity from fashion.

The organization needs them in conversation before architecture hardens.

This sounds obvious.

It is structurally difficult because the two groups are rewarded by different evidence.

The historian can point to years of operating results.

The heretic can usually point only to a possibility.

The historian knows how the current system fails.

The heretic knows how it might stop mattering.

The historian has internal legitimacy.

The heretic often has external comparison.

The historian speaks in exceptions.

The heretic speaks in trajectories.

The mismatch can make each side look unserious to the other.

The veteran says the newcomer does not understand consequences.

The newcomer says the veteran is defending complexity.

Both accusations can be true.

This is why the institution needs rules for the argument.

Not a framework with a clever name.

Just a few durable habits.

Record contested assumptions.

When a major decision turns on the belief that customers will not migrate, write that belief down.

When the decision turns on a safety constraint, state the constraint.

When it turns on a market-size threshold, state the threshold.

When it turns on a regulation, identify the rule.

This does two things.

It respects the historian by preserving the reason.

It empowers the heretic by making the reason falsifiable.

The next habit is to preserve rejected alternatives.

Most organizations remember what they chose.

They are worse at remembering what they considered and why they rejected it.

Later, someone proposes the same idea and receives a vague response: “We looked at that.”

The original context is gone.

A short record of rejected alternatives can prevent both repetition and premature closure.

The institution can see whether the old objection still applies.

This is organizational memory serving adaptation rather than merely preserving precedent.

Another habit is to attach reopening conditions to important decisions.

We are not entering this market because acquisition cost is too high.

Reopen if distribution cost falls below this level.

We are keeping the legacy interface because these customers still depend on it.

Reopen when active use falls below this threshold and migration tooling reaches this coverage.

We are not adopting the new manufacturing process because yield is insufficient.

Reopen when independent production data clears this range.

We are retaining the old rule because the safety evidence remains uncertain.

Reopen after this trial, audit, or external standard changes.

The condition matters because it prevents a conclusion from becoming an identity.

The organization can be strongly committed today without pretending today is permanent.

This is also how the heretic avoids becoming a full-time dissenter.

They do not need to relitigate the decision every week.

They can watch the condition.

When the condition changes, the argument is legitimately reopened.

That is healthier than permanent cultural combat.

The organization needs closure.

It also needs a door.

There is a difficult personnel question here.

Who gets promoted?

Success tends to promote people who reproduced the current system well.

This is rational.

Organizations need leaders who can run what exists.

But if promotion selects only for fluent exploitation, senior leadership can become unusually homogeneous precisely when the environment is becoming less so.

The response is not to promote contrarians regardless of performance.

A person who cannot build trust, execute, or carry responsibility does not become valuable merely because they disagree.

The useful heretic is capable of owning consequences.

They can say what evidence would prove them wrong.

They can run the experiment.

They can explain migration cost.

They can make a decision when the evidence remains mixed.

They do not confuse skepticism with virtue.

Promote that person.

The historian presents a parallel challenge.

Organizations frequently remove experienced people during transformation because their roles belong to the old structure.

Sometimes this is unavoidable.

Sometimes it is correct.

But a transformation that removes memory faster than it makes memory explicit can become dependent on expensive rediscovery.

The new team finds the edge case in production.

The new policy discovers the old constituency after implementation.

The new software learns why the ugly validation step existed.

The new plant discovers why a maintenance interval was conservative.

The new manager learns why two units that look redundant were separated after a past failure.

The cost of this rediscovery rarely appears in the restructuring plan.

It appears later as delay, incident, customer loss, or quiet re-creation of the supposedly obsolete process.

Keeping the historian does not necessarily mean keeping every old role forever.

It means transferring the knowledge before the role disappears.

Pair people.

Document rationale.

Run the new and old process together long enough to expose differences.

Have veterans review exceptions rather than own the whole workflow.

Convert tacit knowledge into tests, playbooks, failure libraries, decision records, and training.

Let people who carried the old system explain which parts they would gladly delete and which parts they would fight to preserve.

That last question is especially useful.

The veteran who resists every change tells you little.

The veteran who wants to delete half the system but insists on keeping one strange control is worth listening to.

Selective resistance contains information.

The same is true of dissent.

The employee who opposes every management initiative may be expressing something real, but the pattern makes diagnosis harder.

The employee who supported the strategy for years and then says, “This assumption no longer holds,” should get attention.

Authenticity matters because adaptation depends on information that has not yet become consensus.

But authenticity is not enough.

Dissent needs evidence.

Memory needs evidence.

Neither side gets permanent authority from identity.

This is where the chapter returns to psychological safety.

A safe organization is not one in which everyone is heard equally on every question.

Expertise still matters.

Responsibility still matters.

Decision rights still matter.

The senior engineer may know more about the bridge than the new analyst.

The regulator may know more about the legal constraint than the product manager.

The surgeon may know more about the procedure than the administrator.

Safety means the analyst can point to a crack without being punished for embarrassing the engineer.

The product manager can ask whether the legal constraint actually says what everyone claims.

The junior clinician can report an unexpected outcome without first proving that a respected specialist caused it.

The organization can receive information before it knows whether the information is important.

That is the learning advantage.

Adaptation debt grows when information has to become undeniable before it becomes speakable.

By then the transition is usually more expensive.

The best institutions therefore make room for early, weak, inconvenient signals while preserving strong filters against noise.

That balance is hard.

There is no policy that guarantees it.

A hotline can be ignored.

A red team can become ritual.

A lessons-learned database can become a graveyard.

A veteran council can become a veto chamber.

An innovation lab can become a place where experiments are safely isolated from the real business.

The mechanism matters less than whether information can change a consequential decision.

That is the test.

Can a dissenting observation redirect resources?

Can historical evidence stop a reckless simplification?

Can a new fact reopen an old conclusion?

Can a veteran say, “That reason no longer applies” without losing status?

Can a newcomer say, “I was wrong; the ugly old thing is carrying a real obligation” without losing identity?

An adaptive culture is visible in those reversals.

Not in the number of workshops about change.

The organization needs people who can challenge the inherited map.

It also needs people who remember why the map was drawn that way.

Promote the heretic.

Keep the historian.

Then make both of them show their work.
