# The Factory for Yesterday

On January 19, 2012, Eastman Kodak Company and its U.S. subsidiaries filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection.

The date belongs to a familiar business story now.

Kodak had been one of the great industrial names of the twentieth century. Its brand was so closely associated with photography that the company name became shorthand for a kind of moment. Then digital photography arrived. Film disappeared. Kodak, the story goes, had even invented an early digital camera and somehow failed to understand what it had created.

A cleaner morality play would be hard to design.

It is also not a very good description of the company that entered bankruptcy.

In the announcement filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission that January, Kodak said approximately 75 percent of its 2011 revenue came from digital businesses.

That number does not make Kodak a digital success.

It does ruin the easiest version of the story.

A company earning most of its revenue from digital businesses cannot be described accurately as one that simply refused to go digital. Kodak had spent years investing in digital imaging, printing, sensors, software, intellectual property, consumer inkjet, commercial inkjet, and digital cameras. Its 2011 annual report described pressure in digital cameras as well as continuing industry declines in film-related businesses. In early 2012 it announced plans to phase out dedicated capture devices, including digital cameras, pocket video cameras, and digital picture frames.

The company saw digital photography.

The more interesting question is why seeing it was not enough.

To understand that, it helps to stop thinking of a company as a mind.

A company does not look at the future with one pair of eyes and then decide whether to accept what it sees. It is a collection of assets, contracts, capabilities, people, channels, incentives, customers, liabilities, habits, and expectations that have been built at different times for different reasons.

Changing the mind is the easy part.

Changing the collection is the work.

Kodak's film business had once been an extraordinary machine for turning chemistry, manufacturing, distribution, branding, and recurring consumption into profit. A consumer could buy a camera occasionally and film repeatedly. The company did not merely sell a device. It participated in a chain that continued after the shutter clicked: film, processing, paper, chemicals, equipment, retail relationships, and the accumulated know-how required to make the whole system reliable at enormous scale.

Digital photography attacked that chain in an unusual way.

It did not simply introduce a better version of one component.

It changed where the value was.

Once an image could be captured electronically, stored on memory, displayed on a screen, transmitted through a network, edited with software, and reproduced without consuming a new roll of film, photography stopped requiring the same physical repetition.

That matters more than the fact that one camera technology replaced another.

A company can replace a component.

It is harder to replace the economics surrounding the component.

This is one reason the famous question—why didn't Kodak embrace digital sooner?—produces so many unsatisfying answers.

Kodak did embrace digital in many forms.

What it could not do was make digital photography recreate the profit architecture that film had built.

That was not merely a technology problem.

It was a transition problem.

The old business continued generating revenue while declining. The new businesses needed investment while competing in markets with different economics. Film manufacturing carried large fixed assets and specialized knowledge. The digital-camera market brought intense competition and falling prices. Inkjet required capital and customer adoption. Intellectual property could produce licensing income, but a patent portfolio is not a replacement for an operating system of factories, retailers, recurring consumables, and consumer habit.

The company entered the transition with history attached.

History appears on financial statements as assets and liabilities, but a balance sheet captures only part of it.

There was also knowledge.

People had spent careers becoming excellent at processes that mattered because film mattered. Organizations had been designed around the commercial logic of imaging products. Retail relationships had been built in a world where physical processing and printing were ordinary parts of photography. Capital allocation had been shaped by businesses that once generated attractive returns. Managers had risen through those businesses. Forecasting systems had history in those businesses. The identity of the company itself had been formed by them.

No one had to be foolish for that history to exert force.

A chemical engineer does not become a software engineer because a strategy presentation says the future is digital.

A factory does not become a cloud service because the board agrees with the forecast.

A profitable channel does not become irrelevant on the date a new technology first appears.

A customer relationship built around one pattern of consumption does not automatically transfer to another.

A company cannot simply announce that its accumulated capabilities will now be valuable in the same proportion under different industry economics.

Sometimes they are.

Sometimes the capability survives while the profit pool moves somewhere else.

This is the uncomfortable part of technological change.

The qualities that made an organization formidable in one regime can remain real after they stop being decisive.

Kodak still knew imaging.

That knowledge was not imaginary.

The problem was that the industry no longer rewarded every part of that knowledge in the same way.

A useful comparison comes from Polaroid, another company built around analog photography. Mary Tripsas and Giovanni Gavetti studied Polaroid's response to digital imaging and argued that organizational capabilities and managerial cognition interacted in ways that constrained adaptation. Their case is not Kodak's case, and the companies should not be collapsed into one anecdote. But the mechanism is relevant: what managers believe about a new market influences where an organization searches, and the capabilities already inside the organization influence what kinds of opportunities it can pursue convincingly.

There is a feedback loop between what a company knows and what it can imagine itself becoming.

That loop is powerful when the environment is stable.

Experience improves judgment. Specialized assets improve quality. Routines reduce error. Distribution gets denser. A brand accumulates meaning. Customers learn what to expect. Suppliers invest in meeting the specification. The system becomes better at being itself.

Then the environment changes.

Now the same reinforcement can become expensive.

The problem is not simply that the organization likes the past.

The problem is that the organization has built a lot of useful things that only make full sense under assumptions inherited from the past.

Consider what it means to say that a factory is obsolete.

The word is easy to use from outside.

Inside the company, the factory may still work.

It may still produce salable goods.

It may still employ people who know exactly how to run it.

It may still have depreciation schedules, maintenance contracts, environmental obligations, supplier commitments, utility connections, safety systems, and customers.

It may be less attractive than a hypothetical new system while remaining more valuable than shutting it tomorrow.

That creates an interval in which the old asset is both rational to operate and dangerous to rely on.

The debt accumulates in that interval.

Every additional year of operation can fund the transition.

Every additional year can also postpone it.

Every improvement can extract more value from the existing system.

Every improvement can make the existing system harder to abandon.

The company is not choosing between a dead past and a proven future.

It is choosing between a productive present with weakening economics and a future whose economics are not yet fully known.

This is why hindsight makes adaptation look easier than it felt.

We see the successful successor and forget the failed alternatives.

We see the curve after it has bent and assume the inflection point was obvious before it did.

We see a bankrupt incumbent and treat every investment in the old business as evidence of denial.

But companies are not allowed to stop serving current customers while waiting for historians to identify the winning architecture.

They have payroll.

They have debt.

They have products under warranty.

They have customers whose needs remain real even if those customers are not where the future growth will come from.

They have businesses whose cash may be required to finance the transition away from those businesses.

That last problem deserves more attention.

Cannibalization is often described as a matter of courage: leaders should be willing to destroy their own products before someone else does.

Sometimes they should.

But the thing being cannibalized is frequently paying for the thing supposed to replace it.

The old product is not just an enemy of the new one.

It is the parent.

A company may need the mature business to generate the cash that funds research, marketing, data centers, factories, acquisitions, and talent for the emerging business. Moving too slowly leaves the company exposed. Moving too quickly can weaken the very balance sheet needed to complete the move.

This tension is visible in successful transitions too.

Adobe's shift from perpetual creative-software licenses to Creative Cloud subscriptions is a useful example because the company told investors in advance that the transition would make some financial measures look worse. In its fiscal 2013 filing, Adobe said accelerating subscription adoption would cause traditional perpetual-license revenue to decline and would reduce total net revenue during the transition. Expenses were not expected to fall in proportion, which would pressure income and margins.

The company was not discovering after the fact that transition had a cost.

It was choosing to incur one.

That is what adaptation debt looks like when it is serviced deliberately.

The old revenue does not vanish politely after the new revenue is ready.

There is overlap.

There is a period when the organization must carry two economic logics at once.

Netflix faced a version of the same problem as streaming grew beside its DVD-by-mail business. The company's earlier filings described DVD as the core business and internet delivery as the direction into which it expected to transition subscribers as the market developed. By 2012, streaming and DVD appeared as distinct reportable segments, and the old DVD business could still produce substantial contribution profit while streaming absorbed more investment and strategic attention.

Netflix's 2011 pricing and branding changes also produced a useful warning against romanticizing self-cannibalization. Customer reaction was sharply negative. The company later acknowledged significant cancellations and reversed the plan to separate the DVD service under the Qwikster brand.

The future may be right while a particular transition plan is wrong.

That sentence should sit beside every case study of an incumbent accused of cowardice.

Adaptation is not a referendum on whether change exists.

It is a sequence of operational choices about timing, customers, capital, compatibility, identity, and risk.

Kodak made many of those choices badly enough that the company ended up in bankruptcy. Its filings make clear that the pressures were real: declining film volumes, digital competition, commodity costs, investment requirements, liquidity needs, and legacy obligations all mattered.

But the bankruptcy itself does not tell us which choice should have been made in which year.

Bankruptcy is an outcome, not a causal model.

The reason to begin here is precisely that the outcome is so famous that it tempts us to stop thinking.

“Failed to adapt” sounds like an explanation.

Often it is only the label we apply after the adaptation did not work.

A better explanation has to identify what made change costly.

For Kodak, that means looking beyond the existence of digital technology to the industrial system that film had created.

Film was not just a product line.

It was a dense web of complements.

The more successful the web became, the more of Kodak's organization learned to depend on it.

This is the paradox at the center of the book.

If the film business had been weak, Kodak might have had less to lose by abandoning it.

Because it had been strong, the company possessed more assets, more expertise, more channels, more routines, more customer expectations, and more financial history attached to the old model.

Success created resources for adaptation.

Success also increased the amount that had to be adapted.

The same fact can do both.

That is what makes adaptation debt different from a simple mistake.

A mistake should be corrected because it was wrong.

Adaptation debt often comes from something that was right long enough to build a world around itself.

The payment arrives when that world begins to change.

Factories make the payment visible because they are hard to move.

But the most consequential factories are not always made of steel.

Some are made of routines.

Some are made of metrics.

Some are made of customer promises.

Some are made of careers.

Some are made of rules.

Some are made of memory.

Once those structures become ordinary, we stop seeing them as things that were built.

We see the room.

That is where adaptation debt usually hides.
