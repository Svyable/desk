# Metrics Remember the Old Game

A number can outlive the reason it was invented.

That is one of the quieter ways institutions become difficult to change.

A metric begins as an answer to a real problem.

Sales are hard to coordinate, so the company counts products sold.

Quality is hard to compare, so the factory counts defects.

Customer service is hard to manage, so the call center measures handle time.

Research is hard to evaluate, so the institution counts publications.

Hospitals need accountability, schools need benchmarks, governments need performance measures, investors need financial statements, managers need dashboards.

The number is not the enemy.

The number is a tool for turning a messy reality into something an organization can see.

Then the organization learns the tool.

Bonuses depend on it.

Teams are compared by it.

Hiring profiles evolve around it.

Software is built to report it.

Meetings begin with it.

Targets are negotiated in its units.

People discover which actions improve it quickly and which improvements it fails to notice.

The metric stops being a window.

It becomes part of the room.

This is measurement commitment.

It is also a form of adaptation debt.

The most familiar warning is Goodhart's Law, usually paraphrased as the idea that when a measure becomes a target, it can cease to be a good measure. The phrase is useful because it captures a recurring failure of proxy optimization. It is also so widely repeated that it can become a substitute for thought.

Not every target corrupts a metric.

Organizations need targets.

A defect rate can improve when people are held accountable for defects. A sales goal can focus effort. A response-time target can make a slow institution faster. A clinical quality measure can reveal variation that anecdotes hide. A budget can constrain spending. A service-level agreement can force a supplier to treat reliability seriously.

The problem is not that measures become targets.

The problem is that objectives move while measurement systems remember the old game.

This can happen even when nobody cheats.

Imagine a company that built its performance system when growth meant acquiring new customers.

The relevant metric might be accounts opened.

Years later the market matures. Retention, trust, product fit, lifetime value, and service quality matter more. The company can say that strategy has changed while managers still live inside a system that rewards the old behavior.

The strategy moves first.

The metric remains behind.

Every week the dashboard reminds the organization what used to count.

Wells Fargo's retail banking scandal is a severe example because the consequences became explicit enough to investigate.

In 2016, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau said sales targets and compensation incentives had spurred employees to open unauthorized deposit and credit-card accounts. Wells Fargo's own analysis at the time identified more than two million accounts that may not have been authorized by consumers.

The company's later board investigation described a deeper organizational problem. It found a growing conflict between Wells Fargo's stated values and the Community Bank's emphasis on sales goals. The investigation said senior leaders had distorted the sales model and performance-management system, creating pressure that encouraged low-quality sales and improper behavior.

This is often told as a story about greed.

Greed is too small an explanation.

The important fact is that an institution had built a system in which one representation of success had become operationally powerful.

Products sold could be counted.

Customer trust was harder to count.

A manager could compare branches by sales numbers every morning.

The cost of violating a customer's expectation might arrive later through complaints, employee turnover, regulator attention, reputational damage, remediation, or lost trust.

The metric had temporal privilege.

It reported immediately.

The damage accumulated slowly.

This is why measurement commitments are so sticky.

A metric creates a rhythm.

People learn what can be improved before the next review.

The shortest feedback loop gains power.

If the organization reviews weekly sales and annual trust surveys, weekly sales will shape daily behavior more strongly even if the chief executive says trust matters more.

The metric does not need to be officially more important.

It needs to be more present.

William Ocasio's attention-based view of the firm is useful here. Organizations do not attend to everything. Structures, rules, roles, and communication channels shape which issues reach decision makers and how those issues are interpreted. A dashboard is one such attention structure.

It decides what repeatedly gets a seat at the table.

Once a measure has that position, changing strategy without changing attention can become ceremonial.

A company announces that it is moving from volume to value.

The Monday meeting still ranks volume.

A software team announces that reliability matters more than feature count.

Promotion still rewards visible launches.

A university says teaching matters.

Prestige systems still privilege research output.

A hospital says patient experience matters.

Managers still optimize the metrics tied to reimbursement.

The old game survives because its score is still printed.

Metrics become even more durable when they are linked to identity.

A salesperson is not simply told a target.

The target can become evidence of whether the salesperson is good.

A researcher's publication record becomes a biography.

A factory manager's defect rate becomes professional credibility.

A teacher's test scores become administrative judgment.

A product manager's growth metric becomes a career story.

Changing the measure then threatens more than compensation.

It changes the definition of competence.

People who mastered the old scoreboard can experience the new one as a claim that their success was mismeasured.

Sometimes it was.

Often it was not.

The old metric may have been useful under the conditions in which it was created.

That distinction matters.

The purpose of this chapter is not to sneer at old dashboards with the benefit of hindsight.

A measure can be correct for one stage of a system and dangerous in another.

Early growth can genuinely matter more than margin.

Later, margin can matter more than growth.

A startup can rationally count users before it has enough history to estimate lifetime value.

A mature business can get into trouble if it keeps celebrating the same acquisition number after retention deteriorates.

A public program can use throughput as an early capacity measure and later need outcome quality.

The metric does not become false.

Its relationship to the objective changes.

That is the core adaptation problem.

Measurement systems have complements too.

Compensation is one.

Software is another.

Reporting lines are another.

Data infrastructure, external benchmarking, investor expectations, regulatory requirements, contracts, and promotion criteria can all gather around a number.

A metric that started as a column in a spreadsheet can become embedded in budgets, databases, job descriptions, and law.

Then replacing it is no longer a matter of choosing a better number.

The organization has to migrate the system that depends on it.

This is why bad metrics often survive obvious criticism.

The critics are arguing about meaning.

The institution is facing migration cost.

A new measure may require new data.

New data can require new software.

New software may not have history, which makes trend comparisons difficult.

Bonuses have to be redesigned.

Managers have to learn what normal performance looks like under the new measure.

Targets have to be recalibrated.

Employees need to trust that the new score will not punish them for behavior previously rewarded.

External stakeholders may not understand the change.

The organization can spend months in a period when nobody knows whether performance improved or the ruler changed.

That ambiguity is expensive.

It can also be exploited.

Whenever metrics change, people who benefited from the old system can accuse the organization of moving the goalposts.

Sometimes that accusation is correct.

Managers can replace an inconvenient metric to hide deterioration.

Governments can redefine categories to improve reported outcomes.

Companies can invent nonstandard performance measures that flatter results.

A new dashboard is not automatically a more honest one.

This is the strongest counterargument to the chapter's thesis.

Stable measurement creates accountability.

If every strategic shift comes with a new score, history becomes impossible to compare.

The organization can escape judgment by changing what judgment means.

So measurement adaptation has two conflicting obligations.

The metric has to change when the objective changes.

The metric has to remain stable enough that performance cannot be rewritten at will.

That tension is real.

It is not solved by telling managers to be more flexible.

One way through it is to preserve old measures while adding new ones during the transition.

This resembles running parallel systems in technology.

Keep the old metric for continuity.

Add the new metric for strategic relevance.

Observe where they diverge.

Learn which behaviors each one rewards.

This can reveal whether the strategy is actually changing or merely acquiring new language.

It also creates metric clutter.

Dashboards expand.

Every department protects the measure that makes its work visible.

No number is retired because someone depends on it.

Soon the organization has fifty indicators and still does not know what matters.

Measurement systems accumulate just like software systems.

Nothing is deleted because deletion feels like loss of information.

The result can be a different kind of adaptation debt: too many scoreboards, each tied to a historical objective, all demanding attention.

This is why retirement matters as much as addition.

A strategy that changes its priorities should eventually stop paying attention to some old signals.

That is uncomfortable because yesterday's metric often has the best data quality.

It has years of history.

People know its quirks.

Benchmarks exist.

The new metric begins as a fragile estimate.

Again, the past has evidence and the future has a proposal.

The same asymmetry we saw with capital appears in measurement.

An old factory can show production.

An old metric can show a time series.

A new metric has to prove that it is worth losing comparability.

This creates an important distinction between metric error and metric debt.

A metric can be bad from the beginning.

That is measurement error.

A metric can also be good for the old objective and increasingly misleading for the new one.

That is closer to adaptation debt.

The second case is harder because the people defending the metric can point to real historical success.

They are not necessarily protecting nonsense.

They are protecting a measure that once coordinated the organization well.

Process management research makes this more complicated in a useful way.

Mary Benner and Michael Tushman have argued that process-management systems can improve efficiency and exploitation while fitting exploratory innovation less comfortably. Standardization, measurement, and continuous improvement are powerful when the task is to refine a stable process. The same disciplines can become constraining when the organization is trying to discover a new process whose relevant variables are not yet known.

This does not mean process management kills innovation.

It means measurement systems inherit assumptions about the kind of problem being solved.

A factory improving a known process wants repeatability.

A research team exploring an unknown design may need variance.

A metric that treats variance as failure in the first setting can be sensible and destructive in the second.

The metric remembers the old game because it remembers what kind of uncertainty used to matter.

This shows why adaptation cannot be separated from context.

The same measure can be useful in one unit and harmful in another.

The same organization may need tight process metrics in manufacturing and looser exploratory measures in research.

Trying to force one scoreboard across both can create false comparability.

The problem is attractive because executives like common metrics.

Common metrics make organizations legible.

Legibility is useful.

It can also erase differences that matter.

A mature product and an early experiment should not necessarily be judged by the same margin target.

A new market and an established one may not deserve the same revenue threshold.

A safety project and a growth project may have benefits on different time horizons.

When organizations insist on one common measure, they can turn the incumbent's scale into a filter that rejects small futures.

This is one reason entrants can survive opportunities incumbents ignore.

The entrant's scoreboard is different.

A million dollars can be proof of life.

Inside a ten-billion-dollar division, it can be noise.

Measurement does not merely report size.

It determines whether size deserves attention.

Customer metrics have a similar problem.

Net promoter scores, satisfaction scores, churn, support volume, usage, renewal, and engagement can all reveal something real.

None is the customer.

The danger begins when the proxy becomes an operational substitute for the relationship.

A company can improve a survey score while making the product worse for users who do not answer surveys.

It can reduce support-call duration by making customers call twice.

It can increase engagement by making exit harder.

It can reduce churn through annual contracts while weakening affection for the product.

It can grow active users through notifications that users experience as interruption.

The point is not that these things always happen.

The point is that the metric creates a local optimization surface.

People learn how to climb it.

As the organization becomes better at the measure, the measure needs more scrutiny, not less.

This is an important reversal.

A new metric is often treated as most dangerous when people do not understand it.

In practice, a metric can become more dangerous after everyone understands it extremely well.

The organization learns its edge cases.

Managers learn how to time activity around reporting periods.

Teams discover which categories help their numbers.

Employees learn what behavior is visible and what disappears.

The metric becomes an environment.

That is organizational learning doing exactly what organizational learning does.

The system adapts to the measurement.

If the measurement does not adapt back, the gap widens.

This is why periodic metric review should not be treated as administrative housekeeping.

It is part of strategic maintenance.

The question is not simply whether a number is still accurate.

The question is whether the behavior produced by optimizing it still serves the objective the organization now claims to have.

That test can be uncomfortable because the metric may be improving.

A rising number feels like evidence that the system works.

Sometimes it is evidence that the system has learned the test.

The distinction resembles overfitting in machine learning.

A model performs well on the objective it has seen.

The institution performs well on the metric it is scored by.

Generalization requires checking performance against reality not fully captured by the training signal.

Organizations need the equivalent of held-out data.

Customer outcomes not used in compensation.

Safety measures not controlled by the team being judged.

Independent audits.

Longer-term consequences.

Qualitative evidence that cannot be reduced to the main target.

Countermetrics that detect whether improvement in one dimension is being purchased by deterioration in another.

This should not become another named framework.

It is a simple discipline.

Do not let the organization grade its own strategy using only the test it has practiced.

The Wells Fargo case is severe because the gap between metric and objective became unethical and illegal.

Most adaptation debt is quieter.

A company keeps maximizing store count after online behavior changes.

A media business keeps celebrating circulation after advertising economics migrate.

A university optimizes enrollment while completion deteriorates.

A public agency tracks cases closed while complexity rises among the cases left open.

A software company counts deployments while customers care about reliability.

A manager rewards utilization when the organization needs spare capacity.

Nobody has to falsify a report.

The metric can be perfectly accurate and strategically obsolete.

This is the important distinction.

False measurement is a governance problem.

Obsolete measurement is an adaptation problem.

The first asks whether the number is true.

The second asks whether the number still matters.

They can coexist, but they require different remedies.

An institution can have impeccable data about the wrong objective.

That can be more dangerous than bad data because precision creates confidence.

Metrics also shape investment.

If a company measures return at the level of mature business units, exploratory projects can look unattractive because their benefits arrive outside the unit or later than the planning horizon.

If a hospital measures throughput, investments in prevention may look weak because the avoided patient does not appear as activity.

If a factory measures equipment utilization, maintenance windows can look like lost productivity until the avoided failure occurs.

If a call center measures handle time, a longer conversation that solves the problem permanently can look worse than two short calls.

The measure changes what counts as cost.

That is why metric redesign can unlock adaptation without changing the underlying technology.

Adobe's subscription transition is a useful counterexample to institutions trapped by old measurement.

As perpetual-license revenue declined and subscription revenue was recognized differently over time, Adobe emphasized measures such as paid subscriptions and annualized recurring revenue to make the emerging business legible while old revenue accounting still dominated the headline.

The company did not stop reporting standard financial statements.

It added a way to see the transition before the old measures could describe it cleanly.

That matters.

Sometimes the first step in adapting is not changing the business.

It is creating a measurement system in which the new business can survive long enough to be judged fairly.

The danger, again, is self-serving measurement.

A new metric can be invented because the old one looks bad.

Investors are right to be suspicious of companies that create flattering definitions whenever performance weakens.

The solution is not to forbid new measures.

It is to make the relationship between old and new explicit.

What does the old metric still tell us?

What does it miss?

What behavior does the new metric reward?

What would make us retire the new metric if it proves misleading?

Can both be reported during the transition?

Does the new measure predict the outcomes we ultimately care about?

A metric should have an exit condition too.

That may be the least common part of measurement design.

Organizations are good at creating indicators.

They are worse at deciding when an indicator has finished its job.

The dashboard becomes a museum of past strategies.

Every chart is there for a reason.

The reason may no longer exist.

This is where metrics resemble capital most closely.

The original investment can be sensible.

Complementary systems gather around it.

The cost of removal rises.

Its continued existence begins to shape future decisions.

Nobody has to love the metric.

They only have to depend on it.

A strategy meeting can change the words at the top of the slide.

The organization changes when the score underneath changes too.

Until then, the old game is still running.
