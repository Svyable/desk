# Metrics Remember the Old Game

In 2013, Adobe did something that made its own revenue look worse.

The company had spent decades selling creative software through perpetual licenses. A customer paid a large amount, received a version of the product, and might return years later for an upgrade. Revenue arrived in chunks. Product launches mattered. Upgrade cycles mattered. The business had grown around transactions that were visible quickly in the income statement.

Then Adobe began moving the creative business toward subscriptions.

The accounting changed before the economics had fully revealed themselves.

A perpetual license could produce a large amount of recognized revenue at the moment of sale. A subscription spread revenue over time. If customers moved from the old model to the new one fast enough, the company could be succeeding at its strategy while reporting lower near-term revenue from the very products making the transition.

Adobe said as much in its 2013 annual filing. Creative Cloud adoption was accelerating. Traditional perpetual-license revenue was declining. Total net revenue fell compared with the prior year. Expenses did not fall in proportion, so net income and operating margin were pressured too.

If the story ended there, the transformation looked bad.

Adobe therefore did something important.

It changed what it watched.

The company began emphasizing measures such as paid subscriptions and annualized recurring revenue. These did not replace audited revenue or profit. They answered a different question.

The old numbers asked, in effect: how much business has been recognized under the economics of the old model?

The new measures asked: how much recurring customer commitment is being built under the economics of the new one?

That distinction is the subject of this chapter.

Metrics remember the game for which they were designed.

A measure is never merely a number. It contains a theory about what matters, when it matters, and how success becomes visible.

Revenue assumes a definition of when value has been earned.

Utilization assumes that productive capacity should be kept busy.

Defect rate assumes that defects are costly enough to count.

Average handle time assumes that speed is an important feature of service.

Inventory turns assume that capital tied up in stock should move.

On-time delivery assumes that the promised date is a meaningful expression of reliability.

Test scores assume that some measured performance represents learning.

Each of these can be useful.

Each can also outlive the conditions that made it useful.

This is one of the quieter forms of adaptation debt because a metric rarely looks old.

A machine shows its age.

A building needs repairs.

A spreadsheet can remain perfectly crisp while measuring yesterday.

That makes metric debt harder to notice.

The number keeps updating.

The dashboard refreshes every morning.

The targets roll forward into the new quarter.

A manager misses one and receives a red cell.

Another manager beats one and receives a bonus.

The organization experiences motion and concludes that the measurement system must still be attached to reality.

But a live number can represent a dead assumption.

Adobe’s transition is unusually clean because the company publicly described the mismatch.

Its strategic goal was to move customers toward a subscription model that management expected to create more recurring, predictable revenue and keep users current on the latest software. Yet the mechanics of the shift meant that successful adoption could depress recognized revenue during the transition. A manager judged only on the old revenue pattern could therefore face a perverse choice: protect the metric by preserving more perpetual sales, or damage the metric by accelerating the strategy.

That is what it means for a metric to remember the old game.

The metric is not false.

Its interpretation is stale.

Organizations often react to this problem by adding more metrics.

That can help.

It can also make the dashboard larger without making the institution wiser.

The real work is to understand which causal story each measure belongs to.

A factory that spent ten years learning to reduce variation may have excellent measures of yield, cycle time, rework, downtime, and unit cost. Those measures can reveal waste with extraordinary precision. They can also train the organization to view deviations as defects.

That is appropriate when the objective is exploitation: make a known process more reliable, faster, safer, and cheaper.

It is less helpful when the organization needs exploration.

Mary Benner and Michael Tushman have written about this tension in process management. Systems designed to improve control and efficiency can strengthen exploitation while making exploratory work harder to evaluate on its own terms. A mature process earns metrics because the organization understands what good performance looks like. An exploratory project exists partly because the organization does not yet know.

The new thing therefore begins at a measurement disadvantage.

The old business has a denominator.

The new business has a hypothesis.

The old team can report defect rates to two decimal places.

The new team may not yet know whether it is solving the right problem.

The old business has targets grounded in years of operating history.

The new business has a range and a lot of uncomfortable verbs: test, learn, discover, revise.

Put both into the same scorecard and the mature business often wins before the meeting begins.

This is not because the scorecard is rigged.

It is because precision carries authority.

A metric that has been measured for ten years feels more real than a question that has existed for ten weeks.

That feeling is often useful. Most speculative projects should not receive unlimited money simply because they are new. Measurement protects organizations from fashion, storytelling, and executive enthusiasm.

The problem appears when the institution mistakes measurability for relevance.

The easiest thing to measure is usually the thing the current system already knows how to produce.

That creates a subtle selection effect.

Projects compatible with the existing measurement architecture look disciplined.

Projects requiring new measurement often look vague.

A business organized around store productivity knows how to compare sales per square foot across locations. A digital channel may initially make some of that measure less important.

A newspaper knows circulation, print advertising, page inventory, and deadline performance. A digital product introduces engagement, subscription retention, conversion, referral, search, and audience behavior. None maps neatly onto the old bundle.

A university knows credit hours, course completion, grades, seat utilization, and degree progression. A different learning model may produce evidence on a different clock.

A government program knows applications processed, claims closed, inspections completed, dollars disbursed, and statutory deadlines met. A redesigned service may aim to reduce the need for some of those transactions entirely.

In each case, the old metric can become a claim on the future.

Once compensation, promotion, budgets, contracts, regulation, and public reporting attach to a measure, changing the measure becomes more than an analytical decision.

It becomes a distributional event.

Someone who was excellent under the old metric may become average under the new one.

A department that looked efficient may look expensive.

A product line that carried the company may appear to be shrinking.

A leader who built a reputation on one definition of performance may now have to explain why that definition is incomplete.

This is why stale metrics persist even after smart people understand the problem.

The metric is embedded in incentives.

It has constituencies.

It is not merely observing the organization.

It is organizing it.

That is also why replacing a metric carelessly can be dangerous.

The current fashion in management is to praise outcomes and criticize outputs. Sometimes this is sensible. Counting tasks can create activity without value. But outcomes are not automatically better measures simply because they sound closer to the mission.

Outcomes can be noisy.

They can arrive too late.

They can be influenced by factors outside the team’s control.

They can encourage hidden risk-taking if people are rewarded for the result without regard to how it was produced.

They can be impossible to compare fairly across cases.

A hospital should care about patient outcomes. It still needs process measures because waiting for mortality statistics to reveal a sterilization failure would be absurd.

An airline should care about safety. It still needs leading indicators, maintenance checks, training records, incident reporting, and operational measures because the final outcome—no crash—is too rare and too blunt to manage by itself.

A company should care about long-term customer value. It still needs nearer-term indicators because “long term” can become a convenient place to hide poor execution.

The lesson is not to abandon metrics.

It is to make their memory visible.

Every important measure should be understood as the answer to a question.

What question?

Under what business model?

Under what technology?

Under what customer behavior?

Under what regulatory environment?

On what time horizon?

With what failure mode?

When those conditions change, the measure deserves re-examination even if the formula still works.

Adobe did not stop reporting revenue because subscriptions changed the business.

It added a measure that exposed something revenue alone could not yet show clearly: the stock of recurring business accumulating underneath a transition that temporarily made the income statement look worse.

This is a useful pattern because it avoids two common errors.

The first is metric fundamentalism.

That is the belief that the old measure remains sufficient because it is objective, audited, comparable, or historically important.

The second is metric opportunism.

That is the habit of inventing flattering new measures whenever the old ones turn ugly.

A company with falling revenue can always discover a “community engagement index.” A failing program can count people touched. A product losing customers can celebrate impressions. A research team can redefine progress until the project becomes unfalsifiable.

New metrics are therefore not automatically adaptive.

They need to earn legitimacy.

Adobe’s recurring-revenue measures were useful because they were tied to the actual economics of the new model. Subscription counts and annualized recurring revenue were not decorative. They described the mechanism management had chosen: customers would pay over time rather than primarily at upgrade moments.

A good transition metric should do the same.

It should measure the causal bridge between the present and the intended future.

If a manufacturer is trying to shift from product sales to service contracts, the useful bridge may include installed base, attach rate, renewal, service margin, and reliability—not merely units shipped.

If a software company is moving from human-operated workflows to agentic automation, old measures of seats, clicks, or time spent may become ambiguous. A product that succeeds by eliminating ten manual steps can make engagement fall while customer value rises.

If a utility is moving toward more variable generation, old measures designed around steady centralized production may need companions that capture flexibility, curtailment, storage, congestion, and reliability across different conditions.

The bridge metric is not the destination.

It is a way to see whether the transition mechanism is actually taking hold.

That distinction matters because transition metrics should often expire.

A company can become trapped by the very measure that helped it adapt.

Annualized recurring revenue is useful when recurring revenue is the strategic transition. Once recurring revenue becomes ordinary, the organization may need to care more about retention quality, product depth, cost to serve, pricing power, or whatever constraint has moved next.

Metrics should not acquire constitutional status simply because they were once insightful.

They too can become installed capital.

One way to detect this is to listen for a particular sentence:

“We’ve always measured it that way.”

The sentence is not proof of a problem.

Some measures deserve continuity. Financial statements benefit from comparability. Safety systems need stable definitions. Long-term scientific series become more valuable because measurement stays consistent. Changing a metric can destroy the very historical information needed to understand whether performance improved.

Continuity has value.

The danger is continuity without remembered purpose.

A stable measure is an asset when the underlying phenomenon remains stable enough for comparison.

It becomes adaptation debt when people continue optimizing it after the link between the measure and the objective has weakened.

That link can weaken in several ways.

The business model changes.

The customer changes.

The technology changes.

The time horizon changes.

The measure becomes a target and behavior reorganizes around gaming it.

The metric improves because the hard cases moved elsewhere.

The process gets faster while the outcome gets worse.

The organization automates the counted work and leaves the uncounted work to humans.

The product begins creating value by reducing an activity the company used to celebrate increasing.

None of these makes measurement impossible.

They make measurement a recurring design problem.

This is where adaptation debt and Goodhart-style problems are related but not identical.

A metric can become corrupted because people game it once it becomes a target.

But a metric can also remain honestly measured and still become strategically obsolete.

Adobe did not need employees to manipulate revenue for the old revenue pattern to become incomplete during the subscription transition. The underlying economics had changed the timing of recognition.

The measure was telling the truth about the old question.

The company needed an additional question.

That is a deeper managerial habit than simply warning people not to game metrics.

It requires leaders to ask what kind of world each number assumes.

A robust measurement system therefore needs at least three forms of memory.

It needs historical memory: enough consistency to compare performance over time.

It needs causal memory: an explicit account of why the measure is believed to represent something important.

And it needs revision memory: a record of when the organization changed the measure, why it changed it, and what became incomparable afterward.

Without historical memory, the organization cannot learn.

Without causal memory, it cannot tell whether the number still means what people think it means.

Without revision memory, it can rewrite the scorecard whenever reality becomes inconvenient.

These are not three new dashboards.

They are three disciplines around the same one.

The practical test is simple enough to ask in a meeting.

If this metric improves, what do we believe will happen because of that improvement?

If nobody can answer except by repeating the metric in different words, the number may have become ceremonial.

If the answer was true five years ago but depends on a customer behavior, technology, or business model that has since changed, the number may be carrying adaptation debt.

If the answer still holds and the measure remains hard to manipulate, comparable, timely, and decision-relevant, keep it.

The purpose is not novelty.

The purpose is correspondence.

A metric earns its place by continuing to correspond closely enough to the thing the institution is actually trying to accomplish.

That is why the hardest measurement transitions usually happen at the same time as strategy transitions.

The organization is changing what it does while also changing how it knows whether it is doing well.

There is no neutral scoreboard waiting outside the process.

The scoreboard is part of the process.

In 2013, Adobe’s old revenue measures did not suddenly become wrong.

They became insufficient to describe a company deliberately moving value recognition onto a different clock.

The strategic act was not merely choosing subscriptions.

It was accepting, for a time, that the old scoreboard would punish the new game.

Then building a better way to see what was happening before the new economics became obvious to everyone else.
