# Cannibalize What, Exactly?

“Cannibalize yourself before someone else does” is the kind of strategy advice that becomes more impressive each time it is repeated.

It has the right emotional ingredients.

Courage.

Urgency.

A willingness to hurt the present for the future.

It also avoids the difficult question.

Cannibalize what, exactly?

A product?

A price?

A margin structure?

A distribution channel?

A customer relationship?

A sales compensation plan?

A brand promise?

An installed technology?

A factory?

A professional identity?

The slogan turns all of these into one thing called the old business.

Organizations do not experience them as one thing.

That is why self-disruption is harder than the advice makes it sound.

Consider Adobe in 2013.

The company had spent decades selling creative software through perpetual licenses. Customers paid large sums for a version of a product. Revenue was recognized in a pattern that matched those transactions. Product launches mattered because upgrades could trigger another purchase.

Then Adobe moved aggressively toward Creative Cloud subscriptions.

The strategic logic is familiar now.

Recurring revenue.

Continuous product delivery.

A lower initial price of access.

A relationship that did not depend on persuading users to buy each major upgrade.

At the time, the transition made some traditional financial measures look worse.

Adobe's fiscal 2013 filing said accelerating Creative Cloud adoption would continue to reduce perpetual-license revenue. Because subscription revenue was recognized over time, the transition contributed to lower total net revenue compared with the prior year. Expenses did not fall in proportion, pressuring income and operating margin.

The company was succeeding at the transition by doing something that damaged the old revenue pattern.

This is the cleanest version of cannibalization.

Adobe could identify what it was willing to give up.

Perpetual-license economics.

The company did not try to destroy everything attached to them.

It wanted to keep customers.

Keep the creative tools.

Keep the brand.

Keep the installed knowledge of designers, photographers, editors, and publishers.

Keep the value of the software suite.

Keep the relationship with professional users.

The write-down was selective.

That is the part slogans omit.

A transition works when an organization can separate the legacy mechanism that has become constraining from the capabilities still worth carrying.

Cannibalization without that distinction is demolition.

Adobe changed its measurement system along with its business. Paid subscriptions and annualized recurring revenue became important ways to see the new model while the old revenue pattern was declining. That mattered because an organization cannot easily finance a transition if every internal measure tells people the transition is failure.

Chapter 6 called this metric memory.

Here the measurement change becomes part of the cannibalization itself.

The company was not only replacing one source of revenue with another.

It was changing what counted as evidence that the business was healthy.

This is why self-cannibalization is institutional, not merely strategic.

A chief executive can announce that the new product should replace the old one.

Finance still has to fund it.

Sales still has to sell it.

Customers still have to accept it.

Compensation still has to reward it.

Support still has to operate it.

Engineering still has to maintain both during the transition.

Investors still have to understand why short-term numbers changed.

The old business is a network of agreements.

Cannibalizing one node can leave the rest of the network fighting back.

Netflix shows a messier version.

The company understood streaming's strategic importance while the DVD-by-mail business still had value. Its filings described an explicit intention to grow DVD while expanding internet delivery as the market developed. The DVD subscriber base was supposed to help move the company toward streaming.

This was not an incumbent asleep at the wheel.

It was an incumbent trying to use one business to finance and distribute its successor.

That sounds elegant in retrospect.

The transition was not.

In 2011, Netflix separated its DVD and streaming plans. Customers who wanted both effectively faced a price increase. The company then announced that the DVD business would be renamed Qwikster and separated onto its own website.

The reaction was sharply negative.

Netflix later reported significant cancellations and a decline in gross subscriber additions. It abandoned the Qwikster branding plan.

The long-term direction toward streaming did not make the particular bridge correct.

This distinction deserves more attention.

An organization can be right about what should eventually shrink and wrong about how quickly, how visibly, or through which customer experience the shrinkage should occur.

The phrase cannibalize yourself collapses endpoint and path.

Customers live through the path.

Employees live through the path.

Financial statements live through the path.

The replacement system has to survive the path.

This creates a sequencing problem.

Cannibalize too late and the old business absorbs capital, talent, and attention while the new market develops elsewhere.

Cannibalize too early and the company can destroy the cash flow, distribution, trust, and customer base that would have financed the transition.

This is not a paradox that disappears with courage.

It is an optimization problem under uncertainty.

The organization does not know exactly how fast the new system will improve.

It does not know how quickly customers will move.

It does not know which parts of the old value proposition the new one must preserve.

It does not know whether competitors will force the timing.

It does not know whether the new economics will become as attractive as forecast.

Chapter 14 will make the countercase explicit: changing too early can be expensive too.

For now, the important point is that deliberate cannibalization has to choose its victim carefully.

The old product may be the wrong victim.

Sometimes the product survives while the business model changes.

Sometimes the business model survives while the distribution channel changes.

Sometimes the channel survives while the underlying technology changes.

Sometimes the technology survives while the customer segment changes.

Sometimes the legacy feature everyone expects to disappear turns out to be the bridge that makes the transition possible.

This is why architecture matters.

A firm with a modular product can replace one layer while preserving others.

A tightly integrated system may require a much larger write-down.

The same is true organizationally.

A company whose salesforce can sell both old and new products may transition differently from one whose compensation, training, and customer relationships make the products enemies.

A company with a single brand may need to manage customer expectations carefully.

A company with separate brands can isolate experiments but may fail to transfer trust.

Cannibalization depends on what can be decoupled.

Microsoft's shift toward cloud services provides a useful countercase to the idea that incumbents have to destroy their past to adapt.

In its 2015 annual report, Microsoft described a broad transformation around a mobile-first, cloud-first strategy while it still possessed enormous Windows, Office, enterprise, and developer ecosystems. Commercial cloud and Office 365 were growing. The company was changing how software was delivered and monetized without treating every legacy capability as an obstacle.

The installed base became distribution.

Enterprise relationships became sales channels.

Developer ecosystems became complements.

Cash flow financed the transition.

This is the version of cannibalization that business folklore often misses because it is less dramatic.

The incumbent does not set fire to the old business.

It harvests it.

Harvesting is a more precise word for many transitions.

A mature business can be managed for cash while investment shifts elsewhere.

Features can be maintained rather than expanded.

Sales capacity can move gradually.

Customers can be offered migration incentives.

New contracts can default to the new model while old contracts remain supported.

The old system is allowed to decline without being treated as the strategic center.

This is a controlled write-down.

It has different politics from sudden destruction.

People know the old business still matters operationally.

They also know it no longer wins every capital allocation argument.

That distinction can reduce adaptation debt because it separates respect for current obligations from belief in future growth.

Organizations often fail to make this separation.

A mature product is either celebrated as core or insulted as legacy.

The language becomes moral.

Core means important.

Legacy means embarrassing.

People inside the old business hear the strategic label as a judgment about their value.

They defend the business more aggressively.

Leadership then treats the defense as proof of cultural resistance.

The loop hardens.

The company would be better served by distinguishing three questions.

Does the old business still create value now?

Does it deserve incremental growth investment?

Which capabilities inside it should survive the transition?

The answers can be yes, no, and many.

That combination is common.

A product can remain profitable while deserving little new capital.

A factory can continue operating while the company stops building more factories of that type.

A software platform can stay supported while new features move to a successor.

A professional team can keep serving existing customers while hiring shifts toward new skills.

A channel can remain important for one segment and irrelevant for another.

This is not indecision.

It is decomposition.

Cannibalization becomes safer when the organization can decompose what the old system actually does.

The financial contribution.

The customer relationship.

The technical capability.

The trust.

The regulatory status.

The workforce knowledge.

The supplier network.

The distribution.

The data.

The brand.

The installed base.

Some elements belong to the old architecture.

Some can become advantages in the new one.

The hard part is that the elements are rarely priced separately.

A business unit's profit can make all of its assets appear strategically valuable.

A declining business can make all of its assets appear obsolete.

Both conclusions are too coarse.

This is where adaptation debt becomes an accounting problem.

The firm needs to know what it would have to rebuild if it destroyed the legacy system.

A startup has to build distribution from zero.

The incumbent may already have it.

A startup has to earn trust.

The incumbent may be able to transfer it.

A startup has no obligation to support an old format.

The incumbent does—but the format may also contain customers worth moving.

The past can be expensive to carry and expensive to lose.

Cannibalization should therefore be thought of as a series of write-down decisions rather than one heroic act.

Write down the margin expectation.

Write down the requirement that the new product serve every old customer on day one.

Write down the compensation plan that pays more for the legacy sale.

Write down the idea that the old channel owns the customer.

Write down the utilization target that keeps a factory producing beyond strategic need.

Write down the metric that makes transition revenue look like failure.

Write down the status hierarchy that gives the mature business permanent authority.

These smaller write-downs can create room for the new system before the old system disappears.

For now, notice that the most effective self-cannibalization often begins long before a product is killed.

It begins when an organization stops allowing the old business to define the standards by which the new one must justify itself.

This is what Christensen and Bower's work makes so uncomfortable.

Established firms can allocate resources rationally toward the needs of important existing customers. The new market can fail the old market's tests because it is smaller, cheaper, simpler, lower-margin, or technically weaker on dimensions current customers care about.

The organization does not need to hate innovation.

It needs only to require the new business to prove itself using the old business's scoreboard.

Cannibalization therefore begins with permission to be economically different.

A new business may have lower gross margin initially.

A new customer may be smaller.

A new distribution channel may bypass the existing salesforce.

A subscription model may worsen reported revenue in the transition.

A cloud product may shift infrastructure cost.

A simpler product may have fewer features.

If every difference is treated as evidence of inferiority, the new business will be optimized back into the old one.

The company will innovate cosmetically and preserve its economics structurally.

This is common because structural cannibalization threatens internal constituencies.

A feature threatens no department.

A business model can.

A new interface threatens no sales territory.

A self-service channel can.

A product improvement can make everyone look good.

A lower-priced offering can make one team's revenue look worse.

A technology transition becomes politically real when the losses acquire names.

That is why leaders often delay the organizational part of a transition.

The new product is funded.

The old incentives remain.

The company announces the future while paying people to preserve the past.

Then leadership complains that adoption is slow.

The system is doing what it is compensated to do.

This is where the phrase cannibalize yourself should be translated into operating terms.

Who loses revenue credit when the customer moves?

Who owns the customer after migration?

Which budget pays for parallel support?

What happens to people whose expertise is less central?

Which old commitments remain contractual?

Which metric declines when the transition succeeds?

Which executive's span of control shrinks?

Which facility becomes underutilized?

Which supplier loses volume?

Which investor expectation has to be reset?

These questions are less inspiring than the slogan.

They are the slogan's actual content.

There is also a governance problem.

The old business can be both the source of cash and the judge of the new business.

That is dangerous.

Managers responsible for current profit have a legitimate duty to protect it. Asking them to sacrifice their own targets for an uncertain future creates conflicting incentives.

This is one reason organizations create separate units for exploratory work.

Separation can protect a new business from the incumbent's metrics, processes, customers, and margin expectations.

But separation can go too far.

The new unit develops its own culture and architecture, then discovers it cannot use the incumbent's distribution, data, brand, or customers because integration was never designed.

The company has two businesses and no transition.

Ambidexterity research addresses this tension: exploitation and exploration can require different organizational arrangements while still needing senior integration.

The phrase senior integration is doing a lot of work.

Someone has to decide which resources cross the boundary.

Someone has to protect the new business from premature demands.

Someone has to prevent the old business from being abandoned recklessly.

Someone has to determine when the experiment has earned scale.

Someone has to close experiments that did not work.

Someone has to move customers.

That is governance of cannibalization.

The absence of governance produces either stagnation or chaos.

A company that refuses to cannibalize preserves everything until the market does the writing down for it.

A company that celebrates destruction can abandon productive capabilities before the replacement is ready.

Both failures come from treating transition as a single decision.

It is a sequence.

Adobe's sequence worked because the company could tell investors that the new economics would make current revenue patterns look worse, and because it created measures that made the subscription trajectory legible.

Netflix's sequence stumbled because a strategic shift in delivery and pricing collided with customer expectations faster than the bridge could support.

Microsoft's sequence shows that an incumbent can use old assets as propulsion when the new system is designed to inherit them.

These cases do not create one formula.

They create a better question.

What must die for the new system to live?

The answer is rarely “the old business.”

Too broad.

The useful answer is specific.

This margin assumption.

This channel monopoly.

This metric.

This interface.

This capital-allocation rule.

This compatibility promise, eventually.

This product line.

This piece of equipment.

This organizational status.

This way of recognizing revenue.

This customer segment we will no longer optimize around.

Adaptation debt becomes payable when the organization can name the write-down.

Until then, self-cannibalization is theater.

The company says it is willing to disrupt itself.

Everyone waits to see whose number changes.

That is when the real strategy begins.
