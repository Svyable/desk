# The Identity Write-Down

A company can replace a machine without asking the machine who it is.

People are harder.

So are institutions.

A business can change its product line, distribution model, technology, customer, or economics and still discover that the most expensive thing to change is the sentence employees use when someone asks what the company does.

We are a film company.

We are a bank.

We are a newspaper.

We are an engineering company.

We are a premium brand.

We are a hardware company.

We are a research organization.

We are the safe choice.

We are the people who know this market.

Identity turns strategy into grammar.

It determines which nouns feel natural and which ones sound like betrayal.

That is why adaptation debt eventually reaches the self.

The organization has already accumulated physical capital, metrics, expertise, customer obligations, routines, and rules. Those commitments become more durable when they are woven into a story about what kind of institution made them.

The story reduces uncertainty.

It tells employees which work deserves respect.

It tells customers what to expect.

It tells managers which opportunities belong inside the company and which ones do not.

It tells recruits why joining matters.

It tells investors how to interpret the business.

It tells people what success looks like when the dashboard is ambiguous.

Identity is coordination technology.

That is why it cannot be dismissed as branding.

Dennis Gioia, Majken Schultz, and Kevin Corley argued against the old assumption that organizational identity must be truly fixed in order to feel enduring. They suggested something more useful: identity can retain a sense of continuity while the meanings attached to it shift through interaction with how outsiders see the organization.

This distinction matters for adaptation.

The organization does not always have to choose between remaining itself and becoming something else.

Sometimes it can reinterpret what “itself” means.

A company that thinks of itself as a maker of one product has fewer routes than a company that thinks of itself as solving a broader problem.

A company that says “we manufacture cameras” faces digital imaging differently from one that says “we help people capture and preserve images.”

The second sentence does not guarantee success.

It simply leaves more strategic moves compatible with identity.

This is why broad purpose statements are attractive during transition.

They widen the room.

They can also become empty.

“We connect people.”

“We empower human potential.”

“We improve lives.”

Any business can fit inside language broad enough.

Identity only helps if it constrains something.

A company with no stable sense of what it will not become has not achieved adaptability.

It has removed the basis on which people coordinate.

This is the central tension of identity.

Too narrow and it turns history into a prison.

Too broad and it becomes decorative.

The challenge is to preserve continuity at the level that still earns its keep.

Polaroid is useful again because the transition from instant photography to digital imaging was not merely technological. The company's historical success joined chemistry, cameras, film, manufacturing, distribution, and a distinctive user experience. Its managers could possess digital capabilities while still interpreting the business through an economic and cognitive model shaped by instant photography.

That is what makes identity expensive.

It is not a sign on the wall.

It is embedded in what people consider a real business.

A new opportunity can be technologically impressive and still fail the identity test.

Who is the customer?

Where is the recurring revenue?

Where is our differentiation?

What do we manufacture?

What does quality mean?

Why would anyone buy this from us?

These are legitimate questions.

They become dangerous when the old identity supplies the answers before the new market has had a chance to develop.

The identity write-down begins when the organization accepts that some part of the old answer no longer deserves permanent status.

This feels different from writing down an asset.

An asset write-down appears in accounting.

An identity write-down appears in status.

A business that was once central becomes one of several businesses.

A profession that defined the company becomes a supporting function.

A product that carried the brand becomes a legacy offering.

A skill that once marked seniority becomes one input among many.

A customer segment that shaped the roadmap stops receiving automatic priority.

A leader's career story becomes history rather than strategy.

These losses are personal even when the strategy is correct.

That is why identity transitions produce more resistance than spreadsheets predict.

The person defending the old system may not be defending a budget.

They may be defending the meaning of twenty years of work.

An executive who built the company's most profitable division can hear “we are moving away from this business” as “the thing you were good at no longer matters.”

An engineer can hear “software now drives the product” as “hardware expertise is secondary.”

A journalist can hear “digital subscriptions” as a threat to norms formed around print.

A physician can hear “AI-assisted workflow” as an attack on professional judgment.

A salesperson can hear “self-service” as a claim that relationships are overhead.

Leadership often responds by insisting that nobody's contribution is being devalued.

Employees are rarely fooled.

Some contributions are being devalued.

That is what a strategic transition means.

The mistake is pretending otherwise.

A credible identity transition has to distinguish value from permanence.

The old capability may have been excellent.

The people who built it may deserve respect.

The business may still generate cash.

The market can still be moving away from it.

All four statements can be true.

This is a more humane way to write down identity because it does not require turning the past into a mistake in order to justify a different future.

Organizations are often tempted to rewrite history during transformation.

The old strategy becomes “complacency.”

The old product becomes “legacy.”

The old leaders become people who “didn't get it.”

The new team casts itself as rescue.

This narrative is politically useful because it legitimizes change.

It is strategically dangerous because it destroys institutional memory.

If the past succeeded for reasons, those reasons still contain information.

A transformation that can only proceed by insulting the old competence may cause the people who understand that competence to leave before their knowledge can transfer.

Then the new organization repeats old failures with newer language.

Chapter 18 will return to this problem in the pairing of the heretic and the historian.

For now, the identity write-down should be understood as selective depreciation.

Some meanings lose weight.

Others survive.

A newspaper can stop defining itself by the printing press while keeping reporting, verification, judgment, local knowledge, and public accountability.

A bank can change interfaces and infrastructure while preserving fiduciary responsibility, risk discipline, and trust.

A manufacturer can change production technology while preserving process control and quality culture.

A software company can change business model while preserving the usefulness of the tools and the communities built around them.

A professional can stop being the person who performs every routine step while remaining the person responsible for judgment when routine ends.

The organization does not preserve identity by preserving every activity.

It preserves identity by deciding which commitments are constitutive and which were historical implementations.

That distinction can be difficult because implementations acquire emotional weight.

The uniform.

The headquarters.

The flagship product.

The annual ritual.

The title structure.

The physical newsroom.

The retail branch.

The factory town.

The codebase.

The customer conference.

These things make identity visible.

A new strategy can feel abstract beside them.

That is why symbolic decisions matter disproportionately in transformations.

Closing a famous factory can signal more than capacity reduction.

Renaming a division can feel like erasure.

Moving headquarters can be interpreted as a change in who the company is for.

Eliminating a product can provoke customers who rarely bought it because the product represented continuity.

Coca-Cola discovered the consumer version of this in 1985.

The company had substantial evidence that consumers preferred a reformulated taste in blind testing. The change was not made randomly. Coca-Cola's own historical account emphasizes that the flagship brand had been losing share and that extensive taste tests favored the new formula.

What the tests failed to measure adequately was the relationship people had with the existing product.

The formula was not only a bundle of sensory attributes.

It had become part of consumer memory and brand identity.

Public reaction was intense enough that the original formula returned as Coca-Cola Classic after seventy-nine days.

This is often presented as a marketing blunder.

It is more useful as an adaptation case.

The company tested the product variable.

The market was also pricing continuity.

That does not mean companies should never alter iconic products.

It means identity is an economic input even when no spreadsheet line names it.

A transition can improve the thing measured in a test and reduce the value produced by the relationship around the thing.

The same mistake happens inside organizations.

Leadership evaluates a new operating model by productivity, cost, speed, or technical quality.

Employees evaluate it partly by what it says about who matters.

If that second variable is ignored, resistance is interpreted as irrational attachment.

Sometimes it is attachment.

Attachment is not therefore irrelevant.

People coordinate through identity because identity answers social questions metrics cannot.

Who has authority?

Whose judgment counts?

Which failures are shameful?

Which work is prestigious?

What kind of person succeeds here?

What promises do we not break even if breaking them would be profitable?

These answers can be valuable constraints.

A company that treats every identity element as negotiable can become strategically agile and culturally untrustworthy.

Employees stop believing slogans because the slogans change with each chief executive.

Customers stop investing in the relationship because nothing feels durable.

Long-term projects become harder because nobody knows whether today's priority will survive next year's reorganization.

The organization accumulates adaptation debt by changing too often.

That sounds contradictory until we remember the thesis.

Commitment creates capability.

No commitment means no compounding.

The goal is not to avoid identity.

It is to prevent identity from becoming an unexamined claim about which implementation must survive forever.

One way to do this is to preserve a distinction between **purpose**, **competence**, and **form**.

Purpose is the problem or obligation the organization considers worth serving.

Competence is what the organization has learned to do unusually well.

Form is the current configuration through which purpose and competence are expressed.

The categories are not perfectly separable, but the distinction helps.

A company can preserve purpose while changing form.

It can discover that a competence transfers into a new form.

It can also discover that an old competence was more form-specific than people hoped.

The identity write-down is where those discoveries become socially real.

People need permission to say that a beloved competence no longer deserves investment.

They also need permission to defend a competence whose surface form looks old but whose underlying value remains.

This is why transformations fail when “new” becomes synonymous with “good.”

The organization starts rewarding novelty as identity.

Every old process is suspect.

Every new technology receives benefit of the doubt.

Experienced employees become defensive because the transition has defined them as a problem class.

The company loses the ability to distinguish debt from memory.

Identity has simply flipped signs.

The future becomes another doctrine.

A better transition allows grief without allowing grief to set strategy.

This sounds sentimental in a business book.

It is operational.

Closing a plant affects communities.

Eliminating a product changes careers.

Automating a task changes professional status.

Reorganizing a company breaks teams.

Moving away from a founding business can make long-serving employees feel that the institution they joined no longer exists.

Those reactions do not disappear because a discounted-cash-flow model is correct.

If leadership refuses to acknowledge them, the emotions move into other channels.

Projects slow.

Information is withheld.

New teams are treated as invaders.

Customers become symbols in internal fights.

Strategic disagreements become moral accusations.

The organization pays for denied identity loss through execution.

This does not require therapeutic management.

It requires truthful language.

This business built the company.

It will not be the growth engine it was.

These skills still matter.

These skills matter less.

We are asking some people to learn work they did not choose originally.

Some roles will disappear.

Some promises will remain.

Some will end.

The company will still be recognizable in these ways.

It will not be recognizable in those ways.

Truth lowers the cost of transition because people can plan around it.

Ambiguity often feels kinder and produces more debt.

A division is told it remains strategic while its investment declines.

Employees notice.

A product is said to have a future while every new customer is steered elsewhere.

Salespeople notice.

A profession is told human judgment is central while staffing is redesigned around automation.

Professionals notice.

False continuity makes identity brittle.

People defend symbols because they do not trust the stated transition.

The identity write-down works best when it happens before a crisis forces it.

In crisis, identity decisions become binary.

Close the division.

Sell the company.

Cut the workforce.

Abandon the product.

The external threat is now undeniable, but the organization has less time to transfer capabilities or build a successor story.

Earlier write-downs can be smaller.

The company can stop saying one product defines the whole enterprise.

It can promote people whose careers cross boundaries.

It can create status in emerging fields before the old field collapses.

It can broaden recruitment.

It can expose experts to adjacent problems.

It can move symbolic resources—executive attention, awards, internal communications—before large capital shifts are required.

These acts look cosmetic until we remember that identity shapes attention.

What gets celebrated gets imitated.

What gets promoted gets studied.

What gets described as core receives protection.

Symbols become resource-allocation signals.

This is why identity can be written down gradually.

A company does not need to announce that everything it was is over.

It can change the weight of meanings over time.

The word “core” can migrate.

The prestige ladder can widen.

The founding story can be retold around a deeper competence rather than a product.

The organization can make room for a future self before the old self has to die.

That is not branding.

It is option creation.

Chapter 16 will make the financial analogy explicit.

For now, the important point is that identity creates option value when it is broad enough to support more than one plausible form and specific enough to coordinate real behavior.

The strongest organizations often carry a paradox.

They know what they are.

They are not certain what that will look like next.

That uncertainty is not weakness.

It is the room in which adaptation happens.

An identity write-down is therefore not an admission that the past was false.

It is an admission that a successful answer became too specific.

The institution keeps the history.

It stops treating the history as a job description for the future.
