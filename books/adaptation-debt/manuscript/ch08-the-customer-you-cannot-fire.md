# The Customer You Cannot Fire

A customer can become infrastructure.

At first the relationship is simple.

A company makes something. A buyer pays for it.

If the product changes, the buyer decides whether the new version is worth buying.

Then success complicates the arrangement.

The customer's workflow begins to depend on the product.

Employees are trained around it.

Files are stored in its format.

Integrations are built.

Procurement rules refer to it.

A supplier certifies against it.

A regulator accepts reports produced by it.

The customer's own customers come to expect behavior that the product makes possible.

What began as a sale becomes a promise distributed across two organizations.

Now the producer cannot change freely without making the customer change too.

This is customer commitment.

It is one of the most valuable forms of success.

It is also one of the most expensive to unwind.

The phrase customer lock-in is often used here, usually with suspicion.

Sometimes suspicion is deserved. Companies can make exit artificially difficult. They can use proprietary formats, contractual penalties, bundling, data friction, or technical incompatibility to raise switching costs.

But not all customer dependence is imposed.

Some of it is created by value.

A system becomes hard to replace because the customer has built useful things around it.

The stronger the ecosystem, the deeper the dependence can become.

This is why installed base is both an asset and a constraint.

Millions of customers create revenue, distribution, trust, feedback, and bargaining power.

They also create millions of migration problems.

A startup can break an interface because hardly anyone depends on it.

A mature platform can turn the same decision into an economic event.

The producer does not merely ship a new version.

It asks customers to rewrite part of their own history.

Microsoft has spent decades living inside this problem.

A new version of Windows can improve security, architecture, hardware support, and system behavior while still carrying the obligation to run old applications. Microsoft's current support material treats application compatibility as a normal part of upgrading: older apps can stop functioning because operating-system architecture, security features, or requirements change, and users are given compatibility tools and migration guidance to keep useful software working where possible.

This is not evidence of technological weakness.

It is evidence of customer success.

The operating system became a platform other people invested in.

Every old application is a vote for continuity.

The vote is not decisive.

Security changes can require breaking behavior. Old interfaces eventually need to disappear. Hardware changes. Unsupported components become liabilities. Compatibility itself can become a source of complexity and vulnerability.

But the cost of change is real because the producer does not own the full system.

Customers do.

This is the essential difference between an internal transition and an installed-base transition.

A company can rewrite its own process by paying its own migration cost.

A platform company has to consider costs imposed on customers it does not control.

If the producer moves too quickly, customers resist.

If it moves too slowly, architecture ossifies.

The customer's dependency becomes part of the product roadmap.

This can look irrational from the perspective of the new design.

Why preserve an old interface that complicates everything?

Because somewhere a factory still uses it.

A hospital has a device attached to it.

A government agency has a workflow nobody has rewritten.

A bank has tested software under it.

A small business relies on a program whose vendor disappeared years ago.

The old interface has become a bridge to organizations that move more slowly than the producer.

Break the bridge and the producer may be technically cleaner but commercially smaller.

This is why compatibility is a strategic choice, not merely an engineering burden.

IBM's mainframe lifecycle offers another version of the same idea.

Long service lives and family-to-family upgrade paths protect investment because customers have accumulated enormous complements around the machines. Software, operations, skills, data, procedures, and reliability expectations make continuity valuable.

If IBM treated each new generation as a clean break, it could reduce some legacy complexity.

It would also destroy part of what customers are buying.

The ability to change without forcing every dependent system to change at once is itself a product feature.

This is an important counterargument to the whole idea of adaptation debt.

Backward compatibility can be a way of adapting.

A company can move the underlying system while preserving the contract customers care about.

The old commitment becomes a migration interface rather than a prison.

The question is whether the compatibility layer lowers transition cost or merely postpones it.

Those two cases can look identical for years.

A bridge is useful when traffic is actually crossing it.

A bridge becomes debt when nobody is allowed to close it because someone might still need it someday.

Customer commitments become dangerous when the organization loses the ability to distinguish these cases.

This can happen because current customers are unusually legible.

They call.

They renew.

They complain.

Salespeople know them.

Account managers know which contracts are at risk.

Their revenue appears in the forecast.

Future customers are quieter.

They may not yet exist as a category.

They may be using a competitor.

They may want a simpler product the current customer would consider incomplete.

They may value a distribution model the incumbent's best customers distrust.

They may have lower willingness to pay at first.

The current customer therefore has institutional weight before anyone makes a conscious decision to privilege it.

Clayton Christensen and Joseph Bower's disk-drive research made this mechanism famous. Established firms could respond strongly to innovations that served current customers. The harder technologies were those aimed at emerging markets whose initial requirements and economics did not look attractive inside the existing resource-allocation process.

This is often simplified into the command to ignore customers.

That would be absurd.

Customers know real problems.

They pay real money.

They reveal defects no strategy team would invent.

The danger is not listening.

It is listening to one population as though it were the whole market across time.

Success creates a sample bias.

The company becomes very good at hearing the people it already serves.

The people it does not yet serve are missing from the dataset.

This is especially powerful in enterprise markets.

Large customers can shape roadmaps directly.

They request features.

They negotiate contracts.

They ask for support commitments.

They require certifications.

They demand notice periods before deprecation.

They can refuse upgrades.

A product that began as software can become a bilateral governance system.

This is valuable because enterprise customers finance development and reveal operational requirements.

It is constraining because each accommodation adds another promise the next architecture has to inherit.

The company accumulates customer-specific history.

A small product may have code.

A mature enterprise product has archaeology.

Some behavior exists because a customer once needed it.

Some remains because removing it would break an integration.

Some survives because nobody knows who still uses it.

Some is documented as deprecated but cannot be killed.

Some was meant to be temporary and has become contractual.

The product roadmap contains fossils of relationships.

This is why product managers can feel as though they are negotiating with ghosts.

The customer who demanded a feature may be gone.

The feature remains because another customer's system copied it.

A temporary compatibility mode becomes permanent because someone built production around it.

The organization cannot fire the dependency even if it no longer knows the original buyer.

This is customer adaptation debt in its purest form.

The commitment migrated from relationship to architecture.

Netflix's transition from DVD-by-mail to streaming shows a different kind of customer obligation because the compatibility layer was not technical.

It was experiential.

Customers had learned a service.

The queue, discs, pricing, delivery expectations, and subscription relationship formed a product in their minds. Netflix could see that internet delivery was becoming strategically central while the DVD business remained profitable and meaningful.

Its 2007 filing described growing the DVD subscription business while expanding internet delivery as the market developed. The old customer base was not merely baggage. It was the bridge into the next service.

Then the 2011 plan separation and Qwikster announcement made the transition cost visible.

Customers reacted badly.

The company later acknowledged substantial cancellations and abandoned the separate Qwikster branding plan.

The lesson is not that customers were right about the long-term strategy.

Streaming became central.

The lesson is that a company cannot assume customers experience a strategic transition as progress.

Customers experience price changes, missing features, new interfaces, split accounts, broken habits, and the feeling that something they already paid for has been made worse.

The producer sees the bridge.

The customer feels the construction zone.

This is why incumbents can be trapped by reasonable promises.

A company says it will support a product for ten years because customers require confidence.

That promise helps win the sale.

Years later the company wants to change architecture.

The support promise is now a constraint.

Was the promise a mistake?

Not necessarily.

Without it, the original customer may never have adopted the product.

The commitment financed success.

The debt appears later when the organization wants options that the commitment made expensive.

This is exactly the structure of adaptation debt.

The initial decision can be rational.

The complement can be valuable.

The problem emerges when conditions move.

There is a public-policy analogy.

A benefit program acquires beneficiaries whose lives depend on it.

A transit line creates development around stations.

A zoning rule shapes property values.

A tax provision changes investment behavior.

A school system creates expectations among families and employees.

Changing the rule later is not equivalent to choosing a different rule at the beginning because people have adapted to the current one.

Customer commitment is one version of a broader institutional fact.

People make plans around promises.

The promise becomes expensive to revise because others have invested on the assumption it will hold.

This is often good.

Stable promises make coordination possible.

A world in which every company, government, or platform can rewrite commitments instantly would be flexible and impossible to trust.

Adaptation therefore has a moral boundary.

The organization cannot treat every dependency as an obstacle created by backward customers.

Some dependencies are obligations the organization deliberately invited.

If a company spent years telling customers to build around its platform, it bears some responsibility for migration.

If a government encouraged investment under a rule, abrupt reversal can impose costs that were foreseeable.

If a professional standard required expensive compliance, changing it overnight can punish people for obeying yesterday.

The past constrains the future partly because trust should constrain the future.

This is why adaptation debt cannot simply be eliminated.

Some of it is the price of credibility.

The better goal is to avoid making promises more irreversible than the value they create.

Technology companies have learned this through deprecation policy.

An interface can be marked for retirement.

Customers receive notice.

A replacement is offered.

Telemetry can identify usage.

Deadlines can be staged.

Compatibility layers can remain for a defined period.

The old path becomes more expensive or less prominent over time.

Eventually it closes.

This is not perfect.

Customers still resist.

Developers miss deadlines.

The new interface can lack features.

Some dependencies are invisible until the old one disappears.

But deprecation turns an abrupt break into a managed write-down.

It makes time part of the migration architecture.

This is one of the most general ways to service adaptation debt.

Do not pretend the old commitment vanishes.

Price the transition into a schedule.

The difficulty is deciding which customers deserve accommodation.

The slogan customer obsession becomes complicated here.

If every current customer gets a veto over change, the product can never simplify.

If no customer does, the company can destroy trust and invite defection.

The organization needs some theory of whose pain matters and for how long.

Revenue is one answer.

Strategic importance is another.

Safety is another.

Contract is another.

Public interest can be another.

None is sufficient by itself.

Large customers can pay enough to preserve architecture long after the broader market has moved.

Small customers can reveal where the future is going before they become large.

Regulated customers can require support no startup wants to provide.

A long-tail user can still depend on a feature whose removal creates disproportionate harm.

The migration decision therefore becomes political inside the firm.

Someone has to decide which constituency bears the write-down.

This is why product transitions generate conflict even when everyone agrees on the destination.

Sales represents current revenue.

Product represents architecture.

Support represents migration pain.

Finance represents economics.

Legal represents commitments.

Engineering represents maintainability.

Strategy represents future opportunity.

Each group is looking at a different customer cost.

The disagreement can be healthy.

The danger is when the current customer's voice has a formal channel and the future customer's voice exists only as a slide.

Then the old market wins before the argument begins.

This is one reason separate units sometimes help incumbents explore new markets.

A new unit can use different margins, product assumptions, sales motions, and customer populations without being forced to justify itself entirely by the legacy business's standards.

But separation creates another debt.

Eventually the organization has to reconnect the new business to the old one.

Customers overlap.

Brands overlap.

Technology overlaps.

Budgets compete.

The new unit may resent the incumbent's processes.

The incumbent may resent subsidizing a business that threatens it.

The customer migration problem returns, only later.

There is no organizational trick that abolishes the obligation.

Someone has to build the bridge.

Microsoft's migration guidance for Windows illustrates this logic in ordinary technical terms. In-place upgrades can preserve applications, settings, and files. Other migration paths may preserve data while requiring applications to be reinstalled or modernized. The system provides tools because migration is not one event for every customer.

Different installed states require different paths.

That is a useful model beyond software.

Adaptation is often not a single cutover.

It is a portfolio of migration paths.

Some customers move early.

Some need compatibility.

Some can be offered a bridge.

Some require a parallel system.

Some should not be moved because the old system still serves them well.

Some eventually have to be told that the old promise ends.

The quality of adaptation depends partly on whether the organization can distinguish these groups.

Without that distinction, customer commitment becomes a universal excuse.

“We cannot change because customers need the old system.”

Which customers?

How many?

For which workflows?

For how long?

At what economic value?

Could an adapter preserve the needed behavior?

Could the customer migrate with support?

Is the customer actually unwilling to change, or has nobody offered a credible path?

These questions turn customer pressure into evidence.

They also expose when the organization itself is using customers as a shield.

That happens.

An internal group can defend the old product by invoking customer demands without testing whether customers would accept a new tradeoff.

Salespeople can resist changes that threaten compensation and describe the resistance as customer advocacy.

Engineers can preserve architecture because migration is difficult and call every dependency mission-critical.

Executives can postpone cannibalization by insisting the market is not ready.

The customer becomes a ventriloquist's dummy for organizational fear.

The remedy is not to dismiss the claim.

It is to ask the customer.

Better yet, test behavior.

Offer a migration path.

Price it.

Measure adoption.

Observe which features block movement.

Find the customers who move first and learn why.

Find the customers who refuse and distinguish inconvenience from true dependency.

The organization needs a map of the installed base, not a mythology about it.

This is another place where the future can be made more legible.

A company that tracks which interfaces customers use, which versions remain active, which integrations depend on deprecated behavior, which contracts promise support, and which workloads can migrate has a better chance of changing without betrayal.

The installed base becomes something that can be managed rather than feared.

That is the optimistic version of customer commitment.

The customer who cannot be fired can become the customer who can be migrated.

The distinction matters because a mature company rarely gets to start over.

Its future usually has to pass through the people who paid for its past.

Those customers are not dead weight.

They are one reason the company exists.

They may finance the new product, distribute it, test it, and make adoption faster than any entrant could achieve.

They can also hold the organization to promises that no longer fit the architecture it needs.

Both can be true at once.

That is why customer relationships are among the most valuable assets on a balance sheet and among the least visible sources of adaptation debt.

Success teaches customers to depend on you.

Then adaptation asks you to change without teaching them that dependence was a mistake.
