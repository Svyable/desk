# Small Write-Downs

A company does not have to wait for a crisis to discover what it can afford to lose.

It can lose something smaller first.

A little margin.

A little utilization.

A little certainty.

One customer segment.

One feature.

One old interface.

One piece of authority.

One budget category.

One assumption about what the business must be.

This is the practical meaning of a small write-down.

The phrase sounds financial because adaptation debt has been an accounting metaphor from the beginning. The organization made commitments that produced returns. Some of those commitments now reduce flexibility. The obvious solutions—keep everything or replace everything—are usually too coarse.

A small write-down asks whether the institution can pay part of the debt before the full balance becomes due.

The logic is similar to maintenance.

A machine can be repaired gradually or run until failure forces a large intervention.

Organizations often choose the second path with strategy because the first path looks inefficient.

A pilot team duplicates work.

A second supplier costs more.

A compatibility layer consumes engineering time.

A low-utilization new facility hurts returns.

A new metric complicates reporting.

A training program removes productive employees from their current roles.

A small experimental business does not meet the incumbent's margin.

Each expense appears before the future benefit is certain.

The old system, meanwhile, is producing today.

This is why adaptation debt hides.

The interest payment is opportunity cost.

It does not arrive as an invoice.

The organization sees the cost of keeping options open more clearly than the cost of allowing options to close.

Rita McGrath's real-options logic for technology investment addresses this asymmetry directly. Under uncertainty, an organization can make limited investments that preserve the right to expand later without committing immediately to the full project. The value lies partly in learning and partly in maintaining access to a future state.

The financial analogy has limits.

A traded option has standardized terms and a market price.

An organizational option has to be built.

It can decay.

People leave.

Suppliers stop investing.

Knowledge goes stale.

A prototype becomes irrelevant.

A small business unit can be absorbed back into the core.

The option is not a contract sitting in a drawer.

It is a capability that needs enough life to remain exercisable.

That is why small write-downs are operational.

Suppose a company believes a new distribution channel may become important.

The extreme choices are clear.

Move the whole business now.

Or wait until the channel is proven.

A smaller write-down might mean accepting channel conflict in one segment.

Let a small team sell directly even though the existing sales organization dislikes it.

Accept that some current revenue credit will move.

Learn which customers respond.

Discover which support tasks change.

Find out whether acquisition cost behaves as forecast.

The organization pays a bounded political and financial cost to obtain information it cannot get from a strategy deck.

If the channel fails, the company has not destroyed the core.

If it works, the company has something more valuable than a forecast.

It has operating memory.

This is the key difference between optionality and observation.

A company can watch a new market from the outside.

That preserves information about the market.

It does not preserve the ability to act inside it.

The organization may know exactly what is happening and still lack the people, contracts, tools, reputation, or customer access needed to respond.

Small write-downs convert passive awareness into limited participation.

They cost more.

That is why they work.

A second supplier is a simple industrial example.

The cheapest procurement strategy under stable conditions may be to concentrate volume with the best supplier. Scale lowers price. Quality management becomes easier. Logistics simplify. The relationship deepens.

Qualifying a second supplier can look wasteful.

Engineering time is spent on a source receiving little volume. Tooling may be duplicated. Unit cost can be higher. Procurement loses leverage from concentration.

The second supplier is a small write-down of current efficiency.

What does it buy?

Knowledge of whether the specification can be produced elsewhere.

A relationship.

A qualified process.

A source of comparison.

Potential capacity if the first supplier fails.

The option can still be illusory.

If the second supplier receives so little business that it exits the market or never achieves quality, the organization paid for the appearance of redundancy rather than the capability.

Again, an option has to remain exercisable.

This is why a small write-down needs a maintenance rule.

How much volume keeps the supplier viable?

How often must the backup process be tested?

How much staff rotation preserves knowledge?

How long can a dormant system remain credible?

The organization is buying future response, not a comforting diagram.

The same principle applies to people.

Chapter 7 described expertise as a form of accumulated commitment. A small write-down can create career options before the old expertise collapses in value.

Move an experienced employee into a new team for six months.

Train a small group in the emerging tool.

Promote someone whose value comes from translating between old and new systems.

Give a senior expert responsibility for an adjacent problem rather than waiting until their current role disappears.

These moves have a current cost.

The expert is removed from work where they are highly productive.

A less experienced person may backfill.

The new team spends time teaching domain context.

The experiment can fail.

That is the write-down.

The alternative is to wait until the old role loses value and then ask the person to transform under pressure.

The transition cost is larger because time has disappeared.

Small write-downs create time.

This is their most underappreciated asset.

Money can sometimes be found quickly.

Competence cannot.

Customer trust cannot.

Regulatory approval cannot.

Supplier learning cannot.

Organizational legitimacy cannot.

A modest investment years early can buy a learning curve that no emergency budget can compress later.

This is why timing matters more than amount in some adaptation investments.

The institution does not need to know the future with certainty.

It needs to know which futures would be expensive to enter late.

Those are the futures worth buying small options on.

A government can do the same thing.

Pilot a new administrative process in one region.

Allow a regulatory sandbox for a bounded class of activity.

Fund demonstration projects before choosing a universal standard.

Maintain a reserve of expertise in an emerging domain.

Build interoperable data systems before a crisis requires agencies to share information.

Create temporary authority with explicit review rather than locking a new rule in permanently.

These approaches can become excuses for endless pilots and special treatment.

That is the counterargument.

“Experimentation” has political appeal because it postpones the moment someone has to choose.

A pilot can remain small precisely because scaling would create losers.

An innovation unit can become a place where new ideas are contained rather than adopted.

A sandbox can protect firms that would fail under ordinary rules.

An experimental metric can coexist with the old one forever.

Small write-downs reduce risk only if evidence can change allocation.

The organization has to be willing to exercise or abandon the option.

This is where many corporate innovation programs fail.

They create the appearance of exploration without changing capital allocation.

Hackathons.

Labs.

Accelerators.

Partnership announcements.

Pilot programs.

The activities can be valuable.

The question is whether success has a route into the operating system.

Who can scale the project?

Which budget grows?

Which incumbent process changes?

Which customer can migrate?

Which leader owns integration?

What metric determines whether the experiment deserves more capital?

Without those answers, the innovation unit may protect the core from adaptation rather than prepare it.

The organization can say, truthfully, that it is exploring.

The core does not have to write anything down.

The small bet becomes a cultural deductible the incumbent pays to avoid larger change.

This is why the language of options should not become a celebration of tiny bets.

An option has two important dates.

The date it is created.

The date it must be exercised or abandoned.

Organizational options often omit the second.

The result is a portfolio of experiments nobody wants to kill because each represents possibility.

Possibility accumulates maintenance cost too.

Old prototypes need support.

Small teams need budgets.

Partnerships need management.

Pilot customers expect continuity.

Data systems remain half integrated.

The organization can become cluttered with futures it never chose.

Adaptation debt is not reduced by preserving every option.

That would be another form of commitment overload.

The purpose of a small write-down is to make later commitment better informed.

This suggests a simple test.

What uncertainty is this expense intended to resolve?

If the answer is vague, the experiment may be theater.

Can customers be acquired through this channel?

Can this process meet the quality threshold?

Can employees learn the new skill in the required time?

Can this supplier produce to specification?

Can the new system carry peak load?

Can this business model produce acceptable retention?

Can a safety control be automated without increasing risk?

Can a customer migrate without losing a critical workflow?

These are option questions because the answer changes whether a larger irreversible investment should be made.

Experiments are most valuable when they attack uncertainty tied to irreversibility.

A company does not need a pilot to discover facts that do not alter the decision.

This is another reason small write-downs are different from general innovation spending.

They are payments against a specific future switching cost.

The organization can also make small write-downs to the old system directly.

Stop adding new features to a legacy product.

Reduce the number of exceptions supported.

Move new customers to the new contract while honoring old contracts.

Shrink the footprint of an old facility instead of closing it.

Remove one approval layer.

Retire one metric.

Move one class of workload.

Stop promoting one role as the only path to seniority.

These changes test whether the old commitment is as essential as assumed.

Sometimes the organization learns that it is.

A supposedly obsolete approval catches real problems.

A legacy feature has more users than telemetry showed.

A specialist role contains tacit knowledge no documentation replaced.

A second supplier performs badly.

The write-down reveals value in the old system.

That is not failure.

The experiment earned its cost by preventing a larger bad decision.

This is why adaptation has to remain falsifiable.

If every experiment is interpreted as evidence for the new strategy, the organization is not learning.

It is campaigning.

Small write-downs should be allowed to strengthen the case for continuity.

This is uncomfortable for transformation leaders because their status can become tied to the direction of change.

A pilot showing the old process still works better may threaten the program.

Then the new system acquires the same identity problem as the old one.

Evidence becomes political.

The disciplined organization protects the option to discover that the option is not worth exercising.

There is a personal version of this.

A professional who suspects their field is changing does not have to resign and start over.

They can write down a small amount of current productivity to learn something adjacent.

Take a project outside the usual specialty.

Use the new tool on real work.

Teach a colleague and notice which parts cannot be explained.

Spend time with customers whose needs do not fit the current model.

The cost is immediate.

Less output in the domain where competence is highest.

The benefit is option value.

The person learns whether a new path is real before the old path closes.

This is why highly optimized careers can be fragile.

Every hour is allocated to the activity with the highest current return.

There is no room to build a capability whose return belongs to a different future.

The same is true of firms.

A business run entirely for current return can be financially efficient and strategically illiquid.

It has assets.

It lacks options.

The balance sheet does not show the missing options because they were never purchased.

Their absence appears only when the environment changes.

Then the organization discovers that every alternative requires a cold start.

That is expensive.

Small write-downs are a way to avoid cold starts.

They create warm paths into uncertain futures.

A trained team.

A qualified supplier.

A migrated customer cohort.

A working prototype.

A new metric with history.

A piece of modular infrastructure.

A relationship with a regulator.

A documented deprecation path.

An experiment that established where the economics break.

Each is modest compared with a transformation.

Together they change what the organization can do when the need becomes obvious.

This is where adaptation begins to look less like prophecy.

The organization does not have to predict which future will win.

It has to identify which plausible futures would be catastrophic to enter from zero.

Then it can buy enough capability to avoid zero.

That still costs money.

There is no free flexibility.

An option is valuable because somebody paid the premium.

Organizations often want option value without option cost.

They want a second supplier at the first supplier's price.

They want employees ready for new work without removing them from old work.

They want a new business with the old business's margin.

They want customer migration without supporting overlap.

They want experimentation without failed experiments.

They want modularity without interface overhead.

They want slack without lower utilization.

These combinations are usually fantasies.

Flexibility has a carrying cost.

The question is whether the expected cost of being trapped exceeds it.

Chapter 19 will eventually try to put that intuition into an adaptation balance sheet.

For now, the operating principle is enough.

Do not wait until the debt demands a restructuring.

Make smaller payments while the old system is still healthy enough to finance them.

The irony is that success creates the best moment to do this.

Cash is available.

Talent is available.

Customers are still loyal.

The old system still works.

The organization feels the least urgency.

That is exactly when small write-downs are easiest to afford.

Crisis creates urgency after it removes the cheap options.

By then, every write-down is large.
