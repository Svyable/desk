# Chapter 5 — The Best-Kept Secret in Endocrinology

This chapter marks the point in this book's account where the story's two halves begin to diverge in pace and character, a shift worth flagging explicitly. The first four chapters moved across decades — a century of Danish corporate history, ninety years of incretin physiology, two decades of fatty-acid-acylation chemistry — each measured in the patient, institutional timescale of basic science and pharmaceutical development this book has already described at length. From this chapter forward, the story compresses dramatically: the events chapters five through twenty describe unfold almost entirely within a single decade, and the most consequential of them, as later chapters will show, within a span of two or three years. That acceleration is itself part of this book's argument, previewed in chapter three's closing pages — a slow scientific foundation, once it finally reached commercial and cultural critical mass, produced changes to industries, markets, and public health policy at a pace the underlying research itself never approached.

For roughly three years after Ozempic's December 2017 approval, semaglutide remained, in the fullest sense, a diabetes drug. It was marketed to endocrinologists and primary-care physicians managing type 2 diabetes. Its television and print advertising, when the drug's sales eventually justified a national campaign, centered on blood-sugar control, built around an earworm jingle that would later become an odd, semi-ironic cultural touchstone in a context the ad's creators could not have anticipated. Insurance coverage followed the diabetes indication: a patient without a diabetes diagnosis had, in the drug's earliest years, essentially no straightforward path to insurance-covered Ozempic, because insurers cover medications according to their FDA-approved indications, and weight loss was not yet one of them.

Inside endocrinology and obesity-medicine circles, though, something different was happening, and it was happening well before most of the wider public had ever heard the drug's name. Physicians who treated both diabetes and obesity — often the same patient population, since the two conditions frequently coexist — were seeing the same pattern in the clinic that Novo Nordisk's own trial data had already shown: patients on Ozempic were losing more weight, and more consistently, than patients on essentially any other medication these physicians had access to. Some of those physicians began prescribing Ozempic off-label specifically for weight management in patients who did not have diabetes, a legal and reasonably common practice in American medicine, in which a physician prescribes an FDA-approved drug for a use the FDA has not specifically approved, based on their own clinical judgment and the physician's assessment of the available evidence. Off-label prescribing does not require a new approval; it does typically mean the patient's insurance is far less likely to cover the cost, since insurers generally tie coverage decisions to a drug's approved indications rather than to the full range of uses physicians might reasonably judge appropriate.

Word moved through the small, professionally networked world of obesity medicine and endocrinology the way genuinely useful clinical information often does: at conferences, in hallway conversations between physicians who had trained together or who ran into each other repeatedly at the same professional society meetings, in the kind of low-visibility clinical grapevine that rarely makes headlines but that shapes prescribing patterns well before any of it becomes a broader cultural phenomenon. By 2020 and into 2021, before Wegovy's formal approval, a meaningful population of patients — some with diabetes using Ozempic as intended and noticing the weight loss as an appreciated side effect, some without diabetes obtaining Ozempic off-label specifically for weight management — were already experiencing what would soon become a much more widely reported phenomenon: substantial, and for many of them unprecedented, weight loss on a once-weekly injection that most of their friends, family, and even many of their own primary-care physicians had never heard of.

Novo Nordisk, meanwhile, was running the trial program that would formalize what the off-label prescribing community was already discovering informally. The company's dedicated obesity trial program for semaglutide, using a higher dose than the diabetes formulation — the same escalation pattern the company had already used once before, with liraglutide's diabetes dose becoming Saxenda's higher obesity dose — was called the STEP program, an acronym for Semaglutide Treatment Effect in People with obesity, and it enrolled thousands of participants across multiple individual trials, each designed to test the drug's effect in a somewhat different population or clinical context. The first and most consequential of these trials, STEP 1, enrolled adults with obesity or overweight, without diabetes, and randomized them to receive either once-weekly semaglutide at 2.4 milligrams — a considerably higher dose than the diabetes formulation's typical maintenance dose — or placebo, alongside a lifestyle intervention involving dietary counseling and increased physical activity for both groups.

The STEP 1 results, published in the New England Journal of Medicine in February 2021, were, by the standards of anything the pharmaceutical industry had previously produced for weight management, extraordinary. Participants receiving semaglutide lost an average of 14.9 percent of their body weight over the course of the trial, compared with roughly 2.4 percent in the placebo group — both groups received the same lifestyle counseling, which meant the difference between the two results was, as cleanly as a randomized trial can demonstrate, attributable to the drug itself rather than to diet and exercise advice alone. Eighty-six percent of participants in the semaglutide group achieved at least a five percent reduction in body weight, a threshold generally recognized in obesity medicine as producing meaningful health benefits, and a substantial share achieved considerably more than that. For a field that had spent decades measuring success in low single-digit percentages, and that had watched several previous pharmaceutical attempts at meaningful weight loss either underperform or get pulled from the market for safety reasons, a headline number close to fifteen percent average weight loss looked less like an incremental improvement than like a different category of result entirely.

It is worth situating STEP 1's specific trial design against the broader history of clinical-trial methodology in obesity medicine, because the trial's rigor was itself part of why its results proved so difficult for the field to dismiss the way earlier, less careful obesity-drug studies had sometimes been dismissed. STEP 1 was randomized, double-blind, and placebo-controlled, run across multiple countries and dozens of sites, with both the semaglutide and placebo groups receiving identical lifestyle counseling — a design specifically constructed to isolate the drug's own pharmacological effect from the effect of behavioral counseling alone, a distinction earlier, less rigorously designed obesity studies had sometimes blurred, allowing skeptics to argue that an observed weight-loss effect reflected the accompanying diet and exercise advice rather than the medication itself. STEP 1's clean separation of those two variables, and the size of the gap it revealed between the drug-plus-counseling group and the counseling-alone group, made the semaglutide-specific effect considerably harder for skeptics to explain away as merely a placebo-adjacent byproduct of increased medical attention and behavioral support — a methodological rigor this book's account of the drug's rapid scientific and regulatory acceptance depends on more than casual retellings of the STEP 1 story usually acknowledge.

STEP 1 was not the only trial in the program, and the others matter to this book's later chapters more than a casual retelling of the Wegovy story usually acknowledges. STEP 2 tested semaglutide specifically in people who did have type 2 diabetes alongside obesity, a population in which weight loss is typically harder to achieve because of the metabolic effects of diabetes itself and, in some cases, the weight-promoting effects of other diabetes medications a patient might already be taking; semaglutide still produced clinically meaningful weight loss in this harder population, though somewhat less than in STEP 1's non-diabetic participants, consistent with a broader pattern that would recur throughout this drug class's development in which diabetes itself seems to blunt the maximum achievable weight-loss effect. STEP 3 added a more intensive behavioral counseling program on top of the drug, testing whether combining semaglutide with a more resource-intensive lifestyle intervention than STEP 1's standard counseling would push the results even higher; it did, modestly, though the marginal benefit of the added counseling was smaller than the marginal benefit of the drug itself, an early data point in what would become a recurring theme in later public debate over how much of this story is about pharmacology and how much is about the surrounding behavioral-support infrastructure a patient has access to. STEP 4 used a withdrawal design specifically built to test what happens when patients who have already lost significant weight on semaglutide stop taking it partway through the trial — a study this book examines in close detail in a later chapter, because its results would turn out to matter enormously for how physicians, insurers, and patients eventually came to understand whether this class of drug was a cure or a chronic, ongoing treatment. And STEP 5 extended the trial period out to two full years, the longest follow-up in the initial program, providing the first solid evidence that the weight loss achieved in the shorter trials could be sustained, not merely achieved and then gradually lost, provided the patient remained on treatment.

Taken together, the STEP program represented one of the largest, most rigorously designed clinical-trial efforts ever mounted for an obesity drug, enrolling several thousand participants across multiple countries and multiple patient populations, and it is worth situating that effort against the discouraging history of pharmaceutical obesity treatment that preceded it. Orlistat, an older weight-loss drug that blocks a portion of dietary fat from being absorbed in the gut, produced average weight loss in the low single digits and came with gastrointestinal side effects — including, notoriously, oily stool leakage in some patients — severe enough to limit its use considerably. Phentermine, a stimulant-based appetite suppressant approved decades earlier and still prescribed today, typically produces modest short-term weight loss and is generally recommended only for short courses because of concerns about cardiovascular effects and dependence potential. The fen-phen combination that briefly seemed to outperform both, before its fenfluramine component was withdrawn in 1997 after being linked to heart valve damage, remains one of the clearest cautionary tales in the field, a reminder that a drug's efficacy on a bathroom scale says nothing on its own about whether it is safe to take for years. Against that backdrop — modest results, real safety scares, an entire subfield of medicine that had grown cautious and a little bit embarrassed after decades of therapies that either did not work well enough or turned out not to be safe enough — a randomized trial showing close to fifteen percent average weight loss, with a safety profile good enough to clear FDA review, was not simply an incremental data point added to a familiar chart. It represented, for many physicians who had spent their careers managing patient expectations downward, a genuine break from everything the field had previously been able to offer.

On June 4, 2021, the FDA approved semaglutide 2.4 milligrams for chronic weight management under a new brand name, Wegovy — the same active ingredient as Ozempic, at a higher maximum dose, marketed and priced separately, approved specifically for adults with obesity, defined by a body mass index of 30 or above, or with a BMI of 27 or above alongside at least one weight-related health condition such as hypertension or type 2 diabetes. It was, per the FDA's own public statement at the time, the first new drug approved for chronic weight management since 2014, and the first to demonstrate this magnitude of effect in a large, well-controlled trial.

The initial public reception, in June and July of 2021, was notable mainly for how comparatively muted it was relative to what would follow within about a year. Health and business journalists covered the approval as a significant medical story — coverage in outlets like the New York Times, the Washington Post, and major medical trade publications treated STEP 1's results as genuinely newsworthy, and some early coverage speculated, correctly as it turned out, that the drug could reshape how obesity was treated. But Wegovy in the summer of 2021 was not yet the subject of celebrity gossip columns, TikTok trends, or Saturday Night Live sketches. It was, for its first year on the market, closer to what this chapter's title describes: the best-kept secret in endocrinology, known and increasingly prescribed within the medical community and among an early population of patients willing to pay out of pocket or navigate insurance hurdles for access, but not yet a subject of mainstream cultural conversation.

What made the early patient experience distinctive, and what would eventually give this entire drug class its own informal vocabulary, was not simply the number on the scale but a change in subjective experience that many patients struggled to describe to physicians who had not yet heard other patients describe it the same way. Patients used words like quiet, or relief, or, eventually, the phrase that would become the closest thing this drug class has to a signature concept: food noise gone silent. What they were describing was not a loss of appetite in the sense of feeling ill or averse to food, but the disappearance of a background mental process many of them had lived with for years or decades without fully recognizing it as separate from ordinary hunger — a near-constant, low-grade preoccupation with food: what to eat next, what was in the refrigerator, whether to have a second helping, whether the vending machine down the hall was worth the walk. For patients who had spent years attributing that preoccupation to a personal failure of discipline, its sudden absence on the drug was, by many later accounts collected in subsequent journalism and in clinical literature on patient experience, less like taking a medication and more like discovering, for the first time in memory, what eating without a constant internal argument actually felt like. This was, notably, not something the STEP trials had been specifically designed to measure using a validated psychological instrument in their earliest iterations; the phenomenon showed up first in patient reports and physician observation, ahead of the formal research literature that would later try to characterize and quantify it more rigorously.

Part of the reason for that initial quiet was mundane and structural rather than medical: manufacturing capacity. Novo Nordisk had built its production infrastructure for Ozempic around demand projections calibrated to a diabetes drug's typical market — a large market, but a far smaller one than the market that would materialize once Wegovy's results became widely known and once a broader population without diabetes began seeking access to semaglutide by any available route, including off-label Ozempic prescriptions when Wegovy itself was hard to obtain. Producing GLP-1 drugs at scale is not a simple manufacturing problem; it involves complex peptide synthesis processes, specialized fill-finish facilities capable of producing pre-filled injector pens to exacting sterility and dosing-accuracy standards, and supply chains for specialized raw materials that cannot be scaled up overnight the way, for instance, a generic tablet manufacturing line often can. Novo Nordisk's manufacturing footprint in 2021, sized for the pre-Wegovy era, was simply not built for what was about to happen to demand.

The insurance landscape in Wegovy's first year on the market compounded the sense, among the patients who did know about it, that this was a kind of insider's medicine rather than a mainstream option. Most private insurance plans in the United States, at the time, explicitly excluded coverage for anti-obesity medications as a category, a legacy of decades in which weight-loss drugs were widely viewed by insurers and by employers designing benefit plans as lifestyle or cosmetic interventions rather than treatments for a chronic disease — a framing this book's later chapter on drug pricing and coverage will examine in detail, because it did not change quickly or evenly even after Wegovy's clinical results made the disease-versus-lifestyle distinction considerably harder to defend. A patient in 2021 or early 2022 who wanted Wegovy and whose insurance excluded anti-obesity coverage faced a monthly list price in the neighborhood of $1,300, an amount well beyond what most households could sustain indefinitely out of pocket, which meant that early Wegovy adoption skewed heavily toward patients with unusually generous insurance coverage, the financial means to pay out of pocket, or physicians willing to prescribe the cheaper, diabetes-labeled Ozempic off-label instead — itself not free of insurance friction, since insurers reviewing an Ozempic prescription for a patient with no diabetes diagnosis on file could and sometimes did deny the claim.

This asymmetry — a genuinely effective new therapy, priced and covered in a way that made it accessible mainly to a narrow, relatively privileged slice of the population that most needed to hear about it, word of mouth, in its first eighteen months on the market — is a useful thing to hold in mind heading into the next chapter, because it shaped who was actually taking these drugs at the exact moment they were about to become a subject of mass cultural fascination. It was not, in late 2021 and early 2022, primarily patients referred by their endocrinologist through a straightforward insurance-covered pathway. It was disproportionately patients with the financial flexibility to pay list price, navigate off-label workarounds, or otherwise route around a coverage system not yet built to treat obesity as the chronic disease the clinical evidence increasingly said it was — a population that happened to overlap considerably with exactly the demographic most likely to have a very large, very engaged social-media following.

What was about to happen to demand had less to do with STEP 1's published trial results — impressive as they were to physicians and to readers of medical journals — than with a single social-media post from one of the most-followed individuals on the planet, made a little over a year after Wegovy's approval, that would do more to introduce this drug class to a mass audience than any clinical trial publication, FDA press release, or television advertisement ever could. Novo Nordisk's own manufacturing planners, weighing demand forecasts built on diabetes-market data and a still-narrow, insurance-constrained early Wegovy rollout, had no strong internal signal in 2021 that they were about to need to more than double, and eventually multiply many times over, their global production capacity for injector pens within a span of roughly eighteen months. That gap between what the manufacturing organization had built and what the market was about to demand of it is the single structural fact that would define this drug's next several years more than any other, shaping which patients could get the medicine, which could not, and what an entire gray market of unregulated alternatives would eventually spring up to fill. That post, and the celebrity-driven cultural explosion it helped trigger, is where the next chapter begins — and it is also, not coincidentally, roughly the moment at which this story stops being primarily a medical narrative and starts becoming, in addition, a story about scarcity, celebrity, social media, and the particular way American culture processes a genuinely new medical technology once it becomes a subject of gossip rather than only a subject of clinical practice.
