# Chapter 3 — The Ninety-Year Argument Over Appetite

This chapter's account of a ninety-year scientific run-up is, in one sense, a story about the ordinary, unglamorous pace of basic physiological research — the kind of steady, incremental accumulation of evidence that rarely produces a single dramatic headline moment, unfolding instead across generations of researchers, most of whom worked without any expectation that their specific contribution would eventually matter to a multi-billion-dollar drug class or a Senate hearing room. It is worth naming that pace explicitly at this chapter's outset, because the remainder of this book, dealing with a drug class that reshaped global culture within a span of roughly three years following Wegovy's 2021 approval, risks leaving readers with a distorted sense of how medical science ordinarily proceeds. The compressed, headline-driven timeline this book's later chapters describe is the exception in the history of medicine, not the rule; the patient, decades-long accumulation of physiological evidence this chapter traces is closer to how most durable medical understanding actually gets built.

In 1902, two English physiologists named William Bayliss and Ernest Starling ran an experiment that is now considered one of the founding moments of endocrinology, the branch of medicine concerned with hormones. They took an acid extract from the lining of a dog's duodenum — the first section of small intestine, just past the stomach — and injected it into the animal's bloodstream. To their surprise, the dog's pancreas began producing digestive juices almost immediately, even though the pancreas itself had no direct nerve connection to the site of the injection. Something in the gut extract was traveling through the blood and telling a distant organ what to do. Bayliss and Starling called the substance secretin, and in describing how it worked, Starling coined a word that would come to define an entire field: hormone, from the Greek for "to set in motion."

The discovery mattered immediately for digestive physiology. Its slower-burning implication took decades to fully land: the gut was not merely a passive tube that processed food and passed along nutrients. It was an active, chemically communicative organ, capable of sending signals to the rest of the body — signals that, it would eventually become clear, included instructions about how much more food the body should want.

That implication moved forward in fits and starts for the next thirty years. The gap of exactly thirty years separating Bayliss and Starling's 1902 experiment from La Barre's 1932 coinage is itself worth a moment's attention, since it illustrates how scientific concepts often require a specific vocabulary to become fully usable by a field, not merely a specific experimental result. Bayliss and Starling had already established, in principle, that a gut-derived substance could act at a distance on another organ; what La Barre's 1932 work added was not a wholly new discovery so much as a name and a more specific, testable hypothesis — that such substances might specifically govern the pancreas's own insulin response — that gave subsequent researchers, including the ones whose 1960s glucose experiments this chapter describes next, a clearer target to test against. In 1932, a Belgian physiologist named Jean La Barre, building on the secretin work and studying digestion in his own laboratory, coined a related term: incretin, from a substance he had extracted from the upper gut lining that, when injected into rabbits and dogs, lowered their blood glucose. La Barre's incretin concept proposed something specific and testable: that the gut, upon sensing food, released factors into the bloodstream that stimulated the pancreas to release insulin — a second signaling pathway, distinct from the direct effect of rising blood glucose itself, that helped the body anticipate and manage a meal's effects before blood sugar had even fully risen.

It took another three decades for anyone to prove La Barre's idea rigorously. The proof came in the 1960s, through an experiment that is elegant in its simplicity: researchers gave human subjects an identical dose of glucose two different ways, once by mouth and once by direct injection into a vein, and then measured how much insulin the pancreas released in each case. If insulin release depended purely on blood glucose levels, the two methods should have produced identical insulin responses, since both delivered the same quantity of glucose into the bloodstream. They did not. Oral glucose triggered substantially more insulin release than the identical dose delivered intravenously. Something about eating and digesting — as opposed to simply having glucose appear in the blood — was itself stimulating extra insulin production. This gap, formally named the incretin effect, was the rigorous experimental confirmation of what La Barre had proposed thirty years earlier: the gut was doing more than digesting. It was actively managing the pancreatic response to a meal, using chemical signals researchers had not yet fully identified.

Identifying those signals took another two decades of increasingly precise biochemistry. Researchers isolated a hormone called glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide, or GIP, in 1970, and confirmed it as one incretin. But GIP alone could not account for the full magnitude of the incretin effect measured in human subjects, which meant at least one more unidentified factor had to be involved. Through the early-to-mid 1980s, several research groups working somewhat independently — reading each other's papers, competing for priority, converging from different angles on the same underlying molecule — identified and characterized glucagon-like peptide-1, GLP-1, as the second, and in many ways more physiologically potent, incretin hormone. By 1987, the core biology was essentially mapped: after a meal, cells in the intestine release both GIP and GLP-1, both hormones travel to the pancreas and stimulate additional, glucose-dependent insulin release, and GLP-1 in particular does several other things as well — slowing gastric emptying, suppressing the release of glucagon (a hormone that raises blood sugar), and acting on brain regions involved in satiety.

It is worth dwelling on how long this took, because the pace of discovery here says something important about how appetite science developed relative to other areas of medicine. Insulin itself, once Banting, Best, and their collaborators found the right extraction technique, went from laboratory discovery to human treatment in a matter of months in 1921 and 1922. The incretin story, by contrast, unfolded across nine decades: from Bayliss and Starling's 1902 secretin experiment, through La Barre's 1932 coinage, through the 1960s proof of the incretin effect, through the 1970 and 1980s identification of GIP and GLP-1, and finally, in the 1990s and 2000s, into the drug-development story this book has already begun to tell. Nothing about appetite regulation turned out to be simple, and nothing about it yielded to a single decisive experiment the way insulin's basic mechanism did.

Part of the reason is that appetite is not regulated by a single hormone, or even a single organ system, in the way that blood-glucose regulation is comparatively centralized around the pancreas. It is regulated by a genuinely distributed network: the stomach signals fullness through mechanical stretch receptors and through hormones like ghrelin, which rises before meals and is sometimes described, loosely, as a hunger hormone; the intestine signals both nutrient content and volume through GLP-1, GIP, peptide YY, and cholecystokinin, among others; fat tissue itself releases a hormone called leptin that signals the brain about the body's overall energy reserves, operating on a timescale of days and weeks rather than the meal-to-meal timescale of gut hormones; and the brain integrates all of these signals, alongside sensory information, memory, stress, sleep, and learned associations with specific foods, in regions including the hypothalamus and the brainstem, before producing the subjective experience a person actually notices: hunger, fullness, cravings, or what patients on GLP-1 drugs would later start calling food noise.

It is useful to compare this distributed appetite system to something engineers would recognize: a control system with multiple redundant sensors and multiple feedback loops operating on different timescales, rather than a single thermostat with a single setpoint. Ghrelin and stomach stretch provide fast, meal-to-meal signals. GLP-1, GIP, peptide YY, and cholecystokinin provide slightly slower, digestion-linked signals that shape how satisfied a person feels in the hour or two after eating. Leptin provides a slow, weeks-long signal about overall fat reserves, functioning less like a meal-by-meal appetite switch and more like a thermostat monitoring the building's overall energy reserves rather than the temperature of any single room. When researchers in the 1990s began studying leptin specifically, there was a brief wave of hope that it alone might explain obesity and provide a clean pharmaceutical target — mice bred to lack a functioning leptin gene became dramatically obese, and injecting leptin into those specific mice reversed the effect almost completely. But most obese humans, it turned out, already have high circulating leptin levels; their problem is not a leptin deficiency but a resistance to leptin's signal, a different and more stubborn problem that leptin injections alone could not fix. The lesson generalized: appetite regulation had no single point of failure waiting to be corrected with one hormone, which is part of why it took so much longer to yield a workable drug than diabetes, where a single missing hormone really was, for a large share of patients, close to the entire story.

This distributed complexity had a real cultural cost, and it is one of the more important, least discussed threads running through the entire twentieth-century history of obesity treatment. Because no single, easily measurable hormone deficiency explained why some people gained substantially more weight than others under similar circumstances — no equivalent of the insulin deficiency that so clearly and mechanically explained type 1 diabetes — medicine and the broader culture around it filled the explanatory gap with a moral and psychological framework instead of a biological one. If the mechanism could not be cleanly identified, the difference between a thin person and a fat person was, in the absence of a better explanation, attributed to differences in willpower, discipline, character, or moral seriousness about self-control. This was not a fringe view confined to unsympathetic commentators; it was, for most of the twentieth century, close to the medical and public-health consensus, embedded in weight-loss advice, insurance underwriting, workplace culture, and ordinary conversation.

The consensus was not entirely wrong to notice that behavior — what and how much a person eats, how physically active they are — determines body weight in an immediate, mechanical sense; calories consumed relative to calories expended remains true as a description of energy balance, in the same limited way that "a fever is caused by an elevated body temperature" is true as a description of a fever. What the consensus missed, because the underlying biology had not yet been mapped and would not be fully mapped until the incretin research of the late twentieth century matured into a drug class, is that the signals governing how much a person wants to eat, how satisfied they feel after eating, and how insistently their brain returns to thoughts of food between meals are themselves biologically variable — set differently in different people by genetics, by prior dieting history, by gut hormone sensitivity, and by other factors substantially outside conscious control. A person whose GLP-1 signaling produces a weaker or shorter-lived satiety response than another person's is not exhibiting less willpower at the dinner table. They are experiencing a different, measurably different, physiological hunger signal, and asking them to override that signal through willpower alone is asking them to fight a battle with worse equipment than the person sitting across the table from them, meal after meal, for years.

The main clinical yardstick used to define obesity throughout this period, body mass index, is itself a reminder of how much of the field's apparent precision was borrowed from tools never designed for the job. BMI derives from a formula devised in the 1830s by the Belgian statistician Adolphe Quetelet, who was studying the distribution of physical traits across large populations for purposes of social statistics, not designing a diagnostic tool for individual patients. Quetelet's index was adopted by insurers and later by public-health researchers in the twentieth century largely because it was simple to calculate from height and weight alone, not because clinical research had established it as the ideal measure of an individual's metabolic health — it does not distinguish muscle mass from fat mass, does not account for where on the body fat is distributed, a factor that matters considerably for cardiovascular risk, and was standardized using population data that, for much of its history, badly underrepresented the ethnic and body-type diversity of the people it was eventually applied to worldwide. None of this makes BMI useless; it correlates with health risk closely enough across large populations to remain a serviceable screening tool, which is why the clinical trials described throughout this book still use it to define enrollment criteria. But its origin as a population-statistics convenience, rather than an individually validated diagnostic measure, is one more piece of the larger pattern this chapter has traced: the tools available for thinking about body weight, for most of the twentieth century, were considerably cruder than the moral certainty with which they were applied.

This is not a retroactive excuse invented to flatter patients on modern weight-loss drugs; it is a direct, well-supported implication of the incretin research this chapter has traced from 1902 onward, and it is worth stating plainly because so much of the cultural reaction to GLP-1 drugs, described in later chapters, replayed exactly the willpower-versus-biology argument that appetite science had already been quietly resolving for decades before Ozempic became a household name. When a drug that simply amplifies and extends a naturally occurring gut hormone signal produces, reliably and reproducibly across tens of thousands of clinical trial participants, the kind of appetite reduction and sustained weight loss that decades of willpower-based interventions almost never achieved and almost never sustained, the plainest reading of that result is not that the drug found some kind of shortcut or cheat. It is that the drug is correcting, or at least substantially compensating for, a real biological variable that behavioral advice alone was never actually equipped to address, because behavioral advice was never really working against a level playing field of identical underlying appetite signaling to begin with.

None of this means environment and behavior are irrelevant — later chapters on the food industry and on public-health policy will return to how much of the modern obesity increase is driven by changes in the food environment itself, not by any change in human biology over the same period. But it does mean that the ninety-year argument this chapter has traced, from Bayliss and Starling's dog experiment through the identification of GLP-1, was never really an argument about digestion in the narrow sense. It was, all along, a slow, technical, unglamorous run-up to a much larger argument about why some bodies want more food than others, and what, if anything, could be done about it that did not simply amount to asking people to want less.

One strand of this research deserves particular attention because it anticipates, decades early, the discontinuation-and-regain pattern this book examines directly in a later chapter on stopping GLP-1 treatment. Physiologists studying weight loss through calorie restriction alone had long observed what came to be called a body-weight set point: when a person loses weight through dieting, the body does not passively accept the new, lower weight as a fresh baseline. Instead, a cluster of compensatory mechanisms activates — resting metabolic rate drops by more than body-composition changes alone would predict, hunger hormones like ghrelin rise, satiety signals including GLP-1 itself become blunted, and the drive to eat intensifies in ways that go well beyond ordinary hunger. Researchers studying formerly obese individuals years after successful diet-based weight loss have repeatedly found this altered hormonal profile persisting long after the weight loss itself, which helps explain a pattern familiar to anyone who has tried and later abandoned a serious diet: the weight does not simply drift back through gradually resumed old habits. The body appears to be actively, biochemically working to restore it, using several of the same hormonal pathways this chapter has already described. This finding, replicated across multiple research groups from the 1990s onward, was one of the clearest pieces of evidence that obesity functions, physiologically, more like a chronic condition with a defended baseline than like a simple deficit that closes permanently once corrected — a reframing that would turn out to matter enormously once GLP-1 drugs arrived and patients, along with the physicians prescribing them, had to decide whether treatment was meant to be temporary or ongoing.

There is a further wrinkle worth naming here, because it will recur throughout this book: the discovery that a strong biological driver of appetite exists does not, by itself, settle every question about how much weight any individual should lose, at what cost, or by what method. The incretin research described in this chapter explains why appetite is not simply a matter of willpower for most people most of the time; it does not answer harder, more contested questions about optimal body weight across a genetically diverse population, about the health risks of weight cycling compared with stable higher weight, or about how much of the modern rise in obesity reflects biology at all as opposed to a food environment that changed faster than human appetite-regulation systems, evolved over a very different nutritional landscape, could adapt to. Those questions do not disappear once a drug exists that can reliably override strong hunger signals. They become, if anything, more urgent, because for the first time in medical history there is a widely available tool capable of substantially resetting the appetite side of the equation — which means society now has to decide, with more clarity than before, what it actually wants that tool used for, on whom, and under what constraints. This book will keep returning to that question because the science in this chapter, however well established, does not answer it on its own.

By the late 1980s, the incretin biology described above was mapped clearly enough that a handful of scientists in Copenhagen and elsewhere could start asking a more practical question: if GLP-1 really did everything the incretin research suggested it did, could anyone actually turn it into a medicine? The physiology was, by then, reasonably well understood, documented across a body of published research spanning three continents and the better part of a century, from Bayliss and Starling's dog experiment to the molecular cloning papers of the mid-1980s. The chemistry required to make it useful — the same half-life problem this book has already introduced through exendin-4 and Byetta, but now facing a Danish research team determined to solve it with a synthetic, patentable, once-weekly molecule of their own rather than a naturally occurring lizard peptide — was not yet solved at all. That gap — between knowing what a hormone does and being able to deliver a durable, safe, manufacturable version of it to a patient — is where the next chapter picks up, inside Novo Nordisk's own laboratories, with a scientist named Lotte Bjerre Knudsen and a research program that would take the better part of two decades to produce a drug anyone could actually prescribe.
