# Chapter 16 — The Industries That Trembled

It is worth stating clearly, before turning to the specific industry-by-industry evidence this chapter assembles, why a single molecule's population-scale adoption could plausibly move so many unrelated sectors' financial results at once. Consumer spending on food, beverages, and related discretionary categories represents one of the largest and most stable components of household budgets across the entire developed world, and appetite itself, this book's third chapter established, sits upstream of an enormous share of that spending — meaning any intervention capable of meaningfully and durably altering how much food a large share of the population wants to buy and consume was always going to ripple outward into whatever industries depend on that consumption remaining at its previous, pre-intervention scale. What makes this chapter's account genuinely remarkable is not that such ripple effects existed in principle, which follows almost automatically from the biology this book has described since its opening chapters, but how quickly, how measurably, and how far beyond the most obviously connected industries — snack food, restaurants — those ripples actually traveled in practice.

This book's prologue opened with a single earnings call in October 2023, when Walmart's U.S. chief executive, John Furner, mentioned that the company had noticed a "slight pullback in overall basket" among customers believed to be using GLP-1 medications — fewer units, he said, slightly fewer calories, phrasing that read, on the page, like the most cautious and hedged comment a retail executive could possibly make about a still-uncertain trend. Markets did not treat it as cautious. Within roughly a day of Furner's remarks becoming public, shares in PepsiCo, Coca-Cola, and Mondelez all fell, alongside declines in Walmart's own stock and in shares of Costco and McDonald's — a synchronized selloff across an entire cluster of food, beverage, and restaurant companies triggered not by any of those companies' own earnings or guidance, but by a single retail executive's offhand, heavily qualified observation about a class of drugs none of the affected companies manufactured or sold.

It is worth acknowledging directly that not every industry-level effect this chapter documents can be attributed to GLP-1 adoption with full confidence, a caveat this chapter has tried to apply consistently rather than treating every plausible-sounding correlation as settled causation. Consumer spending patterns, alcohol consumption trends, and packaged-food demand all move in response to many simultaneous factors — inflation, interest rates, generational shifts in taste, and broader wellness trends unrelated to any specific medication — and disentangling the GLP-1-specific component of any given industry's shift from these other simultaneous forces is genuinely difficult, a difficulty the more careful analysts and researchers cited throughout this chapter generally acknowledged directly rather than claiming false precision. This chapter's account should be read as documenting a real, evidenced, and industry-acknowledged effect operating alongside other contributing forces, not as attributing every consumer-spending shift of the mid-2020s to a single molecule's influence.

That overreaction, if it was one, turned out to be directionally correct even if its immediate magnitude reflected as much market anxiety as settled evidence. Subsequent, more rigorous research bore out the underlying pattern Furner had only anecdotally observed. A study conducted jointly by researchers at Cornell University and the market-research firm Numerator, examining actual household purchasing data rather than a single executive's impression, found that households with at least one GLP-1 user cut their overall grocery spending by roughly 6 percent within six months of starting the medication — a meaningful, measurable reduction in food purchasing behavior at the household level, not merely at the level of the individual patient's own direct consumption, since GLP-1 users typically continue purchasing groceries for other household members whose eating habits have not changed. Consulting firm EY-Parthenon, modeling the cumulative effect of this kind of household-level spending reduction across a growing GLP-1-using population, projected that diet changes linked to the drugs' adoption could strip as much as $12 billion from United States snack sales over the following decade — a large number in absolute terms, though one that needs to be read against the scale of the overall American snack-food market, which itself runs into the hundreds of billions of dollars annually, meaning even a $12 billion decade-long reduction represents a meaningful but not existential dent in the category's overall size.

The pressure showed up concretely in packaged-food companies' own operational decisions as the 2020s progressed, not merely in analysts' spreadsheets. By early 2026, reporting on the sector described major packaged-food companies including PepsiCo, Campbell's, and Smucker's announcing plant closures and production-capacity reductions, citing a combination of GLP-1-related demand softening and broader volume weakness in categories including snacks, cereals, and processed convenience foods — the kind of calorie-dense, highly palatable products that food-science and public-health researchers had long identified as both major profit drivers for the packaged-food industry and, not coincidentally, among the categories of food most associated with the "food noise" phenomenon this book examined in chapter six, the compulsive, hard-to-resist craving response that GLP-1 drugs appear to specifically and effectively quiet.

The packaged-food industry's response to softening demand went beyond the plant closures described above, extending into product-development and portfolio strategy in ways that revealed how seriously even the largest food companies had come to take the shift. Several major manufacturers began actively developing and marketing smaller-portion product lines specifically positioned toward GLP-1 users — snack packs and meal options explicitly framed around lower calorie counts, higher protein content, and portion sizes calibrated to the reduced appetite these consumers reported experiencing, an unusual instance of a consumer-goods industry adapting its core product design in direct, explicit response to a change in the underlying biology of its customers' appetite rather than merely to a shift in taste or marketing trend. This adaptation ran in an interesting, almost inverted direction relative to the "shrinkflation" phenomenon that had dominated consumer-packaged-goods discourse for much of the preceding several years, in which manufacturers quietly reduced package sizes while holding prices steady in response to rising input costs, a practice that had drawn considerable consumer and regulatory criticism precisely because it was implemented without being clearly disclosed or marketed as a deliberate product change. Smaller portions designed and marketed explicitly for a GLP-1-adjusted appetite represented, by contrast, an openly acknowledged, deliberately positioned product strategy rather than a quietly implemented cost-cutting measure — the same underlying trend toward smaller servings, but framed transparently as a response to genuine changing consumer need rather than concealed as an unannounced value reduction.

The restaurant industry, a sector even more directly dependent on discretionary, appetite-driven purchasing decisions than packaged groceries, watched the same trend with particular unease, though the picture that emerged through the mid-2020s proved considerably more mixed than the grocery-spending data alone might have predicted. Fast-food and casual-dining chains serving high-calorie, value-oriented menus faced the most direct version of the demand-softening concern investors had priced into stocks like McDonald's in the immediate aftermath of the Walmart comments; chains with menus emphasizing smaller portions, higher protein content, or premium ingredients, better aligned with the eating patterns GLP-1 patients described adopting, in some analysts' assessments benefited relatively, or at least suffered less, from the same underlying shift in consumer appetite. This bifurcation — broad pressure on high-calorie, high-volume dining formats, alongside relative resilience or even opportunity for restaurants and food brands positioned around smaller portions and different nutritional profiles — mirrored, in the consumer-facing restaurant sector, the same kind of uneven, category-specific effects the grocery and packaged-food data had already begun to show.

The alcoholic-beverage industry, previewed briefly in the addiction-medicine research described in the previous chapter, offered one of the clearer examples of a sector visibly reassessing its own long-term prospects in direct response to GLP-1 adoption, even as industry analysts and the companies themselves were careful to note that the drugs were only one contributing factor among several. Diageo, one of the largest spirits companies in the world and owner of brands including Johnnie Walker and Don Julio, saw its stock decline by roughly 30 percent over a single year in the mid-2020s, alongside double-digit percentage declines in U.S. sales at Diageo, Pernod Ricard, and Constellation Brands. Diageo's tequila category was hit particularly hard, with sales of its Don Julio and Casamigos brands falling by roughly 21 and 31 percent respectively in one reporting period, and the company eventually announced a restructuring plan worth more than a billion dollars in response to broader, sustained sales softness across its North American and Asia-Pacific markets. Analysts and at least one prominent investment fund manager who publicly divested his firm's Diageo holdings pointed to GLP-1 adoption as a genuine, if partial, contributing factor to the sector's decline, describing the drinks industry as being in the "early stages" of a demand shift rather than having already absorbed its full effect — a framing that treated the alcohol industry's troubles as multi-causal, intertwined with separate, longer-running generational trends toward reduced drinking among younger consumers and a broader cultural shift toward health-consciousness that predated GLP-1 drugs' mass adoption, rather than attributing the sector's decline to any single cause in isolation.

Healthcare providers and health systems themselves, beyond the specific bariatric-surgery disruption chapter fourteen examined in detail, began adjusting their own longer-range capital and staffing plans in response to the same population-health shift this chapter has documented across other industries. Hospital systems modeling future demand for diabetes-complication treatment, cardiovascular intervention, and orthopedic procedures tied to obesity-related joint strain — all downstream conditions this book's earlier chapters showed GLP-1 drugs measurably reduce — faced their own version of the actuarial uncertainty described later in this chapter for life insurers: genuine, plausible reasons to expect reduced future demand for several historically reliable, financially significant service lines, weighed against the same multi-year uncertainty about how completely and how durably that demand reduction would actually materialize once discontinuation effects, described in chapter eighteen, and uneven insurance access, described in chapter eleven, were factored into any realistic long-range forecast.

Life insurance and reinsurance, an industry whose entire business model depends on actuarially forecasting mortality risk decades into the future, approached the GLP-1 phenomenon from a considerably different angle than the consumer-facing food and restaurant industries, treating it less as an immediate demand shock and more as a slow-moving but potentially enormous long-run recalculation of the mortality tables the entire industry's pricing depends on. Swiss Re, one of the world's largest reinsurers, published research modeling the potential long-run mortality effect of widespread GLP-1 adoption, projecting that these drugs could reduce overall United States mortality rates by as much as 6.4 percent by 2045 if uptake and long-term efficacy held at levels consistent with the trial data described throughout this book — a genuinely enormous population-level effect, if it materializes as modeled, given that mortality-rate improvements of even a fraction of a percentage point ordinarily represent major public-health achievements measured over an entire generation.

Munich Re, another major global reinsurer, published its own analysis focusing less on the aggregate mortality projection and more on the immediate, practical challenge the drugs posed to underwriters trying to price individual life-insurance policies in real time, well before any long-run mortality data could confirm or refute the more optimistic aggregate projections. A GLP-1 prescription appearing on an insurance applicant's medical history, Munich Re's analysis noted, had become one of the least self-explanatory pieces of information an underwriter might encounter — a prescription that could indicate a well-managed, now lower-risk diabetic patient, a patient with obesity and no other complicating conditions whose overall risk profile had genuinely improved, or a patient who, despite successful weight loss, retained serious underlying comorbidities including hypertension, sleep apnea, or fatty liver disease that the GLP-1 prescription alone did nothing to resolve and that a purely automated or accelerated underwriting process, seeing only "GLP-1 prescription" as a data point, might mistakenly treat as uniformly favorable. This ambiguity, industry analysts warned, created a real risk of what actuaries call mortality slippage in underwriting programs that had streamlined or automated parts of their risk-assessment process specifically to speed up policy issuance — a risk that a medication genuinely capable of improving many patients' health could, paradoxically, make certain underwriting shortcuts less reliable rather than more, precisely because the drug's presence in a patient's history no longer mapped as cleanly onto a single, predictable risk category as older, better-understood diagnostic and prescribing patterns had.

Apparel and fashion retail experienced its own, less headline-grabbing version of this disruption, one that illustrates how far the ripple effects described in this chapter extended into industries whose connection to a hunger hormone was not immediately obvious even to the industry's own executives at first. Retailers and clothing manufacturers began quietly adjusting size-distribution planning and inventory forecasting in response to reported shifts in customer sizing patterns, a slow-moving, operationally significant adjustment that received far less public attention than the more dramatic grocery and pharmaceutical stock-price stories described earlier in this chapter, but that represented real, if gradual, capital reallocation across an entire consumer-facing retail sector responding to a population-level change in body composition playing out over a period of a few years rather than a single earnings-call-driven shock. Plastic surgeons and dermatologists, for their own part, reported rising demand for body-contouring and skin-tightening procedures specifically among patients who had lost substantial weight rapidly through GLP-1 treatment and were left with loose or sagging skin the medication itself did nothing to address — a related, considerably more direct medical-services impact than the retail-sizing adjustment described above, and one this book's chapter on lean-mass loss and "Ozempic face" had already partially previewed from a different angle.

Airlines represented one of the more counterintuitive industries drawn into the GLP-1 economic-impact conversation, through a mechanism with a straightforward physical logic once stated plainly: airplane fuel consumption scales directly with the total weight an aircraft carries, including the combined weight of its passengers, and a population losing meaningful average body weight at scale would, in aggregate, reduce the total weight airlines need to lift and carry on every flight. Industry analysts and airline-sector commentators raised this dynamic as a genuine, if modest and slow-developing, potential tailwind for airline fuel costs — a curiosity-driven thread of commentary that never approached the scale or seriousness of the grocery, restaurant, or insurance-sector analysis described earlier in this chapter, but one that nonetheless illustrates just how far the ripple effects of a single molecule's population-scale adoption had begun extending by the mid-2020s, into industries whose connection to appetite and body weight was, on its face, considerably less obvious than a grocery store's or a life insurer's.

Employer-sponsored health-insurance economics, discussed in chapter eleven primarily from the standpoint of coverage decisions, deserve a second look here specifically through the lens of long-run cost offsets, because the same actuarial uncertainty that complicated life-insurance underwriting complicated employer health-benefit budgeting in a closely related way. An employer covering GLP-1 medication for its workforce faces the certain, immediate cost described in chapter ten, but a genuinely uncertain, longer-horizon set of potential savings: reduced spending on diabetes complications including kidney disease, nerve damage, and vision loss; reduced cardiovascular events following directly from the SELECT trial evidence described in chapter eleven; potentially reduced disability and workers'-compensation claims tied to obesity-related musculoskeletal strain; and, per the addiction-medicine evidence described in the previous chapter, potentially reduced costs associated with alcohol-related health problems and lost productivity. Benefits consultants and health-economics researchers working through this calculation generally concluded, by the mid-2020s, that these offsetting savings were real but arrived on a timescale — often five, ten, or more years — considerably longer than the two-to-three-year planning horizon most employers actually use when budgeting health benefits, and considerably longer than the average employee's actual tenure at any single employer, meaning any individual company covering the drug today bears a good chance of paying the upfront cost while a future employer, after the employee has since changed jobs, captures a meaningful share of the eventual downstream savings — a collective-action problem economists sometimes describe using the framework of external benefits, in which the party bearing a cost is not the same party who eventually captures the full benefit, weakening any single actor's individual incentive to make a decision that would nonetheless benefit the healthcare system, and society more broadly, if enough employers made it simultaneously.

The speed with which financial markets absorbed and repriced around this drug class also illustrates something about how modern capital markets process a genuinely novel piece of biological information, worth naming as this chapter closes. Traditional pharmaceutical-sector analysis tends to focus narrowly on a drug's direct competitors and adjacent therapeutic categories; the GLP-1 story this chapter has documented forced a much wider circle of equity analysts — covering packaged food, restaurants, insurance, travel, and retail rather than pharmaceuticals specifically — to develop, often from a standing start, enough working knowledge of incretin biology and the STEP and SURMOUNT trial data described in chapters five and nine to model its effect on their own, seemingly unrelated coverage universe. That cross-disciplinary scramble, itself a minor but genuine feature of this book's broader story, reflects how unusually far this particular piece of medical science traveled beyond its original clinical context, reshaping how professional investors thought about consumer behavior itself rather than remaining confined to the specialist healthcare-investing community that would ordinarily be the first and primary audience for a new drug class's clinical trial results.

Taken together, these disparate industry reactions — grocery and snack spending contracting at the household level, restaurant chains bifurcating along menu-composition lines, life insurers rewriting mortality assumptions and underwriting practices, and even airlines' fuel-cost modeling picking up a faint but real signal — describe something larger than any single sector's earnings report could capture on its own: a molecule discovered in a Bronx VA laboratory, refined over decades inside a Danish insulin company's foundation-funded research program, had, within roughly three years of its cultural breakthrough described in chapter six, begun measurably reshaping the revenue models, risk models, and operational planning of industries as far removed from pharmaceutical science as commercial aviation and grocery retail. Few prior medical innovations in the modern era have produced ripple effects legible across quite so wide and quite so unexpected a range of unrelated industries, quite so quickly — a testament less to any single company's commercial strategy than to how deeply and how universally human appetite, the specific biological signal this entire book has traced from a lizard's venom to a Senate hearing room, sits underneath the economic architecture of modern life.
