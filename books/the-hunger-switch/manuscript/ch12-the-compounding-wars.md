# Chapter 12 — The Compounding Wars

The scale of the compounding market that grew up during this period is difficult to state with the same precision this book has managed for the branded manufacturers' own disclosed sales figures, described in chapters ten and sixteen, precisely because compounding pharmacies, telehealth platforms, and the broader gray market operate under considerably less stringent public-disclosure requirements than publicly traded pharmaceutical manufacturers do. Estimates that did circulate through industry and press coverage during the shortage years, while imprecise, consistently described a market serving millions of patients simultaneously at its peak — a scale large enough that, as this chapter goes on to describe, it drew direct legal action from both branded manufacturers, sustained congressional and regulatory attention, and, eventually, a coordinated multi-agency wind-down effort spanning the FDA, state pharmacy boards, and the courts, rather than remaining the kind of small, easily overlooked regulatory footnote pharmacy compounding exceptions more typically produce.

The compounding episode this chapter documents offers a useful case study, worth flagging explicitly, in how quickly a narrow legal exception designed for a specific, limited purpose can be stretched by genuine market forces into something its original drafters never anticipated. The shortage-based compounding exception described later in this chapter existed, prior to this book's story, mainly to address localized, short-duration shortages affecting relatively small patient populations — a saline-solution shortage during a bad flu season, a generic injectable running low at a regional distribution level. Nothing in the exception's original design contemplated a shortage this large, this sustained, or this nationally visible, and the resulting mismatch between a narrow legal tool and an enormous market opportunity is a large part of why the compounding industry's growth during this period proved so difficult for regulators to manage cleanly, and why the eventual wind-down process this chapter describes took years rather than the swift, orderly transition a better-fitted regulatory framework might have produced.

Pharmacy compounding is, in ordinary circumstances, a narrow and largely uncontroversial corner of American pharmacy practice. It exists to let a licensed pharmacist prepare a customized version of a medication for an individual patient's specific needs — removing an allergen a patient cannot tolerate, adjusting a dose or a formulation a commercial manufacturer does not produce, or preparing a liquid version of a drug for a child or an elderly patient who cannot swallow a standard tablet. Federal law recognizes two broad categories of compounder: 503A pharmacies, typically smaller operations preparing customized prescriptions for individual, named patients based on a specific prescription, and 503B outsourcing facilities, larger operations registered with the FDA and permitted to produce compounded drugs in bulk, without a patient-specific prescription in hand, under a somewhat more rigorous set of manufacturing-quality requirements closer to, though still meaningfully less stringent than, those governing conventional drug manufacturers.

Compounders are not, under ordinary circumstances, permitted to simply reproduce a patented, commercially available drug wholesale — federal law exists specifically to prevent compounding from becoming a backdoor around patent protection and the FDA's own drug-approval process. But federal law carves out an important exception for drugs that appear on the FDA's official drug shortage list: when a commercially manufactured drug is officially designated as being in shortage, compounding pharmacies are permitted, under specific conditions, to prepare and sell versions of that same drug, using the same or a closely related active ingredient, to help meet patient demand the branded manufacturer cannot currently satisfy. That exception exists for good reason — a patient with a genuine medical need for a drug that a shortage has made unavailable should not simply be left without any option — but it was never designed with a scenario in mind quite like the one this book has already described across its previous chapters: an entire, enormously popular new drug class placed on the shortage list for the better part of two and a half years, with millions of prescription-seeking patients unable to reliably obtain the branded product through ordinary channels.

The compounding industry's response to that opening was immediate and, by the standards of most previous shortage episodes in American pharmaceutical history, unusually large in scale. Compounding pharmacies across the country, along with a fast-growing wave of telehealth companies built specifically around prescribing and dispensing compounded GLP-1 products, began offering compounded semaglutide and tirzepatide at prices dramatically below the branded products' list price — commonly in the range of $100 to $300 a month, compared with the roughly $1,000-to-$1,350-a-month branded list prices described in chapter ten, a gap large enough on its own to explain much of the compounding market's rapid growth even before accounting for the branded shortage's separate, independent effect on availability.

The legal and safety picture around compounded semaglutide and tirzepatide was, from close to the beginning of this gray-market expansion, considerably murkier than the branded products' own tightly regulated manufacturing and distribution chain. Compounded versions of these drugs were generally prepared using semaglutide or tirzepatide base compound sourced from chemical suppliers, frequently overseas, rather than from Novo Nordisk's or Eli Lilly's own patented, FDA-inspected manufacturing facilities — a sourcing arrangement that put the entire compounded-drug supply chain outside the direct oversight both companies exercised over their own branded products, and that left considerable room for variation in purity, potency, and formulation from one compounding pharmacy to the next, variation that a patient filling a branded prescription simply did not have to think about in the same way. The data on this pattern, once regulators and researchers began tracking it systematically, was stark enough to draw sustained national news coverage in its own right. Poison control centers across the United States logged nearly 3,000 calls involving semaglutide between January and November 2023 alone, a more than fifteenfold increase from 2019 levels — a rise that tracked almost exactly the trajectory of the drug's broader cultural and commercial explosion described in earlier chapters, rather than any change in the inherent danger of the FDA-approved branded product itself, whose sealed, dial-calibrated pen delivery system had not changed at all over the same period. A meaningful share of those calls involved dosing errors specifically tied to compounded, rather than branded, product: patients or, in some cases, the clinicians overseeing their treatment miscalculating a conversion between milligrams and the units or milliliters used to measure a dose drawn from a multi-dose vial with a syringe, an error mode that the branded pens' fixed, pre-calibrated dosing mechanism was specifically engineered to prevent. Some of the resulting overdoses were severe: documented cases described patients who had administered five, ten, even twenty times their intended dose, producing not merely the ordinary gastrointestinal side effects familiar to any GLP-1 patient but severe dehydration, fainting, gallstones, and in some cases acute pancreatitis serious enough to require hospitalization. The FDA responded on July 26, 2024, with a formal alert to healthcare providers, compounders, and patients specifically addressing the dosing-error pattern — a warning that, notably, arrived more than a year after poison control centers had first begun flagging the trend publicly, an interval that patient-safety advocates would later cite as evidence that regulatory response to the compounding boom had consistently lagged behind the scale of the problem on the ground.

Not every compounding operation behaved the same way, and painting the entire industry with the same brush risks obscuring a real distinction that mattered enormously to patients trying to navigate it: some 503B outsourcing facilities maintained rigorous, FDA-registered quality controls closer in spirit to conventional pharmaceutical manufacturing, while a considerably larger and harder-to-regulate tier of smaller operators, some operating largely or entirely online with minimal physical oversight, cut corners on sourcing, testing, and labeling in ways that directly produced the adverse-event pattern described above. Patients shopping for compounded semaglutide online had, in practice, very little reliable way to distinguish a careful, well-run compounding pharmacy from a corner-cutting one, since marketing claims and website presentation gave few genuine signals of underlying manufacturing quality — a genuine information asymmetry that a handful of larger, publicly traded telehealth companies would soon come to dominate and, in their own way, partially resolve simply by virtue of their scale and public visibility.

The single largest and most publicly visible player in this space was Hims & Hers Health, a publicly traded telehealth company that had built its original business around more discreet, direct-to-consumer treatment for conditions like hair loss and erectile dysfunction before pivoting aggressively into compounded GLP-1 sales starting in May 2024, offering compounded semaglutide at $199 a month against branded Ozempic and Wegovy list prices that, by the specific figures the company itself cited in its own marketing, ran to roughly $1,799 and $1,999 a month respectively. Hims & Hers backed that pricing gap with an extraordinary marketing budget, spending on the order of $145 million in a single quarter promoting its weight-loss offerings, a scale of advertising spend that made the company's own branding almost as visible in the broader cultural conversation around these drugs as Novo Nordisk's or Eli Lilly's own marketing had been in the drugs' earlier, branded-only years. The bet paid off commercially in the near term: the company's overall 2024 revenue reached roughly $1.5 billion, with its weight-loss product line alone reaching a run rate the company described as approaching $100 million and, in subsequent public guidance, projected to grow substantially further. That commercial success made Hims & Hers simultaneously one of the compounding era's most prominent success stories and one of its most exposed targets once the FDA's wind-down deadlines and its subsequent 2026 proposal to permanently exclude these compounds from the 503B Bulks List began to bite — the company's own share price fell sharply, at one point by roughly a quarter in a single trading session, on news of tightening federal compounding rules, a vivid illustration of how much of the telehealth compounding industry's valuation rested on a regulatory exception that both this chapter's history and ordinary legal logic suggested was always going to be temporary.

Both Novo Nordisk and Eli Lilly, for reasons that mixed genuine patient-safety concern with equally genuine commercial self-interest — the two motivations were, in this instance, difficult to fully disentangle and arguably did not need to be, since a compounded product bypassing patent protection threatens a manufacturer's revenue at the same time a poorly manufactured compounded product threatens patient safety — pursued an aggressive combination of public messaging and direct legal action against the compounding industry through 2023 and 2024. Both companies filed lawsuits against individual compounding pharmacies and telehealth companies, alleging trademark infringement, false advertising, and, in some cases, direct patient-safety violations tied to specific adulterated or mislabeled products traced back to specific compounders. Both companies also lobbied the FDA directly, arguing that the agency should move more aggressively to restrict compounding once the underlying branded-product shortage that had originally justified it began to ease.

The FDA's own posture through this period reflected the genuine tension embedded in the underlying law: an agency responsible simultaneously for protecting patients from genuinely unsafe or fraudulent compounded products and for preserving compounding's legitimate, narrower role in American pharmacy practice, operating under a shortage-based legal exception that had never anticipated a shortage of this scale or duration. The FDA formally declared the tirzepatide shortage resolved in December 2024 and the semaglutide shortage resolved in February 2025 — declarations that, under the statutory framework described above, should have triggered an immediate end to the shortage-based legal justification for mass compounding of either drug. In practice, the transition was staged rather than instantaneous: the FDA established phased wind-down deadlines rather than an immediate cutoff, giving 503A pharmacies until February 19, 2025, to stop producing compounded tirzepatide and until April 22, 2025, to stop producing compounded semaglutide, with 503B outsourcing facilities granted slightly longer windows — March 19, 2025, for tirzepatide and May 22, 2025, for semaglutide — a graduated approach intended to give the compounding industry, and the patients who had come to depend on it, some time to transition rather than facing an abrupt, potentially disruptive cutoff.

The compounding industry did not accept this wind-down quietly. The Outsourcing Facilities Association, an industry trade group representing 503B compounders, filed federal lawsuits challenging both the tirzepatide and semaglutide shortage-resolution determinations, arguing that the FDA's process for declaring the shortages resolved had been procedurally flawed and that genuine, unmet patient demand persisted in various regions and dosage strengths regardless of the agency's national-level determination. Courts denied the association's requests for preliminary injunctions in both cases, allowing the FDA's enforcement deadlines to proceed as scheduled — a legal defeat for the compounding industry that nonetheless did not fully end the underlying practice, since a smaller compounding market, operating under narrower, more genuinely patient-specific 503A exceptions rather than the mass, non-patient-specific production the 503B shortage exception had permitted, continued to exist even after the broad shortage-based justification expired.

The FDA moved to close even that narrower remaining pathway in 2026. On April 30 of that year, the agency proposed to permanently exclude semaglutide, tirzepatide, and liraglutide from what is known as the 503B Bulks List — the formal list of substances that outsourcing facilities are permitted to use in compounding under any circumstances, shortage or otherwise — a proposal that, if finalized, would close essentially the last legal pathway for large-scale compounding of these three drugs regardless of future supply conditions. Stanford Medicine's own clinical reporting on the issue, published around the same period, captured the physician-community rationale behind this more permanent restriction: persistent, documented concerns about dosing accuracy, sourcing verification, and outright adulteration in compounded GLP-1 products, concerns serious enough that a meaningful share of prescribing physicians had already begun actively discouraging patients from compounded alternatives even before the FDA's proposed permanent exclusion made the question largely moot.

The practical consequence of this two-and-a-half-year regulatory arc, for the millions of patients who had come to rely on compounded semaglutide or tirzepatide as their only financially accessible option during the branded shortage, was a wrenching return to exactly the affordability problem chapter ten described in detail: branded GLP-1 medications running well over $1,000 a month at list price, against the $100-to-$300-a-month range many compounded users had grown accustomed to paying. Patients facing that gap, once the compounding wind-down deadlines took effect, generally faced one of a small number of unappealing choices: absorb a several-hundred-dollar-a-month increase in out-of-pocket cost if their insurance situation and financial circumstances allowed it, pursue the newer direct-to-consumer cash-pay programs described in chapter ten that, while considerably cheaper than full list price, still ran well above compounded pricing, discontinue treatment entirely and risk the weight regain and metabolic reversal described in a later chapter on what happens when patients stop taking these drugs, or continue seeking compounded or otherwise unregulated alternatives through increasingly constrained and increasingly risky channels as the legal compounding industry wound down around them.

The compounding episode also left a lasting mark on FDA policy and pharmacy-board practice well beyond the specific GLP-1 drugs at the center of this chapter, prompting broader institutional reflection on whether the existing shortage-based compounding exception, designed decades earlier around much smaller, more localized shortage episodes, remained fit for purpose in an era when a single drug class could generate the kind of sustained, multi-year, nationwide shortage this book's seventh chapter described. Pharmacy boards in several states tightened their own oversight of compounding pharmacies' sourcing and quality-control practices in the aftermath of the dosing-error reports described above, independent of the federal 503B Bulks List proceeding, reflecting a broader regulatory reassessment that the GLP-1 shortage had triggered across multiple layers of an oversight system — federal and state, agency and legislative — that had not previously needed to grapple with compounding at quite this scale or quite this level of public scrutiny.

Interestingly, the relationship between the branded manufacturers and the telehealth distribution channel did not remain purely adversarial once the compounding-specific legal pathway began closing. Novo Nordisk itself, seeking a way to reach the price-sensitive, digitally native patient population the compounding telehealth companies had spent two years cultivating, struck direct commercial partnerships with several of the same telehealth platforms — Hims & Hers among them, alongside competitors like LifeMD and Ro — to distribute authentic, branded Wegovy through those companies' existing prescribing and delivery infrastructure once the compounding pathway was no longer legally available to them. That pivot, announced amid considerable public attention precisely because of how directly it reversed the adversarial posture of the preceding two years, offered a clear illustration of a dynamic that recurs throughout the history of gray markets confronting a dominant incumbent: rather than being permanently destroyed once its original legal justification evaporated, the compounding-era distribution and marketing infrastructure was, in significant part, simply absorbed into the branded manufacturers' own commercial strategy, repurposed to sell the authentic product through the same digital channels and to the same price-conscious customer base the gray market had spent two years training to buy GLP-1 drugs online rather than through a traditional pharmacy counter.

This chapter's account of the compounding wars is not, in the end, a simple story of good-faith patient access battling bad-faith corporate gatekeeping, nor a simple story of responsible regulators protecting patients from a reckless gray market — both framings capture something real about a genuinely difficult situation, and both leave something important out. The branded manufacturers' shortage, described across chapters six and seven, created the conditions that made mass compounding both legally possible and, for a great many patients, practically necessary. The compounding industry that grew up to fill that gap did real good for real patients who had no other affordable option, and it also, demonstrably, produced real, documented patient harm through inconsistent quality control that a more tightly regulated branded product would have been far less likely to produce. Untangling those two truths — that the gray market was, simultaneously, a rational and often life-improving response to a genuine access failure, and a source of real, avoidable risk that grew directly out of the same access failure — is less a matter of assigning blame to a single villain than of recognizing how a shortage this large, this sustained, and this culturally visible was always going to generate exactly this kind of contested, imperfect, second-best market response, in the absence of a manufacturing and insurance system capable of meeting the underlying demand through safer, better-regulated channels from the start.
