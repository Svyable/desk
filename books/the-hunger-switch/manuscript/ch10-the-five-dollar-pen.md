# Chapter 10 — The Five-Dollar Pen

The pricing story this chapter tells cannot be fully separated from the shortage story chapter seven already told, because scarcity and price are, in ordinary economic terms, closely linked variables that reinforced each other throughout the years this book covers. A manufacturer facing demand that dramatically outstrips its own production capacity, as chapter seven described in detail, faces relatively little competitive pressure to lower prices even as public criticism of those prices mounts, since a lower price would do nothing to expand the actual physical supply available to meet the already-overwhelming demand, and might simply worsen rationing by drawing in even more prescription-seeking patients competing for the same constrained pool of pens. This dynamic helps explain why list prices remained largely static through the worst years of the shortage described in chapter seven, only beginning to soften, as the following pages describe, once manufacturing capacity had expanded enough and competitive and political pressure had built enough to make price competition a more viable strategy than simply managing scarcity through allocation and public messaging alone.

In 2024, a team of researchers affiliated with Yale School of Medicine published an analysis attempting to estimate what it might actually cost to manufacture a month's supply of semaglutide, stripped of everything else that goes into a branded drug's list price — patent-protected exclusivity, marketing, distribution margins, insurance-negotiated rebates, and ordinary corporate profit. Their estimate, based on the known chemistry of peptide synthesis at commercial scale and the relatively small quantity of active ingredient required per dose, put the raw production cost of a month's supply of the active ingredient at well under a dollar — by some versions of the analysis, closer to twenty-nine cents. A separate study out of the University of Liverpool, examining the broader manufacturing economics of GLP-1 peptides more generally, projected that a fully generic, mass-produced version of injectable semaglutide could plausibly be manufactured and sold profitably for somewhere between $28 and $140 a year, and that an oral version, once that formulation existed in generic form, could be produced for roughly $186 to $380 a year.

It is worth pausing on the two academic cost estimates cited above and being precise about their limitations, since a number this politically useful — a sub-dollar or low-hundred-dollar production cost quoted repeatedly by members of Congress, journalists, and patient advocates throughout the years this book covers — deserves the same scrutiny this book has applied to every other headline figure in its account. Both the Yale and University of Liverpool analyses are academic modeling exercises, built from published information about peptide-synthesis chemistry and general manufacturing-cost benchmarks across the pharmaceutical industry, not audits of Novo Nordisk's or Eli Lilly's actual, disclosed internal cost accounting, which neither company makes public in the level of detail that would allow an outside researcher to verify a specific per-pen manufacturing cost with full confidence. The true figure almost certainly sits somewhere between the academic estimates' optimistic lower bound and the full list price, and probably closer to the lower end once genuine at-scale generic competition eventually arrives — but readers should treat the specific numbers cited throughout this chapter as well-reasoned estimates rather than confirmed corporate accounting, precisely the caveat this book's introduction promised to apply consistently to figures of this kind.

Set against those estimates, Ozempic's list price in the United States — the price a pharmacy would charge an uninsured patient, or the sticker price against which insurance negotiations and discounts are calculated — ran to roughly $1,027.51 per month by the mid-2020s. Wegovy's list price ran higher still, around $1,349.02 per month. Annualized, that is somewhere in the range of $12,000 to $16,000 a year for a single patient's ongoing treatment, against a production-cost estimate, using the more generous of the two academic analyses cited above, of perhaps a few hundred dollars a year once true generic competition and manufacturing scale were eventually achieved. The gap between those two figures — a difference not of a few percentage points but of one, sometimes two, orders of magnitude — became, through the mid-2020s, one of the most politically charged and most frequently cited numbers in the entire public debate over these drugs, invoked by patient advocates, members of Congress holding hearings on pharmaceutical pricing, and international commentators comparing what Americans paid for these drugs against what patients in other wealthy countries paid for chemically identical products.

This chapter's title, borrowed loosely from the Fast Company headline cited above, is meant to hold two things in tension rather than to simply indict the manufacturers: a genuine, well-documented gap between production cost and list price, and a genuine, well-documented set of economic reasons that gap exists and persists, neither of which fully justifies the other on its own. Understanding why that gap exists, and why it is not simply evidence of straightforward corporate greed even though it is also not fully justified by manufacturing cost alone, requires understanding a few separate and only loosely related pieces of how American pharmaceutical pricing actually works — pieces this chapter takes in turn, because conflating them, as much of the public conversation around GLP-1 pricing tended to do, obscures more than it clarifies.

The first piece is patent-protected exclusivity itself, described in earlier chapters: for as long as a drug remains under patent protection, without generic or biosimilar competition legally permitted to enter the market, the manufacturer faces essentially no price competition on the product itself, and can set list prices according to what it judges the market — meaning, in practice, what insurers, pharmacy benefit managers, and ultimately patients and taxpayers are willing to pay — will bear, constrained mainly by public and political tolerance, competitive pressure from the other branded manufacturer described in the previous chapter, and, in markets with government-negotiated drug pricing, direct regulatory limits that do not exist in the same form in the United States. This is not unique to GLP-1 drugs; it is the standard economic logic underlying the entire patent-based pharmaceutical innovation system, in which manufacturers are granted a temporary period of pricing power specifically as the financial incentive that justifies the enormous upfront cost of drug discovery and clinical trials described across this book's earlier chapters — costs that, unlike the marginal cost of producing one additional dose once a drug is approved, run into the billions of dollars and are borne entirely by the manufacturer, with no guarantee of ever recovering the investment, since the overwhelming majority of drug candidates that enter clinical trials never reach approval at all.

A useful way to see how unusual this specific pricing gap really is, relative to the pharmaceutical industry's ordinary economics, is to compare it against other patent-protected biologic drugs that do not carry anywhere near the same public notoriety. Many chronic-disease biologics — treatments for rheumatoid arthritis, certain cancers, or rare genetic conditions — carry list prices in a broadly comparable range to Ozempic's and Wegovy's, reflecting similarly high research costs and similarly strong patent protection, without generating anything like the same volume of congressional hearings, viral social-media outrage, or op-ed commentary. The difference is not that GLP-1 drugs are more expensive, in absolute or even relative terms, than many other biologics already on the market; it is that GLP-1 drugs reached a scale of use — tens of millions of patients, rather than the much smaller populations typically affected by a rare-disease biologic — that made their pricing structure newly, unavoidably visible to a mass public in a way narrower, rarer-disease pricing controversies rarely achieve, however similar the underlying economics.

The second piece, and the one least understood by the general public despite being arguably more consequential for what an individual American patient actually pays, is the pharmacy benefit manager system — a layer of intermediary companies, most prominently CVS Caremark, Express Scripts, and OptumRx, that negotiate drug prices on behalf of insurers and large employers, in exchange for placement on a given insurance plan's covered-drug formulary. Pharmacy benefit managers extract rebates from manufacturers — discounts off the list price, paid back to the benefit manager or shared in some proportion with the insurer — in exchange for favorable formulary placement, and the size of those rebates is, by industry convention, confidential, not disclosed publicly by either the manufacturer or the benefit manager. This creates a genuinely strange pricing structure in which the list price of a drug like Ozempic or Wegovy — the number most frequently quoted in news coverage and the number an uninsured patient actually pays — bears an increasingly loose relationship to the net price the manufacturer actually receives once rebates are subtracted, a net price that has, by various industry analysts' estimates, been falling even as list prices have continued climbing, because manufacturers have used rising list prices partly as a negotiating lever to offer larger apparent rebates to benefit managers competing to secure the best formulary terms for their own client insurers.

The practical effect of this system is that the patient least equipped to absorb the cost — someone without insurance, or with an insurance plan that specifically excludes anti-obesity medication coverage, discussed in the following chapter — is generally the patient most exposed to the full, undiscounted list price, while large insurers negotiating on behalf of millions of covered lives receive prices that, while still confidential and still generally well above the raw manufacturing-cost estimates cited at the start of this chapter, are meaningfully lower than the sticker price suggests. This is not a phenomenon unique to GLP-1 drugs either; it is a structural feature of American pharmaceutical pricing across most branded, patent-protected medications. But the scale of GLP-1 demand, and the fact that so many patients seeking these specific drugs fell into exactly the uninsured or excluded-coverage category most exposed to list price, made the practical consequences of this pricing architecture unusually visible and unusually painful for an unusually large population, all roughly simultaneously.

International price comparisons sharpened the political salience of the gap further. The same Ozempic pen, manufactured in the same Danish and other global facilities to the same regulatory standard, sold in Denmark itself, in the United Kingdom through the National Health Service's centrally negotiated pricing, and across most of the European Union at government-negotiated prices running to a fraction of the American list price — commonly cited comparisons through the mid-2020s put many European list or negotiated prices in the range of a few hundred dollars a month, sometimes less, compared with the roughly thousand-dollar-plus American list price for the chemically identical product. That gap reflects, in large part, the fundamentally different structure of pharmaceutical pricing in countries with centralized, government-run or government-regulated health systems that negotiate drug prices directly and can credibly threaten to exclude a drug from coverage entirely if a manufacturer will not agree to specific pricing terms — a form of negotiating leverage the fragmented American system, split across thousands of separate private insurers, employer plans, and pharmacy benefit managers each negotiating separately, has historically lacked, and that the Medicare program itself was legally barred for decades from exercising directly, a specific and consequential piece of American healthcare policy history examined in detail in the next chapter.

That gap became the centerpiece of a formal congressional reckoning on September 24, 2024, when the Senate Committee on Health, Education, Labor and Pensions, chaired by Vermont senator Bernie Sanders, convened a hearing specifically to question Novo Nordisk's chief executive, Lars Fruergaard Jorgensen, about the company's American pricing. Sanders came armed with a specific set of country-by-country comparisons that made the hearing's title — "Why Is Novo Nordisk Charging Americans with Diabetes and Obesity Outrageously High Prices for Ozempic and Wegovy?" — read less like editorializing than like a literal description of the data being presented: Ozempic, he noted, cost Americans with type 2 diabetes roughly $969 a month, compared with $155 in Canada, $122 in Denmark — Novo Nordisk's own home market — and just $59 in Germany. Wegovy, at $1,349 a month in the United States, cost $186 in Denmark, $140 in Germany, and $92 in the United Kingdom. Sanders also cited the Yale manufacturing-cost study discussed earlier in this chapter directly in his opening remarks, framing the gap between a sub-five-dollar production cost and a thousand-dollar-plus American list price as, in his words during the hearing, evidence of "greed, greed, greed." Jorgensen, testifying under oath, defended the company's pricing by pointing to the complexity of the rebate system described above, the enormous sunk cost of the underlying research program traced across this book's earlier chapters, and the genuine, if limited, availability of lower-cost cash-pay options — a defense that satisfied few of the senators present, though the hearing did extract a public commitment from Jorgensen to meet directly with pharmacy benefit managers to discuss lowering costs, a modest outcome relative to the hearing's confrontational tone but a genuine, documented instance of American political pressure translating into at least a stated corporate commitment to revisit pricing practices.

That political pressure, combined with the competitive dynamic described in the previous chapter and the parallel gray-market pressure examined in the next, appears to have contributed to a real shift in both companies' pricing strategy over the following year, even if neither company's own public statements credited congressional testimony directly as the cause. In March 2025, Novo Nordisk launched NovoCare Pharmacy, a direct-to-patient sales channel offering all dose strengths of Wegovy to cash-paying, uninsured patients at $499 a month with home delivery — less than half the branded list price, though still, by the manufacturing-cost estimates cited earlier in this chapter, a considerable multiple of the underlying production cost, and still, by later 2025, further reduced from an initial $650-a-month offer as competitive pressure continued to build. Eli Lilly ran a parallel program, LillyDirect, offering Zepbound to cash-paying patients at prices ranging between roughly $299 and $549 a month depending on dose, along with a separate self-pay vial program offering the drug in a non-pen, non-single-dose format at further reduced prices for patients willing to handle their own dosing with a syringe rather than a pre-filled pen device. Neither of these direct-to-consumer cash prices approached the sub-$150-a-year figures the academic manufacturing-cost studies suggested might eventually be achievable under full generic competition, and neither replaced insurance coverage as the preferable path for patients who had it. But both represented a real, substantial retreat from the roughly $1,000-to-$1,350-a-month list prices that had defined the drugs' first several years on the market, achieved not through any change in patent status or regulatory mandate but through the combined pressure of public political scrutiny, direct company-to-company competition, and the parallel threat, examined in the next two chapters, of both government insurance-coverage decisions and an entire unregulated compounding industry offering functionally similar products at a small fraction of even the reduced direct-pay price.

It would be a mistake, however, to read the international pricing gap as evidence that Novo Nordisk and Eli Lilly are engaged in straightforward price discrimination purely at Americans' expense, full stop, with no countervailing logic at all — the more complete picture is that American list prices, run through the pharmaceutical industry's own long-standing account of its economics, functionally subsidize a disproportionate share of the global research-and-development costs that produced these drugs in the first place, costs that a Danish or British government negotiator, focused only on securing the best possible price for its own citizens, has comparatively little institutional incentive to help cover once a drug's clinical value has already been proven and its research costs already sunk. Whether that arrangement is fair, sustainable, or the best available way to fund pharmaceutical innovation globally is a genuinely contested policy question, not a question with an obvious correct answer this chapter can resolve — but understanding that the international pricing gap is not simply an accounting trick, and reflects a real, if uncomfortable, division of research-funding burden across countries with very different healthcare-financing philosophies, is a necessary piece of taking the pricing debate seriously rather than treating it purely as a morality tale.

A separate strand of the pricing debate, less politically dramatic than a Senate hearing but arguably more consequential for how insurers actually make coverage decisions, played out through independent cost-effectiveness research conducted by health-economics organizations that specialize in translating a drug's clinical benefit into a standardized measure — commonly a quality-adjusted life-year, or QALY, an economic unit meant to capture both how much longer and how much better a treatment allows a patient to live — and then comparing that benefit against the treatment's price to judge whether a given price represents reasonable value for a health system as a whole, as opposed to whether the drug works at all. Analyses of this kind, applied to GLP-1 drugs at their initial American list prices, tended to conclude that the prices charged were considerably higher than what the measured clinical benefit alone would justify by conventional cost-effectiveness thresholds — not a claim that the drugs lacked real value, but a claim that the price captured value considerably beyond what a strict clinical cost-effectiveness calculation would recommend, and a recurring data point cited by insurers seeking to justify continued coverage restrictions even as public pressure for broader access continued to build. Whether cost-effectiveness analysis of this kind is the right yardstick for pricing a drug that also, as later chapters describe, appears to reduce downstream healthcare costs from diabetes complications, cardiovascular events, and other conditions in ways that can take years to fully show up in the data, is itself a genuinely contested methodological question — but the analyses' consistent conclusion, that list prices exceeded conventional value-based benchmarks by a wide margin, gave insurers a technical, ostensibly neutral justification for coverage decisions that patient advocates experienced as simply another barrier standing between them and a medicine their physicians had recommended.

None of this contextual complexity, though, changes the lived, immediate reality that shaped how tens of millions of Americans actually experienced these drugs' pricing during the years this book covers: a genuinely life-changing medicine, available and effective, priced in the United States specifically at a level that placed it out of reach for a very large share of the population who might have benefited from it, absent insurance coverage this chapter's companion discusses next — coverage that, as the following chapter explains in detail, large portions of the American insurance system spent years actively declining to provide, for reasons rooted as much in how American health insurance has historically defined obesity itself as in the raw economics of drug pricing described here.
