# Chapter 1 — The Lizard in the Desert

This book tells the story that follows roughly in the order it happened, and it begins here, with an animal rather than a company or a patient, for a specific reason: nearly every subsequent chapter's argument depends on readers first accepting that appetite is a concrete, physical, targetable biological signal rather than an abstract measure of character — and there is no more vivid, no more concrete way to establish that fact than to start with the specific, physical hormone that made it demonstrable, and the specific, unglamorous animal whose venom happened to contain a more durable version of it than the human body's own supply.

The Gila monster does not look like the ancestor of a trillion-dollar industry. It looks like a mistake — a foot and a half of black-and-orange beaded skin, moving so slowly across the floor of the Sonoran and Mojave deserts that it seems less like a predator than a very old rumor of one. It is venomous, one of the few venomous lizards on Earth, though its bite is more likely to make a grown man vomit and swear than kill him. It spends the overwhelming majority of its life doing nothing: buried, still, invisible, sometimes for eleven months at a stretch. Then, three or four times a year, it eats an enormous meal — a nest of eggs, a baby rabbit, whatever it can find and swallow nearly whole — and goes back underground to do nothing for a while longer.

That feeding pattern is, biologically, an absurdity. Most animals, including humans, eat regularly enough that their blood sugar stays inside a narrow, tightly managed range. A Gila monster does not have that luxury. It gorges rarely and starves constantly, which means its pancreas has to do something unusual: flood the body with insulin-triggering signals after a huge meal, then shut that system down almost completely for months, without ever losing the ability to switch back on.

For most of the animal's relationship with the humans who share its range, none of this mattered to anyone. Ranchers and settlers in the nineteenth-century Southwest feared the Gila monster far more than its actual bite warranted, and a body of frontier folklore grew up around it — that its breath was poisonous, that a bite could not be survived, that the animal should be killed on sight. Herpetologists spent decades correcting the record: the venom is real and the bite is genuinely painful, produced by grooved teeth in the lower jaw rather than the hinged fangs of a viper, but a healthy adult human bitten by a Gila monster is very unlikely to die from it. The lizard was, for most of its documented history, an object of fear and myth rather than of laboratory interest.

The idea that a feared, unglamorous animal might be hiding a medically useful biochemistry inside its venom was not, by the time anyone got around to the Gila monster, a new one. In the 1960s and 1970s, Brazilian researchers studying the venom of the fer-de-lance pit viper, *Bothrops jararaca*, isolated peptides that blocked an enzyme central to blood-pressure regulation — a discovery that eventually led to captopril, one of the first drugs in the entire class of ACE inhibitors now taken daily by tens of millions of people with hypertension and heart failure. Snake venom had already, by then, taught pharmacology a durable lesson: venoms are not crude poisons so much as extremely refined biochemical tools, honed by evolution to bind specific receptors with a precision that a chemist in a laboratory might spend a career trying to replicate from scratch. An animal that needs to disable or kill a target quickly, using the smallest possible quantity of material, cannot afford sloppy chemistry. What looks to a rancher like a menace looks, to a biochemist willing to look closely, like a working parts catalog.

In the early 1980s, a gastroenterologist named Jean-Pierre Raufman, working with funding from the National Institutes of Health, became curious about how the Gila monster's body pulled that off. Raufman was not looking for a diabetes drug. He was interested in venom as a tool — venoms are, after all, just extremely concentrated, extremely specific biochemistry, refined by millions of years of evolution to do one thing very precisely to a target animal's physiology. Snake venom had already given medicine several useful drugs by targeting blood pressure and clotting. Raufman wanted to know what was in Gila monster venom that might explain the animal's strange relationship with its own pancreas, and he began isolating and testing individual compounds from it.

What Raufman's group found, and published through the 1980s, was that certain molecules in the venom had striking effects on pancreatic cells — effects that looked, in some ways, like they were interacting with the same signaling systems that regulate insulin in humans. It was interesting science. It did not, on its own, produce a drug. Interesting venom chemistry rarely does; most of it ends up as a footnote in a specialized journal, read by a few dozen people who study reptile physiology for a living, filed away as the kind of curiosity that gets mentioned once in a review article and then largely forgotten outside the small community that studies venomous animals for a living.

Federally funded basic-science research of exactly this kind is unglamorous by design. Nobody applying for an NIH grant to characterize pancreatic-acting peptides in lizard venom in 1981 could have promised, or even reasonably predicted, that the work would matter to a diabetic grandmother in Ohio four decades later. The honest pitch for that kind of research has never been "this will become a blockbuster drug." It has always been closer to "we do not yet understand a basic biological mechanism, and understanding it is worth doing before we know what it is for." That kind of research is chronically underfunded relative to its eventual payoff, precisely because its payoff, if any, arrives on a timescale longer than most funding cycles, most careers, and most companies' patience.

It might have stayed a footnote if not for a Veterans Affairs endocrinologist in the Bronx named John Eng.

Eng was, by his own later accounts and by the accounts of people who worked with him, not a typical academic star chasing a hot field. He worked at the VA Medical Center in the Bronx, a setting about as far from the glamorous end of biotech as American medicine gets, and he had a long-standing interest in gut hormones — the signaling molecules released by the intestine that talk to the pancreas, the liver, and the brain about what has just been eaten. In 1992, building on the general area Raufman had opened up, Eng isolated a specific peptide from Gila monster venom. He named it exendin-4, for the exendin family of Heloderma (Gila monster) venom peptides, and there was a reason the name mattered: exendin-4 turned out to closely resemble a human hormone that a different set of scientists, working at Massachusetts General Hospital and elsewhere, had been characterizing around the same time. That human hormone was glucagon-like peptide-1 — GLP-1.

GLP-1 does, in the human body, something close to what Raufman had suspected the venom compounds were doing in the lizard. It is released by cells lining the small intestine within minutes of eating. It travels to the pancreas and tells the beta cells there to release insulin, but only when blood sugar is actually elevated — a built-in safety feature that ordinary insulin injections lack. It slows the rate at which the stomach empties into the small intestine, which spreads out the absorption of a meal and blunts the resulting blood-sugar spike. And it acts on regions of the brain involved in appetite and reward, producing a sense of fullness that arrives sooner and lasts longer than it otherwise would.

In other words: GLP-1 is one of the body's own natural brakes on eating too much, too fast. It had been known to endocrinologists since the 1980s. It was, on paper, an obvious candidate for a diabetes drug, maybe even a weight-loss drug. There was just one enormous practical problem, and it is the reason nobody had built a usable medicine out of it in the fifteen years between GLP-1's discovery and Eng's 1992 paper.

Natural human GLP-1 lasts, once it hits the bloodstream, for about two minutes. An enzyme called DPP-4 — dipeptidyl peptidase-4 — chews it apart almost as fast as the body releases it. Two minutes is enough time for GLP-1 to do its job during and immediately after a single meal. It is nowhere near enough time to build a once-daily pill or injection around, let alone a once-weekly one. Every attempt to simply synthesize and inject human GLP-1 ran into the same wall: the drug vanished from the bloodstream before it could do sustained work, which meant patients would have needed constant infusions to get a meaningful effect — not a workable therapy for a chronic disease managed at home.

This is a common and frustrating pattern in peptide drug development generally, not unique to GLP-1. The human body is extremely good at clearing short peptide hormones from circulation quickly, for the same reason a nervous system benefits from signals that turn off promptly once they have been sent: a hormone that lingered indefinitely would be a hormone the body could no longer use for fine, moment-to-moment regulation. That same design feature, so useful for regulating a meal in real time, becomes an engineering obstacle the moment someone wants to turn the hormone into a medicine that a patient takes once and expects to keep working for days. Pharmaceutical chemists spent decades developing strategies to get around it — modifying a peptide's amino-acid sequence so the relevant enzyme cannot recognize and cut it, attaching it to larger molecules that slow its clearance from the kidneys, or fusing it to other proteins that give it a longer half-life by association. Each of those strategies carries its own risk: change a peptide's structure enough to dodge an enzyme, and there is no guarantee it will still bind its target receptor the same way, or that the immune system will not start treating the modified molecule as foreign and mount a reaction against it.

Exendin-4, the lizard peptide, did not have that problem. It resembled GLP-1 closely enough to bind the same receptor and trigger the same downstream effects, but its molecular structure made it resistant to the enzyme that destroyed the human version almost instantly. Exendin-4 could remain active in the bloodstream for hours instead of minutes. A creature that evolved to survive on three or four meals a year had, in the course of building venom to subdue prey, produced by evolutionary accident a molecule that solved the central engineering problem standing between GLP-1 biology and a usable human drug.

This is, on its face, a wonderful discovery. It is also, on its own, worth almost nothing, because a molecule sitting in a laboratory freezer does not treat anyone's diabetes. Eng needed a pharmaceutical company willing to take exendin-4 through the extraordinarily expensive, extraordinarily slow process of turning a laboratory finding into an approved drug: synthesizing it reliably at scale, running toxicology studies, running Phase 1, Phase 2, and Phase 3 clinical trials, filing with the Food and Drug Administration, and only then, years and tens of millions of dollars later, finding out whether doctors would prescribe it and insurers would pay for it.

For roughly three years, according to later accounts of the period, Eng could not find that partner. The story that a molecule derived from Gila monster venom might treat human diabetes struck most of the pharmaceutical industry as, at best, a novelty and, at worst, a punchline — the kind of origin story that sounds more like folk medicine than serious biochemistry. Drug development in the 1990s was dominated by companies chasing more conventional chemistry: small-molecule pills that could be manufactured cheaply and swallowed easily, not injectable peptides sourced from a reptile that most Americans had never seen outside a zoo.

The skepticism was not entirely irrational, even if it turned out to be wrong. Peptide drugs are expensive to manufacture at scale compared with small-molecule pills; they generally have to be injected, because stomach acid and digestive enzymes will break most peptides down before they can be absorbed intact, which limits the market to patients willing to self-inject; and the diabetes drug market in the 1990s was already crowded with insulin formulations, sulfonylureas, and metformin, all of which were cheaper to make and easier for patients to take. A publicly traded pharmaceutical company deciding where to spend its research budget had every ordinary business reason to look past a peptide sourced from a lizard's venom, championed by a VA physician without a major research university's backing, competing for the same limited pool of diabetes patients as drugs that had already been on the market for years. Betting on exendin-4 required believing not just that the chemistry would work, but that the eventual therapy would be good enough, for a meaningful number of patients, to justify an injection and a price premium over pills most doctors already knew how to prescribe. That is a lot of belief to ask of a risk committee.

The company that eventually took the risk was Amylin Pharmaceuticals, a San Diego biotech built around a different pancreatic hormone (amylin itself) that was willing to make a long-shot bet on peptide-based metabolic drugs more broadly. Amylin licensed the rights to a synthetic version of exendin-4, developed it under the generic name exenatide, and eventually partnered with Eli Lilly — a company that would resurface fifteen years later as Novo Nordisk's fiercest competitor in this same drug class — to bring it to market. In April 2005, the Food and Drug Administration approved exenatide under the brand name Byetta, the first GLP-1 receptor agonist ever approved for human use.

Byetta was not, by the standards this book will go on to describe, a blockbuster. It had to be injected twice a day, within an hour of the two largest meals of the day, using a pen device that a patient had to learn to load and use correctly at home. Its effects on weight were real but modest — a matter of a few pounds for most patients, not the double-digit percentages that would eventually define its descendants. It caused nausea in a meaningful share of patients, especially early in treatment, and adherence suffered as a result: plenty of patients who started Byetta stopped taking it within the first year, unable or unwilling to tolerate the gastrointestinal side effects long enough to see whether they would fade. It found a real but limited role in type 2 diabetes management, prescribed alongside — not instead of — other diabetes medicines such as metformin, and it never became a household name outside the community of endocrinologists and diabetes educators who prescribed it.

None of that made Byetta a failure. Judged purely as a first-in-class medicine, it succeeded at the one thing a first-in-class medicine most needs to do: it worked well enough, safely enough, and in enough patients that regulators, physicians, and competing drug companies all had to take the underlying biology seriously. A twice-daily injection with a modest weight effect and real tolerability problems is not a drug most people would recognize, a decade and a half later, as the ancestor of Ozempic and Wegovy. But every subsequent GLP-1 drug — every longer-acting version, every reformulation aimed at reducing nausea, every attempt to push the weight-loss effect from a few pounds toward a few dozen — was, in a direct and traceable sense, an argument with Byetta's limitations. Improve the half-life. Improve the tolerability. Improve the dose-escalation schedule so the gut has time to adjust. Each of those improvements was a specific, engineerable response to a specific, documented shortcoming of the first drug in the class, and each of them would take years of additional laboratory work to achieve.

But Byetta proved something that mattered far more than its own sales figures: it proved that a long-acting GLP-1 receptor agonist, injected regularly, was a real, FDA-approvable, prescribable category of medicine. It proved the concept. It gave every major diabetes-focused pharmaceutical company a template and a reason to ask the same question in their own labs: could this be done better? Longer-lasting. Less nauseating. Once a week instead of twice a day. Effective not just on blood sugar, but — and here the story starts to widen out from diabetes into something much larger — on body weight itself.

It is worth sitting for a moment with how contingent all of this was. Change almost any variable in the preceding two decades and the modern GLP-1 drug class either arrives on a different schedule or does not arrive through this path at all. If Raufman had not been curious about an animal most researchers had no reason to study. If Eng, working outside the most prestigious research institutions, had given up during the years no company would return his calls. If Amylin, a small biotech itself perpetually on the edge of running out of cash through the late 1990s, had chosen a safer bet instead of a lizard-derived peptide. If Eli Lilly had decided the partnership with a small San Diego biotech was not worth the risk. Drug development retrospectives tend to smooth these stories into something that reads as inevitable — of course a hormone this useful found its way into medicine eventually — but the actual history of Byetta is a history of a discovery that very nearly went nowhere, saved less by any single brilliant insight than by a handful of people who kept believing in an unfashionable idea longer than the industry around them did.

That pattern — unglamorous basic science, years of institutional indifference, a small biotech willing to take a risk larger companies would not, a modest first product that mattered mainly as proof of concept — repeats itself throughout the early history of this drug class. It is not a story of a single eureka moment. It is a story of accumulated persistence across multiple institutions, most of which never became famous for it.

One of the companies that had been asking a version of that question since long before Byetta reached pharmacy shelves was a Danish firm with an unusual corporate structure, an eighty-year head start in metabolic disease, and, as it would turn out, a great deal of patience. Its scientists had been circling incretin biology — the broader category of gut hormones that includes GLP-1 — since the early 1990s, in the same years that a VA endocrinologist in the Bronx was extracting peptides from a lizard nobody wanted to hold.

That company's own origin story begins, not coincidentally, with another cross-species act of biological borrowing: a Danish physiologist who traveled to Toronto in 1922 to learn how to extract a hormone from the pancreas of a dog.

Every subsequent chapter of this book, in some sense, is an extended answer to a question this opening chapter has only posed: what happens once a signal this fundamental to human survival becomes, for the first time in medical history, something a pharmaceutical company can reliably manufacture, price, and distribute at global scale. Understanding why Novo Nordisk was even in a position to make the multi-decade bet that eventually produced semaglutide — the molecule that would turn this quiet lizard-venom story into a subject of celebrity gossip, congressional hearings, and Wall Street earnings calls — requires going back to that earlier trip, and to the century of insulin business that came before anyone in Copenhagen had heard the word "incretin."
