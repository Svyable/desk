# Chapter 18 — The Cost of Stopping

The question of what happens when treatment stops is, in one sense, an unusual thing for a pharmaceutical industry built primarily around chronic-disease management to have needed to study this deliberately. Patients on blood-pressure medication, cholesterol-lowering statins, or standard diabetes drugs generally understand, as an unremarkable background fact of chronic-disease treatment, that stopping the medication will likely mean the underlying condition returns to its untreated state — nobody expects a blood-pressure pill to produce a permanent cure once discontinued. That GLP-1 drugs required their own dedicated, carefully designed withdrawal trials to establish essentially the same principle for obesity reflects how differently the culture, and to some degree the medical field itself, had been implicitly treating weight loss relative to other chronic conditions before this evidence arrived — as something closer to a project with a completion point, "getting to a goal weight," rather than an ongoing physiological state requiring continuous management the way blood pressure or cholesterol already were understood to require. The withdrawal trials this chapter examines did not merely answer a narrow pharmacological question; they helped complete the broader reclassification of obesity as a chronic disease that this book's third chapter traced back to the underlying incretin biology, giving that reclassification its own dedicated clinical-trial evidence rather than leaving it as a purely conceptual or biological argument.

Every major GLP-1 and dual-agonist trial program this book has described shares a structural feature that received relatively little public attention amid the headline weight-loss percentages: a specific sub-study, built directly into the larger trial, designed to test what happens when patients who have achieved significant weight loss stop taking the drug. Novo Nordisk's STEP 4 trial and Eli Lilly's SURMOUNT-4 trial both used this withdrawal design, randomizing a subset of patients who had already completed an initial period of active treatment and achieved substantial weight loss into two groups: one continuing the medication, the other switched to placebo, with both groups followed for an additional extended period to compare outcomes.

The trial's timing within Novo Nordisk's own broader development and commercialization program is worth noting, because it illustrates a genuine tension between scientific thoroughness and commercial momentum that this book's account of the drugs' celebrity-driven cultural explosion, described in chapter six, tends to obscure. STEP 1's original results, showing the roughly 15 percent average weight loss described in chapter five, were published and drove Wegovy's approval and subsequent cultural rise well before the extension study's discontinuation data became available — meaning the celebrity endorsements, viral social-media enthusiasm, and initial wave of patient adoption chapter six documented all took place in an information environment where the drug's most dramatic headline result was public and widely celebrated, while the considerably more sobering discontinuation data this chapter examines remained, for a meaningful stretch of the drug's most culturally visible period, still being collected rather than yet available for patients and prescribing physicians to fully weigh against the initial enthusiasm.

The results, published for the semaglutide program as what researchers call the STEP 1 trial extension, were unambiguous and, for a field that had spent decades unofficially treating obesity pharmacotherapy as something patients might eventually graduate from, genuinely consequential. Participants in the original STEP 1 trial had lost an average of 17.3 percent of body weight over 68 weeks of active treatment, compared with 2.0 percent in the placebo group. Following treatment withdrawal, participants who had been taking semaglutide regained an average of 11.6 percentage points of the weight they had lost by roughly a year after stopping — resulting in a net weight loss from the trial's original baseline of only about 5.6 percent, down sharply from the 17.3 percent achieved at the point treatment stopped. Put in the simpler, more widely quoted terms that circulated through subsequent medical and popular coverage of the finding: patients who discontinued semaglutide after achieving substantial weight loss regained, on average, roughly two-thirds of that weight within about a year of stopping. Cardiometabolic measures — blood pressure, blood sugar, cholesterol markers — that had improved substantially during active treatment reverted toward their pre-treatment baseline over the same period, tracking the weight regain closely rather than remaining durably improved independent of it.

The financial implications of this discontinuation evidence flow directly, and somewhat counterintuitively, into the manufacturing and shortage story this book examined in chapter seven. A drug class understood as chronic, ongoing treatment rather than a finite course implies a fundamentally different, considerably larger steady-state manufacturing target than a drug class where patients might reasonably be expected to complete treatment and stop within a year or two — meaning Novo Nordisk's and Eli Lilly's multi-billion-dollar manufacturing investments described in chapter nine were not merely responding to the acute shortage crisis of 2022 through 2024, but were implicitly being sized against a demand curve that, if the chronic-treatment framing this chapter establishes holds, does not taper off the way a finite-course medication's demand curve eventually would once its patient population completes treatment and exits the market. This distinction, invisible in most contemporary coverage of the shortage and the subsequent manufacturing buildout, is one more piece of evidence for how central the discontinuation data examined in this chapter turned out to be for understanding nearly every other part of this book's account, once its full implications are traced through.

Lilly's SURMOUNT-4 trial, using tirzepatide and a broadly similar withdrawal design, found essentially the same pattern: patients switched from active tirzepatide to placebo after an initial period of substantial weight loss experienced significant weight regain over the following months, reinforcing rather than complicating the semaglutide finding. That two independently designed, independently run trials from directly competing companies, testing chemically distinct molecules, arrived at essentially the same conclusion did more to establish the discontinuation-and-regain pattern as a genuine, class-wide pharmacological reality — rather than a peculiarity of one specific drug or one specific trial's methodology — than either result could have accomplished in isolation, a point this book's ninth chapter also noted in passing and returns to here in fuller context.

It is worth being precise about what the discontinuation trials do and do not demonstrate, because popular coverage of the finding sometimes overstated its certainty in one direction while critics of the drugs sometimes overstated it in the other. The STEP 1 extension and SURMOUNT-4 data describe average regain across a study population, not a guaranteed individual outcome; some individual trial participants maintained considerably more of their weight loss than the group average, particularly those who made durable, independent changes to diet and activity levels during their period of active treatment, while others regained even more sharply than the average figure suggests. Nor do the trials fully settle how the regain curve behaves beyond the roughly one-year follow-up window the published data covers — whether weight continues climbing back toward pre-treatment baseline indefinitely, or plateaus at some new, still-elevated-relative-to-treatment level, remains a question the published discontinuation trials, limited in duration as most clinical trials necessarily are, cannot yet fully answer.

It is worth situating this chapter's chronic-disease reframing against the broader arc of chronic-disease management this book has referenced only briefly elsewhere. Hypertension medication, statins for cholesterol management, and insulin itself, described at length in chapter two, all share the same basic structure this chapter has now established for GLP-1 drugs: a condition managed rather than cured, requiring sustained treatment to sustain the benefit, with discontinuation reliably producing a return toward the untreated baseline. Framed this way, the discontinuation data this chapter examines is less a surprising new finding about GLP-1 drugs specifically than a confirmation that obesity, once properly understood through the incretin biology chapter three described, behaves the way every other major chronic condition medicine already understood behaves — the genuine surprise, in retrospect, was ever having expected otherwise.

The clinical and conceptual significance of this finding is difficult to overstate, because it directly answers a question that had shadowed this entire drug class since its earliest cultural reception, described across chapters five and six: is this a cure, or is it ongoing, indefinite treatment for a chronic condition? The STEP 1 extension and SURMOUNT-4 data point clearly toward the latter answer. Obesity, on this evidence, behaves pharmacologically the way medicine already understood hypertension or high cholesterol to behave — a chronic condition whose underlying physiology does not permanently reset merely because a medication has successfully controlled it for a period of time, requiring ongoing treatment to maintain the achieved benefit rather than a fixed course of treatment after which the condition is considered resolved. This framing connects directly back to the body-weight "set point" physiology this book's third chapter described in detail: the same hormonal compensatory mechanisms — reduced satiety signaling, increased hunger-hormone activity, a lowered resting metabolic rate — that make diet-based weight loss so difficult to sustain appear to reassert themselves once a GLP-1 drug's artificial amplification of the body's satiety signal is withdrawn, restoring something close to the same underlying appetite-regulation dynamics that produced the original weight gain in the first place.

The practical and economic implications of "chronic, ongoing treatment" rather than "finite course of treatment" ripple through nearly every other chapter of this book once stated plainly. The pricing debate examined in chapter ten becomes considerably higher-stakes once a patient's effective annual cost is understood as a recurring, likely indefinite expense rather than a one-time or short-term intervention — the same roughly $1,000-a-month list price reads very differently as a permanent, ongoing household budget line than as a temporary expense with a clear endpoint. The insurance-coverage fights examined in chapter eleven take on added weight for the same reason: an insurer or government program agreeing to cover these drugs is agreeing, in practice, to an open-ended, likely permanent spending commitment for every covered patient who continues treatment, not a bounded, one-time cost — precisely the dynamic the CBO's more conservative fiscal scoring, described in that chapter, was implicitly modeling when it projected costs running years into the future rather than a short adoption curve followed by a plateau.

The discontinuation data also reframes how this book's earlier chapters on bariatric surgery, in chapter fourteen, and on lean-mass loss, in chapter thirteen, should be understood in relation to each other. Bariatric surgery's durable, non-reversing anatomical change, once described in chapter fourteen mainly as a matter of mechanism, emerges from this chapter's discussion as also a matter of a fundamentally different maintenance requirement: a successful surgical patient does not face the same sharp, well-documented regain risk upon "stopping treatment," because there is no ongoing treatment to stop in the same sense — the altered anatomy continues working without requiring continuous patient action beyond routine follow-up care. A GLP-1 patient, by contrast, faces a lifelong choice, assuming they wish to maintain their results: continue an expensive, ongoing medication regimen indefinitely, or accept that discontinuing it will likely mean losing the majority of the weight-loss benefit within roughly a year, a tradeoff that did not receive nearly the same level of public attention during the drugs' celebrity-driven cultural rise described in chapter six as the initial, headline-grabbing weight-loss percentages themselves did.

Patients who had lived through the food-noise relief described in chapter six generally reported that discontinuation reversed the psychological dimension of treatment as unmistakably as it reversed the physical weight-loss numbers documented in the STEP 1 extension. Physicians managing patients through planned or unplanned treatment interruptions — whether due to the shortage described in chapter seven, the compounding wind-down described in chapter twelve, insurance-coverage disruptions described in chapter eleven, or simple patient choice — described a recurring pattern in which patients reported the return of the same intrusive, near-constant food-related thoughts that had first drawn public attention to the phenomenon, often within a period of weeks rather than months, well before most of the physical weight regain had fully occurred. This sequencing — psychological craving returning noticeably faster than the numerical weight regain that followed it — reinforced the underlying mechanistic story this book has told since chapter three: the drug's effect operates upstream, on the appetite-regulation signal itself, with weight change following as a downstream consequence rather than the primary site of the drug's action, meaning stopping the drug reverses the upstream signal first and the downstream weight outcome only gradually afterward as ordinary eating patterns reassert themselves over subsequent weeks and months.

Researchers and physicians have explored a range of strategies intended to soften this discontinuation effect, short of simply requiring every patient to remain on full-dose treatment indefinitely regardless of cost or preference. Gradual dose tapering, rather than abrupt discontinuation, is one commonly discussed approach, on the theory — not yet fully proven through the same scale of randomized evidence that established the discontinuation-and-regain pattern itself — that a slower, more gradual reduction in drug exposure might allow the body's compensatory hunger and metabolic mechanisms to reassert themselves more gradually, giving a patient more opportunity to adjust behavior and build durable habits before the drug's protective effect fully withdraws, compared with an abrupt stop. Some clinicians have also explored maintenance strategies combining a reduced, lower-cost dose with more intensive behavioral support, dietary counseling, and exercise programming, on the theory that a partial pharmacological effect combined with stronger behavioral infrastructure might sustain more of the treatment benefit than either element could alone — an approach broadly consistent with the STEP 3 trial finding, described in chapter five, that behavioral support and medication produce complementary rather than substitutable benefits. None of these mitigation strategies, as this book was being written, had been validated through trial evidence at anywhere near the scale and rigor of the core STEP and SURMOUNT programs, meaning physicians recommending them were, in a meaningful sense, still practicing ahead of the fully settled evidence base — a reasonable and common state of affairs in clinical medicine generally, but one worth naming plainly rather than presenting tapering or hybrid maintenance approaches as more scientifically established than they currently are.

This discontinuation reality carries particular weight for one specific patient population this book has not yet fully addressed: adolescents. In December 2022, the FDA approved Wegovy for adolescents ages 12 and older with obesity, based on the STEP TEENS trial, a Phase 3 study enrolling 201 teenagers with a body mass index at or above the 95th percentile for their age and sex. The trial found a mean 16.1 percent reduction in BMI among adolescents receiving semaglutide, compared with a 0.6 percent increase in the placebo group — results broadly comparable in magnitude to the adult STEP trials, and a genuinely significant development for a population in which effective treatment options for severe obesity had historically been even more limited than for adults, given the understandably higher bar regulators and physicians apply before recommending bariatric surgery or other more invasive interventions in a still-developing adolescent body.

The adolescent approval, however, raised the discontinuation question in a considerably more fraught form than it presented for adult patients. A twelve-year-old beginning GLP-1 treatment, if the chronic-disease framing this chapter has established holds true for adolescents as it does for the adult trial populations, faces the prospect of a treatment decision with a multi-decade horizon — potentially fifty, sixty, or more years of continuous medication use, filled with unresolved questions this book's evidence base simply cannot yet answer: what continuous GLP-1 receptor activation across an entire adolescence, young adulthood, and beyond does to long-term growth, bone development, reproductive health, and metabolic function that no trial has yet followed for anywhere near that duration; whether adolescent patients and their families, offered a treatment this effective this early in life, can make a fully informed decision about a commitment of that scale and duration; and whether insurance and healthcare systems, already straining under the adult coverage questions examined in chapter eleven, can sustain covering an adolescent population's treatment across the many additional decades of use a twelve-year-old's treatment horizon implies relative to a sixty-year-old's. Pediatric endocrinologists and medical ethicists engaged with these questions seriously and without easy consensus through the mid-2020s, generally concluding that the STEP TEENS evidence justified treatment availability for adolescents with severe, clinically significant obesity and associated health risks, while emphasizing that long-term surveillance of this specific patient population would need to continue for decades before the field could offer confident answers to the questions this paragraph has raised.

The adolescent ethics debate also intersected directly with the affordability and insurance questions this book examined in chapters ten and eleven, in a way that made the theoretical multi-decade cost projection considerably more concrete. A family securing insurance coverage for a twelve-year-old's Wegovy treatment in 2026 had no guarantee that coverage would remain stable across the many subsequent decades the chronic-treatment framing described in this chapter implies — insurance plans change, employers change, state Medicaid policies change, and federal coverage rules, as chapter eleven's account of the 2024-2025 Medicare and Medicaid coverage reversal demonstrated directly, can shift abruptly with a change in political administration. A pediatric patient beginning treatment faced not only the open medical questions this chapter has already described, but a genuinely uncertain, decades-long financial and access landscape that no family could fully plan around at the point of an initial treatment decision — a level of uncertainty considerably harder to reason through responsibly for a twelve-year-old's treatment plan than for an adult patient making a similar decision with a shorter remaining treatment horizon and, generally, more independent financial agency to adapt if coverage or pricing changed unfavorably in the future.

One further, related question drew intense public and clinical attention during this period, though the underlying evidence turned out to be more reassuring, and less dramatic, than early social-media discussion suggested: reports, widely circulated online under the informal shorthand "Ozempic babies," describing pregnancies among GLP-1 users that some attributed to the drugs interfering with hormonal birth control. Formal pharmacokinetic studies examining this specific question, assessing semaglutide's effect on oral contraceptive absorption directly, found no clinically meaningful reduction in the effectiveness of standard-dose oral contraceptives — semaglutide's well-documented effect of slowing gastric emptying does not, the available evidence indicates, meaningfully interfere with hormonal contraceptive absorption at typical doses. A more scientifically supported explanation for the reported pregnancies pointed instead toward a separate and, on reflection, less surprising mechanism: significant weight loss itself is well established in reproductive medicine to restore fertility in women with obesity-related conditions such as polycystic ovary syndrome, a condition frequently associated with anovulation and reduced fertility that substantial weight loss, achieved through any effective method including GLP-1 treatment, can meaningfully improve — meaning at least some of the "Ozempic baby" pregnancies likely reflected genuinely restored fertility following weight loss rather than any direct interference with contraception, a distinction with real clinical importance for patients and physicians navigating family-planning decisions during GLP-1 treatment, even though popular discussion of the phenomenon rarely drew it as carefully as the underlying pharmacology warranted.
