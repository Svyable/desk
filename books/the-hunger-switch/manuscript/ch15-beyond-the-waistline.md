# Chapter 15 — Beyond the Waistline

Addiction medicine, as a field, had particular reason to receive this chapter's findings with cautious optimism rather than either dismissal or uncritical enthusiasm, given its own difficult institutional history. Pharmaceutical treatment for substance-use disorders has historically attracted a fraction of the research investment directed toward other major chronic-disease categories, despite the enormous public-health burden alcohol and other substance-use disorders impose, a funding gap that addiction researchers have attributed to a combination of the same moralized cultural framing this book's third chapter described for obesity — addiction, like obesity, was for decades treated culturally as substantially a matter of individual choice and character rather than a biologically grounded condition deserving the same research investment as, say, cancer or heart disease — and to the more practical stigma-driven reluctance of both patients and the broader public to prioritize research funding for conditions still carrying significant social judgment. A genuinely promising new pharmacological lead arriving not through a dedicated addiction-research investment but as a secondary discovery within a much larger, differently funded obesity and diabetes research program is, in this light, as much a comment on decades of addiction-research underfunding as it is a comment on GLP-1 biology's unusual reach.

Some of the earliest anecdotal reports about GLP-1 drugs, circulating informally among patients and physicians well before they became the subject of rigorous clinical trials, described an effect that had nothing obviously to do with either diabetes or body weight: patients taking Ozempic or Wegovy for their approved indications, without any intention of changing their drinking habits, reported that they simply wanted alcohol less. Some described finishing half a drink and setting it aside, uninterested in continuing, in a way that felt different from ordinary moderation or willpower. Others reported that a nightly glass of wine that had been a longstanding habit simply stopped feeling appealing. These reports were, in the first instance, exactly the kind of informal, uncontrolled patient anecdote that responsible science treats with real caution — the plural of anecdote is not data, and self-reported behavior change in patients who know they are taking a novel medication is exactly the kind of situation prone to expectation effects, placebo-adjacent psychological dynamics, and simple coincidence. But the reports were frequent enough, and specific enough in their description of a genuine shift in subjective desire rather than merely willpower-driven restraint, that they attracted serious scientific attention faster than most informal patient anecdotes typically do.

This chapter's account of alcohol-craving reduction also bears directly on the industry-wide financial ripple effects examined later in this book, since the alcoholic-beverage industry's own market reaction, described in chapter sixteen, drew directly on the same emerging science this chapter documents in more clinical detail — a rare instance in this book's account where a single strand of underlying medical research produced both a serious clinical research literature, discussed here, and a serious, billions-of-dollars financial market reaction, discussed later, on a similarly compressed and overlapping timeline, rather than the more typical pattern in which financial markets lag well behind the underlying clinical evidence by months or years.

The biological plausibility behind the anecdotes turned out to be considerably stronger than a skeptic's first instinct might suggest, once researchers began examining it directly. GLP-1 receptors are not confined to the gut and pancreas; they are also expressed in regions of the brain involved in reward processing — the same general neural circuitry, centered on dopamine signaling in structures including the ventral tegmental area and nucleus accumbens, that governs the brain's response to a wide range of rewarding stimuli, food included, but also including alcohol, nicotine, and other substances capable of triggering compulsive, repeated-use behavior. Animal studies conducted well before the current generation of GLP-1 drugs reached mass human use had already demonstrated that GLP-1 receptor activation could reduce alcohol consumption and drug-seeking behavior in rodent models, evidence that had circulated within a relatively narrow addiction-neuroscience research community for years without translating into major public attention, largely because no GLP-1 drug had yet reached the scale of human use that would generate the kind of large, easily observable patient population capable of turning a theoretical neuroscience finding into a widely noticed clinical phenomenon.

The National Institutes of Health, through its National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism and National Institute on Drug Abuse, took the accumulating signal seriously enough to fund and highlight a wave of more rigorous clinical research through 2023 and 2024. One randomized trial, adding a weekly GLP-1 medication on top of standard cognitive behavioral therapy for patients with alcohol use disorder, found that the combination produced a significantly greater reduction in heavy-drinking days than cognitive behavioral therapy alone — with total alcohol consumption falling by more than 70 percent after 26 weeks of treatment in the combined-treatment group, according to NIH's own reporting on the trial. A separate large real-world observational study, published in Nature Communications in 2024 and examining insurance-claims data from 83,825 patients with obesity, found that patients prescribed semaglutide had a 50 to 56 percent lower risk of both new-onset and recurrent alcohol use disorder diagnoses compared with patients prescribed other anti-obesity medications over a twelve-month follow-up period — a striking, population-scale corroboration of the smaller randomized trial's more controlled finding, using an entirely different research method and a completely different, much larger patient population.

Nora The addiction-medicine research described in this chapter also raises a distinct clinical caution that responsible physicians and researchers were careful to flag alongside the encouraging findings: a reduced desire to drink is not, by itself, a complete substance-use-disorder treatment in the way structured therapy, social support, and, for many patients, specific behavioral interventions have long provided. Addiction medicine specialists engaging with the early GLP-1 findings generally framed the drugs as a potentially powerful additional tool to be layered onto, rather than substituted for, existing evidence-based addiction treatment — the NIH-funded trial described earlier in this chapter tested the drug specifically as an addition to cognitive behavioral therapy rather than as a standalone replacement for it, a study-design choice that reflected exactly this clinical caution and that responsible physician guidance continued to emphasize even as public enthusiasm for the addiction-related findings, discussed further below, sometimes ran ahead of that more careful framing.

This addiction-medicine thread also connects back to a broader theme running through this book's account of the drugs' cultural reception, described most directly in chapters three and six: the gradual displacement of a willpower-centered explanation for compulsive behavior by a biological one. Just as this book's third chapter traced how appetite regulation turned out to rest on identifiable, targetable hormonal signaling rather than pure moral discipline, the emerging alcohol and nicotine research described in this chapter suggests that at least some portion of what culture has long treated as a matter of willpower or character in addiction — the strength of a craving, the difficulty of resisting a drink or a cigarette once the desire arises — may likewise be more biologically malleable, and less a fixed measure of an individual's moral fiber, than several decades of addiction-treatment culture, including some strands of twelve-step and other abstinence-based recovery traditions built substantially around personal accountability and willpower, had generally assumed. That reframing carries real and, for some patients and treatment communities, genuinely contested implications for how addiction itself is understood and treated, implications this book does not attempt to resolve but that the underlying science, however preliminary in its addiction-specific applications compared with the now well-established obesity and diabetes evidence, appears to be pushing toward regardless.

Volkow, the director of the National Institute on Drug Abuse and one of the most prominent addiction researchers in the United States, described the emerging evidence in terms that captured both genuine scientific excitement and appropriate caution about how early this research still was. Speaking to the NIAAA's own publication on the topic, Volkow noted that the field was "beginning to see some of that potential for GLP-1s to treat drug addiction turn into reality" — a carefully hedged formulation from a scientist whose career had been built on the rigorous, often disappointing process of separating genuinely promising addiction-treatment leads from the many that fail to replicate at scale, and whose endorsement of the GLP-1-addiction connection as a real, emerging clinical reality rather than mere hype carried particular weight within the field precisely because of that track record of caution.

The comparative numbers researchers cited to contextualize semaglutide's apparent effect against existing, FDA-approved alcohol use disorder medications were striking enough to draw attention well beyond the addiction-medicine specialty itself. One analysis found semaglutide associated with a number needed to treat — a standard measure in clinical research describing how many patients must receive a treatment for one additional patient to experience the desired outcome, with lower numbers indicating a more powerfully effective treatment — of roughly 4.3, compared with a number needed to treat of 7 or higher for existing approved alcohol use disorder medications such as naltrexone and acamprosate, drugs that had represented the standard of pharmaceutical treatment for the condition for decades. If that comparative finding holds up under the kind of larger, more definitive trials the field still needs, it would represent an unusually significant development in addiction medicine, a field that has historically struggled with a limited number of only modestly effective pharmaceutical treatment options relative to the scale of alcohol use disorder as a public-health problem.

A further randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, published in The Lancet, extended this evidence base with additional rigor specifically focused on patients carrying both alcohol use disorder and obesity together — a population combination this book's earlier chapters suggest is common, since compulsive eating and compulsive drinking share enough underlying reward-circuit biology that the two conditions frequently co-occur in the same patients. That trial's results reinforced the pattern established by the smaller NIH-funded study described above: semaglutide produced a significantly greater reduction in heavy-drinking days than placebo, evidence that, layered on top of the observational Nature Communications findings and the mechanistic animal-model research described earlier in this chapter, gave the alcohol-related GLP-1 signal an unusually multi-layered evidentiary foundation for a finding this recent — randomized trial evidence, large-scale real-world observational corroboration, and a plausible, independently studied neurological mechanism, three distinct types of evidence pointing in the same direction rather than a single study's isolated finding awaiting replication.

The mechanism researchers increasingly point to in explaining this effect connects directly back to the "food noise" phenomenon this book examined in detail in chapter six, extended from food-specific cravings to a broader category of reward-driven, compulsive-seeking behavior. If GLP-1 signaling dampens the intensity of reward-circuit activation broadly — not merely the specific circuitry tied to food consumption, but the more general dopamine-mediated reward response that governs a range of appetitive and compulsive behaviors — then a reduction in alcohol craving alongside a reduction in food craving would represent two expressions of the same underlying neurological effect rather than two coincidentally overlapping but mechanistically separate phenomena. This broader-reward-circuit hypothesis has led researchers to investigate GLP-1 drugs' effects on an expanding range of other compulsive behaviors beyond alcohol specifically, including nicotine use, with early trials examining semaglutide's effect on smoking cessation and nicotine cravings, and more preliminary, considerably less rigorously studied anecdotal interest in effects on gambling behavior and other behavioral, non-substance addictions that share overlapping reward-circuit biology with substance use disorders, even though the clinical evidence for these additional applications remains considerably thinner than the alcohol-specific research this chapter has focused on.

Nicotine represented the next-most-studied application of this broader reward-circuit hypothesis, and for reasons rooted in basic biology rather than mere scientific fashion: nicotine addiction operates through dopamine-mediated reward circuitry substantially overlapping with the pathways implicated in both food-related and alcohol-related craving, making it a natural next candidate for investigation once the alcohol findings began generating serious scientific interest. Clinical trials examining semaglutide's effect specifically on smoking cessation and nicotine craving were underway as this book was being written, registered with government clinical-trials databases and structured, like the alcohol research described above, to test the drug against an established outcome measure — days smoked, successful quit rates, craving-intensity scores — rather than relying on the kind of informal patient self-report that first drew attention to the alcohol effect. Results from these nicotine-focused trials remained preliminary and considerably less mature than the alcohol evidence base at the time of this book's drafting, a reminder that the "beyond the waistline" story this chapter tells is still, in its non-alcohol applications specifically, closer to its beginning than its conclusion.

This chapter's overall arc, from informal patient anecdote to randomized trial to large observational study to a director of a federal research institute publicly endorsing the finding's credibility, mirrors a broader pattern this book has now traced twice: chapter six described a similarly rapid, informal-to-institutional trajectory for the cultural adoption of GLP-1 drugs generally, and this chapter has described much the same trajectory for one specific, scientifically significant secondary application. That two very different kinds of claims — a cultural fact about celebrity endorsement and a scientific fact about addiction neurobiology — could each move from informal, anecdotal beginnings to broad institutional acceptance within a comparably short span of a few years says something about the unusually fast information environment this entire drug class has existed within since 2022, a pace this book has returned to as a recurring theme precisely because it distinguishes this story so sharply from the multi-decade timelines chapters one through four documented for the underlying science itself.

Gambling and other behavioral, non-substance addictions occupy a considerably earlier and more speculative position on this same research spectrum, and this book treats that speculation accordingly. Online patient communities and a small number of case reports circulating in professional and popular discussion described individuals with pre-existing gambling problems noticing reduced urges to gamble after beginning GLP-1 treatment for an unrelated diabetes or weight-management indication — anecdotes structurally similar to the earliest, pre-scientific alcohol reports this chapter opened with, and biologically plausible for the same dopamine-reward-circuit reasons already described, since compulsive gambling is understood in addiction neuroscience to activate substantially the same reward pathways as substance-based addictions despite involving no ingested substance at all. But unlike the alcohol research, which had by the mid-2020s progressed through animal studies, a randomized controlled trial, and a large observational study in the space of a few years, gambling-related GLP-1 research had not, as this book was being written, progressed meaningfully beyond the anecdotal and case-report stage into anything resembling a rigorous clinical trial — a gap this chapter flags explicitly rather than allowing the more mature alcohol evidence to lend unearned credibility to a considerably less-tested application.

The pharmaceutical industry's own commercial response to the addiction-medicine evidence described in this chapter offers a useful, if still early, test of how seriously the major manufacturers actually take this potential application relative to their much larger, already-proven obesity and diabetes franchises. Neither Novo Nordisk nor Eli Lilly had, as this book was being written, launched a dedicated addiction-focused clinical development program on anything close to the scale of the STEP or SURMOUNT programs described in chapters five and nine, instead allowing the addiction-related evidence to accumulate largely through independently funded academic and NIH-sponsored research rather than company-sponsored trials specifically designed around an addiction endpoint. That relative corporate caution is not necessarily evidence of skepticism about the underlying science; it may simply reflect ordinary commercial sequencing, in which a company with an already enormous, still-growing obesity and diabetes market prioritizes defending and expanding that core franchise, described across this book's middle chapters, before committing the additional years and capital an entirely new, addiction-specific regulatory pathway would require.

It would be premature, based on the evidence available as this book was being written, to describe GLP-1 drugs as an established, first-line addiction treatment on the strength of this research alone, and responsible reporting on the topic — including the NIH's and NIAAA's own public communications about the findings — has consistently emphasized that larger, longer, more definitive randomized controlled trials specifically designed to test alcohol use disorder as a primary outcome, rather than as a secondary finding within a trial designed primarily around weight loss or diabetes, remain necessary before any regulatory body would consider approving these drugs for an addiction-specific indication. No GLP-1 drug carried an FDA-approved addiction-treatment indication as of this book's drafting, and physicians prescribing these drugs specifically to address alcohol use, rather than for an approved diabetes or obesity indication with a possible secondary addiction-related benefit, were doing so off-label, in the same broader pattern of off-label prescribing this book's fifth chapter described in the drugs' earliest years on the market, before the STEP trials had even formally established the obesity indication that would go on to reshape the entire story this book tells.

Financial markets, never shy about pricing in a scientific hypothesis well before the underlying science is fully settled, had already begun treating the alcohol-related findings as a genuine commercial threat rather than a mere research curiosity by the time this book was being written. Shares of major alcoholic-beverage companies, including large distillers and brewers with substantial exposure to the American consumer market, experienced periodic selloffs tied specifically to news coverage of GLP-1-and-drinking research, a pattern closely paralleling, and in some analysts' commentary explicitly compared to, the snack-food and packaged-grocery stock reactions this book examines in more detail in its next chapter. That market reaction, whatever its accuracy as a forecast of actual future alcohol consumption trends, is itself a form of evidence worth noting: professional investors allocating real capital were treating the addiction-medicine research described in this chapter as credible enough to price into an entire industry's valuation, well before any regulatory body had approved a GLP-1 drug for an addiction-related indication and well before the largest, most definitive confirmatory trials had even finished enrolling patients.

Still, the scale and consistency of the evidence accumulated by the mid-2020s — animal-model plausibility, a randomized controlled trial, a large real-world observational study, and a converging biological explanation rooted in reward-circuit neuroscience rather than mere correlation — represented, by the standards addiction medicine typically applies to emerging treatment leads, an unusually strong early signal. If confirmed by the larger trials still underway or still being planned as this book went to press, it would extend this drug class's significance well beyond the metabolic and cardiovascular territory this book's earlier chapters have already documented, into one of the most stubborn and consequential categories of chronic disease in medicine: the compulsive, reward-driven behaviors that alcohol use disorder, nicotine addiction, and related conditions represent, conditions that have resisted effective pharmaceutical treatment for decades in a way that makes any genuinely promising new lead, however early-stage, a matter of serious public-health significance rather than a mere scientific curiosity.
