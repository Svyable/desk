# Prologue — The Quiet Hour Before Dinner

In October 2023, a Walmart executive named John Furner said something in an earnings call that most of the people listening did not register as news.

Furner ran Walmart's U.S. business, the largest retail operation in the world, and he was doing what retail executives do on earnings calls: explaining small movements in large numbers. Same-store sales, basket size, unit counts. Somewhere in the call he mentioned, almost as an aside, that Walmart had noticed something in the purchasing data of customers known to be filling prescriptions for a new class of weight-loss drugs. Not a dramatic shift. A "slight pullback in overall basket," he said — fewer units, slightly fewer calories. He was not raising an alarm. He was answering an analyst's question with the closest thing he had to an honest answer.

By the next morning, shares in PepsiCo, Coca-Cola, and Mondelez were down. So were Walmart's own shares, and Costco's, and McDonald's. Analysts who had spent careers modeling grocery demand around income, inflation, and weather were suddenly modeling it around a hormone.

The hormone in question is called GLP-1 — glucagon-like peptide-1 — and until roughly 2021 almost nobody outside of endocrinology had reason to know its name. It is produced by cells in the human gut within minutes of eating, and among the things it does is tell the brain that the body has had enough. In a person with a working metabolism, GLP-1 rises after a meal and falls a few minutes later, one small signal among thousands that regulate hunger, mostly beneath awareness.

The drugs built around a synthetic, long-lasting version of that signal — sold under brand names like Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro, and Zepbound — do not do anything mystical. They occupy the same receptors the body's own hormone occupies, and they stay there far longer than the body's own hormone ever could, for a week at a time instead of two minutes. The effect, for most people who take them, is that the mental static around food — the thing that patients started calling "food noise" long before any doctor gave it a name — goes quiet. Not silent. Quiet.

That quiet has now been priced, argued over, insured against, sued about, patented, counterfeited, and legislated. It has shown up in the stock prices of snack companies and airlines and life insurers. It has emptied waiting rooms in bariatric surgery clinics and filled them in a different specialty entirely. It has made a small Danish company, founded a century ago by a Nobel laureate who talked his way into a Toronto laboratory, briefly worth more than the economy of the country that hosts it. It has revived a 1990s desert-reptile discovery that no pharmaceutical company wanted to touch. It has reopened, in clinics and op-eds and family group chats, an argument about whether people who are overweight lack willpower or lack a hormone — an argument that turns out, on the evidence, to have had the wrong subject for most of a century.

This book follows that argument from a Gila monster's mouth to a factory in North Carolina running triple shifts to a pharmacy counter in Wichita where a woman with type 2 diabetes could not fill her prescription because three thousand miles away, a person without diabetes had gotten to the pen first.

This book is organized, broadly, in the order the story actually unfolded: the biology first, then the corporate history that turned the biology into a marketable molecule, then the cultural explosion that turned the molecule into a household name, then the manufacturing and pricing and insurance fights that explosion set off, then the medical questions — about muscle loss, about addiction, about what happens when a patient stops — that took years of additional research to begin answering properly, and finally the harder, less resolved questions about global access and what this whole episode reveals about how a culture processes a genuine biological correction to a belief it had held, with real moral confidence, for the better part of a century. Readers who want the science without the business history, or the business history without the cultural analysis, can read chapters out of order without losing much; the chapters are written to stand on their own while still adding up, in sequence, to a single continuous argument.

None of it was inevitable. A three-decade string of unglamorous, occasionally abandoned research programs had to happen in a particular order, at particular institutions, funded by particular bets that could easily have gone the other way. John Eng, a Veterans Affairs endocrinologist working with almost no institutional support, had to notice something strange about a lizard's venom and had to keep pushing an idea that most pharmaceutical companies found faintly ridiculous. A Danish company built around insulin, controlled since its founding by a nonprofit foundation rather than ordinary shareholders, had to keep funding an unglamorous research program for the better part of two decades before it produced a drug most patients could tolerate. A cardiovascular trial had to be run — expensively, over years, on tens of thousands of people — before anyone could say with real evidence that these drugs did more than move a number on a scale. And a public that had absorbed a half-century of moralized messaging about weight had to be willing, when the evidence arrived, to revise what it thought it knew.

Some of that revision is still happening as this book goes to press. It will keep happening after. New molecules are already moving through trials that outperform the drugs described in these pages, and the current cast of characters — a Danish pharmaceutical foundation vs. an Indianapolis drugmaker vs. a fragmented compounding industry vs. federal regulators vs. reinsurance actuaries trying to model twenty years of mortality they cannot yet observe — will not be the final cast. The number of people worldwide living with obesity is still projected to rise for years, even as the tools for treating it keep improving, because the food environment that produces the condition has not gone anywhere.

What this book tries to do is slow the story down enough to see how each piece connects to the next: the venom to the peptide, the peptide to the patent, the patent to the shortage, the shortage to the gray market, the market to the insurer's spreadsheet, the spreadsheet to the small country whose current-account balance now moves with a drug's sales figures, the drug's sales figures to a school cafeteria and a bariatric surgeon's shrinking patient list and a distillery watching its numbers for a reason nobody in the industry wants to say out loud.

It also tries to hold two things that are both true at once, because most of the public conversation about these drugs has room for only one at a time. The first is that this class of medicine represents one of the more consequential medical advances of the last several decades — not a vanity treatment, but a therapy with real, replicated evidence behind reduced heart attacks, strokes, and, in early but genuine findings, reduced drinking and possibly reduced risk from other compulsive behaviors. The second is that the way this therapy arrived — through a scarcity-driven, celebrity-accelerated, unevenly priced, unevenly insured, gray-market-adjacent commercial explosion — has produced real harm alongside the real benefit, and that harm has landed disproportionately on people who could least absorb it: diabetics who could not get the drug they had been using safely for years because a manufacturer could not make enough of it fast enough, patients who bought unregulated compounded versions because $1,000 a month was not a price they could pay, and countries that will not see affordable versions of these drugs for the better part of a decade because of where they happen to sit in a global patent system built for a different pace of demand.

Neither of those truths cancels the other. The molecule is real medicine. The market around the molecule has been, by turns, life-changing and reckless. Both belong in the same book, because they are, in fact, the same story.

That story starts, unavoidably, with an animal most people would rather avoid.
