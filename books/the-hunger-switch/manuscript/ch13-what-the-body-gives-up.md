# Chapter 13 — What the Body Gives Up

It is worth acknowledging, before this chapter turns to the specific research findings, why the lean-mass question took several years to receive the level of scientific and public attention chapter five's account of the STEP trials might have led a casual observer to expect. The original STEP and SURMOUNT trial programs, described in chapters five and nine, were designed primarily to measure total body weight and, separately, diabetes-relevant blood-sugar and cardiovascular markers — the outcomes regulators require for drug approval — rather than the more specialized body-composition measurements this chapter examines, which require additional equipment, additional study visits, and a research question that only became an urgent priority for the field once the drugs' scale of use made even a modest percentage of lean-mass loss, multiplied across tens of millions of patients, a genuinely significant population-health question rather than a narrow subspecialty concern. This lag between a drug's initial approval and the field's more detailed follow-up investigation of its secondary physiological effects is common across pharmaceutical history generally, but it meant that millions of patients had already been using these drugs for a year or more before the research base this chapter describes had fully caught up to characterize one of their more clinically significant side effects in real detail.

This chapter turns from the largely triumphant story chapters one through nine have told toward a more cautionary register this book maintains, in varying degrees, through most of its remaining chapters — a deliberate structural choice reflecting where the actual weight of evidence sits at this point in the book's account. The drugs work, as established beyond reasonable dispute by the trial evidence chapters five and nine documented. What this chapter and those that follow examine is less whether they work than what using them costs, in bodily tradeoffs, in dollars, in access, and in the still-unanswered questions any sufficiently new medical technology carries until enough years have passed to answer them properly. Weight loss, described in a single number on a chart, obscures a distinction that matters enormously to a patient's actual health: not all lost weight is the same kind of tissue, and not all of it is equally welcome to lose. The human body's mass divides broadly into fat mass and lean mass, and lean mass itself includes muscle, bone, organ tissue, and water — components that, unlike excess fat, generally serve functions a person benefits from keeping. A weight-loss method that removes fat while preserving muscle, bone density, and organ function is doing something different, physiologically, from a method that removes a similar total number of pounds through a mix of fat and lean tissue indiscriminately, even if the number on the bathroom scale looks identical at the end of either process.

GLP-1 receptor agonists, it turns out, do not spare lean mass entirely. Multiple studies examining body composition — not merely total weight — in patients undergoing GLP-1 and dual-agonist treatment have found that a meaningful share of the total weight lost consists of lean tissue rather than fat alone. A widely cited pooled analysis published in the medical journal Obesity and reported on extensively in early 2025 estimated that roughly 31 percent of the weight lost by adults during GLP-1 therapy comes from lean mass — a figure that sits within a broader range other studies have found, with some estimates running as low as 15 percent of total weight loss and others, particularly for the more potent dual- and triple-agonist drugs described in later chapters, running considerably higher, in some analyses toward 40 percent or more, depending on the specific drug, dose, patient population, and methodology used to measure body composition.

The dual- and triple-agonist drugs described in chapter nine and chapter nineteen appear, on the available evidence, to intensify this lean-mass concern rather than mitigate it, a pattern consistent with the broader relationship this book has traced between a drug's overall potency and the magnitude of essentially every effect it produces, wanted and unwanted alike. Tirzepatide's greater overall weight-loss effect, described in chapter nine, has been accompanied in body-composition sub-studies by lean-mass-loss figures generally running at or above the estimates this chapter has already cited for semaglutide alone, and early body-composition data from retatrutide's still-earlier-stage trials, described in chapter nineteen, suggested a broadly similar or somewhat larger effect at that molecule's own considerably higher overall weight-loss magnitude. This pattern reinforces a theme this book has returned to repeatedly: each incremental gain in this drug class's raw effectiveness has arrived paired with a corresponding, not-yet-fully-solved cost, whether in tolerability, as chapter nineteen's account of retatrutide's Phase 3 safety signal described, or in the specific body-composition tradeoff this chapter has examined in detail.

Context matters enormously in interpreting these figures, because lean-mass loss during weight loss is not, in itself, a novel or uniquely GLP-1-specific problem — it is a feature of essentially every effective weight-loss method available to medicine, including calorie restriction through diet alone and bariatric surgery, both of which also produce meaningful lean-mass loss alongside fat loss, generally in a broadly similar or in some comparisons even higher proportion than what the GLP-1 literature has found. The relevant clinical question is therefore not whether GLP-1 drugs cause any lean-mass loss at all — some degree of lean-mass loss appears to be a nearly unavoidable physiological consequence of any sufficiently large, sufficiently fast reduction in total body mass, regardless of method — but rather whether the specific proportion of lean-to-fat loss these drugs produce is meaningfully worse than available alternatives, and what can be done to mitigate it given how effective and how widely used these drugs have become.

The clinical significance of lean-mass loss varies considerably depending on who is losing it and how much they had to begin with. For a patient beginning treatment with substantial excess fat mass and adequate baseline muscle reserves, a degree of lean-mass loss alongside a much larger proportion of fat loss may carry limited practical consequence, particularly if the patient maintains physical activity and adequate protein intake throughout treatment — factors clinical guidelines increasingly emphasize specifically because of this body-composition concern. For an older adult, however, particularly one who already carries reduced muscle mass and bone density as an ordinary consequence of aging — a condition clinicians call sarcopenia when muscle loss becomes severe enough to affect strength and function — additional lean-mass loss driven by a GLP-1 drug's appetite-suppressing effect on overall caloric intake carries considerably higher stakes. Muscle mass and strength are directly tied, in a large body of geriatric-medicine research predating GLP-1 drugs entirely, to fall risk, frailty, hospitalization risk, and functional independence in older age; a drug that produces substantial weight loss in an elderly patient, including a meaningful lean-mass component, could in principle leave that patient thinner but also frailer, a tradeoff that becomes considerably harder to justify for an older patient with modest excess weight than for a younger patient carrying substantial excess fat mass and facing correspondingly larger cardiometabolic risk from remaining overweight.

Clinical guidelines issued by major endocrinology and obesity-medicine professional societies through 2024 and 2025 increasingly formalized the resistance-training and protein-intake recommendations this chapter has already described, moving them from informal physician best-practice advice toward standardized, published treatment protocols patients could expect to receive as a routine part of GLP-1 prescribing rather than as an optional addition dependent on an individual physician's own initiative. This formalization mattered practically: patients receiving a prescription alongside explicit, structured guidance about resistance exercise and protein targets were, according to early practice-pattern data, more likely to actually follow that guidance than patients receiving only the medication and a general, unstructured suggestion to "stay active," a familiar pattern in behavioral medicine generally, in which specific, concrete guidance embedded directly into a treatment protocol outperforms the same advice offered as a vague afterthought.

This concern has produced a genuinely active and still-evolving area of clinical research and practice recommendation, rather than a single settled answer. Endocrinologists and obesity-medicine specialists increasingly recommend that patients beginning GLP-1 therapy pair the medication with resistance training specifically — exercise that directly stresses muscle tissue and signals the body to preserve or build it, as distinct from purely aerobic exercise, which does less to counteract lean-mass loss during a period of caloric deficit — alongside increased dietary protein intake, since adequate protein availability is a prerequisite for the body to maintain existing muscle tissue even when overall caloric intake falls. Neither intervention fully eliminates the lean-mass-loss effect documented in the clinical literature, but both appear, across the available research, to meaningfully reduce its magnitude relative to patients who lose a similar amount of total weight without deliberately incorporating resistance exercise or elevated protein intake into their treatment plan.

The clearest evidence yet that this problem might be pharmacologically addressable, rather than simply managed through diet and exercise alone, came from a trial pairing semaglutide with an experimental antibody called bimagrumab, which works by blocking activin type II receptors — a different signaling pathway than the incretin biology described throughout this book, one involved specifically in regulating muscle growth rather than appetite. In the BELIEVE trial, sponsored by Versanis Bio, a company Eli Lilly later acquired, patients receiving the bimagrumab-semaglutide combination lost 22.1 percent of body weight at 72 weeks, with a remarkable 92.8 percent of that weight loss attributable to fat rather than lean tissue. Patients receiving semaglutide alone in the same trial lost a smaller 15.7 percent of body weight, with only 71.8 percent of that loss coming from fat — meaning the combination therapy produced not only more total weight loss but a dramatically more favorable ratio of fat loss to lean-mass loss, essentially the outcome obesity-medicine researchers had been seeking since the lean-mass concern first became a serious topic of discussion. The result generated considerable excitement in the field precisely because it suggested the muscle-loss side effect was not an unavoidable, intrinsic feature of rapid weight loss itself, but something that could, at least in principle, be pharmacologically decoupled from the fat-loss benefit using a second, complementary mechanism.

That excitement came with an important caveat that a careful reading of the broader bimagrumab research program requires acknowledging rather than glossing over: this is an actively developing, not yet fully proven, area of drug development, and not every related trial succeeded. Lilly subsequently halted a separate bimagrumab combination trial specifically in patients with type 2 diabetes, a decision reported in trade press without the company disclosing full details of its reasoning, a reminder that a single promising trial result — however genuinely encouraging the BELIEVE data looked — does not by itself guarantee that a muscle-preserving combination therapy will ultimately clear the full clinical-trial and regulatory process required to reach patients broadly, at the scale and reliability that Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro, and Zepbound had already achieved by the time this more targeted research was still working through its own earlier stages.

Pharmaceutical companies themselves have responded to this concern with active research into other combination therapies specifically designed to counteract the muscle-loss side effect while preserving or enhancing the fat-loss benefit — among the more closely watched approaches involves combining a GLP-1 or dual-agonist drug with a separate compound targeting myostatin or activin signaling pathways, biological systems that regulate muscle growth and that, when inhibited, can promote muscle mass retention or growth independent of a patient's caloric intake. Several such combination therapies were in active clinical development as this book was being written, though none had yet reached the same scale of trial evidence or regulatory approval that the underlying GLP-1 and dual-agonist drugs themselves had achieved — an area of research this book's later chapter on the next generation of obesity drugs returns to directly, because it represents one of the more scientifically interesting and commercially significant frontiers in the field's near-term future.

It is worth pausing on how researchers actually measure the fat-versus-lean-mass split described throughout this chapter, because the methodology carries its own limitations that are easy to lose sight of amid headline percentages. The gold-standard tool used in most of the relevant clinical trials is dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, commonly known as a DEXA or DXA scan, which estimates body composition by measuring how different tissue types absorb two different X-ray energy levels differently. DEXA scanning is far more precise than a bathroom scale or a simple body-mass-index calculation, but it is not a perfect direct measurement of muscle tissue specifically; it estimates "lean mass," a category that includes not only skeletal muscle but also organ tissue, connective tissue, and body water, meaning some of the reported lean-mass loss in these studies may reflect fluid shifts or other non-muscle changes rather than a reduction in contractile muscle tissue itself capable of generating strength. More recent research has begun supplementing DEXA data with direct strength and functional-performance testing — grip strength, walking speed, chair-stand tests — specifically to determine whether measured lean-mass loss actually translates into meaningful functional decline, since a patient could, in principle, lose some measured lean mass while experiencing little or no loss in actual physical strength or capability, particularly if resistance exercise is part of their treatment plan. This distinction between statistical lean-mass loss and clinically meaningful functional decline remains one of the more actively contested and still-developing areas of the research, and this book's account should not be read as claiming the science here is fully settled.

The stakes of getting this science right are highest for a specific, growing population that geriatric medicine had already identified, well before GLP-1 drugs existed, as facing a particularly dangerous combination of risk factors: patients with what clinicians call sarcopenic obesity, carrying both excess fat mass and critically low muscle mass simultaneously, a combination associated with worse outcomes than either condition alone, since the excess fat continues driving metabolic and cardiovascular risk while the deficient muscle mass undermines mobility, balance, and the ability to recover from illness, injury, or hospitalization. For this population, a treatment capable of aggressively reducing fat mass carries obvious appeal, but only if it can do so without further eroding an already-inadequate muscle reserve — precisely the scenario the bimagrumab research described above was designed to address, and precisely the population geriatric-medicine specialists have argued deserves the most individualized, most carefully monitored treatment planning of any group being prescribed these drugs, rather than the standardized dosing protocols developed primarily using data from younger, healthier trial populations in the original STEP and SURMOUNT programs described in earlier chapters.

It is worth situating the lean-mass-loss concern within the broader risk-benefit calculation this book has already traced across earlier chapters, rather than treating it as a standalone reason for alarm disconnected from everything else these drugs have been shown to do. The SELECT trial's cardiovascular-risk reduction, described in chapter eleven, and the broader weight-loss benefits documented across the STEP and SURMOUNT programs, described in chapters five and nine, represent real, measured, population-level reductions in serious disease and death — benefits that, for the large population of patients with significant excess fat mass and associated health risk, plausibly outweigh a partial, mitigable lean-mass-loss effect for most patients most of the time, particularly when that effect is actively managed through the resistance-training and protein-intake recommendations described above. But "plausibly outweighs for most patients most of the time" is a meaningfully different, more qualified claim than the unqualified enthusiasm that characterized much of the drugs' earlier cultural reception described in chapter six, and the more cautious, more individualized clinical framing that emerged as the lean-mass research matured through 2024 and 2025 represents a genuine, evidence-driven maturation of how the medical community talks about these drugs relative to their first several years on the market.

Bone density, a slower-moving but equally important component of the broader body-composition picture this chapter has examined, has drawn its own dedicated research attention for reasons closely related to the lean-mass concerns already described. Rapid, substantial weight loss through any method has long been associated in the medical literature with measurable reductions in bone mineral density, since reduced mechanical loading on the skeleton — quite simply, carrying less body weight around during ordinary daily movement — appears to send the body a signal that less bone-maintenance investment is needed, a genuine physiological tradeoff separate from, though related to, the muscle-preservation concerns discussed above. Researchers studying bone health in GLP-1 and dual-agonist trial participants have generally found measurable reductions in bone mineral density accompanying substantial weight loss, though the clinical significance of those reductions — whether they meaningfully increase fracture risk, particularly in younger, otherwise healthy patients who make up a large share of this drug class's overall user base — remains an active area of research rather than a fully settled question, and one that carries particular relevance for the adolescent patients this book examines directly in a later chapter, since bone density accumulated during adolescence forms a foundation the body draws on for the rest of a person's life.

The lean-mass question also intersects directly with a separate, related concern that received less scientific attention but considerable popular and media attention over the same period: changes in facial appearance that patients and observers began describing, informally but with striking consistency, as "Ozempic face" — a hollowed, aged appearance some patients reported noticing after substantial and rapid weight loss, attributed by dermatologists and plastic surgeons to the loss of facial fat volume that had previously filled out and supported the skin, producing an appearance of accelerated aging disproportionate to the patient's actual chronological age. This phenomenon is not unique to GLP-1 drugs specifically — any sufficiently rapid, sufficiently large weight loss, through any method, tends to produce some degree of facial volume loss, a pattern plastic surgeons had observed for years in patients who had undergone rapid weight loss through bariatric surgery or aggressive dieting, long before GLP-1 drugs existed. What made "Ozempic face" a distinctly named and widely discussed phenomenon, rather than simply an obscure clinical observation, was the sheer scale and speed at which large numbers of patients were now achieving this kind of rapid, substantial weight loss simultaneously and publicly, in a culture already primed by celebrity coverage, described in chapter six, to scrutinize and comment on famous people's changing faces and bodies in granular, often unkind detail. This book returns to the broader cultural and psychological dimensions of that scrutiny — what it reveals about beauty standards, aging, and the value the culture places on visible thinness even when that thinness comes at a genuine physical cost — in its closing chapters, once the full arc of the drugs' medical, commercial, and cultural story has been laid out.
