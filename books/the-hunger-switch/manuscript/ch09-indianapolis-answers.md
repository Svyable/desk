# Chapter 9 — Indianapolis Answers

This chapter's account of a second major company reaching essentially the same scientific destination as Novo Nordisk, via a related but distinct research path, is worth reading against the broader pattern of parallel scientific discovery this book's earlier chapters have already illustrated more than once — Bayliss and Starling's secretin work anticipating La Barre's incretin concept, described in chapter three, or the roughly simultaneous mid-1980s identification of GLP-1 by multiple independent research groups referenced in that same chapter. Major scientific advances, this recurring pattern suggests, rarely emerge from a single laboratory working in true isolation from the rest of its field; they tend to emerge from multiple well-resourced teams working the same underlying biological terrain roughly simultaneously, each drawing on a shared, publicly published scientific literature even while competing commercially, arriving at related but distinct solutions within a few years of each other rather than one team achieving a decades-long unilateral head start. Lilly's tirzepatide, arriving roughly five years after Novo Nordisk's Ozempic and outperforming it in key trial measures, fits this pattern closely, and previews the same competitive dynamic this book's nineteenth chapter shows continuing into the current wave of triple-agonist and combination-therapy research, where multiple companies are again pursuing closely related scientific strategies on overlapping timelines.

Eli Lilly and Company had been in the diabetes business for almost as long as Novo Nordisk. The Indianapolis-based firm had been one of the first companies in the world to commercially manufacture insulin in the 1920s, working with the same Toronto researchers, Frederick Banting and his collaborators, whose discovery had sent August Krogh across the Atlantic in the opposite direction to found what became Novo Nordisk. For a century, Lilly and Novo Nordisk had been the two dominant global names in insulin manufacturing, competitors across essentially every major diabetes market in the world, each watching the other's research pipeline as closely as any two companies in the same specialized therapeutic area inevitably do.

So when Novo Nordisk's GLP-1 franchise, first Byetta's exenatide-market predecessor and later Victoza, Saxenda, Ozempic, and finally Wegovy, began demonstrating the scale of both the clinical opportunity and the commercial reward available in incretin-based therapies, Lilly's own research organization was neither surprised by the underlying biology nor starting from zero. Lilly had its own long-running incretin research program, and rather than simply attempting to build a better single-hormone GLP-1 analogue to compete directly with semaglutide on Novo Nordisk's own terms, Lilly's scientists pursued a different, more ambitious engineering strategy: a single molecule that would activate not just the GLP-1 receptor but a second, related receptor as well, for a hormone called GIP — the same glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide that chapter three of this book identified as the first incretin hormone discovered, back in 1970, alongside GLP-1's own more consequential discovery in the 1980s.

GIP had, for decades, occupied a more ambiguous position in incretin research than GLP-1. Some earlier research had suggested that GIP signaling might actually be impaired or less effective in people with type 2 diabetes, which led parts of the field to deprioritize it relative to GLP-1 as a drug-development target through the 1990s and 2000s. Lilly's researchers, working through a more sophisticated understanding of GIP's role that developed over the following years, took a different bet: that a molecule capable of activating both the GLP-1 and GIP receptors simultaneously — a dual agonist, in the pharmacological terminology this book will use throughout its remaining chapters — might produce effects that neither receptor pathway could produce as effectively on its own, potentially compounding rather than merely adding to the appetite-suppression and blood-sugar benefits each pathway offered individually.

Lilly's own corporate history gave it, like Novo Nordisk, a century-long institutional identity built substantially around diabetes rather than a generalist pharmaceutical portfolio that happened to include it. The company had been founded in 1876 by Colonel Eli Lilly, a Union Army veteran and trained pharmaceutical chemist, initially as a manufacturer of pharmaceutical products more broadly rather than a diabetes specialist. But its 1923 licensing agreement with the Toronto team that had discovered insulin — negotiated, in a near-mirror of August Krogh's own Scandinavian licensing arrangement described in chapter two, in the very same year Nordisk Insulinlaboratorium began treating its first Danish patients — made Lilly the first company to manufacture insulin commercially in the United States, and diabetes care remained one of the company's core therapeutic identities across the following century of subsequent growth into other disease areas including oncology, neuroscience, and immunology. By the time GLP-1 biology became a serious drug-development target industry-wide, Lilly's diabetes research organization carried decades of accumulated expertise in exactly the physiological territory tirzepatide would need: insulin biology, glucose metabolism, and — crucially, given GIP's more uncertain scientific reputation through the 1990s and 2000s — enough internal research depth to make a confident, well-informed bet on a receptor pathway that a large share of the rest of the field had deprioritized.

That bet on GIP required Lilly's scientists to work through a genuinely unresolved scientific debate rather than a settled consensus. Some of the earlier research suggesting impaired GIP signaling in type 2 diabetes patients had left open real questions about whether restoring or amplifying that signal, on top of an already-active GLP-1 pathway, would help, do nothing, or even work against the drug's other effects. Lilly's own preclinical and early clinical data eventually supported a more optimistic reading: that combining GIP receptor activation with GLP-1 receptor activation produced effects on appetite, energy expenditure, and glucose metabolism that were not simply additive but appeared, in the trial results that followed, to exceed what either single-receptor pathway achieved on its own — a genuinely novel pharmacological outcome that took Lilly's research organization the better part of two decades of dedicated GIP and GLP-1 biology research, running in parallel with Novo Nordisk's own single-hormone semaglutide program on the other side of the Atlantic, to fully validate in human trials.

The molecule that emerged from this research program was named tirzepatide, engineered using a fatty-acid-acylation strategy broadly similar in principle to the one described in chapter four for liraglutide and semaglutide — a single peptide backbone modified to bind both the GLP-1 and GIP receptors, further modified with a fatty-diacid chain enabling albumin binding and a once-weekly dosing schedule matching semaglutide's own. Lilly ran its own large clinical-trial program, called SURMOUNT for the obesity-focused studies, structured similarly to Novo Nordisk's STEP program in overall design, and the first major results, from the SURMOUNT-1 trial, were published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2022, directly comparable in design and scale to Novo Nordisk's STEP 1 trial the year before.

The SURMOUNT-1 results were, by the standards even semaglutide had already reset, remarkable. Participants receiving tirzepatide lost an average of 16.0 percent of body weight at the lowest of three tested doses, 21.4 percent at the middle dose, and 22.5 percent at the highest dose — compared with 2.4 percent for placebo — meaning tirzepatide's highest dose outperformed semaglutide's STEP 1 result of roughly 14.9 percent average weight loss by a wide and clinically meaningful margin. More striking still were the secondary results describing how many participants crossed specific weight-loss thresholds: 55 percent of participants on the 10-milligram dose and 63 percent on the 15-milligram dose achieved at least 20 percent total body-weight reduction, a threshold that placed a majority of trial participants within range of outcomes historically associated primarily with bariatric surgery rather than with any pharmaceutical intervention — a comparison this book examines directly in a later chapter on what happened to the bariatric-surgery industry once results at this scale became widely known among both physicians and prospective patients.

As with Novo Nordisk's STEP program, tirzepatide's SURMOUNT trials extended well beyond the single headline SURMOUNT-1 result. SURMOUNT-2 tested the drug in patients with both obesity and type 2 diabetes, the same harder-to-treat population Novo Nordisk's STEP 2 trial had examined, and again found tirzepatide producing strong, clinically meaningful weight loss even in a population where diabetes itself tends to blunt the maximum achievable effect. SURMOUNT-3 examined tirzepatide following an initial period of intensive lifestyle-only intervention, testing whether patients who had already made significant behavioral changes could see additional benefit from adding the drug — they could, substantially, reinforcing the broader pattern established throughout this drug class that pharmacological and behavioral interventions tend to compound rather than substitute for each other. And SURMOUNT-4, using a withdrawal design closely comparable to Novo Nordisk's STEP 4 trial, found a similar and by this point unsurprising result: patients who discontinued tirzepatide after an initial period of substantial weight loss experienced significant weight regain over the following months, further corroborating the chronic-disease, ongoing-treatment framing this book examines in detail in its later chapter on what happens when patients stop taking these drugs. That two independently run, competing companies' withdrawal trials reached essentially the same conclusion did more to establish that finding as a robust, class-wide pharmacological reality — rather than an idiosyncrasy of one company's specific molecule — than either trial could have accomplished alone.

The FDA approved tirzepatide for type 2 diabetes under the brand name Mounjaro in 2022, and for chronic weight management under a separate brand name, Zepbound, in November 2023 — following, in broad structural outline, the same two-brand-name pattern Novo Nordisk had already established with Ozempic and Wegovy, a diabetes-labeled product and a separately branded, higher-dose obesity product built around the identical active ingredient. The approval placed Lilly, within a little over a year of Wegovy's own approval, as a direct and immediately credible competitor across essentially the entire commercial territory Novo Nordisk had spent the preceding several years establishing — and Zepbound's trial data gave physicians and patients a genuine basis for comparison, and for many clinicians and patients, a genuine basis for preference, at exactly the moment the market for these drugs was expanding fastest and the two companies' combined manufacturing capacity was least able to keep pace with demand.

It is worth noting that this book's narrative structure, devoting an entire chapter to Lilly's tirzepatide story after five preceding chapters focused almost exclusively on Novo Nordisk, risks understating how genuinely concurrent the two companies' development timelines actually were. Lilly's own GIP and GLP-1 research did not begin only after Novo Nordisk's Ozempic reached the market in 2017; it had been underway for years beforehand, running in parallel with, rather than strictly reacting to, the semaglutide program this book's earlier chapters described in detail. The sequential, chapter-by-chapter structure this book has adopted for narrative clarity — Novo Nordisk's full story first, then Lilly's — should not be read as implying Lilly was a purely reactive latecomer; the two companies' research programs, like the parallel incretin-discovery efforts described in chapter three, developed on substantially overlapping timelines within a shared, competitive, and mutually observant pharmaceutical research landscape.

The competitive dynamic that followed reshaped the financial narrative around both companies through 2023 and 2024 in ways that went well beyond ordinary pharmaceutical-industry rivalry. Eli Lilly's own market capitalization surged as Zepbound and Mounjaro sales scaled rapidly, at various points during this period overtaking Novo Nordisk's own valuation to become, briefly, the most valuable pharmaceutical company in the world by market capitalization — a title the two companies traded back and forth repeatedly as quarterly earnings, trial readouts, and shifting investor sentiment about each company's respective pipeline moved billions of dollars of value in either direction with a frequency that itself became a recurring business-news story independent of either company's underlying clinical progress. By 2024, the combined annual revenue from just these two drug families — Ozempic and Wegovy on one side, Mounjaro and Zepbound on the other — had reached a scale, discussed in more detail in the following chapter on pricing, that placed this single therapeutic category among the largest revenue-generating drug classes in the entire history of the pharmaceutical industry, a two-company duopoly competing for a market that both companies' own internal manufacturing capacity, even after years of expansion, still struggled to fully satisfy.

Both companies translated that competitive urgency into manufacturing investment at a scale that had few precedents in the pharmaceutical industry's history, precisely because both had lived through the shortage years described in the previous chapter and understood, in the most direct way possible, that manufacturing capacity rather than clinical results had become the binding constraint on revenue growth. Lilly committed to a series of expansions at a massive new manufacturing campus in Lebanon, Indiana, roughly thirty miles from its Indianapolis headquarters, breaking ground in April 2023 with an initial investment in the billions of dollars that grew, through successive expansion announcements over the following two years, to a total commitment of roughly $9 billion at that single site alone, specifically earmarked for producing the active pharmaceutical ingredient used in tirzepatide and related pipeline medicines. A separate $1.7 billion plant under construction in Concord, North Carolina, and a further $4.5 billion commitment toward a dedicated advanced-manufacturing and drug-development facility Lilly named the Lilly Medicine Foundry, brought the company's total announced incretin-era manufacturing investment into the tens of billions of dollars — capital commitments that would not begin producing meaningful additional supply until years after they were announced, given the multi-year construction and regulatory-validation timelines chapter seven has already described, but that signaled, as clearly as a corporate balance sheet can signal anything, how large both companies now believed the addressable market for this drug class would ultimately become.

This head-to-head competition, whatever its financial drama, mattered enormously to patients and physicians for reasons more substantive than stock-price rivalry. Having two, rather than one, approved and increasingly available GLP-1-based drug options meant that a patient who tolerated one poorly, or who had access to one manufacturer's supply through a specific insurance formulary or pharmacy relationship but not the other's, had a genuine alternative rather than facing an all-or-nothing choice between a single available therapy and none at all — a meaningful, if partial, mitigant to the shortage crisis described in the previous chapter, since a shortage of Wegovy specifically was somewhat less catastrophic for a patient's overall treatment options once Zepbound existed as a clinically comparable, separately manufactured alternative competing for the same overall pool of raw materials and manufacturing capacity but not identical to it. Competition between the two companies also, over time, began exerting the kind of downward pricing pressure that a genuine duopoly can produce even in a market as historically insulated from ordinary price competition as American pharmaceuticals — both companies, at various points through 2024 and 2025, introduced lower-cost direct-to-consumer purchasing options and vial-based alternatives to the branded pen devices, moves that industry analysts widely attributed at least in part to competitive pressure from the other company's presence in the market, alongside the separate and considerable pressure both companies faced from the compounding-pharmacy gray market described in a later chapter.

The rivalry also played out in the more mundane, day-to-day language of clinical practice, in ways that shaped how physicians and patients actually experienced having a choice for the first time. Endocrinologists and primary-care physicians managing patients on either drug developed informal clinical preferences based on individual patient response, tolerability, and, increasingly as both companies rolled out lower-cost direct channels, cost — preferences that varied considerably from one physician's practice to another and that neither company's marketing department could fully control, since actual prescribing behavior across tens of thousands of independent practices proved, as it always does, considerably messier and more locally contingent than either company's national sales strategy anticipated. Patient communities that had already formed around Ozempic and Wegovy during the earlier celebrity-driven cultural wave described in chapter six absorbed Zepbound and Mounjaro into the same online spaces, generating their own comparative folklore — informal, patient-generated claims about which drug produced fewer side effects, more sustained results, or an easier dose-escalation experience — some of which tracked reasonably well with the actual comparative trial data that direct head-to-head studies would eventually produce, and some of which reflected the ordinary noise of individual variation dressed up as generalizable pattern, a distinction this book's later chapters, dealing with the drugs' effects on specific outcomes like lean muscle mass and cardiovascular risk, try to draw more carefully than casual online discussion generally managed to.

The historical echo between Lilly's and Novo Nordisk's parallel 1920s insulin-licensing origins, described earlier in this chapter, extends into a further symmetry worth noting: both companies, a century apart, made their most consequential GLP-1-era decisions inside institutional cultures shaped by the same founding-era commitment to diabetes care as a core identity rather than one product line among many. Lilly, unlike Novo Nordisk, did not organize itself around a foundation-controlled ownership structure of the kind chapter two described in detail — it remained a conventional publicly traded corporation throughout the twentieth century, answerable to ordinary shareholders in the way Novo Nordisk's foundation-controlled structure was specifically designed to moderate. That Lilly nonetheless sustained a multi-decade GIP and GLP-1 research program through years of uncertain payoff, without the same institutional insulation from quarterly shareholder pressure that chapter two identified as one explanation for Novo Nordisk's own patience, suggests that Novo Nordisk's foundation structure, whatever advantage it provided, was not the only path by which a century-old diabetes-focused pharmaceutical culture could sustain the kind of patient, uncertain research bet this book has traced across its opening chapters — institutional identity and accumulated scientific culture, this comparison suggests, may have mattered at least as much as ownership structure in explaining why both companies, rather than only one, were positioned to make this leap when the underlying biology finally matured.

Lilly's success with tirzepatide also validated, in a way that mattered considerably for the pace of innovation across the rest of the pharmaceutical industry, the broader dual- and multi-hormone-agonist strategy that Novo Nordisk's own CagriSema program, described in the previous chapter, and a wave of competing biotechnology and pharmaceutical companies would go on to pursue through the mid-2020s. If a molecule engineered to hit two separate hormone receptors simultaneously could outperform a well-optimized single-hormone analogue as decisively as tirzepatide's SURMOUNT-1 results suggested it could, the logical next question for researchers across the industry became obvious, and this book examines the most aggressive version of that question — molecules engineered to hit three hormone receptors simultaneously — directly in a later chapter on the next generation of drugs already moving through clinical trials as this book was being written. Indianapolis had not merely matched Copenhagen's achievement. It had demonstrated that the underlying scientific approach still had considerable room to run, and that the race to push it further had, if anything, only just begun.
