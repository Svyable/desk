# Chapter 11 — Who Pays

To understand why a single statutory phrase could carry this much weight, it helps to see how unusual the weight-loss exclusion actually is relative to how Medicare treats other categories of medication. Medicare Part D otherwise covers a remarkably broad range of prescription drugs across nearly every other therapeutic category, from cancer treatment to mental-health medication to rare-disease therapies costing hundreds of thousands of dollars a year — the program's general design philosophy leans toward broad coverage of physician-directed treatment, constrained mainly by formulary tiering and cost-sharing rather than blanket category exclusions. The weight-loss exclusion, along with a small number of other categorical carve-outs written into the original Part D statute — including exclusions for fertility drugs and certain cosmetic treatments — stands out precisely because it treats an entire category of medical need as categorically ineligible for coverage regardless of the specific clinical circumstances of an individual physician's prescribing decision, a blunt, disease-category-based exclusion of a kind Medicare rarely applies elsewhere in its formulary architecture.

Buried in the statute that created Medicare Part D, the prescription-drug benefit added to the American Medicare program in 2003, is a provision excluding coverage for drugs used for "weight loss." The exclusion was not written with GLP-1 receptor agonists in mind — semaglutide would not exist as an approved drug for another fourteen years — but rather reflected a much older set of assumptions, dating back decades further, that treated weight-loss medications as closer to a lifestyle or cosmetic category than to a treatment for a diagnosed chronic disease, assumptions that this book's third chapter has already traced back to a century of moralized thinking about appetite and willpower that outlasted the biology that eventually undermined it. That single statutory exclusion, largely unremarked upon for the better part of two decades, became one of the most consequential access barriers in this entire story once a drug existed capable of producing the STEP and SURMOUNT trial results described in earlier chapters — because it meant that Medicare, the government insurance program covering the majority of Americans over 65 and a population in which obesity-related health complications are especially common and especially costly, could not, under existing law, cover Wegovy or Zepbound for weight management no matter how compelling the clinical evidence became.

It is worth noting how much this chapter's account of Medicare's weight-loss exclusion depends on a distinction — between a drug's approved indication and its actual clinical use — that this book's earlier chapters have already shown to be considerably blurrier in practice than the statutory language assumes. Chapter five described how Ozempic's off-label use for weight management, well ahead of Wegovy's own approval, was already widespread before any formal weight-management indication existed at all; the coverage architecture this chapter examines assumes a considerably cleaner separation between "diabetes drug" and "weight-loss drug" than the actual, blended reality of how physicians and patients used these medications throughout the period this book covers. That gap between statutory categories and clinical reality is not unique to Medicare or to this drug class, but it is unusually visible here, precisely because the same active ingredient, sold under two different brand names for two different approved indications, makes the artificiality of the underlying legal distinction considerably harder to ignore than it would be for two chemically unrelated drugs serving genuinely separate purposes.

The exclusion did not, and does not, apply uniformly across every possible use of these drugs, and the exceptions matter considerably to how coverage actually played out. Ozempic and Mounjaro, approved specifically for type 2 diabetes rather than for weight management, fall outside the weight-loss exclusion and have generally been covered by Medicare Part D for patients with a diabetes diagnosis, subject to the ordinary cost-sharing and formulary rules that apply to any covered medication. And in March 2024, the FDA approved Wegovy for a second indication beyond weight management alone: reducing the risk of major cardiovascular events — heart attack, stroke, and cardiovascular death — in adults with established cardiovascular disease who also have obesity or overweight, an indication built directly on the SELECT trial results this book examines in the following section of this chapter. Because that cardiovascular-risk-reduction indication is not, in the statute's narrow technical sense, a weight-loss indication, it opened a partial pathway for Medicare Part D plans to cover Wegovy specifically for patients who qualified under the new cardiovascular label — a meaningful expansion of access, but one that still left the much larger population of Medicare beneficiaries with obesity alone, without established cardiovascular disease, outside any covered pathway.

Private-sector actuarial analysis produced its own, methodologically distinct estimates of the coverage question's fiscal stakes, generally arriving at figures in the same broad range as the CBO's federal projections once adjusted for the different, larger population employer and commercial insurance plans cover collectively compared with Medicare alone. These parallel private analyses mattered for a reason distinct from the federal budget debate itself: they gave individual employers and insurers, weighing their own coverage decisions independent of what Medicare or Medicaid ultimately did, a basis for estimating their own specific exposure — data that, once available, tended to make employers considerably more cautious about open-ended coverage commitments than the initial wave of enthusiastic early adoption described in chapter six might have predicted, a caution that shaped the more restrictive coverage patterns described later in this chapter.

The SELECT trial itself, whose results underpinned that partial coverage expansion, deserves attention independent of its regulatory consequences, because it represents one of the more genuinely important pieces of evidence in this entire drug class's history — evidence that shifted the scientific and public conversation about these drugs from "an effective treatment for a cosmetic or quality-of-life concern" toward "a treatment that measurably prevents heart attacks and strokes," a considerably higher evidentiary bar that most weight-loss interventions in medical history had never previously cleared. SELECT enrolled 17,604 adults across 41 countries and more than 800 study sites, all of whom had established cardiovascular disease and were overweight or obese but did not have diabetes — a population chosen specifically to isolate the cardiovascular effect of semaglutide from any confounding effect the drug might have on diabetes-related cardiovascular risk, since diabetes itself is such a major cardiovascular risk factor that any trial including diabetic patients would struggle to attribute an observed cardiovascular benefit cleanly to weight loss and appetite regulation rather than to improved blood-sugar control. Over a mean follow-up of just under 40 months, semaglutide reduced the combined risk of cardiovascular death, non-fatal heart attack, or non-fatal stroke by 20 percent compared with placebo, a reduction consistent across age, sex, race, ethnicity, baseline body-mass index, and kidney function — a result robust enough, and clinically significant enough, to represent one of the most consequential cardiovascular-prevention findings published in general medicine in years, not merely within the narrower field of obesity medicine.

The significance of the SELECT results for the coverage debate is worth stating plainly: they moved the argument for insurance coverage away from ground that obesity-medicine advocates had been forced to fight on for decades — that obesity itself deserved treatment as a serious chronic disease, an argument that, however scientifically sound, had never fully overcome the residual cultural skepticism this book's third chapter described — and onto ground that is considerably harder for an insurer or a government program to dismiss: hard, measurable reductions in heart attacks and strokes, the leading cause of death in the country, demonstrated in a large, rigorous, randomized trial. That a drug's proven ability to prevent fatal cardiovascular events still failed to unlock full, unrestricted government insurance coverage says a great deal about how deeply entrenched the statutory and cultural distinction between "obesity treatment" and "legitimate medical care" had become by the time the evidence arrived to challenge it.

The most consequential recent chapter in this coverage fight played out at the federal regulatory level in late 2024 and 2025, and its resolution illustrates how much these access questions remain contested rather than settled even after the SELECT results and the broader accumulation of evidence this book has traced. In November 2024, during the final months of the Biden administration, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services proposed a new statutory interpretation that would have reinterpreted the existing weight-loss exclusion more narrowly, treating obesity itself as a disease deserving of Medicare and Medicaid coverage for approved anti-obesity medications regardless of a separate cardiovascular or diabetes indication — a reinterpretation that legal analysts described as within CMS's existing regulatory authority without requiring new legislation from Congress, since the agency was not proposing to remove the statutory exclusion but rather to redefine which conditions counted as sufficiently disease-like to fall outside it. Had it been finalized, the proposal would have represented the single largest expansion of American insurance coverage for these drugs in the entire history of the drug class, extending Medicare and Medicaid coverage to a population of tens of millions of beneficiaries with obesity who had previously had no pathway to coverage at all.

The scale of the fiscal question at stake in that proposal is worth putting in concrete terms, because it explains much of the political resistance the proposal ultimately met even before the change in administration. CMS's own internal estimate put the cost of the coverage expansion at roughly $24.8 billion in additional Medicare spending over a decade and $14.8 billion in additional Medicaid spending, split between $11.0 billion in federal costs and $3.8 billion falling on state budgets already under their own separate fiscal pressure. The Congressional Budget Office, reviewing the same proposal independently, arrived at a considerably higher figure: net federal spending increases of roughly $35.5 billion from 2026 through 2034, reflecting gross spending on the medications themselves of nearly $39 billion only partially offset by an estimated $3.4 billion in savings from reduced spending on other medical care the drugs were expected to prevent. That gap between the two estimates — CMS's more optimistic accounting and the CBO's more conservative one — became its own point of contention in the policy debate, since it bore directly on the plausibility of the argument, made by coverage advocates, that the drugs' upfront cost would substantially pay for itself over time through avoided downstream healthcare spending; the CBO's analysis, more skeptical of how large and how quickly those offsetting savings would materialize, gave critics of the expansion a credible, nonpartisan basis for treating the proposal as a large net new spending commitment rather than a roughly cost-neutral one. The CBO's analysis also flagged a separate, favorable dynamic working in the opposite direction: its expectation that semaglutide would be selected for inclusion in Medicare's newly created drug-price-negotiation program, with a negotiated price taking effect in 2027, meaning the per-patient cost underlying any future coverage decision was itself expected to fall over the following years regardless of how the specific coverage-expansion proposal was resolved.

It was not finalized. Following the change in presidential administration in January 2025, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, under new leadership, declined to proceed with the Biden-era proposal. The Trump administration's finalized 2026 Medicare Advantage and Part D policies, published in the Federal Register on April 4, 2025, did not include the proposed anti-obesity medication coverage expansion, effectively leaving the original, narrower coverage landscape — diabetes coverage under Ozempic and Mounjaro's labels, partial cardiovascular-risk coverage under Wegovy's newer label, and no direct weight-management coverage for the general Medicare population — in place. The reversal was consistent with a broader set of priorities the incoming administration brought to healthcare and pharmaceutical-spending policy, including general skepticism toward large new entitlement-program spending commitments, but it left millions of Medicare beneficiaries with clinically diagnosed obesity, and no qualifying cardiovascular diagnosis or diabetes diagnosis, without a covered pathway to these drugs precisely at the moment the clinical evidence for their broader health benefits had become strongest.

The disability-rights and civil-rights framing of obesity coverage disputes added a further legal dimension to this fight that ran parallel to, but distinct from, the pure cost-benefit calculations described above. Some disability-rights advocates argued that categorically excluding treatment for a condition increasingly recognized in clinical guidelines as a chronic disease, while covering pharmaceutical treatment for other chronic conditions without a comparable exclusion, raised genuine questions under federal disability-rights and anti-discrimination law, an argument that had not, as this book was being written, produced a definitive court ruling forcing broader coverage, but that added legal uncertainty and litigation risk to insurers' and employers' coverage decisions beyond the pure budget question this chapter has focused on. That legal uncertainty cut in a direction favorable to broader coverage over time, several health-policy attorneys argued, even without a single decisive court victory, simply because the threat of future litigation made narrow, obesity-specific exclusions a somewhat riskier long-term posture for insurers to maintain than the cost data alone might have suggested.

The 2024-2025 reversal described above is worth reading against the broader pattern of policy instability this book has documented elsewhere — the same kind of abrupt, administration-driven reversal shaping the 503B compounding rules examined in chapter twelve, and the same kind of unpredictable regulatory environment that made both patient access and corporate manufacturing planning, described across several of this book's chapters, considerably harder to forecast confidently than a purely evidence-driven policy process would ideally allow.

The private insurance and employer-plan landscape moved somewhat independently of this federal back-and-forth, shaped less by statutory interpretation than by direct cost-benefit calculations employers and insurers made about their own covered populations. A large employer deciding whether to add anti-obesity drug coverage to its health plan faces a genuinely difficult actuarial calculation: the immediate, certain cost of covering a drug running to hundreds or thousands of dollars a month for a potentially large share of its workforce, weighed against uncertain, longer-term savings from reduced diabetes complications, reduced cardiovascular events, reduced disability claims, and improved general workforce health — savings that, even where real, often accrue over a timescale of years, frequently longer than the average employee's tenure at a given employer or the average time before an employee switches insurance plans altogether, weakening the direct financial incentive for any single employer to make the upfront investment even when the aggregate, society-wide case for doing so is strong. Some large employers, particularly larger, better-resourced companies competing for talent in tight labor markets where a generous health benefit could function as a meaningful recruiting and retention tool, added coverage during this period; many others, especially smaller employers and self-insured plans facing the direct, immediate cost exposure most acutely, did not, or added coverage with strict prior-authorization requirements, BMI thresholds, and step-therapy rules requiring patients to first try and fail cheaper alternatives before qualifying for GLP-1 coverage.

Some large employers moved in the opposite direction after initially adding coverage, once the actual cost of covering a genuinely popular, high-uptake drug class for a large workforce became clear in their own claims data. Several major employers and at least one large state government employee health plan, having added GLP-1 coverage in the early enthusiasm following the STEP and SURMOUNT results, subsequently narrowed eligibility criteria, added stricter BMI or comorbidity thresholds, or in a small number of well-publicized cases eliminated weight-management coverage for these drugs entirely, citing costs that had grown far beyond initial actuarial projections as uptake among eligible employees exceeded what benefits consultants had originally modeled. This pattern — enthusiastic initial coverage expansion followed by retrenchment once real-world utilization and cost data arrived — illustrates a structural tension that runs through this entire chapter: a drug effective enough that a very large share of any given eligible population will want to use it, once its coverage is announced, produces cost exposure that is inherently difficult for any single payer, whether a government program or a private employer, to predict accurately in advance, since ordinary actuarial modeling assumes utilization rates closer to those of previous, considerably less effective and less popular treatments.

Veterans Health Administration coverage followed its own, separate policy track worth noting briefly, since the VA operates outside the Medicare Part D statutory framework this chapter has focused on and sets its own formulary policy independently. The VA's own coverage decisions for GLP-1 medications evolved through this period largely along clinical-guideline lines similar to the private-sector patterns described above — covering the drugs readily for diagnosed diabetes and, increasingly, for the cardiovascular indication described earlier in this chapter, while applying more restrictive criteria for weight-management-only prescriptions — illustrating that the basic coverage tensions this chapter has documented were not simply an artifact of the private insurance system's fragmented, employer-driven structure, but recurred even within a unified, single-payer-style federal health system operating under its own separate statutory authority.

State Medicaid programs, which provide coverage for lower-income Americans and which each state administers with considerable independent discretion over which optional benefits to include, produced an even more fragmented picture, with some states electing to cover anti-obesity medications under their Medicaid programs, generally with restrictive eligibility criteria, and others declining to do so at all, citing budget constraints — a reasonable concern given that state Medicaid budgets, unlike the federal government's, generally operate under balanced-budget requirements that leave little room to absorb an open-ended new spending category without offsetting cuts elsewhere or new revenue. The result, across this entire patchwork of Medicare, Medicaid, and private and employer insurance, was a coverage landscape that varied enormously depending on a patient's specific diagnosis, specific insurer, specific employer, and specific state of residence — a genuinely two-tiered system in which access to one of the most significant advances in chronic-disease treatment in decades depended less on medical need, in any strict clinical sense, than on the particular administrative category a given patient happened to fall into.

That fragmented, unevenly resourced coverage landscape, layered on top of the shortage described in chapter seven and the list prices described in chapter ten, created exactly the conditions under which an alternative, considerably less regulated market could flourish: a large population of patients who wanted, and in many cases had a legitimate clinical case for, a medication they could not reliably obtain through their insurance, at a price they could not sustainably pay out of pocket, precisely as press coverage and social media made them more aware than any previous generation of patients had ever been of a drug's existence and its results. That combination of unmet demand, price pressure, and a legal loophole tied to the same federal shortage designation described in chapter seven is where the compounding-pharmacy industry examined in the next chapter found its opening — and, eventually, its considerable regulatory reckoning.
