# Chapter 4 — The Molecule That Would Not Behave

This chapter's account of a molecule engineered, quite literally, one modification at a time over two decades illustrates a broader truth about pharmaceutical chemistry that is easy to lose amid this book's larger story of markets, shortages, and celebrity culture: a drug like semaglutide is not discovered so much as built, through hundreds of individual laboratory decisions about which amino acid to substitute, which fatty-acid chain length to test, which linker chemistry to try next, each decision informed by the results of the last and each carrying its own risk of dead-ending the entire program. The chemists and biologists who made those decisions across the Novo Nordisk research organization this chapter describes are, for the most part, not individually named in this book, not because their contributions were unimportant, but because pharmaceutical discovery of this kind is, almost without exception, a collective achievement distributed across dozens or hundreds of researchers over many years, in a way that makes the popular tendency to attribute a drug's discovery to a single named scientist — useful as shorthand, and not wholly inaccurate for the specific leadership role this chapter has attributed to Knudsen — something of a simplification of how the underlying laboratory work actually got done.

By the early 1990s, Novo Nordisk had a GLP-1 research program, and the program had a problem that its own leadership was increasingly willing to say out loud: the molecule would not behave. Native human GLP-1, as the previous chapters have already established, lasted only a couple of minutes in circulation before an enzyme called DPP-4 broke it apart. Every early attempt to build a usable drug around it ran headlong into that fact. Researchers could demonstrate, in short-term experiments, that GLP-1 did everything the physiology predicted — improved insulin release, slowed gastric emptying, reduced appetite — and then watch the effect vanish within minutes as the molecule itself vanished from the bloodstream. It was, from a drug-development standpoint, a hormone that worked beautifully in principle and almost not at all in the practical terms a pharmaceutical company needed: something a patient could inject once, or at most a few times a week, and expect to keep working.

By the middle of the decade, several of Knudsen's superiors at Novo Nordisk had, by later accounts of the period, largely lost confidence in the program. It was expensive, unglamorous, technically frustrating, and competing for research budget against other projects with clearer paths to market. The company had a strong, functioning insulin business; a GLP-1 program that could not solve its central engineering problem looked, from a portfolio-management perspective, like exactly the kind of project a disciplined research organization should quietly wind down. Knudsen has since said, in interviews given once the program's eventual success made her early career worth profiling, that she was told more than once that the effort was not worth continuing.

She kept working on it anyway, and the specific technical bet she and her collaborators made is worth explaining in some detail, because the same underlying trick would reappear, refined further, in semaglutide a decade later, and reappear again, refined even further, in the dual- and triple-hormone agonists described in a much later chapter. The core idea drew on a property of a different, unrelated protein: human serum albumin, the most abundant protein in blood plasma, whose job is partly to act as a kind of biological chauffeur, binding loosely to a wide range of smaller molecules — including many drugs — and carrying them through the bloodstream while protecting them from rapid clearance by the kidneys and from certain types of enzymatic breakdown.

If a GLP-1-like peptide could be chemically modified to bind reversibly to albumin, the theory went, most of it would spend most of its time riding along attached to this abundant, slowly cleared protein rather than circulating freely and exposed to the enzymes that would otherwise destroy it within minutes. Only a small, steady fraction would be released from albumin at any given moment to interact with GLP-1 receptors, but that fraction would be continuously replenished as more molecules cycled off the protein, producing a sustained, low-level drug effect instead of a single short spike. The engineering challenge was to attach something to the GLP-1 peptide that would make it stick to albumin without destroying the peptide's own ability to bind and activate its target receptor — modify it too little and the half-life problem remained; modify it too much and the drug lost its biological activity entirely.

The solution Novo Nordisk's chemists eventually built involved attaching a fatty-acid side chain — a molecule chemically similar to a fat found in food — to a specific point on the GLP-1 peptide's structure, along with a small number of additional amino-acid substitutions designed to protect the modified peptide from the DPP-4 enzyme that had destroyed every earlier attempt. The resulting molecule, eventually named liraglutide, spent the overwhelming majority of its circulating time bound to albumin, extending its functional half-life from GLP-1's native two minutes to somewhere around eleven to fifteen hours — not long enough for once-weekly dosing, but long enough, crucially, for a single injection to last a full day. On January 25, 2010, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration approved liraglutide for type 2 diabetes under the brand name Victoza, the first once-daily human GLP-1 analogue ever brought to market, roughly five years after Byetta had proven the broader drug class could work at all and roughly eighteen years after John Eng had isolated exendin-4 from Gila monster venom in a Bronx VA laboratory.

Victoza was, commercially and clinically, a substantial step forward from Byetta: once a day instead of twice, better tolerated for many patients, and — this is the detail that mattered most for everything that followed — associated in clinical trials with more meaningful weight loss than the earlier drug had produced. That weight-loss signal was not, initially, the drug's primary selling point. Victoza was developed, marketed, and prescribed principally as a diabetes medicine, evaluated by regulators and by most of the physicians who prescribed it primarily on its ability to lower blood sugar. But the weight data kept showing up in the trial results, consistently, across multiple studies, and it was large enough that it was hard for anyone paying close attention to dismiss as noise.

This is the point in the story where Knudsen's account of her own career, given later in interviews once she had become a recognized figure in the field, becomes central to the narrative — not because this book treats a single individual's persistence as sufficient explanation for a discovery that in fact depended on hundreds of scientists, technicians, and clinical-trial participants, but because the internal argument she describes making, repeatedly, to a skeptical leadership is directly relevant to understanding why Novo Nordisk pursued an obesity indication as aggressively as it eventually did, years before most of the rest of the pharmaceutical industry took obesity drug development seriously as a category. By her own account, she argued internally, well before Victoza's 2010 approval, that the weight-loss effect showing up in the diabetes trials was not a side effect to be managed or minimized but a therapeutic effect worth developing in its own right — that a drug capable of producing meaningful, sustained weight loss in a population with a chronic, historically undertreated condition represented an enormous unmet medical need, separate from and potentially larger than the diabetes market the company already understood well.

This was, at the time, a genuinely unfashionable position inside the pharmaceutical industry as a whole, not merely inside Novo Nordisk specifically. Obesity drug development in the 2000s had a poor track record and a poor reputation. Several previous weight-loss drugs approved in the preceding decades had been withdrawn from the market after serious safety problems emerged post-approval — cardiac valve damage linked to the fenfluramine component of the fen-phen combination in the 1990s, and cardiovascular and psychiatric safety concerns that led to the withdrawal of the appetite suppressant rimonabant in Europe in the late 2000s, among other cautionary examples that had taught regulators, and the industry that had to satisfy them, to treat weight-loss drug candidates with unusual scrutiny. Several large pharmaceutical companies had also tried and abandoned their own obesity-focused research programs over the same period, concluding that the regulatory bar, the safety risk, and the historically modest efficacy of available approaches made the category a poor use of research investment relative to other therapeutic areas. Pursuing obesity as a primary indication, rather than treating weight loss as an incidental benefit of a diabetes drug, meant swimming against a fairly recent and fairly bruising set of industry-wide lessons.

Novo Nordisk pursued it anyway, running a dedicated obesity trial program for liraglutide at a higher dose than the diabetes formulation, and in 2014 the FDA approved that higher-dose liraglutide specifically for chronic weight management, under a different brand name, Saxenda, making Novo Nordisk one of the only major pharmaceutical companies still actively developing and marketing an anti-obesity medication at a moment when much of the rest of the industry had concluded the category was not worth the risk. Saxenda worked — trial participants lost meaningful weight, more than most previously available options — but it worked modestly by the standards that would later define semaglutide, produced meaningful gastrointestinal side effects in a portion of patients, and required a daily injection that limited its appeal relative to a pill. It was not, on its own, the breakthrough that would eventually make headlines. It was proof, at commercial scale, that a dedicated obesity drug built on this hormonal mechanism could clear the FDA's bar and find real, if limited, uptake among patients and physicians — proof that mattered enormously for what Novo Nordisk's leadership was willing to fund next.

What came next was an attempt to solve the same half-life problem all over again, this time aiming considerably higher than liraglutide's eleven-to-fifteen-hour improvement. If a fatty-acid side chain and a handful of protective amino-acid substitutions could stretch GLP-1's functional half-life from two minutes to half a day, could a more aggressive version of the same chemistry — a longer fatty-diacid chain, additional modifications further shielding the peptide from DPP-4, and a linker chemistry engineered to bind albumin even more tightly and reversibly — stretch it further still, to the point where a single injection might last not a day, but a full week? That was the specific chemistry question Novo Nordisk's researchers spent much of the 2000s and early 2010s working through, in incremental laboratory steps that rarely produced dramatic breakthroughs so much as accumulated small, hard-won improvements: slightly better binding affinity here, slightly reduced immunogenicity there, formulation adjustments to keep the modified peptide stable in solution over the shelf life a commercial product would require.

The molecule that eventually emerged from that work was named semaglutide, and its functional half-life in the human body turned out to be roughly a week — long enough that a single subcutaneous injection, administered once every seven days, could maintain a therapeutic concentration of the drug in a patient's bloodstream continuously, smoothing out the sharp peaks and troughs that had limited the tolerability of earlier, shorter-acting GLP-1 drugs and producing, in the early trials that tested it, weight-loss and blood-sugar results that were not merely incrementally better than liraglutide's but dramatically better — different enough in magnitude that some of the researchers reviewing the early data were reportedly skeptical of their own results, checking and rechecking dosing records and trial protocols before accepting that a molecule built from essentially the same fatty-acid-acylation trick as its predecessor could be producing an effect this much larger.

It is worth being precise about why the jump from liraglutide to semaglutide mattered so much more than the earlier jump from Byetta to liraglutide, because the difference is not simply that semaglutide lasted longer between injections, though that convenience mattered too. A once-weekly dosing schedule, compared with once-daily, meant far more consistent drug exposure over time — none of the daily peak-and-trough pattern that had limited how aggressively liraglutide could be dosed without provoking intolerable nausea in a meaningful share of patients. That steadier exposure allowed physicians to titrate patients up to considerably higher effective doses than liraglutide had permitted, using a gradual dose-escalation schedule specifically designed to let the gastrointestinal system adjust before increasing the dose further. Higher sustained doses of a molecule that was already, at any given concentration, a more effective appetite suppressant than its predecessor produced an effect on both blood sugar and body weight that was not just better than liraglutide's — it was, for the first time in this drug class's history, large enough to plausibly compete with the results traditionally associated only with bariatric surgery, a comparison this book will return to directly in a later chapter on what happened to the bariatric-surgery industry once that comparison became widely known.

There is a broader point about pharmaceutical innovation embedded in the liraglutide-to-semaglutide progression that is easy to miss if the story is told only as a sequence of increasingly impressive molecules. Semaglutide was not the product of a single breakthrough insight that Novo Nordisk's scientists lacked in the 1990s and suddenly discovered in the 2010s. It was the product of the same fatty-acid-acylation strategy, applied more aggressively, refined through roughly two additional decades of incremental medicinal chemistry, informed by everything the company had learned from a decade of real-world liraglutide use in millions of patients — how the drug behaved in different bodies, what side effects emerged at scale that smaller trials had not fully captured, which formulation and delivery-device choices patients actually tolerated well enough to stay on the drug long term. Innovation of this kind rarely arrives as a single dramatic leap. It arrives as a long accumulation of unglamorous engineering improvements, each building on real-world data the previous version generated, until the cumulative result looks, from the outside, like a sudden and dramatic breakthrough rather than what it actually was: the visible tip of a research program that had been quietly compounding for the better part of two decades.

It is also worth noting what did not change across this entire progression, because it will matter considerably once this book turns to questions of drug pricing and patent strategy. Byetta, Victoza, Saxenda, Ozempic, and Wegovy are all, at bottom, variations on the same core biological insight — amplifying and extending the GLP-1 signal — refined through successive rounds of the same general chemical strategy, fatty-acid acylation combined with protective amino-acid substitutions, rather than through entirely different mechanisms invented from scratch each time. Each successive molecule, however, was chemically distinct enough from its predecessors to support its own new patent filings, extending Novo Nordisk's period of market exclusivity well beyond what a single original patent on the core GLP-1-analogue concept would have provided on its own. This pattern of iterative molecular improvement generating iterative patent protection is common across the pharmaceutical industry, not unique to Novo Nordisk, and it is not, by itself, evidence of bad-faith gaming of the patent system — genuine clinical improvements did accompany each new molecule. But the practical effect, whatever the intent behind any individual patent filing, was that no generic competitor could simply wait out a single patent's expiration and enter the market with a copy of the entire GLP-1 franchise. They would face a staggered, decades-long sequence of separate exclusivity periods, one for each successive molecule — a structural feature of the story this book returns to directly in the chapters on drug pricing and the looming patent cliff.

Getting semaglutide from a promising laboratory molecule to an approved product still required the same expensive, multi-year, multi-phase clinical-trial process that governs every new drug: safety studies, dose-finding studies, and eventually large randomized trials measuring the drug's effect against placebo across thousands of patients, run according to strict regulatory protocols and reviewed by the FDA and its counterpart agencies in other countries before any of it could reach a pharmacy shelf. One further regulatory requirement shaped semaglutide's path to approval and deserves explanation, because it produced evidence that would matter enormously years later. Following a controversy in the late 2000s over cardiovascular safety signals associated with an older diabetes drug, rosiglitazone, the FDA issued guidance in 2008 requiring that any new diabetes medication demonstrate, through a dedicated large-scale trial, that it did not meaningfully increase cardiovascular risk before it could be approved. This requirement, which applied to semaglutide as it had applied to liraglutide before it, meant that Novo Nordisk had to run a trial — for semaglutide, a study called SUSTAIN-6, enrolling thousands of patients with type 2 diabetes at elevated cardiovascular risk — specifically designed to catch a safety problem, not to prove a benefit. What the trial found, instead of the neutral safety result regulators were primarily looking for, was a reduction in cardiovascular events among patients taking semaglutide compared with placebo. That was a genuinely unexpected outcome for a trial designed as a safety check, and it planted an early, still-preliminary signal that this molecule might be doing something for the cardiovascular system beyond its effects on blood sugar and weight alone — a signal serious enough that Novo Nordisk would go on to fund a much larger, purpose-built cardiovascular trial in patients without diabetes at all, a trial this book examines in detail once the story turns from diabetes to obesity as the drug's primary commercial identity.

The FDA approved semaglutide under the brand name Ozempic on December 5, 2017, for adults with type 2 diabetes — a diabetes drug, on its FDA label and in the marketing that immediately followed its launch, not yet a weight-loss drug in the way the world would come to think of it within a few short years. That distinction, between what a drug is approved for and what a drug turns out to actually do in the hands of tens of millions of patients and the physicians willing to prescribe it off-label, is where this story's next, considerably stranger chapter begins.

It is worth pausing, before moving on, to name plainly how unusual this entire arc looks against the ordinary pattern of pharmaceutical development. Most new drugs, even genuinely useful ones, improve on their predecessors by modest, single-digit percentages in whatever outcome matters most — a few percentage points of additional blood-sugar control, a marginally better side-effect profile, a somewhat more convenient dosing schedule. The jump from Byetta's roughly one-to-two-percent weight effect to semaglutide's roughly fifteen-percent weight effect, achieved across less than fifteen years and several successive molecules built from a shared chemical strategy, is not the ordinary pace of pharmaceutical improvement. It is closer to what technologists in other industries call a step change: a threshold crossed, rather than a gradual approach toward one, after which a product that had occupied a narrow clinical niche suddenly becomes relevant to a vastly larger population who had never previously considered an injectable prescription medicine as part of their relationship with food. Semaglutide crossed that threshold quietly, inside a diabetes-drug approval that made no headlines outside trade publications in December 2017. What happened next did not stay quiet for long.
