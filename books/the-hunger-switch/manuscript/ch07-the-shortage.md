# Chapter 7 — The Shortage

This chapter's account of scarcity sits in a broader historical lineage of pharmaceutical shortages that, while none matched this episode's scale or cultural visibility, offer useful comparative context for readers wondering whether a shortage of this severity and duration was itself unprecedented. American drug shortages, tracked formally by the FDA's shortage database referenced throughout this chapter, occur with some regularity across the pharmaceutical industry, generally driven by manufacturing quality problems, raw-material supply disruptions, or, less commonly, a sudden demand shock of the kind this chapter describes. Most such shortages resolve within months rather than years, and most affect a comparatively narrow patient population rather than the tens of millions of Americans this book's earlier chapters describe as potential GLP-1 candidates. What made this particular shortage historically unusual was not the underlying mechanism — demand outstripping supply is a familiar pharmaceutical-economics story — but its combination of scale, duration, and the specific, celebrity-amplified demand shock chapter six described, a combination that pushed an ordinarily contained, sector-specific problem into sustained national news coverage and, eventually, the congressional scrutiny examined in chapter ten.

Somewhere in the middle of 2022, a woman with type 2 diabetes who had been stably managed on Ozempic for a year or more walked into her regular pharmacy and was told, for the first time since she had started the medication, that it was not in stock. The pharmacist could not say when more would arrive. This scene, repeated with only minor variation in pharmacies across the United States and, before long, in other countries as well, became one of the defining, most consequential features of the entire GLP-1 story — arguably more consequential, in terms of its effect on public trust and public policy, than the celebrity endorsements described in the previous chapter, because it was the shortage, more than anything else, that turned an enthusiastic cultural story into a genuinely angry one.

The mechanics of the shortage were, at bottom, a straightforward supply-and-demand mismatch, but the specifics of pharmaceutical manufacturing made that mismatch far harder to resolve quickly than an equivalent shortage in almost any other consumer product category. GLP-1 drugs are not simple tablets that a factory can be retooled to produce more of within a few weeks. They are injectable peptide biologics, requiring specialized synthesis processes to manufacture the active peptide itself, followed by sterile fill-finish manufacturing to load that peptide into the pre-filled, multi-dose injector pens patients use at home — pens that themselves have to be manufactured to precise mechanical tolerances, since they are responsible for delivering an accurate, patient-safe dose without any pharmacist or physician present to check the process at the moment of use. Building new capacity for any stage of that process — new peptide-synthesis reactors, new fill-finish lines, new pen-assembly and packaging facilities — typically requires years of capital investment, regulatory inspection, and validation before a single additional unit of approved product can reach a patient, an inherent lag that no amount of urgency or capital could simply erase once demand, driven by the cultural explosion described in the previous chapter, began outpacing supply by a wide and rapidly widening margin.

The FDA's official shortage designations, once they were formally lifted, offer a useful, precisely dated way to measure just how long this disruption actually lasted, and the length is worth stating plainly because lived experience of a shortage — for a patient standing at a pharmacy counter being told, again, that nothing is available — tends to blur into an undifferentiated sense of "a long time" that official timelines can sharpen considerably. The FDA declared the tirzepatide shortage resolved in December 2024. The semaglutide shortage was not declared resolved until February 2025. Measured from the point in 2022 when both drugs first appeared on the shortage list through those resolution dates, the disruption ran for the better part of two and a half years — long enough for an entire cohort of newly diagnosed diabetic patients to be started, managed, and in some cases transitioned through multiple different treatment regimens without ever experiencing a period of straightforward, reliable access to the drug their physician judged most appropriate for them. Even after each formal resolution date, the FDA's own subsequent public guidance to compounding pharmacies described a market still in the process of "stabilizing," a word that signals residual unevenness in supply rather than a clean, immediate return to full and reliable availability everywhere at once.

Novo Nordisk's own public communications through 2022 and 2023 repeatedly acknowledged the mismatch and described ongoing efforts to expand capacity — new manufacturing sites, expanded contracts with third-party contract manufacturers, and, notably, a strategic decision to prioritize the lower-dose starting pens over higher maintenance doses at various points, an allocation choice intended to let as many new patients as possible begin treatment even if it meant some existing patients faced delays accessing the higher doses their treatment plan called for. None of these measures could plausibly close a gap that had opened as fast as demand for these drugs had grown once a genuinely enormous population — people managing diabetes, people managing clinically diagnosed obesity, and an unknown but clearly substantial number of people seeking the drugs primarily for more modest weight loss outside either of those categories — all began competing for the same constrained pool of pens simultaneously.

Diabetes advocacy organizations tracked and publicized the human cost of this mismatch throughout the period, collecting and amplifying patient accounts specifically to keep pressure on regulators and manufacturers alike — an advocacy effort that, while it could not by itself resolve the underlying manufacturing constraint described later in this chapter, played a meaningful role in keeping the diabetic-access dimension of the shortage visible to journalists and policymakers who might otherwise have focused primarily on the more culturally prominent weight-management side of the story chapter six described. The FDA's own drug shortage database formally listed both semaglutide and, once Eli Lilly's competing tirzepatide products reached the market in significant volume, tirzepatide as well, as being in shortage for extended periods running from 2022 through late 2024 and into early 2025 — an official, government-tracked acknowledgment that this was not merely anecdotal frustration but a documented, sustained national supply failure across an entire drug class. A shortage listing carries real regulatory consequences: it activates certain flexibilities for pharmacy compounding that would not otherwise be legally permitted, a detail that matters enormously for the next two chapters of this book, because it is the legal mechanism that allowed an entire alternative, less-regulated compounding industry to grow up around these drugs during the shortage years, filling gaps the branded manufacturers could not fill quickly enough on their own.

For diabetic patients specifically, the shortage was not an abstract inconvenience or a matter of a slightly delayed prescription refill. Ozempic and its competitors are not, for many patients with type 2 diabetes, interchangeable with older diabetes medications on a one-to-one basis; switching a stably managed patient to a different drug class can mean weeks or months of readjustment, different side-effect profiles, and, for some patients, meaningfully worse blood-sugar control during the transition. Diabetes educators, endocrinologists, and patient advocacy organizations spent much of 2022 and 2023 raising increasingly urgent alarms about exactly this problem: patients with a serious, well-documented chronic disease, on a medication regimen their physician had carefully calibrated, unable to reliably obtain refills because the same drug, at the same dose in some cases, was being prescribed in enormous and rapidly growing volume to a much larger population using it primarily or exclusively for weight management — a population that, unlike diagnosed diabetics, generally had other treatment options available to them even if none were quite as effective.

This tension produced one of the more genuinely difficult ethical and policy arguments running through the entire GLP-1 story, and it does not resolve neatly in either direction. On one hand, obesity is itself a serious, well-documented chronic disease with major downstream health consequences, and a physician's clinical judgment that a specific patient needs GLP-1 therapy for weight management, rather than for diabetes, is not inherently less medically legitimate than a diabetes prescription — the entire premise of the STEP trial program and the subsequent Wegovy approval was that clinically significant obesity deserves serious pharmaceutical treatment in its own right, not that it is a lesser concern than diabetes. On the other hand, a genuinely constrained supply of a specific medication being allocated, in practice, on a largely first-come-first-served basis across two overlapping but distinct patient populations — one facing an immediate, potentially dangerous disruption to an already-established treatment regimen, the other beginning a new treatment they had not yet started — produced outcomes that struck many patients, physicians, and outside observers as unjust regardless of how legitimate any individual prescription might have been in isolation. Diabetic patients who had depended on the drug for years, sometimes achieving blood-sugar control they had never previously managed with other therapies, found themselves rationing doses, delaying refills, or being switched to less-effective older medications through no fault of their own — a genuinely harmful outcome that fell disproportionately on patients who, unlike much of the newly arriving demand, had no discretion about whether to seek an alternative.

Novo Nordisk, along with Eli Lilly once its own tirzepatide products faced similar strain, took several public steps intended to address the imbalance, including public messaging discouraging prescribing for cosmetic or minor weight-loss purposes, restrictions on certain marketing practices, and, at various points, formal statements urging physicians to prioritize patients with the most clinically significant need. These measures had only limited effect, for reasons that reveal something important about how pharmaceutical markets actually function in practice: a manufacturer can influence but cannot directly control which individual patients an independent physician chooses to prescribe a legally approved medication to, and a manufacturer facing genuinely enormous, still-growing demand across its entire product line had limited practical tools, short of an allocation system it had no clear legal authority to impose unilaterally, to enforce any particular priority ordering among the millions of individual prescribing decisions being made by tens of thousands of independent physicians across the country.

The shortage was not a purely American phenomenon, either, even though this book's account of it draws primarily on U.S. regulatory and reporting sources. The United Kingdom's National Health Service, along with health systems and regulators across the European Union, issued their own warnings and guidance through 2023 and 2024 as global demand for semaglutide and tirzepatide outstripped Novo Nordisk's and Eli Lilly's combined manufacturing capacity everywhere at once, not merely in the United States. Because global production capacity, not any single country's distribution network, was the actual binding constraint, expanding a factory in Denmark or in Eli Lilly's Indiana or North Carolina facilities helped ease shortages everywhere those additional pens could eventually be shipped, but the timeline for building that capacity did not move any faster for being an international rather than a purely domestic problem. If anything, the international dimension of the shortage sharpened its underlying unfairness in a different register: countries with smaller markets, less negotiating leverage with the manufacturers, or less-established existing distribution relationships tended to receive priority lower than the largest markets, meaning patients in some countries experienced the shortage as considerably more severe and more prolonged than patients in the United States, the European Union's largest markets, or the United Kingdom — an early, small-scale preview of the much larger global-access gap this book examines in a later chapter on patent timelines and the developing world.

The shortage also had a second, less publicly visible effect that would prove just as consequential as the direct patient-access problem: it created, almost immediately, an enormous financial incentive for an entirely separate industry — compounding pharmacies, licensed under a different regulatory framework than traditional drug manufacturers, legally permitted under specific circumstances to prepare customized versions of drugs that were officially listed as being in shortage — to begin producing and selling their own versions of semaglutide and tirzepatide, generally at a fraction of the branded products' list price. That industry, and the two-year regulatory battle that would eventually determine its fate once the branded shortage began resolving in late 2024 and 2025, is the subject of a later chapter in this book. But its emergence during this period is impossible to understand without first understanding the shortage described in this chapter: a gap between overwhelming, celebrity-accelerated demand and a manufacturing base built for a much smaller, much more predictable market, wide enough and persistent enough that an entire secondary industry grew up specifically to fill it, largely outside the regulatory and safety infrastructure that governs the branded pharmaceutical products it was, in effect, substituting for.

Employers and health plans that had already committed to covering GLP-1 medication before the shortage fully hit, described in more detail in chapter eleven, faced their own distinct version of the allocation problem: a benefit an employer had promised its workforce, framed in benefits communications as newly available coverage for a genuinely effective treatment, that the employer itself had no ability to guarantee delivery on once branded supply tightened nationally. Several large employers found themselves fielding employee complaints about a benefit that existed on paper but proved difficult or impossible to actually use, a frustrating mismatch between announced policy and lived availability that added its own layer of reputational cost to the broader shortage story this chapter has documented, independent of the manufacturers' and pharmacies' own struggles described elsewhere in this chapter.

Pharmacists themselves, caught in the middle of this dynamic with no real power over the underlying supply, developed their own informal rationing practices during the worst months of the shortage, practices that varied considerably from one pharmacy chain, one region, and even one individual pharmacist to the next in the absence of any single, binding national allocation policy. Some pharmacies maintained waiting lists and attempted to prioritize patients with a documented diabetes diagnosis on file; others filled prescriptions strictly in the order received, regardless of the underlying indication, on the reasoning that it was not a pharmacist's role to second-guess a physician's clinical judgment about why a given patient needed the medication; still others simply stopped accepting new patients for the drug entirely for extended stretches, filling only existing, established prescriptions as limited allotments arrived. This patchwork of informal, pharmacy-by-pharmacy rationing rules meant that two patients with functionally identical medical circumstances — one a diagnosed diabetic, one not, or vice versa — could have starkly different experiences of the shortage depending entirely on which specific pharmacy, in which specific city, happened to fill their prescription, an arbitrariness that added its own layer of frustration on top of the shortage itself and that several patient-advocacy organizations cited directly in subsequent calls for clearer federal guidance on shortage-period allocation priorities.

By 2023, the shortage had also begun reshaping the geography and economics of who could reliably access these drugs at all, in ways that foreshadow several of this book's later chapters. Scarcity of this kind also produced predictable, and in at least one documented case genuinely dangerous, patient behavior and criminal opportunism. Patients facing empty pharmacy shelves developed and shared, through the same online patient communities that had helped popularize the drugs in the first place, informal strategies for stretching a limited pen supply further than its label directions intended — splitting doses, extending the interval between injections beyond the once-weekly schedule the clinical trials had actually tested, or drawing medication from a pen using a syringe rather than the device's built-in dial mechanism, a practice that introduced real risk of dosing inaccuracy and, more seriously, contamination, since Novo Nordisk's pens were designed and validated as sealed, single-patient delivery devices rather than as a source vial meant to be repeatedly punctured. The scarcity also created a lucrative opening for outright criminal counterfeiting: in December 2023, the FDA issued a formal public warning after confirming that counterfeit Ozempic pens, identifiable by a specific lot and serial number, had entered the legitimate U.S. pharmaceutical supply chain — not merely an online gray-market website easily dismissed as obviously illegitimate, but wholesalers and retail pharmacies that believed, at least initially, they were receiving authentic product. Laboratory analysis of the seized counterfeits found that the needles themselves were counterfeit and that their sterility could not be confirmed, a genuine infection risk for any patient who had unknowingly injected with one, and the FDA confirmed at least five adverse events associated with the counterfeit lot before the seizure was completed. That a shortage of a prescription medication, sold through the ordinary, regulated pharmaceutical supply chain in the world's largest pharmaceutical market, could become severe enough to create an opening for counterfeit product to infiltrate legitimate wholesale distribution is, on its own, a fairly remarkable measure of just how far demand had outrun supply by the end of 2023.

Patients with the financial means and the geographic flexibility to shop around among multiple pharmacies, to pay cash prices when insurance-covered refills were unavailable, or to seek out telehealth providers offering compounded alternatives, could generally find some way to maintain continuous access even during the worst months of the shortage. Patients without those advantages — often, disproportionately, patients in rural areas with fewer pharmacy options, patients on fixed incomes unable to pay cash prices as a stopgap, and patients whose insurance plans offered no coverage at all and who had been managing on off-label access that became considerably harder to maintain once pharmacists began prioritizing on-label diabetes prescriptions during the worst shortage periods — bore a disproportionate share of the disruption. A drug whose entire promise rested on offering, for the first time, a genuinely effective and broadly applicable treatment for two of the most common chronic conditions in the country had, within about eighteen months of its viral cultural breakthrough, become a case study in exactly the kind of unequal access that its supporters had hoped it might eventually help correct.

The country where that irony landed hardest, in a very literal financial sense, was not the United States at all, but the small Scandinavian nation whose century-old insulin business had made this entire story possible in the first place. As Ozempic and Wegovy sales climbed through the shortage years despite — or, in a strange market-logic sense, partly because of — the persistent scarcity that kept demand from ever being fully satisfied and kept prices from softening under normal competitive pressure, Novo Nordisk's revenues and stock price rose to a scale that began to reshape not just a pharmaceutical company's balance sheet but an entire national economy's growth statistics. That transformation, and what it meant for Denmark, is where this book turns next.
