# Chapter 2 — A Company Owned by a Foundation

This chapter's account of a century-old corporate origin story might seem, on first encounter, like a considerable detour from the drug-pricing controversies, celebrity endorsements, and shortage crises that dominate this book's later chapters. It is included at this length deliberately, because so much of what makes Novo Nordisk's later behavior comprehensible — its patience through decades of unglamorous GLP-1 research, described later in this chapter and the next, its resistance to hostile takeover even at the scale of value the company eventually achieved, its particular relationship to the Danish state and Danish national identity examined in chapter eight — traces back directly to decisions made by three men in Copenhagen in 1923, none of whom could have imagined the scale their institution would eventually reach. Corporate history of this kind rarely receives the same narrative attention as a company's more recent, more dramatic decisions, but this book's account suggests it deserves more of that attention than it typically gets, since the 1923 foundation charter turns out to explain considerably more about the 2020s pharmaceutical story than any single recent boardroom decision could.

In 1922, a Danish physiologist named August Krogh crossed the Atlantic to give a lecture at Yale. He had won the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine two years earlier for work explaining how blood flow adapts to the oxygen demands of muscle tissue — the kind of foundational discovery that made him, by the standards of interwar European science, a significant public figure, invited to speak at the world's leading universities. He was not, on paper, a man with any obvious reason to become a pharmaceutical entrepreneur.

He had a personal one. His wife, Marie Krogh, was a physician in her own right and lived with diabetes at a time when the disease carried a death sentence for anyone whose body could not produce enough of its own insulin. Before 1921, there was no treatment beyond starvation diets that could, at best, buy a diabetic patient a few extra months of a badly diminished life. Then, in Toronto, a young surgeon named Frederick Banting and a medical student named Charles Best, working in a borrowed laboratory at the University of Toronto with modest support from their supervisor J.J.R. Macleod, managed to extract a pancreatic hormone that could keep diabetic dogs alive, and shortly after that, keep diabetic humans alive.

The Kroghs heard about the Toronto discovery and, on their way home from the American lecture circuit, made a detour to see it for themselves. What August Krogh found in Toronto convinced him this was not a laboratory curiosity but a treatment his own wife, and thousands of other Danish and Scandinavian diabetics, urgently needed access to. Banting's team, recognizing both the scientific credibility Krogh brought and the practical need, granted him the rights to manufacture insulin in Scandinavia.

It is worth remembering how recent and how desperate the diabetes situation was in 1921, because it is easy, a century later, to read the Banting and Best story as a tidy scientific triumph rather than what it actually was: a race against a disease that was, at the time, reliably fatal for anyone whose pancreas could not produce insulin on its own. Before the Toronto discovery, the only treatment physicians could offer a child or adult with type 1 diabetes was a starvation diet, sometimes reducing caloric intake so severely that patients died of malnutrition before the disease itself killed them, buying a few extra months of a severely diminished life at the cost of a slower and equally certain death. Diabetic ketoacidosis, coma, and death within a year or two of diagnosis were the ordinary expected course of the disease for a child. Physicians and families experienced the discovery of insulin not as an incremental clinical improvement but as something closer to a resurrection, and hospital wards where diabetic children lay dying in early 1922 filled, within weeks of insulin's first clinical use, with the same children recovering. That was the emotional and medical backdrop against which August Krogh, watching his own wife live with the disease, made his detour to Toronto — not scientific curiosity in the abstract, but the urgent, personal calculation of a husband who had just learned that a treatment existed and that his own country did not yet have access to it.

Krogh could produce the science, but he was not a businessman, and turning a laboratory extraction technique into a reliable, scalable medical product required people who understood both clinical medicine and industrial manufacturing. He partnered with H.C. Hagedorn, a physician who specialized in diabetes care, and August Kongsted, a pharmacist. Together the three of them founded Nordisk Insulinlaboratorium in 1923. By March of that year, the company's insulin was already being used to treat patients — a remarkably fast turnaround from "we saw a demonstration in a Toronto laboratory" to "we are manufacturing and administering a hormone extracted from animal pancreases," made possible only because the underlying biochemistry, while difficult, was not something Krogh's team had to invent from scratch. Banting and Best had already done the hardest part; Krogh's contribution was securing the license, the credibility, and the manufacturing capacity to bring it to an entire region of patients who would otherwise have had no access to it at all.

One structural decision made at the company's founding would turn out, more than a century later, to matter enormously to the story this book tells. Hagedorn, Krogh, and Kongsted did not organize Nordisk as an ordinary joint-stock company answerable primarily to shareholders seeking the highest possible return. They placed it, from the outset, inside a foundation structure, with an explicit agreement that profits from the sale of diabetes medicine would be used for scientific and humanitarian purposes rather than simply distributed to owners. This was not merely a branding choice or an early instance of what a later century would call corporate social responsibility messaging. It created a genuinely different set of incentives at the top of the company, ones that would persist — with modifications, through public listings, mergers, and a century of pharmaceutical-industry consolidation around it — into the twenty-first century.

Nordisk was not, for its first several decades, the only Danish insulin company, and its early rival's origin story is almost a parable in miniature about how personality and disagreement, as much as strategy, shape corporate history. A separate company, Novo Terapeutisk Laboratorium, was founded by two brothers, Harald and Thorvald Pedersen, after a rupture with the Nordisk group — the kind of founder's-quarrel split that recurs throughout the history of technical industries, from semiconductor firms to software startups, in which a disagreement over direction or credit produces not a merger but a permanent rival built by people who once worked side by side. For most of the twentieth century, Novo and Nordisk existed as competing Danish insulin manufacturers, both selling into the same small domestic market and both, over time, expanding into international insulin sales as diabetes treatment spread globally. Denmark, a country of a few million people with no natural claim to global pharmaceutical leadership, ended up hosting not one but two significant insulin manufacturers throughout most of the century — an accident of a 1920s personal falling-out that neither Banting, Best, nor Krogh could have anticipated when the original license was granted.

For decades the rivalry produced a peculiarly Danish version of industrial competition: two companies drawing on the same small national talent pool of chemists, physicians, and engineers, competing for the same international insulin contracts, each pushing incremental improvements in purification and formulation that the other would then have to match. Novo built its own foundation-adjacent ownership structure over time as well, so that by the second half of the twentieth century Denmark had, in effect, two parallel foundation-linked pharmaceutical companies pursuing the same disease area within a few kilometers of each other, an arrangement so peculiar that when the companies finally discussed consolidation, it was framed less as one side acquiring the other than as two long-estranged branches of the same original insulin-manufacturing impulse finally recombining.

The two companies finally merged in 1989, forming Novo Nordisk A/S — the name under which the company would, three and a half decades later, become one of the most consequential pharmaceutical companies in the world. The merger consolidated nearly seventy years of accumulated Danish expertise in a single, highly specific area of medicine: the biochemistry of blood sugar regulation, the manufacturing of injectable protein and peptide therapies, and the decades of relationships with regulators, physicians, and diabetic patients that come from being one of only a handful of companies in the world that had ever mass-manufactured insulin.

This is worth pausing on, because it explains something that otherwise looks almost accidental about the company's later, much larger bet on GLP-1 biology. Novo Nordisk was not a general pharmaceutical conglomerate that happened to stumble into diabetes as one product line among many, the way a company might stumble into a category through acquisition. It was, from its 1923 founding through the 1989 merger and beyond, a company that had never really done anything else. Diabetes was not a division. It was close to the entire identity of the institution — its scientific culture, its manufacturing infrastructure, its regulatory relationships, its sense of what it existed to do. When incretin biology, and specifically GLP-1, began attracting serious scientific attention in the 1980s and 1990s, Novo Nordisk's scientists were not evaluating a speculative new market the way a generalist pharmaceutical company might. They were watching a new mechanism emerge inside the one disease area the entire company had organized itself around for the better part of a century.

The governance mechanics behind that ownership structure are worth spelling out plainly, because they explain why, decades later, no activist investor or rival pharmaceutical company could simply buy their way into control of Novo Nordisk once its GLP-1 franchise made it one of the most valuable companies in Europe. The Novo Nordisk Foundation does not hold the majority of the company's shares outright. Through its wholly owned investment arm, it holds a controlling share of the company's *votes*, structured through a dual-class-adjacent arrangement in which the foundation's shares carry outsized voting power relative to their economic share of the company. Ordinary investors can buy Novo Nordisk stock freely on the Copenhagen exchange and elsewhere, and have participated fully in the extraordinary run-up in the company's value that this book will describe in later chapters. But control of the board, and therefore control of the company's strategic direction, remains with the foundation, by explicit design, regardless of how large the free-floating shareholder base becomes or how tempting an acquisition target the company's cash flows might otherwise present to a larger multinational rival. A takeover of Novo Nordisk, in other words, is not merely difficult in the way large mergers are generally difficult. It is close to structurally impossible without the foundation's own consent, a design choice made in 1923 that would still be governing the company's fate more than a hundred years later.

That mattered because the road from "GLP-1 looks biologically promising" to "GLP-1 is a marketable drug" was long, expensive, and full of dead ends — considerably longer than the three years John Eng spent looking for a partner for exendin-4. A generalist pharmaceutical company, weighing GLP-1 research against a dozen other therapeutic areas competing for the same research budget, might reasonably have deprioritized an unglamorous, technically difficult peptide-engineering problem in favor of projects with a clearer, faster path to revenue. Novo Nordisk did not have that option in the same way, or rather, it had less reason to want it: its researchers, its manufacturing engineers, its regulatory affairs staff, and its commercial sales force were all already embedded in the diabetes disease area, in relationships with the same endocrinologists, the same patient populations, and the same regulatory pathways that any GLP-1 drug would eventually need. The institutional infrastructure that would eventually make Ozempic possible had been built, piece by piece, for insulin, over sixty-plus years, long before anyone in Copenhagen had used the word "incretin" in a research meeting.

The foundation structure mattered here too, in a way that is easy to understate. A conventional public company, answerable each quarter to shareholders comparing its returns against alternative uses of their capital, faces continuous pressure to show near-term progress on every major research bet. A company whose controlling shareholder is a foundation chartered to fund science and humanitarian work over the long run — a foundation that, by its own governing documents, is not permitted to sell its controlling stake and cannot be acquired against its will — has more latitude to fund a research program for a decade or more before it produces a marketable product. That does not mean Novo Nordisk's GLP-1 research proceeded free of internal skepticism, budget fights, or moments where a more impatient owner might have pulled the plug. It means the company had, built into its ownership structure from 1923 onward, a mechanism that made patient, multi-decade research bets somewhat easier to sustain than they would have been inside an ordinary public company optimized purely for quarterly earnings.

This is not a uniquely Danish or uniquely Novo Nordisk phenomenon; foundation-controlled and family-controlled companies across the world have made similar long-horizon bets that quarterly-earnings-driven public companies often cannot. Carlsberg, another major Danish industrial name, is controlled by a foundation with a similar mandate to fund scientific research; a number of major European industrial firms trace their stability through similar ownership arrangements that separate day-to-day operating capital from a founding family's or foundation's long-run charitable mission. What sets the Novo Nordisk arrangement apart, and what will matter increasingly as this book moves into its second half, is simply the scale that a single successful drug franchise eventually reached inside that structure: by the 2020s, a foundation originally built to fund modest Danish scientific and humanitarian grants out of insulin sales found itself, almost overnight in institutional terms, sitting atop one of the most valuable pools of capital controlled by any charitable entity on Earth — a transformation this book will return to directly once semaglutide's commercial success is fully on the table.

That transformation was not remotely visible in 1989, when Novo and Nordisk merged, or for most of the following two decades. Novo Nordisk after the merger was a large, respected, profitable insulin company — the kind of firm analysts described as reliable rather than exciting, a steady compounder in a mature therapeutic category rather than a candidate to reshape global consumer behavior. But it is a useful corrective to a story that later chapters will tell about celebrity endorsements, viral tweets, and a shortage crisis that made headlines for two straight years, because all of that noisy, fast-moving 2022-and-after history sits on top of a foundation laid with almost the opposite temperament: slow, patient, decades-long, funded by a company that had spent most of a century convinced that blood sugar regulation was worth understanding for its own sake, long before anyone knew that understanding it would eventually reshape the world's snack-food industry, its life-insurance actuarial tables, and its bariatric-surgery waiting rooms.

None of that patience guaranteed success. Plenty of long, well-funded pharmaceutical research programs run for decades and never produce an approved drug, let alone a commercially transformative one. Novo Nordisk itself pursued research programs in other disease areas over the same decades — hemophilia treatments, growth-hormone therapies, and various diabetes-adjacent projects — with results ranging from modest commercial success to outright abandonment, the ordinary attrition rate of pharmaceutical research that rarely makes it into a company's public narrative once a single blockbuster reshapes how its history gets told in hindsight. What the century of institutional focus and the foundation ownership structure provided was not a guarantee but a runway — the time and the internal permission to keep funding an unglamorous peptide-engineering problem through years of incremental progress, false starts, and internal debate about whether the payoff would ever arrive.

It is tempting, looking backward from the vantage point of a drug that reshaped grocery-store balance sheets and life-insurance actuarial tables, to tell this as a story of visionary foresight: a wise, patient institution that saw the future others missed. The more honest version is less flattering and more instructive. Novo Nordisk's GLP-1 program survived not because anyone inside the company could see, in the 1990s, that it would someday produce a drug capable of moving the stock price of PepsiCo. It survived because the company's ownership structure made it unusually tolerant of a long stretch of years in which the program produced interesting data, modest internal excitement, and no product anyone could sell — the ordinary, undramatic condition of most research that eventually succeeds, indistinguishable at the time from most research that never does. Understanding why that runway existed requires understanding what, exactly, the scientific problem was that Novo Nordisk's researchers were trying to solve, and why it had defeated most of the pharmaceutical industry for the better part of two decades before anyone solved it convincingly.

It is also worth noting what this chapter's history does not explain, because later chapters will need the distinction. A century of institutional focus on diabetes gave Novo Nordisk the infrastructure, patience, and scientific culture to pursue GLP-1 research seriously. It did not, by itself, give the company any special insight into obesity as a distinct condition, or any early strategic plan to build a weight-loss franchise. For most of the twentieth century, and well into the GLP-1 research program's early years, Novo Nordisk's institutional identity was built around blood sugar control in diagnosed diabetics, not around body weight in the general population. The idea that a diabetes-focused molecule might become one of the most consequential weight-loss and, eventually, cardiovascular and addiction-adjacent therapies of the century was not part of the founding vision in any of its forms — not Krogh's, not Hagedorn's, not the generations of Novo and Nordisk scientists and executives who followed them. It emerged, gradually and then suddenly, out of the biology itself, as researchers noticed that patients taking GLP-1 drugs for diabetes were also, as a side effect nobody had specifically designed for, losing meaningful amounts of weight.

That observation — not a boardroom strategy document, but a repeated, hard-to-ignore pattern in clinical trial data — is where the story of this drug class stops being purely a diabetes story and starts becoming the much larger story this book is mostly about. Getting there required Novo Nordisk's scientists to solve the same core engineering problem that had taken Amylin and Eli Lilly most of a decade to solve for exendin-4's descendants: how to take a fragile, two-minute human hormone and turn it into something that could survive in the bloodstream long enough to be injected once a week, in a dose small enough to manufacture at scale, without triggering an immune response or intolerable side effects. That problem is the subject of the next two chapters: first, the century-long argument inside medicine and physiology over what actually controls human appetite, an argument considerably older and stranger than most people assume; and then the specific, grinding, laboratory-bench story of how Novo Nordisk's scientists finally engineered a GLP-1 molecule that could survive in the human bloodstream long enough to matter.
