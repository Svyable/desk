# Chapter 6 — The Tweet Heard Around the World

This chapter sits at the hinge point of this book's larger argument, the moment where the patient, multi-decade scientific and corporate history traced across chapters one through four collides with the considerably faster, considerably less predictable dynamics of modern celebrity culture and social media — dynamics this book's remaining chapters, on shortage, pricing, insurance, and industry disruption, all trace back to this collision in one way or another. Understanding precisely how that collision happened, and why it happened through this particular channel rather than through the more conventional pathways of medical journalism or public-health messaging that had, at least in principle, been available to communicate the STEP 1 results to the public in the months before Musk's tweets, is this chapter's central task.

In October 2022, a Twitter user replied to a photo of Elon Musk, remarking that he looked unusually fit. Musk, who had spent much of the preceding two years in the process of acquiring Twitter itself — a deal that would close later that same month — replied with two words that would do more to introduce an entire category of prescription medicine to a global mass audience than any advertising campaign, medical journal publication, or FDA press release had managed in the drug's first five years on the market: "Fasting" and "Wegovy."

The reply was brief, almost throwaway, one tweet among the thousands Musk sent that year. But Musk's account, at the time, carried tens of millions of followers, and his growing role as Twitter's incoming owner meant that essentially anything he posted was already receiving unusual attention from journalists and casual observers who might not otherwise have followed his feed closely. A little over a month later, in November 2022, Musk tweeted again on the subject, this time volunteering a specific figure: "Down 30 lbs!" A separate reply that same month, responding to a follower's question about his changed appearance, offered a fuller account of his method: "Fasting + Ozempic/Wegovy + no tasty food near me."

It is difficult to overstate how much cultural work those few sentences did, relative to their length. Before October 2022, "Wegovy" and "Ozempic" were, for the overwhelming majority of the public, either entirely unfamiliar words or names vaguely associated with diabetes advertising seen during a television commercial break. After Musk's tweets, and the wave of press coverage, meme-making, and casual conversation they triggered almost immediately, "Ozempic" in particular began entering the culture as something closer to a household name — not always accurately, since Musk himself was taking Wegovy specifically for weight loss while many people who heard the story afterward conflated the two closely related products, using "Ozempic" as an informal umbrella term for the entire GLP-1 drug category regardless of which specific branded product a given person was actually using. That linguistic slippage — Ozempic as brand name, Ozempic as verb, Ozempic as a loose shorthand for an entire pharmacological category including drugs the speaker had never taken and might not even be able to name correctly — became, within about a year, one of the more linguistically interesting features of the drug's cultural absorption, not unlike the way "Kleenex" or "Xerox" once became generic stand-ins for entire product categories, except accelerated by social media into a matter of months rather than decades.

This chapter's account of Musk's specific role in the story also illustrates a broader pattern in how information now travels that this book's account of scientific discovery in its earlier chapters, built on the comparatively slow, institution-mediated pace of peer-reviewed publication and regulatory review described in chapters three and four, throws into sharp relief by contrast. The STEP 1 trial results, described in chapter five, took years of careful randomized-trial design, execution, and peer review to produce and represented, by any reasonable scientific standard, considerably stronger evidence of these drugs' effectiveness than a single billionaire's offhand social-media reply. Yet the tweet, not the trial, is what most non-medical readers of this book likely first heard about Ozempic through, if they can recall the specific moment at all — a genuine asymmetry between the institutional weight of evidence and the cultural weight of its transmission that this book's account of the drugs' reception has to take seriously as its own subject, not merely as an unfortunate distraction from the "real" scientific story chapters three through five told.

Celebrity weight loss had, of course, been a subject of intense public fascination long before 2022; tabloid culture had spent decades speculating about, celebrating, and criticizing famous people's changing bodies, usually without any reliable information about method. What made the Musk tweets different, and what made the subsequent wave of celebrity acknowledgment that followed them different from earlier eras of tabloid weight-loss speculation, was the specificity and the traceability: Musk was not vaguely alluding to a diet or a trainer. He was naming a specific, prescribable, brand-name medication, one that had a genuine, well-documented, randomized-trial evidence base behind it, freely searchable by anyone curious enough to type the name into a search engine. Within weeks, other public figures began speaking openly about their own use of the drugs — some, like the comedian Tracy Morgan, the television personality Sharon Osbourne, and the comedian and talk-show host Chelsea Handler, discussing their use candidly in interviews and public appearances, part of a wave of celebrity acknowledgment that continued to build through 2023 and into 2024 as the drugs became more widely available and less socially unusual to admit taking.

Social media amplified the celebrity story in a way earlier eras of tabloid gossip could not have matched. TikTok in particular became a central venue for the drug's viral spread through 2023, with videos documenting personal weight-loss journeys, before-and-after transformations, tips for managing side effects, and — inevitably, given the platform's incentive structure — a steady stream of content from people selling weight-loss coaching, supplements falsely implying GLP-1-like effects, or access to compounded or gray-market versions of the actual drugs, a phenomenon this book examines closely in a later chapter on the compounding-pharmacy gray market that emerged directly out of this period's surging, poorly served demand. Search interest, hashtag volume, and prescription data all moved in the same direction at the same time through late 2022 and 2023, each amplifying the others in a feedback loop that is now a familiar pattern for how a genuinely significant medical or technological development crosses over from specialist knowledge into mass culture in the social-media era: a high-profile, highly shareable trigger event, followed by a wave of ordinary people sharing their own related experiences, followed by media coverage treating the volume of that sharing itself as the story, followed by even more ordinary people encountering the topic for the first time through that coverage and beginning to search for it themselves.

Musk continued to reference his own use of the drugs periodically over the following two years, at one point in December 2024 jokingly referring to himself as "Ozempic Santa" in a social-media post about distributing the drugs, or discussion of them, more broadly — by then a running motif in his public persona rather than a single newsworthy disclosure, evidence of how thoroughly the subject had moved from novel confession to ordinary, repeatable cultural material in barely two years. Other celebrities' relationships with the drugs would go on to become considerably more fraught than Musk's relatively breezy public commentary, especially once questions about appropriate use, appropriate dosing, and the visible physical side effects some long-term users experienced — a set of concerns this book returns to directly in a later chapter — began generating their own wave of critical coverage, memes, and backlash. But in the earliest phase of the cultural explosion, in the final months of 2022 and the first half of 2023, the dominant public narrative was overwhelmingly one of fascination and enthusiasm: a genuinely effective, apparently near-miraculous solution to a problem — struggling with body weight — that a very large share of the adult population had spent years or decades experiencing as a source of private frustration, and that celebrity culture had spent just as long treating as a subject worthy of relentless, often cruel public commentary.

It is worth pausing on why the specific framing of "food noise" quieting down, rather than simply "losing weight," became such a durable and recognizable part of how patients described their experience during this period, because the phrase captured something the earlier, purely numerical framing of clinical trial results had not. A weight-loss percentage on a chart describes an outcome. "Food noise" describes a mechanism a patient could feel directly and immediately, often within days of a first injection, well before any meaningful change was visible on a scale or in a mirror. Patients reported, with striking consistency across independent accounts that had no obvious way of having influenced each other, a sudden absence of a kind of mental static they had not fully recognized as a distinct, nameable experience until it was gone — the constant, low-grade background process of thinking about food, planning the next meal, negotiating with themselves about second helpings, that many had simply assumed was a universal part of being a person with a body, rather than a variable neurological signal that some people experienced far more intensely than others. When that signal quieted, patients described feeling, for the first time in memory, mental space that had previously been occupied by food-related thoughts becoming available for other things — a description recognizable enough, once articulated by a handful of early patients and physicians, that it spread rapidly as shorthand precisely because so many other patients recognized their own experience in it immediately upon hearing the phrase.

By early 2023, the drug's absorption into mainstream entertainment culture was complete enough that awards-show comedy writers could assume audience-wide familiarity with the reference without any explanation. Hosting the 95th Academy Awards in March 2023, Jimmy Kimmel opened his monologue by surveying the room and remarking, "When I look around this room, I can't help but wonder, is Ozempic right for me?" — a line that worked as comedy only because the audience, both in the theater and watching from home, already understood the reference well enough to laugh at the implication that a room full of unusually thin celebrities might share a common pharmaceutical explanation. A year later, in March 2024, Saturday Night Live aired a sketch called "Ozempic for Ramadan," featuring the comedian Ramy Youssef alongside cast members Kenan Thompson, Ego Nwodim, and Andrew Dismukes, imagining the drug marketed specifically to help observant Muslims manage the appetite-suppression challenges of a fasting month — a joke that depended, like Kimmel's monologue line a year earlier, on an audience already fluent enough in the drug's basic mechanism (appetite suppression) and its cultural saturation (already a fixture in unrelated contexts, ready to be spliced into any existing cultural occasion) to find the premise immediately legible rather than requiring setup. The distance between a diabetes drug approved in December 2017 that most of the public had never heard of, and a shared cultural reference stable enough to build network-television comedy sketches around without explanation, had closed in a little over six years — and the sharpest part of that closure, the part responsible for most of the acceleration, took place within roughly eighteen months of Musk's original October 2022 tweet.

This convergence between the drug's underlying pharmacology — a hormone-based satiety signal, artificially extended and amplified, as chapters three and four of this book have already explained in mechanistic detail — and its subjective description by patients as psychological relief rather than mere appetite suppression, is part of why the cultural response to these drugs took the specific shape it did, rather than resembling the cultural response to earlier weight-loss drugs that had, at various points, also produced real if more modest results. Earlier obesity pharmacotherapies had generally worked through mechanisms patients experienced as effortful suppression — stimulant-driven appetite reduction that could feel jittery or artificial, or fat-absorption blockers whose main subjective effect was digestive discomfort rather than any change in the underlying desire to eat. GLP-1 drugs, by contrast, were widely described by patients as making healthy eating feel less like a fight against their own mind and more like their mind had simply stopped fighting them — a distinction that mattered enormously for how the drugs were perceived, discussed, and eventually defended once the backlash phase of the cultural conversation began in earnest.

The speed of that cultural transition also produced, almost immediately, a fault line in public conversation that would harden over the following two years into one of the more contentious and durable arguments running through this entire story: the division between patients using these drugs to manage a diagnosed medical condition — type 2 diabetes, clinically defined obesity with associated health risks — and a much larger, harder-to-measure population using them for what critics quickly began calling vanity weight loss, a relatively small amount of weight sought primarily for appearance rather than diagnosed medical necessity. That distinction is, on inspection, considerably harder to draw cleanly than the framing suggests; obesity is a diagnosed medical condition with its own FDA-recognized clinical criteria, not merely a euphemism for "wanting to look thinner," and a patient with a BMI just above or below a diagnostic threshold does not experience a meaningfully different relationship with food or health risk on either side of that line. But the framing took hold in public discourse quickly regardless, driven in large part by exactly the celebrity dynamic this chapter has already described: a wealthy, already-thin-by-most-standards public figure like Musk discussing his use of a drug approved for a BMI of 30 or above created an obvious, visible mismatch between the drug's approved clinical purpose and its most visible celebrity endorsement, a mismatch that became one of the central, recurring grievances voiced by diabetic patients and their advocates once actual physical shortages began making the drug genuinely difficult for people who needed it most to obtain — a conflict this book examines directly in the next chapter.

It is worth noting, too, how unevenly the celebrity-driven wave of attention landed across the population that would go on to become the drugs' actual, long-term user base. Coverage through 2022 and into 2023 skewed heavily toward a narrow demographic slice — famous, wealthy, disproportionately based in media and entertainment industries clustered in Los Angeles and New York — that bore only a loose resemblance to the population data would eventually show was using these drugs in the largest numbers as insurance coverage slowly expanded and prices, discussed at length in a later chapter, began to fall through alternative channels. This gap between the highly visible early adopters who drove the cultural narrative and the much larger, much less visible population of people with diagnosed diabetes and obesity who had been quietly using and benefiting from these drugs since Ozempic's original 2017 approval created a recurring, somewhat unfair dynamic in public perception: a medicine whose primary clinical purpose was managing serious chronic disease became known to much of the public first and most durably as a celebrity weight-loss shortcut, a framing that took years of subsequent public-health messaging, physician advocacy, and, eventually, hard data on cardiovascular and other health outcomes to meaningfully counterbalance.

None of this cultural momentum, however enthusiastic, changed the underlying manufacturing reality Novo Nordisk was contending with. A company that had built its production capacity for a diabetes drug, then approved a second, higher-dose weight-loss formulation of the same molecule, was now facing demand driven not primarily by the medical community's steady, predictable prescribing patterns, but by a viral social-media phenomenon capable of adding hundreds of thousands of new prescription-seeking patients to the market within a matter of months — a kind of demand shock that pharmaceutical manufacturing planning, built around multi-year capital investment cycles for new production facilities, was structurally unequipped to absorb quickly. It is worth asking, too, why Musk specifically became the trigger for this cultural explosion rather than any of the other public figures who had almost certainly been using these drugs quietly in the months before his October 2022 tweets. The answer has less to do with the substance of what he said than with the specific communicative position he occupied at that exact moment: a person in the process of acquiring the very platform on which he made the remarks, already the subject of intense, sustained media coverage for reasons entirely unrelated to weight loss, with a follower base and a cultural visibility that few if any other early GLP-1 users could match. A quieter celebrity disclosure, made under different circumstances, might well have generated a smaller wave of attention that faded within days rather than catalyzing the sustained, multi-year cultural absorption this chapter has described — a reminder that virality of this kind depends heavily on the specific platform, timing, and pre-existing visibility of whoever happens to say the triggering words, not merely on the inherent newsworthiness of the underlying fact being disclosed.

The term "food noise" itself took an interesting path from patient vernacular to something closer to formal clinical vocabulary over the following two years. It began, as this chapter has already described, as an organic description patients reached for independently, without any coordinated origin or marketing push behind it, simply because it captured something existing clinical language did not have a ready word for. By the mid-2020s, researchers had begun publishing formal academic attempts to define and measure the phenomenon more rigorously — describing it, in peer-reviewed terms, as a pattern of persistent, often intrusive cognitions about food and eating, and developing survey instruments intended to quantify its intensity in a way that could be tracked before and after treatment rather than relying purely on informal patient description. That a piece of internet-born, patient-generated slang could migrate into peer-reviewed nomenclature within a few years is itself a small but telling marker of how unusually fast the entire cultural and scientific conversation around this drug class was moving relative to the ordinary pace at which medicine typically absorbs new patient-reported concepts into its formal vocabulary — a pace considerably faster than the decades it took incretin research itself to move from Bayliss and Starling's 1902 experiment to a marketable drug, and a reminder that the second half of this book's story, unlike its first half, would be governed far more by the accelerated tempo of social media, financial markets, and twenty-four-hour news cycles than by the patient, decades-long tempo of laboratory science that had produced the molecule in the first place.

By early 2022, before Musk's tweets had even fully landed, reports of Wegovy shortages had already begun appearing in trade press and patient forums. By the second half of 2022 and through 2023, the shortage would become one of the defining, most consequential features of the entire GLP-1 story — not a footnote to the celebrity-driven cultural explosion this chapter has described, but its direct and largely inevitable consequence, and the subject to which this book now turns.
