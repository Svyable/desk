# Chapter 18 withdrawal-evidence audit

Scope: `manuscript/ch18-the-cost-of-stopping.md`, especially the opening discussion of STEP 1, STEP 4, and SURMOUNT-4. This note is deliberately narrow: it does not propose a chapter rewrite. It records the evidentiary distinctions that should govern the next prose pass.

## Why this is worth correcting

The chapter has the right central observation: stopping semaglutide or tirzepatide after substantial treatment-associated weight loss commonly leads to meaningful regain, while continued treatment better preserves or extends the loss. But the current prose collapses three different evidentiary designs into one story and occasionally pushes beyond what those designs establish.

The stronger version is more interesting because the studies answer different questions.

## Primary evidence

### STEP 4: the clean randomized semaglutide withdrawal test

Rubino et al., JAMA (2021): https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2777886

STEP 4 gave all 902 enrolled participants open-label semaglutide during a 20-week run-in. The 803 who reached the 2.4 mg maintenance dose were then randomized 2:1 either to continue semaglutide or switch to placebo for another 48 weeks; both groups continued lifestyle intervention.

At randomization, participants had already lost a mean 10.6% of body weight. From week 20 to week 68, those who continued semaglutide lost another 7.9% on average, while those switched to placebo gained 6.9%. The between-group difference was 14.8 percentage points (95% CI, 13.5 to 16.0; P < .001).

This is the chapter's strongest causal evidence that continuing semaglutide preserves/extends weight loss better than withdrawing it. It is also an enriched trial: only participants who tolerated the run-in and reached the maintenance dose were randomized. The authors explicitly note that this selection can limit generalizability and may overstate the effect expected in ordinary clinical use.

### STEP 1 extension: useful, but not the same experiment

Wilding et al., Diabetes, Obesity and Metabolism (2022): https://dom-pubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/dom.14725

STEP 1 originally randomized 1,961 adults to semaglutide or placebo for 68 weeks. At week 68, **both trial medication and the structured lifestyle intervention were discontinued**. The off-treatment extension then followed an exploratory subset of 327 participants for another year. The extension was not a fresh randomized withdrawal comparison between continuing semaglutide and stopping it.

Among extension participants who had received semaglutide, mean loss at week 68 was 17.3%. By week 120 they had regained 11.6 percentage points, leaving mean weight 5.6% below the original baseline. The familiar shorthand that participants regained roughly two-thirds of their prior loss is accurate at the group level.

Two boundaries matter for the manuscript:

1. Because medication and structured lifestyle intervention stopped together, STEP 1 cannot cleanly assign the subsequent regain to withdrawal of semaglutide alone.
2. The finding was not uniform. At week 120, 48.2% of the former semaglutide group still had at least 5% weight loss from baseline. The paper does **not** establish the current chapter's suggestion that people who maintained more weight loss did so particularly because they had made more durable independent diet/activity changes; participation in lifestyle interventions during the extension was not recorded.

### SURMOUNT-4: independent randomized evidence for tirzepatide

Aronne et al., JAMA (2024): https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2812936

SURMOUNT-4 used a randomized-withdrawal design after a 36-week open-label tirzepatide lead-in. Of 783 enrolled participants, 670 were randomized either to continue tirzepatide or switch to placebo for 52 weeks, with diet and physical-activity intervention in both groups.

Participants had lost a mean 20.9% before randomization. From week 36 to week 88, those continuing tirzepatide lost another 5.5% on average; those switched to placebo gained 14.0%. At week 88, 89.5% of the continued-treatment group versus 16.6% of the placebo group had maintained at least 80% of the weight lost during the lead-in.

This strongly corroborates the maintenance problem in a second molecule with a different receptor profile. It does **not**, by itself, prove a universal "class-wide pharmacological reality" across every GLP-1 or incretin therapy, dose, population, stopping strategy, or duration.

## Fresh counterevidence / complication

Horn et al., JAMA Internal Medicine (2026), post hoc analysis of SURMOUNT-4: https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/2841273

The post-withdrawal distribution is important because it prevents the chapter from turning a strong average effect into an individual destiny. Among participants randomized to placebo after tirzepatide, 82.5% regained at least 25% of the weight they had lost during the lead-in. About half regained at least 50%, and about one quarter regained at least 75%. But 17.5% regained less than 25%, and roughly 4% continued losing weight after withdrawal while lifestyle intervention continued.

This is not a rebuttal to the maintenance thesis. It is the strongest useful counterargument to an overly deterministic version of it: withdrawal raises the risk of substantial regain markedly, but the response is heterogeneous and the available trials do not yet tell us which patients can maintain most of the benefit, why, or under what lower-dose/taper/behavioral strategy.

## Manuscript corrections to freeze for the next prose pass

- Do not describe STEP 1 extension as though it were the same randomized-withdrawal design as STEP 4 or SURMOUNT-4. Lead with STEP 4 for the semaglutide causal contrast; use STEP 1 extension for the one-year off-treatment trajectory.
- Replace "every major GLP-1 and dual-agonist trial program" with a narrower description of the specific STEP and SURMOUNT withdrawal studies actually discussed.
- Remove or source the claim that participants who maintained more loss did so "particularly" because of durable independent diet/activity changes. STEP 1 did not record extension-period lifestyle participation.
- Avoid saying the two molecules establish a universal class-wide stopping law. They establish a replicated pattern for semaglutide 2.4 mg and tirzepatide 10/15 mg in the studied adult populations.
- Preserve the residual benefit in STEP 1: mean weight remained 5.6% below baseline at one year off treatment, and nearly half the extension sample remained at least 5% below baseline. "Regain" is not synonymous with "complete reversal."
- Preserve heterogeneity from the 2026 SURMOUNT-4 analysis. The reader should leave with a probability distribution, not a prophecy.

## Strongest defensible chapter proposition

The withdrawal studies do not show that every patient who stops an incretin drug inevitably returns to baseline, nor do they establish that lifelong full-dose therapy is the only possible maintenance strategy. They show something narrower and sturdier: in adults who responded to and tolerated semaglutide or tirzepatide in these trials, continued treatment preserved weight loss substantially better than withdrawal, while withdrawal produced substantial average regain with wide individual variation. That is enough to make maintenance, access, cost, and discontinuation part of the treatment decision from the beginning rather than an afterthought at goal weight.
