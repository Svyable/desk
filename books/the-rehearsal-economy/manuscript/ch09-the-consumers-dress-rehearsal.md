# The Consumer's Dress Rehearsal

People are unreliable narrators of their own future purchases.

This is not because consumers are foolish or dishonest. It is because a purchase is an event inside a context. Asked in a quiet interview whether she cares about price, sustainability, privacy, convenience, quality, status, local production, or simplicity, a person may give an answer that is both sincere and weakly predictive of what happens at 6:12 on a Tuesday evening when she is tired, hungry, carrying a child, and staring at a shelf.

Consumer research exists partly to close that gap between stated intention and situated behavior.

The field contains surveys, experiments, ethnography, usability testing, mock stores, concept tests, diary studies, conjoint analysis, eye tracking, observational work, A/B tests, and more. These methods are not interchangeable. Each creates a different rehearsal of the market.

The survey gives the consumer a verbal future. The mock shelf gives her a visual and spatial one. The prototype gives her something to touch. The live experiment gives her consequence. Ethnography sometimes does the opposite: instead of asking the consumer to rehearse the future, the researcher enters the consumer's present.

That reversal matters.

Organizations tend to see customers through categories that make management possible: segment, account, demographic, persona, cohort, lifetime value. These abstractions are useful and flattening. A household does not experience itself as a segment. It experiences a broken appliance, a school morning, a grocery budget, a desire to impress friends, a habit learned from parents, an app notification, a shelf too high to reach.

Ethnographic research restores friction to the model.

A researcher watching how people actually use a kitchen appliance may discover that the official function is less important than cleaning, storage, noise, or the awkward moment when guests are present. A banking team may learn that “financial planning” is not experienced as a spreadsheet problem but as negotiation between spouses, fear of opening an envelope, or the timing of irregular income. A technology company may discover that users share accounts because the product's model of the household is wrong.

These findings often look obvious after they are seen. That is why they are dangerous to dismiss.

Obviousness after observation is not evidence that the organization would have designed for it beforehand.

The rehearsal economy depends on this distinction. A good rehearsal reveals what planning forgot. Consumer research is a rehearsal of exchange designed to expose the assumptions hidden inside a product.

The most important assumption is often that the product is the center of the consumer's attention.

It rarely is.

Companies organize around products because products are what companies make. Consumers organize around lives. The toothbrush competes with sleep. The budgeting app competes with avoidance. The streaming service competes with conversation, fatigue, and another screen. The grocery brand competes with whatever is eye-level and on sale.

This is why context changes preference.

Behavioral economics documented a wide range of framing, default, reference-point, and choice-architecture effects. The strongest lesson is not that humans possess a fixed list of biases that marketers can exploit like buttons. It is that preferences are often constructed in interaction with the environment.

A default can matter because opting out requires attention. A decoy can alter comparison. A price can become a reference point. Scarcity can change perceived urgency. Social proof can reduce uncertainty. Yet effect sizes depend on context, population, stakes, and implementation. The popular catalog of biases often sounds more universal than the evidence warrants.

For researchers, this means consumer rehearsal should be designed to falsify neat psychological stories.

Suppose a team believes customers choose a premium product because of status. A survey may support the story because people recognize the language. An ethnographic visit may reveal that the premium version is simply easier to clean. A pricing test may show little sensitivity to status cues. The competing explanations should survive long enough to meet behavior.

This is another form of adversarial rehearsal. The consumer is the opponent of the company's favorite explanation.

Good research protects that role.

Bad research recruits the consumer into the company's narrative. Questions lead. Concepts are explained until they sound attractive. Moderators reward enthusiasm. Teams behind a one-way mirror interpret polite interest as demand. The rehearsal becomes a sales meeting with no cash register.

The market later supplies the missing cruelty.

This is why willingness to pay deserves special attention. People can like an idea without valuing it enough to displace money. A real price forces competing preferences into the room. Even then, test conditions matter. Early adopters, promotions, novelty, and selection can distort results.

The closer the rehearsal moves toward real exchange, the more kinds of reality enter.

But higher fidelity costs more. An online survey is cheap. A functional prototype is expensive. A pilot store is more expensive. A national launch is reality. The research program should climb this ladder only when cheaper methods have answered what they can.

This is the same architecture we saw in engineering. Start with the cheapest representation capable of killing the current assumption.

Consumer research adds a complication: the act of testing can change the consumer.

Ask someone repeatedly about sustainability and the topic becomes more salient. Show three package designs and you reveal that packaging is being evaluated. Invite a user into a laboratory and she knows she is observed. The rehearsal is not a neutral window into behavior; it is an environment.

Researchers have developed many methods to reduce this problem, but none abolish it. Field experiments trade control for realism. Laboratory studies trade realism for control. Ethnography trades standardized comparison for depth and context. Large-scale behavioral data trades declared motives for traces whose meaning must be inferred.

There is no view from nowhere.

The useful question is which distortion matters for the decision.

If a team needs to understand why older users abandon onboarding, a usability session may reveal enough. If it needs to estimate national demand, a dozen interviews are insufficient. If it needs to discover unknown routines, a survey with predefined answers may prevent discovery. Method follows uncertainty.

Organizations often reverse this. They choose the method they already know and then ask it whatever question happens to be important.

This is equivalent to using the same simulator for every aircraft.

Ethnography is particularly valuable early because it can widen the space of variables. It is not primarily a measurement instrument. It is a variable-discovery instrument.

That makes it strategically important during disruption. Incumbents typically possess abundant data about existing customers performing existing behaviors. They are weakest where the category itself is changing.

A spreadsheet can tell a retailer how customers moved through last year's channels. Observation can reveal that younger consumers no longer think of the channel distinctions the company uses internally. A bank can have exquisite transaction data and weak understanding of why people use informal financial networks. An automaker can know vehicle preferences and miss that urban consumers are reorganizing around access rather than ownership.

The new future appears first as behavior that does not fit the database.

This is why edge cases matter. The mainstream customer optimizes the existing system. Unusual users often reveal where the system's boundaries are porous.

Design researchers sometimes study extreme users for this reason. A person with an unusually intense need can make hidden constraints visible. The insight does not automatically generalize, but it can expose possibilities that average behavior smooths away.

Strategic rehearsal benefits from the same technique. Imagine not only the median customer but the customer who hates the category, hacks the product, combines it with something unintended, or refuses the ownership model entirely.

The point is not to chase every fringe behavior. It is to prevent averages from defining imagination.

Consumer markets are also theaters of identity. Products do not merely solve tasks; they signal belonging, aspiration, taste, care, thrift, rebellion, competence, and virtue. These meanings are socially produced. A brand cannot fully specify them.

This makes consumer rehearsal recursive. The company proposes a meaning. Consumers reinterpret it. Other consumers observe the reinterpretation. The company responds. Culture moves.

Fashion makes this obvious, but the pattern reaches technology, cars, food, financial products, and even workplace tools. A product marketed as efficient can become status-coded. A cheap object can become collectible. A professional tool can become a consumer toy. Meanings migrate through use.

This is one reason trend forecasting is so fragile. The object is embedded in a social game whose players notice one another.

A trend becomes less exclusive as it spreads. The success of a signal can destroy the signal. Consumers rehearse how other consumers will interpret them.

Thorstein Veblen's language of conspicuous consumption captured one form of this social recursion more than a century ago. Pierre Bourdieu later analyzed taste as connected to social position and distinction. Modern consumer culture has multiplied the speed and visibility of these processes through social media, but the underlying problem is old: value can depend on what an object says about the person who has it and about the people who do not.

This complicates product testing because isolated preference may not survive social exposure. A shoe admired alone may feel different once everyone wears it. A luxury brand can grow revenue and weaken scarcity. A platform can become less desirable when parents arrive.

The market is a group rehearsal in which everyone watches everyone else.

Companies try to simulate this with communities, influencer campaigns, seeding, waitlists, and social metrics. These interventions are not merely observation. They are attempts to create the future being tested.

This is a morally important distinction.

Market research traditionally imagines a company learning about demand. Growth systems increasingly manufacture contexts that shape demand and then measure the response. Recommendation algorithms, notifications, ranking systems, limited-time offers, and personalized interfaces alter the environment while observing behavior inside it.

The consumer becomes both participant and experimental subject.

This can produce useful personalization and predatory design with the same technical machinery.

The ethics turn on agency, transparency, asymmetry, and consequence. Does the experiment help the user achieve a goal she endorses, or exploit a vulnerability against her longer-term interest? Can she understand or escape the manipulation? Who captures the value of what is learned?

These questions become sharper when machine learning makes experimentation continuous. A platform can run thousands of micro-rehearsals across users, adjusting interfaces in near real time. The old product launch becomes an ongoing adaptive test.

This is economically powerful because learning and production merge.

It is also epistemically messy. When the system is constantly changing, causal interpretation becomes harder. Interactions accumulate. Users adapt. Metrics shift. Local improvements can degrade long-term trust.

The classic A/B test appears clean because it compares variants under controlled assignment. Real product ecosystems contain network effects, spillovers, repeated exposure, and strategic users. What improves click-through today may train behavior that changes tomorrow's baseline.

The rehearsal changes the actor.

This is the book's recurring loop again. A consumer sees a recommendation, changes behavior, produces data, and changes future recommendations. The system rehearses the consumer while the consumer rehearses the system.

Feedback can narrow taste. It can also help people discover. The outcome depends on objective functions, exploration, diversity, and design.

From a business perspective, the temptation is to optimize what can be measured quickly. Clicks arrive faster than trust. Conversion arrives faster than habit quality. Retention arrives faster than social consequence.

Fast feedback dominates slow values.

This is a general risk in rehearsal economies. The parts of reality that return signals quickly become overrepresented in learning systems. Music practice has immediate auditory feedback. Corporate culture does not. A model can optimize latency in milliseconds and miss resentment growing over years.

Consumer research can counter this by mixing timescales. Immediate behavior, longitudinal diaries, retention, complaints, interviews, ethnography, and market outcomes answer different questions.

Plural evidence is less elegant and often more truthful.

It also reveals contradictions. People say one thing and do another. Short-term conversion rises while long-term satisfaction falls. A feature is beloved by heavy users and repels newcomers. The contradiction is not necessarily noise. It may be the market's structure.

Executives prefer a single customer truth because decisions require action. Rehearsal's job is not to preserve ambiguity forever. It is to make the ambiguity visible before action pretends it never existed.

The best consumer researcher therefore behaves less like an advocate for the customer and more like a translator between incompatible forms of evidence.

What people say. What they do. What they can afford. What they aspire to. What they avoid. What changes in groups. What changes over time.

A market is not a referendum on a survey question.

It is a performance under constraints.

The consumer's dress rehearsal matters because it lets companies see fragments of that performance before spending the full cost of launch. But the company must remain humble about the stage it built.

A lab is not a kitchen. A focus group is not a checkout line. A click is not a life.

The closer research gets to real behavior, the more ethical responsibility it acquires. The farther it remains from real behavior, the more inferential humility it requires.

Between those two limits lies the practical craft of learning what people might do before betting the company on what they said.
