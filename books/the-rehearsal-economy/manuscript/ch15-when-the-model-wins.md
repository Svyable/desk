# When the Model Wins

The most dangerous rehearsal is the one everyone passes.

Success feels like evidence. The alarms sounded, the team responded, the simulator landed, the portfolio survived, the product passed the test, the model hit the benchmark. The organization receives a certificate of readiness.

Then reality chooses a different question.

This is the recurring failure mode of rehearsal economies: people become excellent at the representation.

Schools teach to exams. Banks manage to regulatory models. Software teams code to tests. Military units learn exercise rules. Machine-learning systems optimize benchmarks. Employees learn performance metrics. Consumers learn promotion cycles. Every evaluator creates an environment, and adaptive agents respond.

Adaptation is not cheating. It is intelligence.

The problem is alignment between the rehearsal and the purpose.

When an exam captures important knowledge, studying for it can be learning. When the exam samples a narrow proxy, studying can improve scores while leaving competence unchanged. When a safety test captures real hazards, design improvement is the intended outcome. When the test omits a failure mode, passing can concentrate attention away from the omission.

This is why proxies degrade under pressure.

The phenomenon often associated with Goodhart's law has many versions, but the intuition is enough: once a measure becomes consequential, behavior reorganizes around it. The statistical relationship that made the measure informative can weaken.

Rehearsal accelerates this because rehearsal is repeated contact with the evaluator.

A student sees past questions. A trader sees risk limits. A model developer sees benchmark results. A team sees what the exercise controller rewards. Each repetition reveals the game.

The better the participant learns, the less independent the test becomes.

This is why high-stakes evaluators need rotation, hidden cases, out-of-sample testing, qualitative judgment, and periodic redesign.

The principle is familiar in machine learning: a model tuned repeatedly against a validation set can effectively overfit it even if the set was initially held out. Information about performance leaks through iterative development.

Human organizations overfit too.

A hospital can become excellent at an accreditation ritual. A company can perfect board materials while operational reality worsens. A government agency can meet response-time targets by redefining when the clock starts. A factory can optimize reported defects while shifting rework elsewhere.

The dashboard becomes the workplace.

Rehearsal is supposed to prepare people for reality. Overfitting makes reality prepare people for rehearsal.

This can happen through ritualization. The first crisis exercise is uncomfortable. The third is smoother. By the tenth, everyone knows where to stand. Improvement may reflect genuine learning. It may also reflect familiarity with the scenario architecture.

The exercise stops producing surprise.

Organizations often mistake this smoothness for maturity.

A better signal is whether the exercise continues to discover consequential unknowns. If every run ends with minor procedural tweaks, either the system is truly robust or the rehearsal has become domesticated.

The test needs mutation.

Change timing. Remove a resource. Introduce conflicting information. Swap roles. Use an unfamiliar facility. Involve outsiders. Attack a dependency everyone assumes stable. Ask the adversary to pursue a different objective.

Variation keeps the rehearsal from becoming choreography.

But variation can be theatrical too. Randomness is not rigor. A bizarre scenario may create drama without testing plausible vulnerability.

The design principle is adversarial relevance.

The scenario should threaten assumptions that matter.

This is where counterevidence becomes valuable. After each rehearsal, ask what the test failed to represent. Which real historical cases would have defeated our setup? Which critics would say the model is too easy? What changed in the environment since the scenario was designed?

The rehearsal needs a rehearsal review.

The problem becomes more serious when many institutions use related models. Shared frameworks improve coordination and comparability. They also create model monoculture.

Finance provides an obvious case. Risk practices, ratings, regulations, and market conventions can push firms toward similar assumptions. When conditions change, synchronized responses create cascades.

Technology has similar risk. If many companies rely on the same cloud provider, model family, cybersecurity library, or data source, local redundancy can hide common dependence.

Rehearsal should test the shared dependency, not merely each participant.

This is hard because organizational boundaries hide system structure. A company sees two suppliers and calls them diversified without knowing both depend on the same port. A bank sees different counterparties whose exposures converge elsewhere. A city sees independent telecom providers sharing physical infrastructure.

The system is less diverse than the contracts suggest.

Model victory can also occur politically. A quantified representation becomes the language decision makers trust, so unmodeled harms lose standing.

Suppose a transportation model optimizes average travel time. The result may be excellent on that objective while worsening neighborhood division or access for people not well represented in data. The model has not failed mathematically. It has won the authority to define success.

This is more dangerous than an error message.

Errors invite correction. Successful optimization can make its own omissions invisible.

The rehearsal economy therefore requires objective audits, not only model audits.

What is being optimized? Who chose it? Which values are absent because they are difficult to quantify? Which population bears the downside? What tradeoffs are hidden inside a single score?

These are governance questions.

The same applies to corporate metrics. A customer-support center can optimize call duration and make customers call twice. A warehouse can optimize pick speed and raise injury risk. A hospital can optimize throughput and weaken communication. Local excellence can create global failure.

Systems thinking is the discipline of asking where the cost moved.

Rehearsal should follow the cost.

If the model says performance improved, inspect adjacent variables. If a process got faster, ask what quality changed. If a portfolio got safer, ask where tail risk went. If automation reduced labor, ask who now handles exceptions.

Every optimization has a boundary.

Failure often accumulates just outside it.

The Challenger disaster is frequently invoked in discussions of organizational risk, sometimes too casually. The specific history deserves care: engineers and managers wrestled with evidence about O-ring performance under cold conditions, organizational pressures, and launch decisions in a complex program. The broader lesson is not a slogan about ignoring engineers. It is that technical risk lives inside organizational communication, schedule, normalization, and evidence standards.

Prior success can change the meaning of anomaly.

If a system repeatedly survives a concerning condition, people may gradually treat the condition as acceptable. Diane Vaughan's analysis of the Challenger launch decision described “normalization of deviance” as part of a wider organizational process, not simple recklessness.

Rehearsal can counter normalization or accelerate it.

A test that repeatedly shows a warning without consequence may teach people to ignore the warning. A simulation where operators regularly override alarms because they know the scenario can train dangerous habits. A model that underpredicts risk during calm periods can make users more comfortable with its blind spots.

Absence of failure becomes evidence of safety even when exposure has been limited.

This is a classic induction problem. The turkey is well fed every day until a different day arrives.

Statistics helps when sample sizes and assumptions are understood. Culture helps when people preserve anomalies instead of averaging them away.

Near misses are especially valuable.

A near miss is an event in which failure almost occurred but consequence was avoided. Organizations can treat it as success or as free information.

The rehearsal mindset chooses information.

A system that records near misses gains more learning events than one that studies only disasters. Aviation safety cultures, medicine, industrial operations, and cybersecurity all use versions of this logic.

Yet reporting depends on incentives. If near misses create punishment, people hide them. If they create no response, people stop reporting.

Again, culture determines the data available to the model.

This is why risk systems cannot be purely technical. Information is produced by people deciding what is safe to say.

Model failure also arises from regime change. A relationship that held historically can stop. Consumer behavior shifts. Monetary policy changes. Technology alters cost curves. Regulation rewrites incentives. Climate changes physical baselines.

Rehearsal based on stationary history becomes backward-looking precisely when novelty matters most.

The answer is not to throw away history. Base rates remain powerful. The answer is to distinguish stable mechanisms from contingent patterns.

A bridge's material properties may be more stable than a market correlation. A demographic trend may change slowly. A platform's distribution advantage can change quickly after regulation or a new interface.

Different variables deserve different confidence horizons.

Scenario design should make this explicit.

Which assumptions are laws, which are engineering constants, which are contractual, which are behavioral regularities, and which are recent habits?

Organizations often mix them inside one spreadsheet.

The result is false solidity.

Another failure mode is automation bias. When a system produces recommendations reliably, users can defer even when they possess contradictory information. The representation wins because it has a history of convenience.

This is particularly dangerous in rare edge cases, exactly where the model has less evidence and human attention is most needed.

Training should therefore include model disagreement.

Give operators cases where automation is wrong. Teach them what evidence justifies override. Preserve manual skills. Make uncertainty visible.

A backup that is never practiced decays.

The same problem appears in management. Leaders delegate forecasting to analytics teams and gradually lose the ability to interrogate assumptions. Dashboards become oracles.

Quantitative literacy should mean asking better questions of numbers, not surrendering to them.

What data generated this? What is missing? What changed? How sensitive is the result? What would make this estimate wrong? Which decision actually depends on it?

These are rehearsal questions.

The final form of model victory is psychological. People begin preferring the rehearsal because it is controllable.

Planning feels productive. Simulation produces outputs. Workshops create language. More analysis postpones the irreversible choice.

Rehearsal becomes avoidance.

This can happen to individuals, companies, and governments. The future is studied until the option expires. Another prototype is requested. Another scenario is run. Another committee reviews.

The cost of uncertainty is converted into the comfort of process.

No model can identify the exact moment when enough rehearsal is enough because that decision depends on stakes, reversibility, and opportunity.

But some signs are clear. New runs no longer change beliefs. The same findings recur. The remaining uncertainties can only be resolved through action. Delay has a growing cost. The organization is using analysis to avoid ownership.

At that point the rehearsal has done its job.

It must lose.

Reality has to win eventually.

That is the discipline the rehearsal economy can easily forget. We build second worlds because the first world is expensive. The second worlds become rich, instrumented, and persuasive. We spend careers improving them.

Then a door opens, the market moves, the patient arrives, the rocket launches, the vote occurs, the machine starts, the conversation begins.

The representation becomes history's footnote.

The best rehearsal cultures are not the ones with the most sophisticated models.

They are the ones that know how to be corrected by the world.
