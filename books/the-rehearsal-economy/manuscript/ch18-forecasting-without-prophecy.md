# Forecasting Without Prophecy

A forecast becomes more useful when it stops pretending to be fate.

This sounds paradoxical because the apparent purpose of forecasting is to say what will happen. In practice, many of the best forecasting systems do something more modest. They attach explicit uncertainty to claims, update as evidence arrives, compare predictions with outcomes, and improve calibration over time.

The cultural shift is from prophecy to bookkeeping.

Prophecy has prestige. A person makes a bold call, events follow, and the call becomes biography. Misses are forgotten more easily than hits. Specificity after the event makes foresight look cleaner than it was.

Bookkeeping is less glamorous. On Tuesday I believed this outcome had a 60 percent chance. On Friday new evidence moved me to 45. Across many comparable judgments, my 60 percent forecasts happened about this often.

The forecaster becomes measurable.

Prediction tournaments studied by Philip Tetlock and colleagues helped popularize this discipline. Some participants consistently produced better probabilistic forecasts than others across geopolitical questions. Techniques included decomposition, base rates, frequent updating, and avoidance of ideological certainty. The results do not mean the future is broadly predictable. They show that judgment can improve when it is made explicit enough to receive feedback.

This is rehearsal with a score.

A forecast is a miniature commitment. It records what the person expects before the event. The outcome then provides feedback.

The scoring rule matters. If people are rewarded only for dramatic correct calls, they will make dramatic calls. If probability estimates are evaluated for calibration and discrimination, the incentive shifts toward honest uncertainty.

This has management implications.

Organizations often ask executives for point forecasts: next quarter's revenue, project completion date, customer demand. The format hides uncertainty and encourages negotiation. A sales forecast can become a target, which means the forecast is no longer simply a belief. People shade numbers based on incentives.

The estimate becomes politics.

Separating forecast, target, and commitment can improve truth.

A target is what the organization wants. A forecast is what it expects. A commitment is what someone promises to deliver. Conflating them corrupts all three.

If the sales team knows a realistic forecast will be treated as insufficient ambition, it learns optimism. If a project manager knows a padded estimate will be cut by leadership, she adds more padding. The organization develops a game around numbers.

Rehearsal becomes bargaining.

Explicit probability can help, but only if culture permits it. Saying “70 percent” sounds weak in institutions that reward certainty. Leaders may demand one answer.

One answer is necessary for some actions. The mistake is pretending the action proves certainty.

A company can choose one factory location while maintaining uncertainty about demand. A government can choose a policy while acknowledging multiple scenarios. A doctor can recommend treatment under probabilistic evidence.

Decision and certainty are different variables.

This distinction is psychologically important because people often use certainty to justify action. “We need to decide” becomes “we know.” Rehearsal lets us decide while keeping alternative worlds alive.

That improves updating.

If a leader publicly claims absolute confidence, contrary evidence threatens identity. If the original decision was framed as conditional—best choice given current evidence—revision becomes less humiliating.

Calibration is therefore cultural infrastructure for adaptation.

Probability also disciplines vividness. A scenario can be memorable and low probability. A boring outcome can be likely.

Forecasting asks both likelihood and consequence. Rehearsal often chooses scenarios by consequence rather than probability. The two tools complement each other.

A severe bank stress test may represent an unlikely configuration worth practicing. A revenue forecast should represent expected outcomes. Confusing their purposes creates bad decisions.

The same problem appears in news and politics. Extreme scenarios receive attention because they are narratively strong. Public discourse can then confuse salience with probability.

Institutions need a portfolio of imagination.

Some resources go to likely futures because they drive ordinary planning. Some go to high-consequence tails because ruin deserves attention. Some go to unknowns through flexible capacity and monitoring.

No single forecast can serve all three.

This is where scenario planning and probability meet.

A scenario should not always carry a precise probability. Some uncertainties are too structural. But decision makers can still ask whether the world is central, plausible, or exploratory and what evidence would move it closer.

The point is to prevent all scenarios from becoming equally serious just because they have equal page length.

Narrative symmetry is not probabilistic symmetry.

Forecasting also benefits from decomposition. Large questions are difficult. Break them into drivers.

Will a product succeed? That contains adoption, price, retention, costs, competition, regulation, and execution. Which pieces can be estimated from data? Which are strategic? Which are binary dependencies?

Decomposition turns a story into inspectable claims.

It can also create false independence. Variables interact. Customer adoption affects competitor response. Regulation affects cost and demand. The modeler must know when recombination loses the system.

Again, rehearsal alternates between simplification and reintegration.

The outside view is especially powerful for timelines. Organizations repeatedly believe their project is special. Sometimes it is. Usually it also belongs to a reference class.

What happened to similar construction projects, drug programs, enterprise software migrations, mergers, or regulatory approvals?

Reference classes provide a prior.

Specific evidence updates the prior.

This is Bayesian in spirit even when nobody calculates a posterior. Begin with what usually happens, then adjust for what is genuinely different.

The danger is choosing a flattering reference class.

A startup compares itself with surviving category leaders rather than all entrants. A megaproject compares itself with the rare similar project completed on time. A politician selects historical analogies that support the preferred policy.

Reference-class selection is itself a strategic judgment.

Good forecasting exposes it.

Prediction markets offer another institutional technology for aggregating beliefs. Participants buy and sell contracts tied to outcomes, turning willingness to risk money or points into prices that can be interpreted as collective expectations under certain assumptions.

The appeal is that information can enter without hierarchy. A junior employee who knows a project is delayed can trade on that belief even if she would not contradict a senior manager in a meeting.

Markets create an alternate communication channel.

They also have limits: liquidity, participant selection, incentives, contract design, legal constraints, and correlated information. A market price is not truth.

Still, the mechanism illustrates a broad principle. Rehearsal systems should make it possible for distributed knowledge to alter the shared model.

Organizations are full of private forecasts.

The engineer knows the schedule is fantasy. Sales knows the customer is hesitant. Procurement knows the supplier is fragile. Finance knows cash is tighter than the plan implies. Each fact can remain local because speaking upward is costly.

Forecasting mechanisms can surface the disagreement before the deadline does.

This is less about numerical sophistication than institutional honesty.

A beautifully calibrated model fed political inputs will be politically calibrated.

The strongest forecasting cultures therefore record disagreement. Do not average everything into one number too early. Preserve the range. Ask why estimates differ.

Disagreement can reveal information structure.

One person knows something. Or one person has a different model. Or incentives differ. Or the question is ambiguous.

The reason matters more than the average.

Forecast review should also distinguish process from outcome. A 20 percent event occurs one time in five. When it happens, the forecaster was not necessarily wrong. The probability assignment should be judged across repeated opportunities, not by whether one event surprised us emotionally.

This is difficult for organizations because they live through single outcomes.

The project either missed the date or did not. The acquisition either worked or did not. The hurricane hit or turned.

Humans prefer verdicts.

Calibration requires populations of decisions.

This is why long-term institutional records matter. A leader can maintain a decision journal. A company can score forecasts across projects. An investment committee can compare expected and realized outcomes over years.

The sample remains messy, but memory becomes less selective.

Forecasting without prophecy also improves character.

It makes “I don't know” granular.

Not knowing can mean 50/50. It can mean no model. It can mean evidence is missing. It can mean outcomes depend on another actor. It can mean uncertainty is irreducible.

Different ignorance requires different action.

If more data can reduce uncertainty cheaply, gather it. If the uncertainty is strategic, rehearse responses. If the uncertainty is irreducible, preserve flexibility. If a tail risk threatens ruin, buy protection.

The rehearsal economy needs this taxonomy because otherwise every unknown becomes a demand for more analysis.

Sometimes another forecast is not useful.

A person choosing whether to have a child cannot calculate the life. A founder cannot know the exact market. A country cannot assign credible probabilities to every geopolitical transformation.

Decision remains.

Forecasting is valuable when it improves the decision, not when it postpones commitment until certainty appears.

The mature forecaster knows the boundary.

Probability is a language for uncertainty, not an escape from it.

The future does not become less alive because we attach numbers to our beliefs.

It becomes harder for us to pretend we always knew.
