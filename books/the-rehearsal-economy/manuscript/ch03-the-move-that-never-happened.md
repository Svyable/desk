# The Move That Never Happened

A chess player can spend most of a game on moves that are never made.

The board shows one position. The player's mind branches. If knight takes, then pawn recaptures; if the queen checks, the king moves; if the exchange is delayed, perhaps the file opens. Lines are explored, abandoned, revisited. The visible game advances one move at a time while an invisible population of possible games appears and dies behind each decision.

This is rehearsal stripped to its cognitive minimum: action without action.

Chess is attractive to scientists because the rules are explicit and the outcome is legible. It is also misleading if treated as a model for life. Real decisions rarely offer a stable board, complete information, or an opponent whose legal moves are known. Still, chess reveals a basic economic fact about thought. Imagining a move is usually cheaper than making it. A mind that can simulate consequences before commitment has an advantage over one that must learn only through physical trial.

Animals appear to exploit versions of this advantage, and humans have turned it into culture. We tell stories before journeys. We draw plans before buildings. We calculate trajectories before launch. We ask a child what she thinks will happen if the glass tips. The capacity to hold a possible future apart from the present is so ordinary that its strangeness disappears.

Game trees gave the idea a formal shape. In finite games with well-specified rules, possible moves can be represented as branches from a current state. Each choice opens further choices. Minimax reasoning asks a player to consider not only the move she wants but the opponent's best response, then her best response to that response. Strategy becomes recursively social. I rehearse your rehearsal of my rehearsal.

The recursion matters outside games because anticipation changes behavior. A price is not merely an estimate of value; it can contain estimates of other people's estimates. A military posture can be designed around beliefs about an adversary's beliefs. A negotiation offer can be chosen partly for the reaction it is expected to produce. Once agents model one another, the rehearsal becomes part of the environment being rehearsed.

This is why markets are difficult to simulate. If a strategy becomes known and widely used, its effects can alter the patterns that made it attractive. The rehearsal leaks into reality. The observer becomes a participant.

John Maynard Keynes made a related point with his famous beauty-contest metaphor for markets: participants may care less about their own judgment than about what they expect average opinion to judge average opinion to be. The metaphor persists because it captures a real recursive pressure. You do not simply predict the asset. You predict other predictors.

That logic produces both intelligence and absurdity. A trader may hold a sophisticated model of a security and still lose because the relevant short-term variable is everyone else's model. A company may correctly foresee a technology's promise and enter too early because customers, suppliers, regulators, and capital markets have not reached the same expectation. A government can announce a policy partly to change expectations before the policy itself has done anything.

Rehearsal in strategic environments is therefore not just a private preview. It is a move in a game of previews.

Children discover this before they know the language. Much play consists of trying social roles in low-consequence form. One child becomes the doctor, another the patient. Rules are negotiated. Violations are punished with argument rather than law. The game teaches not only the role but what other players expect from the role. Pretend worlds are laboratories for social prediction.

Adult organizations preserve this structure in more formal clothing. Negotiation training assigns parties confidential interests and asks them to bargain. Intelligence analysts conduct red-team exercises. Sales teams role-play difficult customers. Lawyers hold moot courts. Diplomats run crisis simulations. The important feature is not theatricality. It is that another mind is allowed to surprise the plan.

A rehearsal with no adversarial freedom is often a presentation.

This becomes clear in computer games. Early game-playing programs searched trees more deeply than humans could. Their strength came partly from computation: examine many possible continuations, evaluate resulting positions, prune branches that look inferior, and continue. Modern systems changed the balance between explicit search, learned evaluation, and self-play, but the underlying power remains familiar. Performance improves by generating encounters that do not need to occur in the external world.

Self-play is one of the purest examples of a rehearsal economy. A system creates opponents from versions of itself and produces experience at machine speed. It does not need to wait for the world to supply millions of high-quality human adversaries. The cost of another game approaches the cost of compute.

This is a dramatic form of what human institutions have long tried to do more slowly. A military creates an opposing force. A debate team assigns someone to defend the other side. A company asks a group to attack a product strategy. The point is to manufacture disagreement rather than wait for reality to provide it at full cost.

Manufactured disagreement has a quality-control problem. The simulated opponent may be too stupid, too polite, too culturally similar, or too constrained by the host institution's assumptions. A chess engine is formidable because the rules of chess tell it exactly what counts as a legal surprise. A business competitor is not so cooperative. It may change the business model, recruit your employees, lobby a regulator, bundle the product with something else, or decide the market is not worth entering at all.

This difference separates closed-world rehearsal from open-world rehearsal.

Closed worlds have boundaries that participants agree not to violate. Chess is closed. A flight simulator is partly closed: the aircraft model and scenario define what can occur, though instructors can create many combinations. Markets, wars, politics, epidemics, and technological transitions are open in a more troubling sense. Their participants can invent new moves while the game is being played.

The more open the world, the less useful exhaustive search becomes and the more important analogy, judgment, and diversity become. You cannot enumerate every future of a company. You can, however, practice several different causal stories about how the company might fail.

This suggests a different view of strategic intelligence. It is not the ability to predict the branch that reality will choose. It is the ability to maintain enough plausible branches that surprise does not collapse decision-making.

That sounds inefficient, and it is. Optionality costs money. Multiple plans duplicate effort. Maintaining alternate suppliers is more expensive than relying on the cheapest one. Holding cash has an opportunity cost. Training people for rare events consumes time that could produce immediate output. Rehearsal is a tax on the present paid for resilience in uncertain futures.

Organizations under short-term pressure therefore underinvest in it. The return is hard to show when nothing goes wrong. A successfully prevented disaster leaves weak evidence of its prevention. The unused backup looks wasteful. The war game that changes a plan may be credited less than the plan itself. The scenario that prepares a company for a shock cannot prove that it caused the eventual response.

There is a familiar asymmetry here. Performance is visible; preparedness is counterfactual.

Chess avoids this problem because the counterfactual lines can be analyzed after the game. Players review positions, compare candidate moves, and ask where evaluation changed. The game becomes a dataset for improving future games. Many fields would like this clarity and cannot have it.

The difficulty of learning from decisions grows with the delay and ambiguity of feedback. A chess blunder may be exposed within moves. A corporate acquisition can look brilliant for two years and disastrous in year five. A foreign-policy decision may be judged differently across decades. The farther feedback is from action, the easier it is for narratives to replace evidence.

Rehearsal can help by forcing assumptions to become explicit before outcomes are known. If a board records why it believes an acquisition will create value, what would falsify the thesis, and which scenarios would require retreat, later evaluation becomes harder to rewrite. The rehearsal leaves a trace of what people actually believed.

That trace has moral value. Hindsight protects reputations by making history look more predictable than it was. A recorded decision tree preserves uncertainty. It reminds later judges that serious alternatives existed.

Probability enters here. A tree without probabilities is a catalog of possibilities. A forecast assigns weights. Humans are not naturally graceful at this. We overweight vivid outcomes, neglect base rates, anchor on available numbers, and often treat confidence as evidence. Behavioral economics documented many such regularities, though effect sizes and generality vary by task and context.

Rehearsal can reduce some biases and strengthen others.

It can reduce availability bias by making neglected possibilities vivid. A disaster exercise gives emotional and operational weight to an event that has never happened locally. A pre-mortem asks people to imagine that a project has failed and generate reasons, legitimizing doubts that social pressure might otherwise suppress. Scenario planning can force executives to inhabit futures inconsistent with the current plan.

But vivid rehearsal can also make rare events feel too likely. Simulating a cyberattack repeatedly may inflate its psychological probability relative to quieter risks. A dramatic war game can dominate strategic imagination because it is memorable. Scenario participants can confuse narrative coherence with likelihood.

The solution is not to remove narrative. Humans reason partly through stories because stories preserve sequence, agency, and consequence. The solution is to keep possibility and probability separate. A scenario can be worth rehearsing because it is consequential, not because it is most likely.

This distinction is obvious in emergency training. Firefighters practice catastrophic events precisely because rarity does not eliminate consequence. Airlines train for failures that crews may never encounter in service. A financial institution can stress a severe recession without claiming that the recession is its central forecast.

The useful question is often not “What will happen?” but “Which futures would we regret being unable to handle?”

Games also expose the danger of scorekeeping. Once a rehearsal has winners and losers, participants optimize the scoring system. This can improve engagement, but it can distort learning. Military exercises can become competitions between units. Sales training can reward closing the simulated customer rather than diagnosing whether the product is appropriate. Cyber teams can chase flags in environments unlike the networks they actually defend.

The metric begins as feedback and becomes strategy.

Good game design therefore tries to align the score with the purpose while preserving room for unexpected behavior. This is the same challenge regulators face when creating bank stress tests, schools face when designing exams, and platforms face when evaluating recommendation systems. Every proxy invites gaming because humans and machines are good at rehearsal too. They learn the evaluator.

There is a recursive joke in this. The institution builds a game to model behavior. Participants model the game. The institution then models the participants modeling the game. Soon everyone is training for everyone else's training.

Finance can look like this. Banks anticipate regulatory models. Regulators anticipate bank responses. Traders anticipate central bank communication. Central banks anticipate market interpretation of that communication. Signals are chosen partly for the reactions to signals. The system becomes reflexive not because anyone is irrational, but because everyone is reasoning about other reasoners.

At high levels of recursion, simplicity becomes valuable. Strategy cannot consist of thinking one step deeper forever. Chess has finite clocks. Organizations have deadlines. Humans have limited working memory. The practical question is where to stop.

Experts often stop not because they have exhausted the tree but because they recognize structures. A chess master sees patterns that reduce search. An experienced emergency physician recognizes a syndrome. A skilled negotiator notices a familiar concession pattern. Expertise compresses rehearsal.

That compression can be powerful and dangerous. Pattern recognition is fast because it ignores detail. When the environment has changed, the same compression can hide novelty. Experienced investors can be trapped by regimes they have not lived through. Military institutions can carry doctrine from the last war. Executives can map a new technology onto an old industry structure and miss what changed.

This is why diverse rehearsal matters. Newcomers may lack refined patterns, but they also lack some entrenched ones. A team composed entirely of experts from the same tradition may simulate the future with impressive consistency and shared blind spots.

The strongest rehearsal systems alternate between depth and disruption. They let experts exploit knowledge, then introduce conditions that make the knowledge less comfortable. Remove a resource. Change the opponent. Reverse an assumption. Ask a junior participant to play the adversary. Make the winning condition different.

The purpose is not chaos. It is to test whether competence survives outside the narrow path that produced it.

Chess players sometimes study positions from openings they do not play because unfamiliar structure exposes habits. Musicians practice in different acoustics. Athletes vary drills. Engineers test boundary conditions. A company can rehearse a future in which its strongest asset becomes a liability.

This last exercise is especially useful because success creates hidden dependencies. A firm known for a particular distribution channel may organize everything around that channel. A nation with a military advantage may build doctrine around preserving it. A person praised for decisiveness may stop noticing situations that reward patience. Strength becomes an assumption.

Rehearsal lets us temporarily confiscate the assumption.

Imagine the company without its largest customer. The city without cellular service. The hospital without the electronic record. The portfolio after correlations jump. The executive team without the chief executive. The software service after a cloud region fails. These are not forecasts. They are moves that never happened, played so that the organization can discover what it has accidentally made indispensable.

The most useful branch in a decision tree may be the one no planner believes will occur.

That is because rehearsal is not only about selecting action. It is also about discovering structure. A hypothetical shock reveals dependencies that ordinary operation conceals. The question “What would we do if this vanished?” is a form of X-ray.

At the individual level, mental simulation can serve the same purpose. Before a difficult conversation, a person may imagine several responses. The benefit is not scripting the other person. It is noticing her own triggers. What answer would make her defensive? What information would change her position? Where is she assuming agreement?

The danger, again, is overconfidence. Rehearsed conversations can make the other person feel strangely disobedient when they fail to follow the script. The more vivid the internal simulation, the easier it is to confuse familiarity with knowledge.

Mature rehearsal carries an expiration date. It prepares action while leaving room for reality to invalidate it.

That is the difference between a script and a strategy.

A script says what will happen next. A strategy says what matters when what happens next is uncertain.

The chess player eventually reaches for a piece. The invisible games disappear into one visible move. At that moment rehearsal ends and consequence begins.

The discipline is not to have imagined everything. It is to have imagined enough that the move is chosen with open eyes—and to remain capable of thinking again when the opponent refuses to cooperate.
