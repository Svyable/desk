# Managing the Second World

Every large organization now has at least two versions of itself.

One is the company that operates. The other is the company represented in plans, budgets, dashboards, forecasts, org charts, risk registers, simulations, digital models, and performance systems.

Management happens between them.

The represented company is necessary because the real one is too large to perceive directly. A chief executive cannot stand in every warehouse, read every customer ticket, inspect every line of code, and hear every sales call. Management requires compression.

Compression creates the second world.

Revenue summarizes transactions. Headcount summarizes people. utilization summarizes time. Customer satisfaction summarizes experiences. Risk ratings summarize uncertainties.

The danger is obvious and unavoidable: the second world becomes easier to manage than the first.

Dashboards obey meetings. Reality does not.

This is why good management is not merely reading representations. It is maintaining the feedback between representation and operation.

Walk the floor. Listen to calls. Read raw complaints. Visit customers. Inspect samples. Ask the person closest to the exception.

These practices can become executive theater too. The staged factory tour and selected customer meeting simply add another curated representation.

Contact with reality has to preserve the possibility of surprise.

One practical method is exception sampling. Instead of looking only at averages, inspect cases that do not fit: the order that took ten times longer, the customer who uses the product strangely, the employee who bypassed the process, the region whose economics differ.

Exceptions test the model's boundary.

Another is decision review. Choose a consequential decision after enough time has passed and reconstruct what was known, what was assumed, and what happened.

This turns management into a learning system rather than a sequence of verdicts.

The key is to avoid hindsight prosecution.

If every review asks who was wrong, people learn to hide uncertainty. If no review assigns responsibility, learning becomes vague.

A mature review separates decision quality from outcome and still asks whether the process met standards.

Was the evidence appropriate? Were alternatives considered? Were incentives disclosed? Did the decision owner update when conditions changed?

These are rehearseable behaviors.

Management training often focuses on abstract competencies. The rehearsal economy suggests organizing around recurrent decisions instead.

Hiring. firing. pricing. launching. stopping. escalating. allocating capital. resolving conflict. responding to an incident. communicating uncertainty.

Each can be practiced through cases, role-play, shadowing, after-action review, and supervised responsibility.

The manager develops a repertoire.

This is especially important because management has weak feedback. A bad manager can hit a good quarter. A good manager can inherit a declining market. Employees leave for multiple reasons. Culture changes slowly.

Without designed feedback, managers learn stories about themselves.

Rehearsal provides artificial feedback loops.

A manager role-plays a difficult conversation and receives immediate response. A strategy case reveals whether she asks for base rates. An incident simulation shows whether she centralizes too much.

These are partial signals, not diagnoses.

They are better than waiting five years for a pattern to become undeniable.

Organizations should also rehearse governance, not only operations.

Boards often meet in highly structured formats that favor management's representation of the company. Risk appears as slides. Strategy appears as options already filtered.

A board rehearsal can invert the flow. Start with a hypothetical failure. Ask directors to identify what information they would wish they had seen earlier. Compare with current reporting.

This tests the information architecture.

The same applies to committees. Before a real acquisition vote, rehearse how the committee would respond to an adverse scenario. Which covenant matters? Which integration assumption is weakest? Who can stop the deal?

Governance becomes behavior rather than bylaws.

This matters because formal authority can be unused authority. A board technically able to challenge management may lack practice doing it.

Rehearsal gives dormant powers muscle memory.

Management of second worlds also requires model inventory.

Large organizations rely on countless models: demand forecasts, credit models, staffing models, pricing systems, recommendation algorithms, maintenance models, financial plans.

Some are regulated. Others quietly shape decisions with little governance.

Which models are decision-critical? Who owns them? When were they validated? What data do they use? Which assumptions changed?

This is model risk management generalized beyond banks.

Not every spreadsheet deserves bureaucracy. Governance should scale with consequence.

A small forecasting tool can be informal. A model allocating healthcare, credit, employment, or safety intervention deserves stronger scrutiny.

The principle is proportional rehearsal of the rehearsal.

Management must also decide when to centralize second worlds.

One corporate forecast improves coordination. Multiple independent forecasts preserve diversity.

One risk taxonomy improves comparison. Local categories preserve context.

One simulation platform reduces cost. Alternate tools reduce common-mode error.

These are architectural tradeoffs.

The correct answer changes with scale and stakes.

A practical compromise is common interfaces with plural models. Teams agree on data definitions and decision outputs while allowing different methods underneath.

This mirrors good science: shared questions, competing hypotheses.

The organization gains comparability without epistemic monopoly.

Disagreement then becomes an asset.

If two models diverge sharply, do not average immediately. Investigate why.

Different data? Different assumptions? Different time horizon? Different incentives?

The gap contains information.

Management culture often suppresses such gaps because executives want one number.

The one-number demand pushes disagreement underground.

A better executive question is, “What would I need to believe to choose the high estimate?”

Now the range becomes decision material.

The rehearsal economy also changes meeting design.

Most meetings are presentations of second worlds. Slides arrive. Metrics are reviewed. Decisions are requested.

A rehearsal meeting is different. Participants must act inside the representation.

What do we do if the supplier fails tomorrow? Who calls whom? Which order is delayed? What cash consequence follows? Which customer promise changes?

The meeting turns nouns into verbs.

This is why tabletop exercises are powerful despite low technology. They force sequence.

Sequence exposes handoffs.

Management is mostly handoffs disguised as hierarchy.

Strategy hands to finance. Finance to operations. Product to sales. Sales to support. Headquarters to region. Day shift to night shift.

The org chart shows boxes. Failure travels across lines.

Rehearsal should follow the path of work, not the shape of reporting.

This is also how consumer experience should be managed. A customer moves horizontally across departments. The company measures vertically.

Journey rehearsal reconnects the two.

Have executives attempt the onboarding. Return the product. Call support. Apply for the service. Use the accessibility feature.

Again, avoid theater. Use real systems when safe.

The manager should occasionally inhabit the company as a user.

There is a risk of anecdotal leadership. One executive has one bad experience and reorganizes the company.

Contact with reality is not a substitute for representative data.

It is a way to interpret it.

The mature manager alternates altitude.

Aggregate, then inspect. Model, then observe. Forecast, then ask the outside view. Decide, then preserve conditions for revision.

This oscillation is the practical craft of the rehearsal economy.

Too high and management loses texture. Too low and it loses system.

The same principle applies to AI-assisted management. Models can summarize meetings, forecast staffing, identify anomalies, generate scenarios, and advise decisions.

This can increase cognitive leverage and distance leaders further from raw reality.

The more summarization a manager uses, the more intentional direct sampling becomes.

Otherwise the organization is managed through a model's editorial choices.

AI should expand the range of inspectable reality, not become a velvet curtain in front of it.

One useful pattern is disagreement routing. Ask multiple analyses. Preserve citations. Surface low-confidence areas. Make it easy to drill from summary to source.

The interface should invite skepticism.

This is the opposite of the polished executive dashboard that hides uncertainty.

Good management tools show where they are weak.

That is a strange design requirement in a market that sells confidence.

The organizations that value it will learn faster.

Managing the second world finally requires a stopping discipline.

Every analysis team can produce another scenario. Every forecast can be refined. Every simulation can add detail.

Management exists to convert representation into action.

The question is whether more rehearsal has expected decision value greater than its delay and cost.

If a new customer interview could change the launch decision, do it. If the hundredth simulation will not, stop.

This is not anti-analysis.

It is respect for analysis as a scarce resource.

The second world exists to serve the first.

The manager's deepest responsibility is to preserve that direction of authority.
