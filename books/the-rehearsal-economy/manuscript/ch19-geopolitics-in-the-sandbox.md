# Geopolitics in the Sandbox

Nations rehearse one another.

A military exercise is watched by intelligence services. A tariff threat enters corporate planning. A technology restriction changes investment. An alliance announcement changes the scenarios adversaries run. A central bank statement moves currencies before policy changes.

At geopolitical scale, rehearsal itself becomes a signal.

This makes preparation difficult to separate from performance. A country may hold an exercise partly to train forces and partly to demonstrate capability. The same movement can reassure an ally and alarm an adversary. Each side interprets the other's rehearsal through its own history and fears.

The sandbox is public.

This is a profound difference from a factory simulator or private board exercise. Strategic rehearsal can alter the environment before the event it prepares for.

Deterrence depends on this. A capability hidden perfectly cannot shape an adversary's expectation. A capability displayed too aggressively can create fear, arms racing, or pressure for preemption. States must communicate enough readiness to influence behavior without making the signal indistinguishable from preparation for attack.

Ambiguity is not a bug in geopolitics. It is part of the medium.

That makes scenario planning especially hazardous. Analysts build models of adversaries who are simultaneously building models of them. Each side consumes public doctrine, speeches, procurement, exercises, history, and intelligence. The resulting beliefs shape posture, which creates new evidence.

The loop is reflexive.

This is why strategic surprise does not always come from secret information. It can come from familiar information interpreted through the wrong model.

An adversary may possess a different tolerance for cost. Domestic politics may make an apparently irrational action rational. Leaders may value symbolic objectives outsiders discount. Military doctrine may be constrained by organizational history invisible in order-of-battle charts.

The strongest geopolitical rehearsal therefore includes culture and institutions, not only capability.

This does not mean replacing analysis with stereotypes. Quite the opposite. Serious area expertise resists caricature. Language, political structure, historical memory, elite incentives, economic constraints, and public narratives all shape strategic choice.

Ethnography at national scale is impossible in the literal sense, but the humanistic habit still matters: reconstruct action from the actor's world rather than ours.

This is difficult because empathy can be politically misread as sympathy.

To predict an adversary, one must sometimes explain why its leaders believe they are acting defensibly. That explanation can feel like moral concession. It is not.

Strategy requires understanding motives one may reject.

The failure to do so produces mirror imaging: assuming the other side values what we value, fears what we fear, and reads signals as we intend.

Rehearsal can expose mirror imaging by assigning teams to argue from different institutional positions.

What does this sanction look like from the targeted ministry? What domestic coalition benefits? Which alternative suppliers become more valuable? What narrative does the government offer its public? What does our “defensive” deployment look like on their map?

The purpose is not to predict a single response. It is to widen agency.

Economic statecraft makes this especially important because measures reshape markets. Sanctions, export controls, subsidies, tariffs, and investment restrictions create incentives for substitution, evasion, localization, and coalition building.

A static estimate can capture first-order cost and miss adaptation.

The rehearsal must run several rounds.

Round one: we impose the control. Round two: firms reroute. Round three: the targeted state subsidizes domestic capacity. Round four: allies object to extraterritorial effects. Round five: technology development changes.

Now the policy is a game, not a shock.

This is why long-term strategy is hard to infer from short-term effectiveness. A measure can impose immediate pain and accelerate the capacity that reduces leverage later.

The same is true of military advantage. A dominant capability becomes a target for asymmetric response.

Strength attracts invention.

This principle appears across business too. A platform's advantage encourages complementors to build alternatives. A monopoly's margin attracts entrants and regulators. A cybersecurity defense changes attack methods.

Rehearsal should treat advantage as a changing incentive landscape.

Geopolitics also demonstrates the importance of industrial rehearsal. A state can possess sophisticated weapons and lack capacity to replace them during prolonged conflict. Stockpiles, production lines, skilled labor, logistics, critical minerals, and supplier networks become strategic.

War reveals the economy behind the order of battle.

Peacetime exercises often focus on operational deployment because it is visible. Industrial resilience requires slower rehearsals: surge production, supplier substitution, port capacity, transport, maintenance, repair, financing.

These are less cinematic and potentially more decisive.

The same lesson applies to public health and energy security. Reserves are useful only if they can be distributed. Production capacity is useful only if inputs and skills exist. A strategic plan is only as strong as its industrial choreography.

This is rehearsal at national scale.

Countries also rehearse through reserves and stockpiles. Strategic petroleum reserves, medical supplies, food stocks, and critical-material inventories are physical options held against uncertain futures.

They are expensive because they tie up capital and require maintenance. Political pressure to reduce them rises during calm periods.

Preparedness looks like waste until it becomes scarcity.

The governance question is how to value low-frequency, high-consequence states without allowing every imagined threat to justify permanent stockpiling.

This is a portfolio problem under political uncertainty.

Diversification helps. So does substitution capacity. Strategic inventories are only one form of rehearsal. Domestic production, allied production, adaptable manufacturing, recycling, and standards that permit switching can reduce dependence.

The most resilient system may not have the largest warehouse. It may have the most practiced ability to change source.

Alliances themselves are rehearsal structures. Joint exercises, common standards, officer exchanges, shared logistics, intelligence cooperation, and planning create familiarity before crisis.

The political statement of alliance is only part of the asset.

The operational asset is practiced coordination.

Two countries can sign a treaty and discover during crisis that communications, legal authorities, rules of engagement, logistics, or procurement are incompatible. Joint rehearsal converts political promise into operational knowledge.

It also exposes asymmetry. Who bears which cost? Whose command structure dominates? Which capabilities are assumed? These disagreements are healthier before crisis.

This resembles integration testing in engineering. Components that work separately may fail at the interface.

Geopolitical interfaces are human and sovereign, which makes repair harder.

Diplomacy is therefore partly a rehearsal economy. Summits and negotiations produce protocols, working groups, hotlines, confidence-building measures, and habitual contacts. These relationships can seem ceremonial until a crisis requires someone to call someone else who will answer.

Relationship is latent infrastructure.

Backchannels are especially interesting because they preserve communication when public signaling becomes constrained. Leaders may need to reassure privately while sounding firm publicly.

The existence of multiple stages—public and private—complicates interpretation.

Observers see performance and infer preparation.

Markets participate. Currency moves, insurance prices, shipping routes, commodity inventories, and capital flows respond to geopolitical expectations. Private actors rehearse policy and conflict through portfolios.

This can provide information to governments and amplify fear.

A rumor changes prices. Prices are interpreted as evidence. Policymakers react. The rehearsal becomes causal.

George Soros used the term reflexivity in financial contexts to describe feedback between participants' perceptions and fundamentals. Whatever one thinks of the broader theory, geopolitics provides clear cases where belief changes behavior and behavior changes conditions.

This makes prediction fragile.

A widely believed invasion forecast can cause evacuation and market movement before invasion. A credible deterrent can prevent the event it predicts would be costly. A panic about shortages can produce shortages.

Forecast accuracy is not the only metric.

The intervention effect matters.

Strategic communication therefore requires thinking about second-order consequences. What will actors do because we said this? How will that behavior affect the underlying risk?

Central banking has institutionalized this logic more than many fields. Communication is understood as policy because expectations affect economic decisions.

Foreign policy often behaves similarly without equivalent calibration.

The sandbox is also shaped by technology. Satellite imagery, open-source intelligence, commercial data, social media, and ubiquitous sensors have made many actions more visible. Secrecy is harder in some domains.

Visibility changes rehearsal.

A military exercise can be analyzed globally. Commercial shipping can be tracked. Procurement leaves traces. Public analysts build models from data once restricted to states.

This broadens participation and creates noise.

The public rehearsal of geopolitics now includes online communities generating scenarios, maps, claims, and counterclaims. Some analysis is excellent. Some is propaganda or confident error.

Institutions must operate in an environment where speculative rehearsal can shape public belief rapidly.

Information resilience becomes strategic resilience.

The answer is not simply secrecy. Trustworthy institutions need ways to communicate uncertainty, correct errors, and distinguish confirmed facts from analysis.

Otherwise the vacuum fills itself.

The tragic side of geopolitical rehearsal is that preparation can become destiny through security dilemmas. One state's defensive measure looks offensive to another. The second responds defensively. The first sees confirmation of threat.

Each side's rehearsal validates the other's.

This is a feedback loop with weapons.

Breaking it may require costly signals, verification, arms-control mechanisms, communication, or shared rules that make some preparations legible.

Institutions that reduce uncertainty can be forms of mutual rehearsal governance.

Inspection regimes, notification agreements, hotlines, and confidence-building measures create constraints on interpretation.

They do not eliminate rivalry. They reduce the space in which worst-case imagination is the only available model.

This is an important counterpoint to the book's emphasis on rehearsing bad futures. Worst-case rehearsal can improve resilience and create escalation if it becomes the only lens.

A country that constantly practices betrayal may become less capable of recognizing cooperation.

A company that rehearses only competitor attack can miss partnership. A person who rehearses humiliation can enter conversation defensively.

Preparedness shapes perception.

The rehearsal economy therefore needs plural futures, including cooperative ones.

What if the adversary wants an exit? What if the competitor prefers a standard? What if a crisis creates room for institutional innovation? What would make de-escalation possible?

Optimistic scenarios can be naive. Pessimistic scenarios can be self-fulfilling.

The discipline is conditional imagination.

If these incentives shift, this cooperative path becomes plausible. If this capability changes, this deterrent path weakens. If this domestic coalition rises, policy may move.

Strategy becomes a map of branches rather than a personality test of nations.

Geopolitics makes the rehearsal economy morally serious because errors can kill people far from the room where assumptions were made.

The obligation is therefore higher: adversarial analysis without dehumanization, preparation without inevitability, modeling without pretending people are pieces.

The sandbox is never the world.

Its pieces do not have families.

The people represented by them do.
