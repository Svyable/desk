# The War Before the War

Wars produce maps with arrows after the outcome is known. Before the outcome, the arrows are questions.

Military institutions have long tried to move those questions earlier. Officers study campaigns, push pieces across maps, run command-post exercises, assign opposing forces, and ask units to fight versions of battles that may never occur. The appeal is obvious. Actual war is an intolerably expensive classroom.

The history of war gaming is often told as a progression from sand tables and painted blocks to networked simulations and synthetic battlefields. The deeper continuity is organizational. A war game creates a place where doctrine can encounter an opponent before doctrine encounters an enemy.

That distinction is more important than graphical fidelity.

A perfect digital landscape with a compliant enemy is less useful than a paper map with an imaginative adversary. The essential resource in adversarial rehearsal is agency on the other side.

This is harder to provide than it sounds. Institutions do not like being surprised by people they employ. The officer playing the enemy may know which tactics are culturally acceptable. The exercise designers may constrain moves to keep the event on schedule. Senior leaders may be invested in the doctrine under examination. Rules intended to create fairness may accidentally protect assumptions.

The result can become ceremonial combat: everyone appears to be tested, but the future has been house-trained.

The problem is not unique to militaries. Every organization that runs competitive simulations confronts it. A bank's model assumes relationships between variables. A technology company defines a benchmark. A board chooses the acquisition scenarios worth considering. A security team decides what the attacker is allowed to know. The test contains a theory of the threat before the threat arrives.

War gaming makes the politics of that theory unusually visible because somebody is assigned to embody the threat.

The Prussian tradition of Kriegsspiel turned military problems into structured games in the nineteenth century, using maps, units, rules, and umpires. It became part of a broader professional culture of staff work and planning. Later militaries developed their own forms. During the Cold War, RAND and other institutions used games, scenario analysis, systems analysis, and strategic modeling to explore nuclear deterrence and escalation under conditions where direct experimentation was impossible for moral and practical reasons.

The nuclear case is the limit condition of rehearsal economics. If the thing you want to understand cannot be safely tested even once, representation becomes unavoidable.

This does not make the representation reliable. It makes it necessary.

Strategists confronting nuclear weapons had to reason about adversaries, command systems, warning, second-strike capabilities, accidents, misperception, and political resolve without empirical experience of nuclear war between major powers. Models could clarify logic. Games could expose interactions. Historical analogy could provide fragments. None could validate the whole system.

The rehearsal therefore occupied a strange epistemic position. It was simultaneously indispensable and unprovable.

Many modern risks have this structure in weaker form. We cannot repeatedly run global financial crises to calibrate regulation. We cannot test climate policy on duplicate Earths. We cannot know the full political consequences of transformative artificial intelligence by deploying several versions of civilization. The higher the stakes and irreversibility, the more we need rehearsal—and the less able we are to verify its fidelity.

This creates a dangerous psychological effect. A detailed simulation can feel like evidence even when detail comes from assumptions.

Military models are especially vulnerable because they contain quantities that invite calculation: range, speed, detection probability, ammunition, attrition, logistics. Quantification is essential for many questions. But political conflict includes will, deception, morale, alliance behavior, domestic legitimacy, industrial adaptation, and invention. These variables resist clean specification and may dominate outcomes.

A simulation can therefore become most confident where reality is least cooperative.

History offers repeated warnings. Plans that look elegant before conflict meet weather, terrain, maintenance, frightened people, bureaucratic delay, enemy innovation, and the simple fact that the other side has been planning too. The enemy is not a random disturbance. The enemy is an adaptive model-builder.

This is what makes war fundamentally different from many engineering problems. A bridge does not study the engineer's safety margin and choose a new failure mode. An adversary can.

Strategy under competition is recursive rehearsal. Each side prepares for what it thinks the other side will do, knowing the other side is doing the same. Deception enters because altering the opponent's rehearsal can be as valuable as altering the battlefield. If you can cause an adversary to practice the wrong war, you have changed its future behavior before combat begins.

Business competition often has a milder version of the same logic. A dominant company can make rivals prepare for a price war it never intends to fight. A startup can signal a market direction that causes incumbents to reorganize. A central bank can change economic behavior through communicated expectations. The rehearsal economy is not always private preparation; sometimes it is strategic theater intended for an audience.

This complicates the idea of transparency. In safety domains, sharing scenarios can improve coordination. In adversarial domains, revealing the rehearsal can surrender information. Organizations must decide which learning benefits from openness and which advantages depend on secrecy.

Military exercises themselves can serve multiple audiences. They train participants, test equipment, signal readiness to allies, deter adversaries, reassure domestic publics, and create bureaucratic evidence for budgets. A single exercise can be both rehearsal and performance.

That dual use distorts design. An exercise optimized to look competent may become less useful for discovering weakness. Political visibility can push failure underground.

The same occurs in corporate crisis exercises when senior executives or regulators are watching. Participants may become more concerned with appearing prepared than with finding the place where preparation breaks.

The cure is not invisibility. External evaluation can expose complacency. The cure is clarity about purpose. Is this exercise for learning, certification, signaling, or deterrence? Trying to do all four at once creates incentives that conflict.

The military concept of a red team is one response. Give someone explicit permission to challenge assumptions, emulate an opponent, and make the plan uncomfortable. The role matters because ordinary hierarchy suppresses some kinds of contradiction. An officer who would be seen as obstructive in a planning meeting can be useful when institutionally authorized to attack the plan.

Permission, however, is not enough. The red team needs information, competence, independence, and the ability to alter outcomes. A decorative skeptic changes little.

This is a broader lesson for organizations that say they value dissent. Dissent without decision rights, access, or consequence is theater. Rehearsal tests whether disagreement can actually change the plan.

A good war game also distinguishes between exploring a concept and predicting a war. This distinction is often lost when results travel upward. A game can reveal that a communications dependency is fragile without establishing that a campaign would fail. It can show that one strategy creates an unexpected escalation pathway without assigning a probability to that pathway.

The output is insight, not prophecy.

This matters because people like winners. Games end with scores, maps, surviving units, and narratives. Senior audiences ask who won. But the most important finding may be that the rules made victory meaningless.

When a simulation produces a surprising result, the first question should not be whether the result is true. It should be which assumptions generated it and whether they deserve further test.

That sounds cautious. It is also more productive. A dramatic outcome can function as a hypothesis generator. Why did the logistics network collapse? Why did decision time expand? Why did the supposed advantage create congestion? What happens if we vary the rule?

Rehearsal becomes science-like when it supports controlled comparison.

Run the scenario again. Change one constraint. Give the adversary a different objective. Remove a capability. Vary the information available to commanders. Record where outcomes are stable and where they are sensitive. The goal is not to find the single future but to map the decision's dependence on assumptions.

Sensitivity is one of the most underappreciated properties of a plan. Two strategies can look equally successful in a baseline scenario while one fails under small changes and the other degrades gradually. The robust strategy may be less impressive in the center and more valuable across uncertainty.

Finance calls this stress. Engineering calls it margin. Ecology calls it resilience. Strategy often hides it beneath confidence.

War games can also reveal what cannot be rehearsed well. Morale is difficult. Civilian behavior is difficult. Coalition politics are difficult. The emotional reality of casualties cannot be honestly reproduced by moving symbols. The exercise may expose a command process while remaining almost silent about social endurance.

Knowing the boundary is part of competence.

The most dangerous model is not the inaccurate one. Inaccuracy is inevitable. The dangerous model is the one whose users do not know where it becomes fiction.

This is why serious simulation cultures maintain distinctions between model verification and validation. Verification asks whether the model implements its intended rules correctly. Validation asks whether those rules are adequate for the purpose. A perfectly coded simulation can still represent the wrong world.

Businesses often skip this distinction. A spreadsheet calculates flawlessly, so the assumptions acquire the glow of precision. A market model fits historical data, so its regime dependence is forgotten. A strategic forecast uses sophisticated software, so executives treat the visualization as information rather than argument.

The war game is valuable precisely because everyone can see the argument being played.

Participants make choices. Umpires interpret rules. The enemy responds. Disagreements become events rather than footnotes. The model's politics are exposed through use.

This social feature distinguishes interactive rehearsal from static analysis. A report can be read privately and assimilated into existing beliefs. A game makes beliefs collide in sequence. One team's assumption becomes another team's obstacle.

That collision can produce institutional learning that no memo could.

It can also produce resentment. Exercises threaten identities. A doctrine may be someone's career. A platform may be an organization's reason for funding. A scenario that undermines a preferred strategy is not merely analytical; it redistributes status.

This is why rehearsal quality depends on governance. Who writes the rules? Who adjudicates ambiguous events? Who sees the results? Who can suppress a finding? Who owns the data? Who decides what changes afterward?

These questions sound procedural until the result is inconvenient.

Organizations that want honest rehearsal need protections for inconvenient results. Otherwise participants learn a different lesson: do not discover what leadership does not want discovered.

The same principle applies to science, finance, and product development. A test that can threaten the project needs enough independence to survive success at its job.

Rehearsal is most valuable when failure is allowed to become organizationally real before it becomes physically real.

That does not mean every simulated defeat should overturn strategy. Games are artifacts. They can be badly designed, gamed, or misunderstood. The correct response to a surprising result is inquiry, not capitulation.

The discipline is to keep the result alive long enough to investigate it.

Modern computing makes it possible to run more scenarios, with more variables, at lower marginal cost. This looks like an unqualified improvement. It is not. Cheap simulation can flood decision makers with synthetic evidence. Thousands of runs create distributions that look statistically rich while remaining conditional on the same questionable model.

Quantity of rehearsal is not diversity of assumption.

Ten thousand battles simulated with the same theory of morale are still one theory of morale.

This problem is becoming central beyond war. Machine-learning systems train in vast synthetic environments. Autonomous vehicles encounter generated edge cases. Banks run model-based shocks. Manufacturers test digital twins. The abundance of virtual experience can produce confidence faster than reality produces correction.

The scarce resource shifts from simulation to model criticism.

That is the deeper inheritance of war gaming. The value was never the painted blocks or the graphics. It was the institutionalization of the question, “What if the future refuses our plan?”

A competent adversary is one answer. Nature is another. Markets are another. Human beings are often the hardest.

The world does not owe us the war we practiced.

The purpose of the war before the war is to make sure we know that before the first arrow becomes real.
