# Rehearsing Society

A government cannot run a second country beside the first and compare policies.

This obvious limitation explains much of the difficulty of public decision-making. Companies can pilot products. Engineers can build prototypes. Software teams can create staging environments. Societies make many choices once, under contested values, with effects distributed across millions of people.

The need for rehearsal rises with consequence while the possibility of faithful rehearsal falls.

Policy therefore relies on partial substitutes: models, trials, historical analogies, scenario exercises, war games, emergency drills, consultation, simulations, forecasts, and small-scale pilots. Each creates a miniature public world and asks the real one to learn from it.

The translation is politically difficult because the miniature never contains everyone who matters.

A pandemic exercise can simulate hospital capacity, supply chains, school closure, communication, border measures, and vaccine distribution. It cannot reproduce public fear, partisan conflict, rumor, fatigue, unequal exposure, household economics, or the legitimacy of rules after months of disruption with full fidelity.

A climate model can represent physical systems with extraordinary sophistication and still not tell a government which distribution of present cost and future benefit is just.

An election-security exercise can test infrastructure and communication while leaving voter trust partly outside the model.

Public rehearsal contains technical and moral uncertainty at once.

This is why “follow the model” is never a complete instruction for politics.

Models can estimate consequences under assumptions. They cannot choose values.

The distinction matters most in emergencies, where technical expertise rightly gains influence because time is short and specialized knowledge matters. Yet even excellent technical advice must enter institutions with law, rights, budgets, legitimacy, and uneven burdens.

Rehearsal should include those constraints rather than treating them as noise.

A public-health scenario that ignores political feasibility may identify the theoretically optimal intervention and fail operationally. A security plan that ignores civil liberties may protect one value by silently consuming another. A transportation model that optimizes aggregate flow may distribute inconvenience unjustly.

The social world pushes back through legitimacy.

Legitimacy is difficult to model because it is relational. People accept constraints partly based on trust, process, identity, fairness, and whether others appear to comply. Policy changes behavior, which changes the conditions under which policy operates.

This is reflexivity again.

A forecast of disease transmission depends on behavior. Announce the forecast and behavior may change. A forecast of a bank run can help cause a run. A warning about a storm can reduce casualties if people evacuate, making the warning appear excessive afterward.

Successful public forecasts can invalidate themselves.

This is one reason prevention is hard to evaluate politically. If action works, critics may say the danger was exaggerated. If action fails, leaders are blamed for insufficient preparation. The counterfactual remains invisible.

Rehearsal can help by making causal assumptions explicit before the outcome.

What do we expect if no action occurs? What behavior change do we expect from the intervention? Which indicators would cause escalation or relaxation? Which harms are we accepting?

Recording these expectations improves later learning.

It also exposes disagreement that cannot be solved by more data.

Some public decisions are value conflicts. How much risk should one group bear to protect another? How much present consumption should be sacrificed for future resilience? Which freedoms can be constrained under emergency authority? What inequality is acceptable during transition?

A simulation can show consequences. It cannot vote.

This is why inclusive rehearsal matters. Tabletop exercises run only by technical agencies can miss household, community, disability, labor, education, or local-government realities.

The people who live with the plan possess data the model does not.

Community participation can improve scenarios by revealing practical dependencies: medications requiring refrigeration, informal caregiving networks, language barriers, transportation constraints, mistrust rooted in prior events, small businesses operating on thin cash margins.

These are not “soft factors.” They determine whether policy can be executed.

Ethnography becomes infrastructure for public rehearsal.

But participation can become ceremonial. Invite representatives, collect comments, proceed unchanged. The exercise then teaches communities that consultation is performance.

As with red teams, consequence determines credibility.

This creates a demanding standard. Not every participant can have veto power. Public decisions require tradeoffs. Yet decision makers can show how input altered assumptions, why recommendations were rejected, and which uncertainties remain.

Transparent disagreement is a form of institutional honesty.

Scenario planning at national scale also intersects with geopolitics. Energy security, food systems, migration, trade, technology controls, climate adaptation, and defense all involve actors responding to one another.

A national plan changes other nations' plans.

This makes geopolitical rehearsal especially recursive. Sanctions produce evasion and substitution. Export controls encourage domestic capability. Alliances change deterrence. Industrial policy alters investment. Public statements become signals read by adversaries and markets.

The rehearsal must therefore include adaptation.

Static policy models can underestimate this because they treat actors as parameters rather than strategists.

Game theory offers formal tools for strategic interaction, but real geopolitics has incomplete information, domestic politics, identity, history, and leaders who may not share utility functions. Games clarify structure without eliminating interpretation.

The most useful policy rehearsal often combines methods.

Quantitative models for physical and economic constraints. Historical cases for institutional patterns. Games for strategic adaptation. Ethnography for lived implementation. Legal analysis for authority. Deliberation for values.

Plural methods are less elegant than one master model.

They are more appropriate to plural societies.

This is a recurring theme of the book: as the system becomes more human, single-fidelity simulation becomes less plausible.

Society is not a machine with a sufficiently complicated dashboard.

Yet the desire for one persists because centralized legibility is administratively attractive. James C. Scott documented how states simplify populations, land, and production to make them governable, sometimes with destructive consequences when simplified schemes override local knowledge.

Rehearsal can reproduce the same tendency.

A policy model defines the population in variables. What is not measured becomes hard to represent. The rehearsal then validates interventions against the representation.

This does not make state measurement bad. Census data, maps, health surveillance, economic statistics, and infrastructure models are essential public goods. The danger is forgetting that legibility is selective.

The rehearsal economy needs feedback channels from the unmodeled world.

Appeals, local discretion, public comment, ombuds systems, audits, field observation, complaint data, and independent research all create routes for reality to contradict the center.

These are institutional sensors.

A resilient state is not one with a perfect model. It is one whose models can be challenged without requiring catastrophe.

This is why federalism, local experimentation, and policy pilots are sometimes described as laboratories. Different jurisdictions can try different approaches. Comparison can generate evidence.

The laboratory metaphor has limits. Jurisdictions differ. People move. Policies spill over borders. Political selection affects adoption. No state is a randomized treatment arm.

Still, variation creates information that uniform policy cannot.

Diversity is rehearsal capacity.

The same insight applies globally. Different countries respond to common challenges with different institutions. Comparative social science can learn from variation without pretending the cases are identical.

This is slower and messier than controlled simulation.

It is also contact with reality.

Public institutions face another problem: rehearsal itself can frighten people. A government preparing for war, pandemic, financial crisis, or cyberattack may be accused of predicting, causing, or desiring the event. Scenario documents can be misread as plans.

Communication therefore matters.

A scenario should clearly distinguish possibility from forecast and preparedness from intent.

This sounds administrative until misinformation enters. In high-trust environments, the distinction may be accepted. In low-trust environments, rehearsal can become evidence for conspiracy.

Trust becomes part of preparedness.

It cannot be manufactured during the emergency. It is accumulated through ordinary governance: honesty about uncertainty, consistency, competence, fairness, correction of error.

Society rehearses institutions every day.

When officials explain a small mistake transparently, people learn how larger mistakes may be handled. When agencies hide inconvenient data, people learn too. When rules apply differently by status, the public updates its model of legitimacy.

Emergency response is a performance built from these prior repetitions.

This is why social capital can function as resilience infrastructure. Communities with strong networks may distribute information, check on vulnerable people, share resources, and coordinate informally during disruptions.

Such networks are difficult to command from a central plan because their value comes from local relationships.

Policy can support them or crowd them out.

The rehearsal economy therefore includes civic practice: volunteer groups, neighborhood associations, mutual aid, local institutions, religious communities, clubs, unions, professional societies. They rehearse cooperation before crisis.

The decline of some forms of association can make society more dependent on formal systems precisely when formal systems are overloaded.

This connects to the themes explored in Built to Be Alone elsewhere on this Desk. Here the point is operational: relationships are latent coordination capacity.

A society with fewer practiced ties may have more communication technology and less capacity to act together.

Technology cannot fully substitute because coordination requires trust about motives, not only channels.

Public rehearsal should therefore ask not merely, “Does the alert reach people?” but, “Whom do people believe?”

That question cannot be solved during the drill with a better notification system.

It belongs to institutional character.

The hardest societal rehearsals concern transformations rather than emergencies. Energy transition, demographic aging, automation, artificial intelligence, climate adaptation. These unfold over decades and alter the variables being modeled.

Scenarios become necessary and especially uncertain.

Long-term models can clarify resource constraints and path dependencies. They can also create pseudo-precision. A number for 2050 can contain dozens of assumptions about technology, politics, behavior, and cost.

Decision makers need ranges, sensitivities, and branching conditions more than false decimal points.

The question is not which 2050 is true.

It is which decisions today remain sensible across several 2050s.

This is robust policy rather than optimal policy for one forecast.

Robustness often looks inefficient because it preserves options, redundancy, and adaptation capacity. Politics favors visible efficiency and immediate benefits.

Preparedness has weak constituencies until after failure.

Societies can counter this through institutions with longer horizons: independent regulators, fiscal rules, infrastructure standards, strategic reserves, research funding, and professional civil services. These structures imperfectly represent future citizens who cannot vote today.

They are temporal rehearsal devices.

They force some future conditions into present decisions.

The danger is technocracy without accountability. Long-horizon institutions can become insulated, captured, or wrong.

Again, the answer is contestability.

Expertise needs independence and correction. Democratic authority needs information and limits. Rehearsal can inform the argument without replacing it.

A society cannot practice every future.

It can practice how it updates.

That may be the most important public capability: the ability to change policy when evidence changes without treating correction as humiliation.

Political systems often punish revision as weakness. Leaders become locked into prior statements. Partisans reinterpret evidence to protect identity.

This makes adaptation expensive.

A rehearsal culture would treat conditionality as competence: if these indicators move, we change course. If this assumption fails, the policy changes. If harm exceeds this threshold, we stop.

Such rules do not eliminate politics. They create clearer terms for it.

The public performance will always exceed the model.

Millions of people will improvise. Interests will conflict. New technology will appear. Institutions will fail in unanticipated ways. Some communities will surprise planners with resilience.

The purpose of rehearsing society is not to make society behave like the rehearsal.

It is to make power less surprised that society is alive.
