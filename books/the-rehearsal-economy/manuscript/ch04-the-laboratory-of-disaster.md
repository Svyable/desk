# The Laboratory of Disaster

The first time a team practices a catastrophe, the catastrophe is usually less revealing than the team.

Somebody cannot find the key. A radio channel is wrong. Two people believe they are in charge. A binder contains a phone number that belonged to someone who left three years ago. A door assumed to be open is locked. A piece of equipment has been moved. A person with a crucial role is standing in the wrong place because the plan was written from an organization chart rather than from the building.

Then the exercise ends, everyone exhales, and the institution faces a choice. It can treat these failures as embarrassment, or as purchased information.

Emergency drills are among the oldest and least glamorous technologies of preparedness. Their logic is so familiar that organizations can forget why they exist. The stated purpose is often compliance: conduct the evacuation, document participation, satisfy the rule. The deeper purpose is behavioral. A drill converts a sentence—use the east stairwell—into movement under interruption.

This matters because emergencies are not ordinary work at higher volume. Stress changes perception. Time compresses. Information arrives incomplete. People seek authority, imitate others, narrow attention, and fall back on practiced routines. Procedures that are obvious in a calm conference room can become strangely inaccessible when alarms are sounding.

The rehearsal cannot reproduce the danger without defeating its purpose, so it must decide which aspects of danger to preserve. Smoke can be theatrical. Casualties can be mannequins or actors. Communications can be deliberately degraded. Time can be compressed. The design problem is not realism in general but fidelity to the decisions that matter.

A medical simulation illustrates this. A mannequin does not need to look fully human to expose whether a team recognizes a deteriorating airway, assigns roles, calls for blood, or speaks up when a senior clinician misses a signal. The emotional truth of the exercise can come from coordination pressure rather than visual realism. The plastic patient is useful because the humans around it are real.

That is a recurring feature of the rehearsal economy: the model may be crude if the behavior being tested is authentic.

Aviation learned this at scale. Early flight training obviously required real aircraft, but as commercial aviation became safer and more complex, the simulator became a place to experience failures too dangerous, too rare, or too expensive to produce deliberately in flight. The machine is impressive, yet much of the learning concerns the crew. Who notices? Who says something? Who verifies? Who becomes fixated? Who is willing to challenge the captain?

Crew resource management emerged from recognition that technical competence alone did not eliminate accidents. Communication, workload management, leadership, situational awareness, and the use of all available resources mattered. The cockpit became not just a machine-control problem but a small organization under pressure.

That reframing is important. Institutions often respond to failure by asking which individual made the mistake. Rehearsal can expose a more uncomfortable answer: the organization produced conditions in which a predictable human error became consequential.

If a nurse notices a dosage problem but the hierarchy makes interruption socially costly, “speak up” is not a complete safety system. If a copilot sees a problem but the culture rewards deference, technical training has reached its limit. If an operator must remember an obscure sequence during a rare emergency, memory is carrying risk that interface design could remove.

A good simulation makes these system properties visible. It gives the organization a chance to redesign the work rather than lecture the worker.

This is where rehearsal intersects with human factors. People are not noisy components that must be disciplined into perfect compliance. They are adaptive parts of systems. They compensate for poor design, resolve ambiguity, improvise around missing resources, and notice conditions designers did not anticipate. The same flexibility that produces error also produces resilience.

The rehearsal economy is valuable partly because it lets designers watch adaptation before the stakes are real.

Consider a hospital code. The formal process can be rendered as algorithms and roles. The lived process includes bed placement, room size, equipment access, competing alarms, family members, handoffs, medication availability, and whether the person documenting events can hear the team leader. Simulation exposes the distance between process as described and process as inhabited.

Fire drills do the same for buildings. An architect can model egress capacity. A drill reveals human behavior: which exit people choose, whether they return for belongings, whether visitors understand signage, whether supervisors hesitate to interrupt work, whether people with mobility limitations are accommodated. The plan meets bodies.

Bodies are inconvenient. They are also the point.

This suggests a broader distinction between verification and rehearsal. Verification asks whether a system meets a requirement. Rehearsal asks what happens when people use the system to pursue a goal under conditions that matter. The two overlap but are not identical. A fire door can pass inspection while an evacuation plan fails because nobody knows the door exists.

Organizations prefer verification because it produces cleaner evidence. Pass or fail. Checked box. Certificate. Rehearsal produces stories, disagreements, and findings that cross departmental boundaries. It is administratively messy.

That mess is useful.

Complex failures rarely respect boundaries. A technical fault becomes an operational problem, then a communication problem, then a management problem. The rehearsal that confines itself to one department may miss the propagation that makes the event dangerous.

This is why large exercises are often built around cascades. A storm damages power. Backup generation starts. Fuel delivery becomes uncertain. Communications degrade. Staffing drops. A supplier fails. None of these conditions is unimaginable. The difficulty is their interaction.

Humans are bad at intuitively combining low-probability dependencies. We tend to think in stories with a manageable number of causes. Systems produce conjunctions. Rehearsal gives the conjunction a clock.

Once time enters, hidden assumptions appear. A backup may work for eight hours, but the disruption lasts three days. A second team can take over, but roads are closed. A vendor can deliver replacements, but the same regional event has affected the vendor. Resilience is not the existence of a backup; it is the ability of the backup to survive the conditions that made it necessary.

This leads to one of the most valuable rehearsal questions: what else fails for the same reason?

Redundancy is often illusory because supposedly independent systems share dependencies. Two data centers use the same network provider. Two suppliers depend on the same sub-tier manufacturer. Two emergency generators require the same fuel contract. Two decision makers rely on the same dashboard. Rehearsal can reveal common-mode failure that a static inventory misses.

The economics are subtle. Organizations can always imagine more disasters than they can afford to practice. A hospital could spend all year simulating rare events and neglect ordinary care. A company could run scenario workshops instead of selling products. Preparedness competes with production.

So rehearsal needs a portfolio logic. Frequent, high-consequence, and weakly controlled hazards deserve more attention. Rare events may deserve practice when response has to be fast and improvisation would be costly. Some scenarios are valuable because they reveal multiple vulnerabilities at once. Others are spectacular but low-yield.

The temptation is to rehearse what is dramatic. The better discipline is to rehearse what is diagnostic.

A cyber incident involving a famous kind of attack may attract executive attention. A mundane identity-system failure could expose more fundamental dependencies. A pandemic exercise can become theatrically complex while missing procurement, staffing, and local decision rights. A mass-casualty drill can feature impressive staging and weak debriefing.

The debrief is where rehearsal becomes learning.

Without it, the exercise is experience without conversion. Participants remember different things. Senior people may dominate the interpretation. Embarrassing findings soften. The organization congratulates itself on completion and returns to work.

A serious after-action process reconstructs what happened, what was expected, where the difference arose, and which changes will be made. The distinction between blame and accountability matters. If people fear punishment for revealing confusion, the next rehearsal will become performance.

This does not mean nobody is responsible. It means the exercise should maximize truth before status repairs itself.

The most valuable sentence after a rehearsal is often, “I didn't know that.” It identifies a boundary in the shared model.

I didn't know facilities owned that decision. I didn't know the vendor required forty-eight hours. I didn't know the backup radio could not reach the basement. I didn't know legal approval was required. I didn't know the team in another time zone used a different escalation path.

Organizations accumulate these unknowns because ordinary work allows local competence to mask global ignorance. People solve problems inside their domains. The institution appears coherent because the interfaces are rarely stressed simultaneously.

Disaster is an interface stress test.

This makes exercises culturally revealing. Some organizations become curious when plans fail. Others become defensive. Curiosity is not a soft virtue here. It determines whether rehearsal can produce information.

A culture that punishes every deviation will receive fewer reports of weak signals. A culture that treats all failure as harmless learning will tolerate negligence. The balance is difficult: preserve psychological safety for honest diagnosis while maintaining standards for preparation and conduct.

High-reliability work often develops rituals around this balance. Checklists, briefings, cross-checks, callouts, and debriefs create permission structures for attention. They make certain interruptions legitimate. A junior person does not need to invent a social reason to raise a concern if the procedure already requires the check.

Rehearsal makes the ritual credible because people practice using it.

A written escalation policy can be ignored. A team that has repeatedly experienced a junior member stopping a simulation may internalize a different hierarchy for emergencies than for ordinary work. Practice can create temporary constitutional rules.

This is one reason drills have political content. They define who can act, who must wait, who can spend, who can speak, and whose judgment overrides whose. Emergency powers are rehearsed governance.

The same is true in corporations. Incident-response simulations reveal whether local teams can make decisions without executive approval. Business-continuity exercises expose whether authority is distributed or merely claimed to be. A crisis plan that requires the chief executive to approve every deviation is not a plan for a chief executive who is unreachable.

Rehearsing absence is especially powerful. Remove the usual leader. Disable the preferred tool. Close the normal room. Pretend the first backup fails. These constraints can feel artificial, but they test whether resilience belongs to the institution or to one person and one path.

This is also where rehearsal meets character. People discover things about themselves under simulated pressure. Some become louder. Some become quieter. Some hoard tasks. Some seek help. Some protect status. Some become unusually generous.

Simulation is not truth serum. People know the stakes are lower. Yet repeated exercises create evidence about behavior that resumes and interviews cannot provide.

The rehearsal economy increasingly sells this evidence. Assessment centers, military exercises, management simulations, medical boards, and training platforms observe behavior in constructed environments. That creates opportunities and risks. The more consequential the evaluation, the more participants train for the evaluator. Once again, the rehearsal becomes a game.

A candidate who excels in a leadership simulation may be skilled at simulations. A hospital that knows accreditation exercises may optimize the visible ritual. A company may stage readiness for auditors. The observer changes the behavior.

This is not a reason to abandon rehearsal. It is a reason to vary it.

Unannounced drills, surprise scenarios, rotating evaluators, multiple formats, and cross-functional participation can reduce simple gaming. More important, organizations can distinguish exercises designed for learning from exercises designed for certification. Mixing the two makes honesty expensive.

If a team believes every mistake affects promotion, it will hide mistakes. If an exercise has no consequences at all, preparation may be casual. Mature systems separate developmental practice from formal evaluation while maintaining seriousness in both.

The best rehearsal has stakes without catastrophe.

That balance is why realistic simulation can feel emotionally strange. Participants know the patient is plastic and still feel their pulse rise. The body responds to tempo, uncertainty, social evaluation, and responsibility even when the intellect knows there is no corpse.

This physiological response is not noise. It is part of the training substrate. Skills learned only in calm conditions may not transfer cleanly to stress. Rehearsal introduces enough pressure to test retrieval and coordination.

But there are limits. Trauma should not be manufactured casually. Exercises involving violence, death, or disaster can affect participants with relevant lived experience. Realism is not automatically ethical or effective. The purpose is learning, not theatrical domination.

The same principle applies to organizational stress tests. Leaders sometimes believe making an exercise harsher makes it better. Difficulty without diagnostic purpose is just suffering. A scenario should be demanding because the demand reveals something actionable.

This returns us to the laboratory metaphor. A good experiment varies conditions to learn. It does not simply smash the specimen.

Disaster rehearsal at its best is experimental governance. It temporarily suspends normal assumptions, applies a controlled shock, observes response, and updates the system. The shock may be fictional, but the dependencies it reveals are real.

The strange achievement is that catastrophe becomes a source of information before it becomes history.

That is a form of wealth. An institution that can learn from disasters it has not suffered possesses something like borrowed experience.

Borrowed experience is never complete. The real event will contain grief, fatigue, scarcity, politics, media, legal exposure, and moral weight that no exercise can reproduce. People who perform smoothly in simulation can still freeze. Plans can still fail.

The point is not immunity.

It is to remove avoidable ignorance before reality charges full price for it.
