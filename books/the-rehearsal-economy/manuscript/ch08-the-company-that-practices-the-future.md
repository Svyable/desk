# The Company That Practices the Future

The hardest future for a successful company to imagine is one in which its success becomes irrelevant.

Managers are paid to operate systems that already work. They allocate capital, hire people, negotiate contracts, improve products, and defend margins. The organization's daily evidence rewards continuity. Customers keep buying. Suppliers keep shipping. Metrics keep arriving. The future enters mostly as a forecast attached to the present.

Then something changes that does not look large enough at first.

A new technology serves a worse customer. A distribution channel moves. Regulation alters the economics. A geopolitical shock changes input costs. A platform absorbs a product category. A social habit shifts slowly until a business model loses its emotional fit. The company does not fail because nobody considered the future. It fails because the futures it considered were shaped by the logic of the company that already existed.

Scenario planning is one institutional attempt to escape that trap.

Shell began developing formal scenarios in the 1970s, and the practice became famous partly because the oil shocks made the idea of rehearsing discontinuity look prescient. The mythology can be overstated. Scenarios did not give Shell a crystal ball. Their value was closer to cognitive preparation: managers had spent time inside futures where assumptions about energy markets and politics differed from the baseline.

That distinction matters because stories about strategic foresight tend to become too flattering after the fact. Once an unusual event occurs, the scenario that resembles it is remembered. The many branches that did not happen fade. A company can start believing it predicted the future when it merely practiced surprise.

Practicing surprise is enough.

A scenario is useful when it changes a decision before the event, broadens the range of signals people notice, or makes a strange development legible sooner. It does not need to win a forecasting contest.

This makes scenario planning closer to theater than to econometrics, though good scenarios use data. Participants enter internally coherent worlds and ask what their current strategy would mean there. The narrative form is not ornamental. It preserves causal sequence. If energy prices rise because of one mechanism rather than another, the strategic implications differ. A scenario needs enough texture that participants can inhabit the consequences.

But narrative creates its own risks. Humans reward coherent stories. A beautifully written scenario can feel more plausible than a messy one. The exercise can become a contest in imagination rather than a test of strategy.

The discipline is to use story as a vessel for assumptions, not as evidence.

A strong scenario process therefore exposes its drivers. Which forces are relatively stable? Which uncertainties matter most? Which relationships are assumed? What would make this world internally inconsistent? Which early indicators would suggest movement toward it?

The last question turns rehearsal into sensing.

A company that has practiced several futures can build a wider radar. A competitor's odd pricing move, a regulatory consultation, a supplier's capital investment, a change in customer language—signals acquire meaning because the organization has already imagined a world in which they matter.

Without a rehearsal, the signal may look like noise.

This connects scenarios to disruption theory. Incumbents do not always miss change because they are stupid or complacent. They can be rational inside systems that reward current customers, margins, and capabilities. A technology that initially performs worse on the dimensions the incumbent values may be correctly rejected by existing processes.

The problem appears later when the basis of competition changes.

Rehearsal can create temporary permission to value different dimensions. In a scenario where low cost matters more than performance, a weak technology looks different. In a scenario where customers accept automation, labor economics changes. In a scenario where regulation favors interoperability, a proprietary advantage can become a liability.

This does not solve the innovator's dilemma. The organization still has to allocate resources against present incentives. But rehearsal can at least reveal when the present incentive system is the thing being tested.

That is a subtle shift. Most strategy exercises ask whether a plan works under different external conditions. Better ones also ask whether the organization itself would be capable of responding.

Suppose a manufacturer imagines a sudden trade restriction. The obvious analysis concerns suppliers and costs. The deeper rehearsal concerns decision speed. Who can qualify alternate suppliers? Which engineering standards can change? How long does legal review take? Which customer promises are rigid? The scenario becomes an organizational diagnostic.

This is why strategy without operations often feels intelligent and changes little.

A future is not practiced until somebody knows what she would do Monday morning.

The best scenarios therefore descend from macro forces to decisions. Capital expenditure. Hiring. Inventory. Product architecture. Contract terms. Research priorities. Geographic exposure. Pricing. The exercise earns its cost when it changes one of these or creates an option.

Options are the bridge between uncertainty and action. A company uncertain about a market may make a small investment that preserves the right to scale later. It can establish a supplier relationship, acquire a license, run a pilot, hire a specialist, or build an interface. These are strategic rehearsals with assets attached.

Real options thinking gives formal language to some of this. Under uncertainty, the ability to delay, expand, abandon, or switch can have value. The practical lesson is less mathematical: do not force every uncertain future into an all-or-nothing bet.

Companies often do the opposite because large commitments are easier to narrate. A bold acquisition signals seriousness. A factory is visible. A platform rewrite feels decisive. A portfolio of small options can look indecisive even when it is strategically rational.

Culture therefore shapes rehearsability.

Organizations that demand certainty before small experiments may end up making large decisions with less evidence. Organizations that tolerate cheap ambiguity can learn before commitment.

This is one reason budgeting systems matter to innovation. If every experiment requires the same approval process as a major program, the organization taxes rehearsal. If teams have bounded discretionary budgets, they can buy information quickly.

But decentralization can create noise and duplication. A hundred teams may run similar pilots, each unaware of the others. The rehearsal economy needs memory and coordination, not just permission.

The challenge is to centralize learning without centralizing every experiment.

Some companies solve this with internal platforms, shared test infrastructure, research repositories, review forums, and communities of practice. The details matter less than the principle: the result of a rehearsal should travel farther than the people who ran it.

Otherwise the organization buys the same lesson repeatedly.

Strategic rehearsal also interacts with incentives. A manager whose bonus depends on this year's margin may sincerely participate in a workshop about a five-year threat and still rationally defer the investment. The future loses not because it is unimaginable but because it has no constituency in the compensation system.

This is a governance problem disguised as foresight.

Boards are supposed to extend the time horizon, but boards have their own informational limits. They see management-prepared materials, episodic meetings, and selective metrics. A useful board rehearsal does not merely ask directors to approve management's scenarios. It gives them enough independent context to challenge the causal structure.

What would have to be true for our largest profit pool to disappear? Which competitor are we dismissing because it looks economically unattractive today? What regulatory change would turn our asset into a stranded cost? Which customer segment would we abandon if it became the center of the market?

These questions are uncomfortable because they attack identity.

A company is not only a bundle of assets. It is a story about what the company is good at. Strategy is constrained by that story. Rehearsal matters when it creates a temporary world in which the story can be wrong without the organization collapsing.

That temporary permission is psychologically important. Executives have careers invested in prior choices. Engineers love technologies they helped build. Sales organizations defend customers they know. Founders can mistake personal identity for product truth.

A scenario moves the criticism one step away. “You are wrong” becomes “In this world, what breaks?” The distance can make honesty easier.

It can also make honesty too easy. People may say radical things inside the workshop and return to ordinary incentives afterward. The rehearsal becomes catharsis rather than preparation.

This is why scenario planning has acquired skeptics. Workshops can be expensive theater. Consultants produce memorable axes and polished worlds. Executives enjoy intellectual novelty. The output enters a binder.

The criticism is fair when no decision changes.

A practical test is simple: six months later, what is different because this future was rehearsed?

Maybe nothing should be different. A scenario can deserve consideration and still fail to justify action. But if repeated exercises never alter options, monitoring, capital, design, or contingency plans, the organization may be rehearsing concern rather than strategy.

The distinction between forecasting and rehearsal helps here. Forecasting can be evaluated by accuracy. Scenario work should be evaluated partly by decision quality and preparedness. Did it reveal a vulnerability? Did it create a leading indicator? Did it prevent overcommitment? Did it speed response when conditions changed?

These outcomes are harder to measure, which makes scenario programs politically fragile.

Their success often looks like nothing happening. The firm held more flexibility than it needed. It avoided an acquisition. It qualified a supplier before a disruption. It noticed a market shift earlier. Counterfactual value leaves few monuments.

This is true of risk management generally. Prevention suffers from an evidence problem.

Companies respond by turning rehearsal into routines. Annual planning, quarterly risk reviews, launch checklists, incident simulations, pricing experiments, customer research. Routine lowers the political cost of asking strange questions because the process itself legitimizes them.

Routine also deadens attention.

An annual scenario exercise held every October can become as ceremonial as any meeting. Participants know the templates. The same uncertainties return. The same slides are updated. Rehearsal becomes bureaucracy.

The solution is not constant novelty. Novelty for its own sake produces entertainment. The solution is to reconnect rehearsal to real decisions and fresh evidence.

What changed since the last run? Which assumption was falsified? Which weak signal strengthened? Which option expired? What new dependency appeared?

This gives rehearsal a version history.

Technology companies understand versioning because software makes it explicit. Strategy should be versioned too. A plan written eighteen months ago was built against an older world. The organization should know which premises have changed.

Yet constant updating has costs. Firms can become twitchy, reacting to every headline. Long-term investments require commitment. Strategy cannot be a weather vane.

Rehearsal helps by separating robustness from reaction. If a strategy survives several plausible worlds, managers can hold it more confidently through noise. If it depends on one narrow future, the organization knows which signals deserve attention.

This is the opposite of forecasting bravado. The goal is not to be right about the world. It is to know what would make the strategy wrong.

That knowledge changes capital allocation.

Consider a company deciding whether to build a giant specialized facility. A single-point forecast may produce an attractive net present value. Rehearsal may reveal that profitability depends on demand, policy, input prices, and one technology path all aligning. The company can still build. But it now understands that the investment is a bundle of assumptions.

Alternatively it might stage capacity, negotiate flexibility, design equipment for multiple products, or preserve land for expansion. The rehearsal moves from narrative to architecture.

Good strategy often looks like this: assumptions become design features.

A company uncertain about regulation builds compliance modularity. A software firm uncertain about model providers creates abstraction layers. A manufacturer uncertain about demand designs flexible lines. A retailer uncertain about channels maintains fulfillment options. The organization spends money to remain less certain.

This can look inefficient next to an optimized competitor.

Efficiency wins in the rehearsed world. Flexibility wins when the world changes enough.

The management problem is deciding how much uncertainty deserves to be bought down or insured against. Too much flexibility creates mediocre systems. Too little creates fragility. There is no universal ratio because uncertainty differs across industries and moments.

This is where character enters corporate strategy. Leaders have temperaments. Some prefer commitment. Others preserve options too long. Some enjoy scenarios because possibilities excuse decision. Others dismiss scenarios because ambiguity threatens control.

An institution needs processes that compensate for temperament without pretending to remove judgment.

One useful design is to assign roles. Someone owns the base case. Someone owns the strongest alternative. Someone is responsible for disconfirming evidence. Someone translates scenario implications into operational options. This prevents the charismatic leader's imagination from becoming the whole future.

Diversity matters here in more than a demographic sense, though demographic diversity can widen experience. Functional, geographic, generational, and disciplinary differences change what futures are easy to imagine. A supply-chain manager sees dependencies a marketing executive may not. A younger employee may treat a technology as ordinary that senior leaders treat as exotic. Someone from a different market may question a customer assumption everyone else sees as natural.

The future is partly limited by who is in the room.

This gives ethnography a strategic role. Companies cannot rehearse consumer futures only from conference rooms. They need contact with how people actually live, improvise, substitute, and assign meaning. The next chapter turns to that problem.

For now, the essential point is that the company that practices the future is not trying to become prophetic.

Prophecy concentrates authority in the person who claims to see farther.

Rehearsal distributes authority across possible worlds.

It says: we do not know which future is coming, but we can make our assumptions visible, create options, and practice being wrong before being wrong is expensive.

That is a less glamorous promise than foresight.

It is also more useful.
