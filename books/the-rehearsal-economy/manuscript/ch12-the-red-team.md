# The Red Team

The easiest plan to defend is one nobody is allowed to attack.

Organizations rarely state this rule. They create it socially. The plan belongs to a senior person. The deadline is near. Funding depends on approval. The group has already invested months. Objections have been raised before and answered. Everyone is tired.

Then the meeting begins with a request for honest feedback.

This is not hypocrisy so much as institutional physics. Dissent has costs, and the costs rise as commitment grows. The closer a project gets to launch, the more likely criticism is to be interpreted as disloyalty, delay, or failure to understand context.

Red teaming tries to alter that physics by assigning somebody the job of being difficult.

The term has military roots, where red conventionally represents the opposing force. It spread into cybersecurity, intelligence, technology, and organizational risk. The forms differ. A security red team attempts to penetrate systems. An analytic red team challenges assumptions. A strategy red team may play a competitor. A product red team tries to produce harmful or unintended behavior.

The common move is institutionalized opposition.

This is rehearsal because the organization manufactures an adversary before the real adversary arrives.

The quality of the rehearsal depends on whether the manufactured adversary has teeth.

A red team that can write a memo but cannot alter a decision is a theater critic. A security team that is barred from testing the systems everyone privately worries about is a demonstration. A junior analyst asked to challenge a chief executive without protection has been given a title, not power.

Adversarial rehearsal requires permission, independence, competence, scope, and consequences.

Permission says the attack is legitimate. Independence reduces the pressure to protect the plan. Competence makes the attack realistic. Scope prevents the most sensitive assumptions from being declared out of bounds. Consequence means findings travel into decisions.

Remove any one and the red team weakens.

The social purpose is subtle. Most organizations already contain skeptics. They do not need to hire negativity. They need to make dissent usable.

Without structure, skeptics develop reputations. The person who repeatedly points out risks becomes “not commercial,” “not a team player,” “too academic,” or simply exhausting. Some deserve the reputation; endless objection can be a way to avoid responsibility. But when all criticism is personalized, the organization loses information.

A role can depersonalize the attack.

Today you are supposed to break the plan.

This works because social permission changes what can be said. The same sentence that sounds obstructive in an approval meeting can sound valuable in a pre-mortem. Framing alters the local norm.

Gary Klein popularized the pre-mortem as a technique in which a team imagines that a plan has failed and generates explanations. The inversion matters. Ordinary planning asks people to predict whether a plan will work while sitting among colleagues who built it. The pre-mortem declares failure as the premise. Doubt becomes task completion.

The method is cheap because it requires almost no technology. Its value is social engineering.

Suppose a launch team is asked, “What are the risks?” People may produce the known list. Ask instead, “It is six months from now and this launch was a humiliating failure. What happened?” The future perfect tense creates a small fictional world. Participants can populate it with causes that feel impolite in the present.

The strongest answer may concern the team itself.

The executive sponsor left. The key engineer burned out. Customers hated the pricing. The supplier lied. The model was tuned to the demo. Legal review arrived too late. Nobody owned the migration path. Sales promised features engineering never agreed to build.

Each sentence is a rehearsal seed.

The next move is not to mitigate everything. Infinite mitigation is another form of paralysis. Rank the findings by plausibility, consequence, detectability, and control. Decide which deserve experiments, monitoring, buffers, or explicit acceptance.

Red teaming is useful when it sharpens decisions, not when it produces an impressive catalog of doom.

This is where some organizations go wrong. Once skepticism becomes professionalized, it can become self-protective. The red team is rewarded for finding problems, so problems multiply. Product teams begin treating review as a bureaucratic tax. The relationship becomes adversarial in the wrong sense.

A mature system aligns both sides around the same mission. Blue wants the product to work. Red wants the product to survive contact with reality. The conflict concerns claims, not purpose.

This is easier in theory than in career systems.

If the red team delays a lucrative launch, who receives credit if the avoided failure never occurs? If it approves a launch that later fails, who receives blame? Prevention again confronts its counterfactual problem.

Institutions solve this partly through rules. Certain findings must be addressed. Certain risk thresholds require escalation. Certain classes of vulnerability block release. Formal thresholds protect the red team from having to win every argument politically.

Rules also invite gaming.

Teams can redefine severity, narrow scope, or fix the metric while preserving the underlying risk. The red team can become captured by its own taxonomy.

This is why adversarial rehearsal needs judgment.

Cybersecurity makes the problem vivid. A penetration test can discover exploitable paths, but the absence of a discovered exploit does not prove security. The test is bounded by time, skill, information, and rules of engagement. A real attacker may be more patient, differently motivated, or willing to use methods the test forbids.

The result should therefore be interpreted asymmetrically. A successful attack is strong evidence of a weakness. An unsuccessful attack is weaker evidence of strength.

This asymmetry appears in science too. A counterexample can kill a universal claim. Failure to find a counterexample after a limited search cannot prove the claim.

Red-team thinking is scientific thinking applied to plans.

What observation would make us abandon this belief? Where is the strongest disconfirming case? Which assumption is carrying the most weight? What would a hostile reviewer attack first?

Organizations often ask these questions too late, after narrative coherence has formed.

Early red teaming can feel inefficient because the idea is still fragile. Yet that is when change is cheap. The prototype chapter gave us the same lesson from engineering: move error forward.

There is a counterargument. Some ideas need protection before criticism. A nascent concept can be killed by demanding proof appropriate to a mature program. Creative work requires generative phases in which judgment is temporarily suspended.

This is true.

The answer is temporal separation. Generate, then attack. Do not ask the red team to sit inside every brainstorming session. Do not wait until launch either.

Rehearsal has a cadence.

This is visible in writing. A writer drafting a paragraph and editing every sentence simultaneously can lose momentum. A writer who never edits produces confusion. Creation and criticism are complementary modes that often work better when alternated.

Organizations should design the same rhythm.

Build enough of the strategy that there is something specific to attack. Attack it while change remains affordable. Rebuild. Attack again under different assumptions. Then commit.

The red team should also rotate perspectives. One team can become predictable. A finance-oriented skeptic finds financial weakness. A technical skeptic finds technical weakness. A regulator sees compliance. A customer sees inconvenience.

Diverse adversaries reveal different failure surfaces.

This is why intelligence analysis developed structured analytic techniques that attempt to challenge assumptions, compare hypotheses, and reduce groupthink. The methods are not magic. Their value lies in forcing alternative explanations into explicit competition.

Competing hypotheses are a form of rehearsal. Instead of telling one story about the evidence, analysts maintain several and ask which observations discriminate among them.

Business analysis often does the reverse. Teams collect evidence for a preferred thesis. The competitor is weak because of A, B, and C. The market is attractive because of D. Every slide advances the narrative.

A red-team version asks what evidence would look the same if the opposite thesis were true.

Customers are adopting our product. Is that because the product is superior, because of discounts, because the incumbent is temporarily constrained, or because early adopters are unrepresentative? Revenue is growing. Is unit economics improving? Are cohorts stable? Are returns or support costs lagging?

Alternative explanations protect against premature causality.

Markets punish causal vanity. A company can attribute growth to strategy when a macro tailwind did the work. When the tailwind reverses, the organization discovers it rehearsed the wrong capability.

Red teams can become especially valuable during success because success suppresses skepticism.

Failure already produces critics. Success produces stories.

The successful executive's intuition gains status. The winning strategy becomes doctrine. Risk controls feel conservative. People who warned about failure may stop raising the issue because events appear to have disproved them.

This is when rehearsal should become harder.

Ask the red team to explain how success itself creates vulnerability. Growth can weaken controls. Scale can create bureaucracy. Market leadership can attract regulation. A dominant product can narrow attention. High margins can invite entrants. Cheap capital can hide poor economics.

Strength changes the opponent's incentives.

In geopolitics, deterrence produces similar recursion. A capability intended to prevent conflict causes others to adapt. Alliances reassure members and alter adversaries' planning. Sanctions create substitution. Technology controls accelerate domestic alternatives. Every strategic move enters someone else's rehearsal.

Red teams should therefore model responses, not just shocks.

A common strategic exercise asks, “What if competitor X cuts price?” A stronger exercise asks, “What does competitor X believe we will do if it cuts price, and how might it exploit that belief?” Now the recursion begins.

There is a practical limit. Infinite levels of strategic reasoning become parlor games. The purpose is not to outthink imaginary minds forever. It is to expose assumptions about agency.

The competitor is not weather.

Neither is the employee, consumer, regulator, activist, supplier, or voter. They respond to the system.

This human agency is what many quantitative rehearsals omit. A model can impose a tax and calculate static effects. Real people change behavior. A company can raise fees and assume demand elasticity from history. Customers organize, complain, switch, or invent workarounds. A platform changes rules and creators migrate.

The red team restores people as strategists.

This is one reason role-playing can outperform spreadsheets for some questions. Put somebody inside the incentives of the other party and ask what she would do. The output is not a forecast. It is an expansion of the possibility space.

The quality depends on empathy.

A weak red team caricatures the opponent as irrational or evil. A strong one reconstructs motives from the opponent's position. What constraints do they face? What do they value? Which domestic audience matters? Which metrics? Which history?

This is ethnography turned adversarial.

It can also reduce moral clarity if empathy becomes excuse. Understanding an adversary's incentives does not require endorsing actions. It requires distinguishing explanation from approval.

Strategic competence often depends on this distinction.

The red team also has an internal cousin: the shadow board or alternate committee. Some organizations invite younger employees or different functions to review strategic questions in parallel with senior leadership. The concept can become gimmicky, but it addresses a genuine problem. Hierarchies compress information upward and narrow the set of lived experiences inside decisions.

An alternate group can expose what the official group does not notice.

Again, consequence matters. If the shadow recommendation is never allowed to alter anything, participants learn that inclusion is ceremonial.

Rehearsal without consequence teaches cynicism.

This is perhaps the deepest cultural risk. Every exercise trains beliefs about the institution. If problems found in drills are never fixed, people learn that drills are theater. If red-team findings disappear, people learn that dissent is decoration. If postmortems produce no change, people learn that truth has no operational value.

The next rehearsal becomes worse because participants bring the memory of futility.

Institutional learning compounds positively or negatively.

A serious red-team culture therefore closes loops. Findings have owners. Decisions are recorded. Rejected recommendations include reasoning. Later events are compared with prior concerns. The red team can be wrong too, and its errors should be visible.

Accountability must run both directions.

Otherwise skepticism acquires false authority. “We warned you” is cheap if warnings are broad enough to cover every outcome.

The goal is calibrated opposition.

A useful red team makes specific claims, ranks concerns, identifies evidence, and states what would change its view. It does not merely perform pessimism.

This distinction matters for public discourse. Societies need criticism, but criticism becomes less informative when every institution is assumed corrupt, every policy catastrophic, every technology salvation or doom. Universal suspicion is not rigor.

Adversarial rehearsal works because it is bounded by a shared attempt to improve contact with reality.

That shared mission is fragile. Politics can turn red teaming into factional combat. Corporate rivalry can turn review into sabotage. Personal identity can turn critique into humiliation.

Good process cannot eliminate these motives. It can reduce their leverage by making claims testable, roles temporary, evidence inspectable, and decisions explicit.

The red team is not the enemy.

It is an institution's attempt to hire the enemy before the enemy arrives.

The bargain is uncomfortable by design. You pay someone to make your plan weaker in the room so that it may be stronger in the world.

Whether the bargain works depends on a final question: when the red team discovers something true that nobody wants to hear, what happens next?

That is the real exercise.
