# Research Brief

## Thesis under test

*Marked to Model* asks a narrower question than whether private credit is a bubble: **what happens to the pricing of risk when the institution that originates a loan, the institution that values it, and the institution that sells it to a saver are increasingly the same institution — and no daily market price exists to check any of them?**

The weak version of the argument is not worth writing. It says that private credit is a Ponzi scheme, that every deal is fraudulent, or that the entire asset class will collapse on a predictable schedule. Some of that rhetoric already exists in financial media and should be treated skeptically rather than reproduced.

The stronger, testable claim is:

> Private credit grew because it solved real problems — regulatory capital costs inside banks, speed and confidentiality for borrowers, and a genuine illiquidity premium for patient capital. It also grew, in part, because it moved lending outside the two disciplines that most reliably reveal credit risk early: bank supervisory examination and public, continuously traded market pricing. Replacing those disciplines with a manager's internal valuation model, quarterly marks, and gated redemption periods can be prudent risk management or can quietly launder deteriorating credit into a "low volatility" number, and only the exceptions — a forced bankruptcy, a redemption run, a fraud indictment — reveal which. As that structure gets sold to retail savers and folded into insurance and retirement products, the group least able to absorb a bad surprise is the group with the least visibility into the underlying model.

The book must be able to survive either outcome. If private credit performs through the current stress episode with genuinely low losses, the evidence should force the argument to soften into "the risk was real but well-managed by disciplined underwriters." If a real credit event cascades through the interconnections traced in the book, it should explain the mechanism without claiming to have called the date.

## Core empirical tests

1. **Why lending left the banks.** Did post-2008 capital and leveraged-lending rules actually push leveraged and middle-market lending toward nonbank managers, and by how much?
2. **The measurement problem.** How much does the estimated size of the private credit market vary by source, and what does that disagreement itself reveal?
3. **Valuation practice.** How are private credit positions actually valued quarter to quarter, who sets the inputs, and what does the empirical literature say about smoothing and stale pricing versus public benchmarks?
4. **Liquidity mismatch.** Under what conditions have "evergreen" or interval retail private credit vehicles gated or capped redemptions, and what does that reveal about the daily-liquid framing sold to investors?
5. **Distress signaling.** Does rising use of payment-in-kind (PIK) interest correlate with credit deterioration, and how does that compare with reported default rates?
6. **Case evidence.** What do the First Brands Group and Tricolor Holdings 2025 collapses show about diligence, collateral verification, and loss allocation inside private credit syndicates specifically — not private markets in general?
7. **Systemic interconnection.** How exposed are regulated banks to nonbank lenders through warehouse lines, subscription facilities, and direct loans, per the Federal Reserve, IMF, and Financial Stability Board?
8. **The insurance channel.** How much of the balance sheet of private-equity-owned life insurers is now invested in affiliated private credit, and what have the NAIC and state regulators done about it?
9. **Ratings practice.** How do private ratings on privately placed debt compare with public ratings on comparable public risk, and what oversight exists?
10. **Retail and retirement access.** What did the August 2025 executive order and subsequent Department of Labor action actually change, and what has not changed yet?
11. **Regulatory visibility.** What data do any single regulator actually receive about private credit funds today, and what changed when the Fifth Circuit vacated the SEC's private fund adviser rule?
12. **Historical comparison.** Where does the 2007–2008 shadow-banking / repo-run literature (Gorton and others) genuinely predict vulnerabilities in today's structure, and where does the analogy break down (leverage levels, funding duration, investor lock-ups)?
13. **The bull case.** What do long-run private credit benchmarks (for example the Cliffwater Direct Lending Index) actually show about realized returns and realized losses across a full cycle including 2008 and 2020?

## Falsification and counter-evidence

Every chapter should actively look for evidence against the book's central worry.

- Direct lending can be a genuinely better financing tool for the borrower: faster, more flexible, confidential, with covenants that prevent — not just punish — deterioration.
- Illiquidity premia are a real, long-documented feature of financial markets, not an excuse invented to justify opacity.
- Locked-up, multi-year capital structures may be *more* stable than daily-traded markets precisely because they cannot be redeemed in a panic — a real advantage over a mutual fund or bank deposit run.
- Some gates and caps (Blackstone's BCRED, Barings, Cliffwater in 2026) are the mechanism working as designed, not evidence of failure; a fund that honors its stated redemption terms during stress is different from a fund that breaks a promise.
- Realized credit losses on diversified direct-lending indexes have historically been comparable to or lower than public high-yield bonds over long periods, including 2008.
- Not every gated fund is a failing fund, and not every private-credit borrower that fails proves systemic risk; company-specific fraud (double-pledged collateral) is a different phenomenon from macro credit-cycle risk.
- Regulators are not blind: state insurance regulators, the Federal Reserve, the IMF, and the Financial Stability Board are all actively producing new disclosure requirements and monitoring frameworks in 2025–2026, which cuts against a pure "nobody is watching" narrative.
- Banks retain large capital buffers built after 2008; direct bank failure from private credit exposure is not the same claim as fee and reputational damage, or losses concentrated in specific warehouse lines.

## Evidence lanes

### I. Why lending moved

Dodd-Frank, Basel III capital treatment of leveraged loans, and the 2013 interagency leveraged lending guidance and its December 2025 rescission. Growth of Apollo, Ares, Blackstone, KKR, Blue Owl, and other direct lenders as bank market share receded.

### II. History

Michael Milken and Drexel Burnham Lambert's 1980s junk-bond market as the closest historical precedent for high-yield credit intermediated outside the traditional bank/public-bond channel, its 1990 collapse, and what does and does not transfer to today's direct-lending structure.

### III. Valuation and liquidity mechanics

Fair-value accounting practice inside business development companies (BDCs) and interval funds; Ludovic Phalippou's and others' research on valuation smoothing in private markets; the mechanics and June 2026 gating of Blackstone's BCRED, and the 2026 cap reductions at Barings and Cliffwater vehicles.

### IV. Distress signals

Lincoln International, T. Rowe Price/O'Flex, and S&P Global Ratings data on the rising share of direct-lending investments carrying payment-in-kind interest, and the "shadow default rate" implied by PIK amendments versus reported default rates.

### V. Case studies

First Brands Group's September 28, 2025 Chapter 11 filing, its alleged off-balance-sheet and double-pledged receivables financing, and lender exposure (Jefferies, UBS, Millstream and related entities). Tricolor Holdings' September 2025 Chapter 7 filing, the Fifth Third Bank and JPMorgan write-downs, and the federal collateral-fraud charges against its executives.

### VI. Systemic interconnection

Federal Reserve FEDS Notes on bank lending to nonbank financial institutions and private credit; IMF Global Financial Stability Report (October 2025); Financial Stability Board Report on Vulnerabilities in Private Credit (May 2026).

### VII. The insurance channel

Apollo/Athene and KKR/Global Atlantic structures; NAIC Private Equity and Private Credit working-group activity, its "13 Considerations," and Actuarial Guideline 55 (adopted August 2025).

### VIII. Regulatory visibility

The SEC's 2023 Private Fund Adviser Rule and its vacatur by the Fifth Circuit in June 2024; what private fund advisers do and do not have to disclose afterward.

### IX. Retail and retirement access

The White House's August 7, 2025 executive order "Democratizing Access to Alternative Assets for 401(k) Investors," the Department of Labor's August 12, 2025 rescission of its 2021 guidance, and the DOL rulemaking process that followed into 2026.

### X. The bull case

Cliffwater Direct Lending Index long-run performance data; the documented illiquidity premium; serious industry and academic arguments for private credit as a durable capital-markets evolution rather than a bubble.

## Source discipline

- Prefer primary sources: court filings, regulatory releases, central bank notes, company disclosures, and named academic research, over aggregated commentary.
- Distinguish realized figures (write-offs, filed losses, index returns) from estimates, projections, and disputed market-size figures; when sources disagree materially (as they do on total private credit AUM), the disagreement itself is reported as a finding, not resolved by picking a favorite number.
- Do not quote an executive, regulator, or researcher without a traceable public statement.
- Preserve uncertainty about litigation still in progress (First Brands and Tricolor bankruptcy proceedings were ongoing as of this writing) and mark disputed allegations as allegations.
- Every chapter states, at least once, what evidence would weaken its own argument.
