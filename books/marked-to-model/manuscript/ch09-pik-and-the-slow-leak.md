# PIK and the Slow Leak

Suppose a mid-sized industrial supplier borrowed $150 million from a direct-lending fund three years ago, priced at a floating rate that today works out to roughly 11 percent a year. Every quarter the company is supposed to wire the fund about $4 million in cash interest. For a while it does. Then a big customer delays a contract renewal, freight costs spike, and the company's earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization — the cash flow measure every covenant in the loan agreement is written around — comes in 20 percent below the number the lender underwrote to. The $4 million payment is now a problem. The company could still scrape the cash together, probably, by drawing down a revolver or slowing payments to its own suppliers, the way businesses have always managed a rough quarter. Or the lender and the borrower's private equity owner could get on a call with counsel, redline a few pages of the credit agreement, and solve it a different way: instead of paying the $4 million in cash, the company adds it to the loan's principal balance. The debt grows from $150 million to $154 million. No cash changes hands. The loan, on the fund's books, keeps its label as a performing asset. Nobody has technically missed a payment, because the payment was redefined rather than skipped. The company's management gets to walk out of the meeting having solved a problem instead of having caused one, at least on this quarter's paperwork.

This is payment-in-kind interest, PIK for short, and it is one of the plainer pieces of financial engineering in the direct-lending toolkit, which is exactly why it deserves more attention than it usually gets. There is no derivative involved, no structured tranche, no synthetic exposure. A borrower that cannot or would rather not pay interest in cash is permitted, under the terms of the loan or an amendment to it, to pay some or all of that interest by capitalizing it — folding it into the principal, so that the lender collects it later, with interest accruing on the enlarged balance in the meantime. The borrower's cash burden goes down this quarter. The borrower's total debt goes up. That trade is either a sensible bridge across a rough patch or a way of feeding a problem instead of confronting it, and from the outside, looking at a single loan on a single quarter's schedule, it is often impossible to tell which.

It is worth pausing on why a lender would ever agree to this rather than simply declaring the borrower in default. The honest answer is that default is expensive and PIK is cheap, at least in the short run, and the incentives on both sides of the table point toward cheap. A missed cash payment under most credit agreements is an event of default, which triggers a cascade: the lender can accelerate the loan, seize collateral, force the company into a workout or a bankruptcy filing, and in a syndicated deal, coordinate — or fail to coordinate — with other lenders who may have different priorities. Workouts are slow, adversarial, and expensive; they eat legal fees, distract management from actually running the business, and frequently destroy value that a little patience might have preserved. A fund that pushes a borrower into default also has to write the loan down, which shows up in that quarter's net asset value, which is the number investors see, the number a fund's own manager is often compensated against, and, if the fund is a retail-facing vehicle of the kind this book has already spent two chapters on, the number that gets printed on someone's account statement. Allowing PIK instead lets everyone avoid that reckoning. The company gets runway — time to fix the underlying problem, refinance, or simply wait for a cyclical trough to pass. The private equity sponsor that owns the company's equity avoids injecting new cash or losing control of the asset. And the lender avoids marking the loan down, avoids triggering cross-defaults with other creditors, and keeps a loan on its books that, on paper, is still earning its full coupon — just not in cash.

It helps to notice how many parties around that table share an interest in reaching for PIK before reaching for a workout. The private equity firm that owns the borrower's equity has already marked its own stake in the company at some multiple of earnings; a default, or even a public acknowledgment of distress, threatens that mark and threatens the sponsor's ability to raise its next fund on the strength of this one's track record. Losing control of the company to its lenders in a restructuring is the outcome the sponsor least wants, and injecting fresh equity to plug the cash gap is the outcome it least wants to pay for. The lender, meanwhile, is usually not a single decision-maker weighing pure credit risk in isolation; it is a fund manager whose own quarterly performance, discussed in an earlier chapter, is built from marks that manager largely controls, and whose investors — some of them pension funds and insurers, increasingly some of them ordinary savers in a retirement account — are shown a net asset value that assumes the loans behind it are worth roughly what the fund says they are worth. A loan reclassified from cash-pay to PIK stays, for accounting purposes, a performing asset earning its stated coupon; a loan pushed into default has to be marked toward whatever the fund believes it could actually recover, which is very often less, sometimes much less, than par. Between an amendment that costs a signature and a write-down that costs return, the incentive runs in one direction almost every time, whatever the merits of the underlying business turn out to be.

None of that makes PIK dishonest. In a genuine liquidity squeeze — a business that is fundamentally sound but temporarily short of cash, the kind every company in a cyclical industry runs into now and then — trading a cash payment for a slightly larger IOU can be exactly the right call for both sides. It is less disruptive than a formal restructuring, it keeps the company operating, and it gives a lender that has done real underwriting work a chance to be repaid in full, with the deferred interest, rather than forcing a fire sale that recovers less. The trouble is that the same mechanism that rescues a sound company buying time can also let an unsound one avoid ever facing its numbers. A loan that goes onto PIK because a borrower is having one bad quarter looks, from the fund's valuation model, identical to a loan that goes onto PIK because a borrower's underlying business no longer generates enough cash to service its debt and probably never will again. The first is a bridge. The second is a slow leak — a company's true condition draining out of view, one uncollected interest payment at a time, while the balance sheet keeps insisting nothing is wrong. Distinguishing the two, from outside the room where the amendment was negotiated, is close to the central difficulty this book keeps returning to: a private loan produces very little independent signal, and the people who set the terms are the same people who would prefer, at least for now, that the signal stay quiet.

There is a distinction inside PIK itself that the data makes it possible to draw with some precision, and it matters enormously, because collapsing it is the easiest way to misread everything that follows. Some loans carry a PIK option from the day they are originated. A growth-stage software company, a healthcare platform still building out its footprint through acquisitions, a business in a genuinely cyclical industry with a known seasonal or economic rhythm — these borrowers sometimes negotiate, up front, a contractual toggle that lets them elect to pay a portion of interest in kind during specified periods, in exchange for a higher overall coupon to compensate the lender for the deferral. A dental-services roll-up buying three or four practices a year, say, might reasonably want the flexibility to direct cash toward acquisitions rather than interest for the first eighteen months of a loan, and a lender comfortable with the underlying growth story might reasonably price that flexibility into the deal rather than treat it as a concession. This is disclosed, priced, and underwritten before a dollar changes hands. Both sides know what they are agreeing to, and the lender has built the possibility of deferred cash flow into its own return expectations from the start. That is a structuring choice, not a warning sign, and treating every PIK-toggle loan as evidence of distress would be a serious misreading of the market — the equivalent of treating every adjustable-rate mortgage as proof the borrower is about to default. Trade-press analysis of the direct-lending market has taken pains to draw exactly this line, distinguishing what one account called structural flexibility, built in at origination as a known feature of the deal, from what it termed shadow distress: the far more meaningful category, and the one that should draw a reader's attention, of PIK added later, by amendment, to a loan that began its life as an ordinary cash-pay credit. That is the industrial supplier from the opening of this chapter: nobody built flexibility into the deal at origination because nobody thought they would need it, and now, under pressure, the lender and borrower are rewriting the loan's terms to relieve a strain that has already shown up. PIK-by-amendment does not prove a company is failing. But it is a company's own lender, sitting closer to the numbers than almost anyone outside the room, deciding that the cash the loan originally called for is not there, and choosing to paper over that fact rather than force the issue.

The data on how often that is happening has become one of the more closely watched threads in private credit research, and it points in one direction, even as reasonable people disagree about exactly what it means. T. Rowe Price's private credit research unit, in an analysis of its own direct-lending book and market-wide amendment activity, found that the share of loans receiving PIK by amendment — again, cash-pay credits later modified to allow some or all of their interest to be paid in kind — rose from roughly 2.6 percent of direct lending investments in 2021 to roughly 6.1 percent by the third quarter of 2025. That is not a small move. It is more than a doubling, concentrated in the years when the interest-rate environment shifted sharply upward from the near-zero conditions many of these loans were originated under, and it lines up with when a floating-rate borrower's debt-service burden would have climbed the fastest relative to its earnings. A separate, broader data set tells a similar story from a different angle. Lincoln International, a middle-market investment bank whose valuation and advisory practice puts it inside thousands of private credit deals every quarter, tracks not just PIK added by amendment but the full universe of debt investments carrying any form of PIK interest, structural or otherwise. On that broader measure, the share of tracked debt investments with some PIK component rose from about 7 percent in the fourth quarter of 2021 to about 11 percent by the first quarter of 2025. Two different lenses, two different numerators, and both point the same way: PIK is more common in the direct-lending market in 2025 than it was in 2021, and the growth has been fastest in the category — amendment-driven PIK — that most plausibly reflects borrowers under real strain rather than borrowers exercising a feature they negotiated for on day one.

One more detail from the amendment data sharpens where that strain is concentrated. Roughly 70 percent of PIK-by-amendment loans involve borrowers with less than $50 million in annual EBITDA. These are small companies by the standards of the leveraged-lending market generally — well below the size of the businesses that dominate headlines about the largest direct-lending deals, the multibillion-dollar unitranche facilities financing buyouts of household-name companies. A $50 million EBITDA business is closer to a regional manufacturer, a specialty distributor, a healthcare services roll-up still working through its acquisitions, the kind of company that has no public debt, no equity ticker, no analyst coverage, and no obligation to disclose anything to anyone outside its own lender group and its private equity owner. It is, in other words, almost exactly the population this book has already described as the least visible corner of an already opaque market — invisible not because anyone is hiding it, but because there was never a mechanism, short of the lender's own internal file, through which the outside world would see it. When a loan to a company like that goes onto PIK, the fact that it happened at all may never surface anywhere except in the aggregate statistics researchers assemble from thousands of these amendments after the fact.

Those statistics have produced an argument worth taking seriously, and worth treating with real caution at the same time. Researchers at T. Rowe Price, working from the amendment data, have suggested that if PIK-by-amendment activity is read as a rough proxy for underlying credit stress — a leading indicator of borrowers who are struggling to service their debt in cash, whether or not that stress ever shows up as a missed payment or a bankruptcy filing — it implies something like a 6 percent rate of meaningful distress running through the direct-lending market as of late 2025. Call it, as some in the research community have started to, a shadow default rate: not defaults that have happened, but defaults-in-waiting, obscured by amendments that let a company keep its loan technically current. Set beside that figure, the default rate that KBRA, the rating agency, reported for the broader private credit universe as of June 2025 — roughly 2.1 percent — looks considerably smaller. If the shadow rate is closer to the real state of the market than the reported rate, then private credit's headline default statistics, the ones cited constantly in industry marketing and investor presentations as evidence of a resilient, low-loss asset class, understate the trouble sitting inside it by something like a factor of three.

That argument deserves to be presented plainly, because it is a serious one made by people who work with the data every day. It also deserves to be treated as exactly what it is: an interpretation, not a settled fact, and a contested one even among people who broadly agree that PIK activity is rising. A shadow default rate built from amendment data measures something upstream of an actual default — a lender's decision to defer, not a borrower's failure to pay — and those two things are not interchangeable. A company that goes onto PIK for two quarters and then returns to cash-pay as its business recovers has experienced real strain, but it has not defaulted, and treating it as equivalent to a company that eventually files for bankruptcy erases a distinction that matters enormously to an investor trying to estimate actual losses. It is also true that KBRA's reported figure and the shadow-rate estimate are not measuring identical populations or identical events in the first place; one counts realized payment defaults and restructurings across a defined universe of rated or tracked credits, the other extrapolates from amendment activity across a broader and less precisely bounded set of direct-lending investments. Comparing them tells you that two different measurement approaches produce two different numbers, which is true of almost every statistic in this market, as earlier chapters of this book have already had occasion to note. What the comparison does not settle, on its own, is which number better predicts the losses investors will actually realize. Researchers who advance the shadow-default framing acknowledge this; it is presented as a signal worth watching, not a replacement for the reported figure, and a reader should hold it the same way — as evidence that the picture painted by headline default statistics is probably incomplete, not as proof of a specific, larger loss rate waiting to be revealed.

Other practitioners looking at the same underlying trend have reached for softer language on purpose. TCW's own analysis of rising PIK prevalence through 2025 framed it less as a hidden default wave and more as a market-wide stress test still in progress — a signal that underwriting standards set during a period of near-zero rates were being reworked in real time as those rates stayed higher for longer than many of the original loans assumed, without presuming to know yet how many of those reworkings would end in a loss and how many would end in a company simply working its way back to cash-pay. That is a meaningfully different emphasis from a shadow default rate stated as a single number, even when the two accounts are looking at data that point the same direction. Both readings can be responsible interpretations of an incomplete picture; the disagreement between them is itself part of what this chapter is describing, in a market where the absence of a traded price leaves room for serious people to look at the same amendment data and describe its meaning differently.

There is a further reason not to let the PIK story run away from itself, and it comes from the cash-flow side of the ledger rather than the amendment side. S&P Global Ratings, examining a sample of business development companies — the publicly registered funds that make up a large, disclosure-rich slice of the direct-lending market and that this book has already spent time inside — found that PIK interest represented only about 7 percent of those funds' net investment income in the second quarter of 2025. Put differently: even after several years of rising PIK activity, and even inside a fund structure specifically built to hold the kind of loans most exposed to amendment-driven PIK, the overwhelming majority of what these funds actually collected and distributed to investors that quarter was ordinary cash interest, paid the way interest has always been paid, by borrowers meeting their obligations in dollars rather than in additional debt. Ninety-three cents of every dollar of net investment income, roughly, came in as cash. That figure is the necessary corrective to everything else in this chapter. PIK is rising, PIK-by-amendment specifically is rising fastest, and the amendment data points toward a level of underlying stress that outpaces what the industry's own headline default statistics show — all three of those things can be true, and still leave PIK as a minority phenomenon inside an asset class where cash-pay lending remains, by a wide margin, the norm. A chapter built entirely around a rising percentage can make a five-point move sound like a flood. It is closer to a slow leak, both because it moves gradually and because it has not, as of the most recent data available, swamped the boat.

The precision that researchers bring to the PIK-by-amendment number is itself worth noticing, because it is a case of the market's own participants trying to build the diagnostic tool that a daily traded price would otherwise provide for free. Nobody watching a public bond needs to infer stress from an amendment filed quietly between a fund and a borrower's counsel; the bond's price falls, and the market has already done the work of pricing in the bad news, publicly, in real time, for anyone to see. A direct loan generates no such signal on its own. The amendment that shifts $4 million of interest from cash to kind produces no ticker movement, no headline, no immediate mark-down, because the mark is set by the same manager who negotiated the amendment, using a model that manager controls, discussed at length in earlier chapters of this book. What Lincoln International, T. Rowe Price, TCW, and the trade press covering this data are doing, in effect, is reconstructing after the fact the kind of stress signal that a public market would have generated automatically, by aggregating thousands of private, individually invisible amendments into a single visible trend line. That the trend line is disputed, imprecise, and open to more than one interpretation is not a flaw specific to this exercise. It is what happens when the only tool available for gauging distress in a market this size is statistical reconstruction rather than a price.

Almost none of this reaches the people whose money is ultimately behind it. An investor in a business development company, or increasingly a saver whose target-date fund carries an allocation to a private-credit sleeve, sees a quarterly statement with a net asset value and a distribution yield, not a loan-by-loan ledger of which credits are being serviced in cash and which are being serviced by growing their own principal. Even a sophisticated reader of that statement would have to dig into a fund's regulatory filings, where PIK income is broken out in the footnotes for those who know to look, to learn that some slice of the yield they are being paid is not cash the fund collected from a borrower at all, but cash the fund itself is choosing to distribute anyway, out of its own capital, to keep the number on the statement looking like ordinary income. That is not fraud; it is standard practice, and funds are permitted to distribute cash from other collections or from capital while PIK income accrues on the books. But it means that a rising PIK share, even the modest one S&P Global found, is not simply a fact about the underlying loans. It is a fact about the composition of the very statement a retail investor reads as a plain account of what their money earned that quarter.

All of which leaves a reader with a pattern rather than a verdict, which is the most honest place this chapter can end. PIK interest is not new, is not inherently a sign of trouble, and remains — even on the more alarmed reading of the amendment data — a minority share of what private credit funds actually collect from their borrowers each quarter. But the amendment data specifically, the loans that started life as ordinary cash-pay credits and were later rewritten under pressure, has moved further and faster than the headline default statistics would suggest on their own, concentrated among smaller companies that carry no public disclosure obligations and that most investors, retail or otherwise, will never hear named. Whether that gap between the amendment data and the reported default rate is a leading indicator of losses still to come, or simply a wider band of ordinary financial flexibility than the market previously measured, is a genuinely open question, and this book does not resolve it here, because the evidence available as of this writing does not resolve it either.

What statistics like these cannot do, no matter how carefully they are assembled, is show what any of this looks like from inside a single company — what a lender actually saw, or failed to see, in the weeks before a loan stopped being a candidate for a quiet PIK amendment and became a bankruptcy filing instead. For that, the aggregate has to give way to the particular. In September 2025, within about three weeks of each other, two private credit borrowers collapsed in ways that had nothing to do with PIK amendments or toggle elections and everything to do with a more elemental failure: collateral that lenders believed was there, and was not. One was First Brands Group, an industrial auto-parts supplier. The other was Tricolor Holdings, a subprime auto lender built on a very different business but undone by a related kind of gap between what a lender thought it held and what it actually did. The next chapter starts with the first of the two, and with a question that sounds almost too ordinary to matter until it doesn't: what, exactly, did First Brands' lenders think they were holding as security for their loans?
