# Tricolor and the Warehouse Line

On September 10, 2025, a used-car dealer and subprime auto lender called Tricolor Holdings filed for Chapter 7 bankruptcy in Texas, the liquidation chapter, the one that assumes there is nothing left worth reorganizing. The filing listed more than 25,000 creditors. That figure is worth sitting with for a moment before the rest of the story, because 25,000 is not a number that describes a company failing to make its bond payments to a syndicate of institutional lenders. It is a number that describes tens of thousands of individual car buyers, auto-parts suppliers, servicers, and small vendors who had done business with a company most of them had never heard was in trouble until the dealerships near them went dark. Roughly sixty Tricolor locations across Texas, California, and the Southwest had shut abruptly that same month, doors closed with little warning to employees or customers who had payments due on cars parked outside. By the time the Chapter 7 petition was filed, Tricolor was not a company negotiating its way through a hard year. It was a company that no longer existed in any operating sense, being formally buried a few weeks after it had, in practice, already stopped.

Tricolor's collapse happened before First Brands Group's did — the Chapter 7 filing in Dallas predates the industrial supplier's Chapter 11 filing by more than two weeks — but it belongs later in this book's argument rather than earlier, because it teaches a different lesson about the same underlying disease. First Brands showed how a company can look, quarter after quarter, like an ordinary industrial credit right up until a lender discovers that the collateral securing its loan had already been pledged somewhere else. Tricolor shows the same failure mode transplanted into a completely different corner of the private-credit ecosystem: not a $10 billion industrial conglomerate borrowing from direct lenders against inventory and receivables, but a subprime consumer lender funding its business through a structure this book has not yet examined closely — the warehouse line, the short-term bank credit facility that sits underneath nearly every securitization business in America, doing work almost nobody outside structured finance ever has reason to think about until it fails. Cambridge Associates, writing for institutional investors trying to make sense of both bankruptcies in the same stretch of weeks, framed First Brands and Tricolor together as a joint stress test of private credit's underwriting discipline rather than two unrelated accidents, and that framing is useful precisely because it invites a comparison this chapter has to take seriously rather than flatten: the same underlying vulnerability, collateral nobody outside the borrower's own books could fully verify, showing up in two businesses that otherwise have almost nothing in common.

Tricolor itself was not a fly-by-night operation dressed up to look established. By the time it collapsed, the company had spent close to two decades building a network of dealerships and a loan-servicing operation large enough to become one of the more prominent names in subprime auto finance, a business built, deal by deal and lot by lot, on the same underlying premise every buy-here-pay-here lender depends on: that a borrower with no credit file, or a damaged one, will still make a car payment reliably if the terms are structured around what that borrower can actually afford each month, priced high enough to cover the losses when some of them do not. That history matters because it complicates the instinct to read Tricolor's ending back into its whole existence, as though the company had been a fraud from its first sale. The evidence assembled by prosecutors describes a scheme operating inside the company's financing arrangements, not necessarily evidence that the underlying used-car-and-loan business was a sham from the outset — a distinction this book's discipline about separating allegation from established fact requires it to preserve, even while treating the scale of what is alleged as serious enough to have ended the company entirely within a matter of weeks.

To understand what went wrong at Tricolor, it helps to understand what the company was actually doing, because the business itself was neither exotic nor, on its face, predatory in the way its critics would later describe it. Tricolor sold used cars — many of them older, higher-mileage vehicles — to buyers with limited or no credit history, the population traditional auto lenders and captive finance arms of the major manufacturers routinely decline. It financed those purchases itself, extending installment loans at interest rates well above what a borrower with an established credit score would pay, priced to compensate for a default rate that runs meaningfully higher in this segment of the market than it does in prime auto lending. This is not, by itself, an unusual or inherently fraudulent business model. Subprime auto lending is a large, long-established, heavily competed industry that serves a real population of borrowers who would otherwise have no path to vehicle ownership at all, and a car, for many of those borrowers, is the difference between having a job within reach and not having one. The interest rates are high because the credit risk is high, and a lender who prices that risk honestly and underwrites carefully can run a legitimate business inside it, the same way a legitimate direct-lending fund can price and underwrite a leveraged loan to a risky corporate borrower rather than a blue-chip one.

Where Tricolor's model became more consequential than a single dealership's finance arm is in what it did with the loans after it made them. Like most scaled subprime auto lenders, Tricolor did not simply hold the loans it originated on its own balance sheet and wait to collect the payments. It pooled them — thousands of individual auto loans bundled together into a single portfolio — and sold interests in that pool to investors as asset-backed securities, rated and structured the way mortgage pools or credit-card receivables are rated and structured, sliced into tranches that absorbed losses in a defined order so that an investor buying the senior tranche was, in theory, insulated from all but the most severe wave of borrower defaults. This is the securitization machine that funds an enormous share of consumer credit in the United States, auto loans very much included, and it works because it lets a lender that does not have a bank's deposit base recycle its capital: originate loans, pool them, sell the pool, use the proceeds to originate more loans, repeat. Rating agencies review the pools. Institutional fixed-income investors buy the rated tranches. The individual borrower making a monthly car payment in Dallas has no idea that payment is flowing, eventually, into a bond held by an insurance company or a pension fund somewhere else in the country.

The gap in that cycle — the part that does not run on its own — sits between the moment a lender originates a loan and the moment it can pool enough of those loans to actually complete a securitization. A securitization is not built loan by loan; it is built in batches, because rating agencies and investors want diversified pools of meaningful size, not a trickle of individual auto notes sold one at a time. Building a batch takes weeks or months of continued lending, and a lender still has to fund every loan it originates during that window, still has to pay the dealership, still has to advance the money to the customer driving the car off the lot, long before there is a completed securitization to sell. That funding gap is what a warehouse line exists to close. A warehouse line is a revolving credit facility, typically extended by a bank, that lets an originator like Tricolor draw down cash to fund new loans as it makes them, secured by the collateral of the loans themselves — the auto notes, the liens on the vehicles, the payment streams the borrower owes. The bank extending the warehouse line is not making a long-term bet on Tricolor's underwriting; it expects to be repaid, loan by loan, batch by batch, as each pool gets securitized and sold, with the warehouse facility then refilled and drawn down again for the next batch. It is short-term, revolving, collateral-secured credit sitting underneath a long-term securitization business, and its entire logic depends on one thing holding true without exception: that the specific loans pledged as collateral for a specific draw on the warehouse line actually belong, free and clear, to the bank that extended that draw.

That is the assumption federal prosecutors allege Tricolor's own executives set out to defeat. In the criminal case that followed the collapse, prosecutors described what they alleged Tricolor had been running internally as a "financial crimes enterprise," a phrase chosen for a charging document, not a phrase this book adopts as settled fact — the allegations against Tricolor's executives were, as of this writing, charges, not convictions, and the presumption of innocence attaches to every individual named regardless of how damning the government's own account reads. What prosecutors alleged, specifically, was collateral fraud on a scale that made First Brands' alleged double-pledging look almost modest by comparison: roughly $800 million in loan collateral that had, according to the charges, been pledged more than once, the same underlying auto loans presented as security to multiple different lenders simultaneously, each lender believing it held a first and exclusive claim on a specific pool of loans that, in reality, another lender also believed it held. A warehouse line only works if the bank extending it can trust the borrowing company's own representation of what collateral is actually free to pledge. Verifying that representation independently, loan by loan, across thousands of individual auto notes changing hands in real time, is expensive and slow in a way that the entire warehouse-lending business model is built to avoid — the bank is relying, at some point, on the originator's own books being honest, the same way a term-loan lender in the First Brands case was relying on a borrower's own representation of which receivables had already been factored elsewhere. If that representation is false, and false at the scale prosecutors alleged, no amount of ordinary loan-level diligence was designed to catch it, because it was never built to assume the borrower was lying about its own collateral on this scale.

The lenders caught inside that structure were not marginal players experimenting at the edges of subprime auto. Fifth Third Bank, a large regional bank with a long-established auto-finance business, had extended Tricolor a warehouse facility reported at roughly $200 million. After the collapse, Fifth Third alleged fraud in its own filings and disclosures tied to the exposure and recorded an impairment reported in the range of $170 million to $200 million — in other words, a loss approaching the full value of what it believed it had lent, secured, against collateral that turned out not to be exclusively its own. JPMorgan Chase, the country's largest bank by assets and one of the most sophisticated underwriters of structured and asset-backed credit in the world, took a $170 million charge-off tied to its own Tricolor exposure. Jefferies disclosed its own financial exposure tied to Tricolor-linked financing in a public filing, one more name on a growing list that came to include UBS and Millennium as firms with money caught in the same structure, each institution discovering its piece of the same underlying problem more or less independently, in the days and weeks after Tricolor's dealerships went dark. Reported figures on the combined obligations across the full network of lenders exposed to Tricolor, once every warehouse line, securitization interest, and related financing arrangement is added up, ran above $10 billion — a number that dwarfs the roughly $800 million in specifically double-pledged collateral prosecutors alleged, because the fraud allegation describes only the mechanism of the deception, not the full scale of capital that had been extended to Tricolor across its entire funding structure and was now, in the wake of a Chapter 7 filing, of uncertain value.

It is JPMorgan's exposure, and JPMorgan's own public reckoning with it, that carries the most weight in this story, not because the dollar figure was the largest — Fifth Third's reported impairment was comparable or larger relative to its own facility — but because of who was saying so, and in what setting. Earnings calls are rehearsed. A bank the size of JPMorgan prepares for the questions analysts are likely to ask about a disclosed loss, and the instinct of any large public company under that kind of scrutiny is to frame a loss in the language of containment: immaterial to the balance sheet, already reserved for, a known and bounded exposure rather than an open question about what else might be sitting undiscovered in the same book. On JPMorgan's October 14, 2025 third-quarter earnings call, the same call on which Jamie Dimon delivered the cockroach line this book opened with, Dimon was asked, more or less directly, how a bank with JPMorgan's underwriting resources, credit expertise, and risk infrastructure had ended up exposed to a fraud of this kind at all, alongside the $170 million charge-off the bank had already disclosed. His answer, reported afterward under headlines built around the sentence itself, was blunt in a way that bank CEOs on earnings calls, coached to avoid handing reporters a quotable admission of failure, generally are not: it is not our finest moment. Four words, no qualification, no immediate pivot to how small the loss was relative to JPMorgan's overall balance sheet, though that was also true and Dimon said versions of it elsewhere on the same call. The sentence that survived into headlines was the plain one, and it is worth taking seriously exactly because of how plain it is. This is a bank that built, over decades, one of the most extensive credit-risk and structured-finance underwriting operations in the world, with dedicated teams whose entire job is evaluating exactly this kind of warehouse-lending exposure before a dollar goes out the door. Its own warehouse-lending diligence reportedly failed to catch what prosecutors now allege was a deliberate, sustained scheme to pledge the same collateral to more than one lender. If JPMorgan's own systems missed it, the honest question is not whether some smaller or less disciplined lender might have missed it too — plenty did — but what, specifically, a bank of JPMorgan's sophistication is actually able to see into a borrower's books before it extends this kind of credit, and what it cannot, no matter how many examiners and credit officers stand behind the decision.

That question is where this chapter's argument has to become more careful than the headlines that followed Dimon's remark, because the easy version of this story — a subprime lender committed fraud, banks got burned, private credit is dangerous — collapses two distinct claims that deserve to stay separated. The first claim is about the nature of the risk. What prosecutors allege happened at Tricolor is not a legitimate lending business that underperformed in a downturn, the ordinary credit-cycle story of a borrower whose revenue fell short of its debt service and who defaulted honestly on obligations it could no longer meet. It is alleged outright fraud, criminally charged, against the executives who ran the company — a deliberate scheme, on the government's own account, to deceive multiple lenders about what collateral actually belonged to whom. That is a different category of risk from the kind this book has spent its earlier chapters examining: rising leverage, thinning covenants, payment-in-kind interest quietly substituting for cash, valuation marks drifting away from what a public market would say a loan is worth. Those are risks embedded in how private credit prices and monitors ordinary business performance over time. Collateral fraud of the kind alleged at Tricolor is a risk that no amount of sophisticated valuation modeling or covenant design addresses, because it depends on someone inside the borrowing company deciding to lie, and a lender's defense against that kind of lie is verification, not pricing. Treating Tricolor as proof that private credit's business model is broken conflates a pricing-and-monitoring failure with a criminal-fraud failure, and the evidence here supports only the second claim, not the first.

The second claim worth separating out is about where the diligence failure actually occurred, and this is the detail most retellings of the Tricolor story skip past on their way to a tidier moral. The lenders burned at Tricolor were not, for the most part, the kind of lightly regulated nonbank private credit funds this book has spent its middle chapters worrying about — the funds marking their own positions quarterly, raising capital from investors who cannot independently verify a manager's internal model, operating outside the examination regime that governs a chartered bank. Fifth Third Bank and JPMorgan Chase are two of the most heavily regulated financial institutions in the country, subject to routine, invasive supervisory examination, stress-tested annually against severe recession scenarios, staffed with credit-risk functions built and refined over decades specifically to catch exactly the kind of collateral misrepresentation that got past them here. The warehouse lines that funded Tricolor's origination business were bank credit, extended by banks, underwritten by bank credit officers, examined — at least in the ordinary supervisory sense — by bank regulators. If the story this book is assembling were simply "regulated banks do careful diligence and shadow lenders do not," Tricolor is the case that refuses to fit it. The diligence failure here happened inside the regulated banking system, at institutions with every structural advantage this book has otherwise attributed to bank supervision over private fund opacity, and it failed anyway, against a scheme specifically designed to exploit the trust that warehouse lending structurally requires. That is an uncomfortable fact for a book built partly around the argument that bank examination and market pricing are the two disciplines private credit has grown by avoiding, and it belongs in the record precisely because it is uncomfortable rather than because it is convenient.

What that fact actually shows, read carefully, is narrower and more useful than either the alarmed version or the dismissive version of the Tricolor story. It does not show that bank supervision is worthless, because nothing about Fifth Third's or JPMorgan's exposure here suggests either institution is at risk of failure, or that its capital buffers were inadequate to absorb a loss of this size — a $170 million or $200 million charge is a real loss and a real embarrassment, not a threat to either bank's solvency, and the distinction between "this hurt" and "this endangers the institution" matters throughout this book, not only here. It does not show that private credit specifically, as opposed to subprime auto lending and warehouse-line diligence generally, is uniquely broken, because the mechanism that failed — a lender trusting a borrower's own representation about which collateral is unencumbered — is not a private-credit innovation at all; it is one of the oldest vulnerabilities in secured lending, present in commercial banking since long before anyone used the phrase private credit, and Tricolor's alleged fraud would have worked the same way against a warehouse line extended by a bank in 1995 as it did against one extended in 2024. What it does show, plainly, is that determined, sophisticated fraud by a borrower's own management can defeat diligence performed by even the most capable, most heavily regulated lenders in the system, and that the specific tool built to bridge the gap between loan origination and loan securitization — the warehouse line, present not only under subprime auto lenders but under a wide range of asset-based private credit structures this book has already described — depends on a form of trust that is genuinely difficult to verify at scale, structurally difficult, in a way that no amount of regulatory pedigree on the lending side fully solves.

None of that should read as a defense of Tricolor's own conduct, or as minimizing what its executives are alleged to have done to the lenders who trusted them and, less directly but no less seriously, to the tens of thousands of borrowers whose car loans became collateral in a scheme most of them never knew existed. A person making a monthly payment on a used car in Dallas had no reason to know that the note behind that payment might have been pledged twice, and no way to protect themselves from what their own lender's management was allegedly doing behind the scenes. The fraud, if it happened the way prosecutors allege, was not victimless simply because the immediate financial losses landed on banks with the balance sheets to absorb them. It is simply a different kind of story than the one this book has been building toward one chapter at a time — a story about a specific set of people accused of specific, deliberate deception, rather than a story about an entire asset class quietly mispricing risk in ways nobody intended.

It is also worth being precise about what this chapter is not claiming, because the temptation to fold Tricolor into a broader indictment of private credit as an asset class runs strong, and the evidence here does not support it. Tricolor was not a private-credit-backed direct loan in the sense the earlier chapters of this book have used that phrase — it was not an Apollo or Ares or Blue Owl fund lending directly to a mid-sized corporate borrower against a set of privately negotiated covenants. It was a subprime consumer lender funded through bank warehouse credit and securitization, an older and more conventional structured-finance model that predates the modern direct-lending industry by decades and that operates under its own long-established conventions for collateral verification, servicing, and securitization disclosure. What connects Tricolor to the rest of this book is not that it was a private-credit fund in the technical sense, but that it shared the same underlying vulnerability this book keeps finding wherever a loan's true collateral position depends on a single party's own representation rather than an independently verifiable, continuously priced record: a warehouse lender extending credit against loan collateral it could not fully audit in real time is functionally exposed to the same blind spot as a term-loan lender extending credit against inventory and receivables it could not fully audit in real time. The label on the financing differs. The structural gap does not.

Two case studies, taken together, are still only two case studies. First Brands and Tricolor both involve allegations of collateral misrepresentation at the borrower level — receivables pledged twice in one case, loan pools pledged twice in the other — and both cases left real, disclosed losses at real lenders, some of them banks, some of them the nonbank funds this book has spent more time examining. But two borrower-level frauds, however serious, however large the dollar figures attached to them, are not by themselves evidence of a systemic financial crisis, and treating them as if they were would be exactly the kind of overclaiming this book set out, in its introduction, to avoid. What First Brands and Tricolor can responsibly show is that the diligence infrastructure surrounding both direct lending and bank warehouse credit has real, demonstrated gaps, and that determined fraud inside either structure can move a very large amount of money before anyone outside the company itself notices. Whether those gaps are isolated failures — two unusually determined management teams who happened to get caught in the same six-month window — or early symptoms of something spreading more broadly through a financial system that has grown more interconnected, and in places more opaque, than most of the people holding exposure to it fully appreciate, is not a question two bankruptcy filings can answer on their own. It is a question for the people whose actual job is watching the connections between banks, funds, and the borrowers both of them lend to — the Federal Reserve, the International Monetary Fund, the Financial Stability Board — and what they have found, in the reports this book turns to next, is the subject of the chapter that follows: When You See One Cockroach, which returns to the four words Dimon used to open this book and asks how far past Tricolor and First Brands the rest of the sentence actually reaches.
