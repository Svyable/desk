# Pricing the Unpriced

On the morning of October 14, 2025, when Jamie Dimon told a room of analysts that when you see one cockroach there are probably more, he was answering a question about a $170 million write-off, not making a prediction about a market. Everyone listening turned it into a prediction anyway, because that is what a memorable sentence from a trusted source does to an audience that has no other instrument for reading the temperature of the water. This book has spent nineteen chapters building that instrument by hand, one piece at a time — what private credit is, why lending moved there, how a loan gets a price when no market ever sets one, what happens when investors try to get their money back, what a rising share of payment-in-kind interest can and cannot tell you, what two real bankruptcies looked like from the inside, how exposed the regulated banking system actually is, how an insurance company can end up lending money to its own owner, who rates a loan nobody else can see, and what happened the week the retirement system's front door opened. It is worth asking, now, plainly: did the book find more cockroaches, or did it mostly find an earnings call?

The honest accounting is neither triumphant nor reassuring, and that is probably the most accurate thing that can be said about it. In the year after Dimon's remark, no bank failed because of private credit. No systemic run swept through the direct-lending market the way one swept through money-market funds and asset-backed commercial paper in the fall of 2008. The funds that gated redemptions — Blackstone's BCRED, Barings, Cliffwater's retail vehicle — did not default on anything; they read the provisions they had already written into their own prospectuses and enforced them, prorating investors rather than fire-selling loans, which is a real distinction and not a technicality. The reported default rate across the tracked private credit universe sat near 2 percent through the middle of 2025, not far from where it had sat for years. PIK interest, the quiet mechanism by which a struggling loan can keep looking healthy on paper, kept climbing — from roughly 2.6 percent of direct lending investments carrying amendment-driven PIK in 2021 to roughly 6.1 percent by the third quarter of 2025 — but it stayed, even on that reading, a minority phenomenon inside a market still collecting the overwhelming majority of its income in ordinary cash. First Brands and Tricolor turned out, on the evidence available as this book went to press, to look less like a preview of a market-wide credit event and more like two specific frauds, criminally charged in one case and alleged in the other, that private credit's own diligence processes and a major bank's own warehouse-lending desk both failed to catch. None of that is nothing. All of it is less than the cockroach line implied to the people who heard it stripped of context on a phone screen the same afternoon.

And yet the year also produced exactly the kind of evidence that keeps a careful reader from closing the book on a note of relief. The Federal Reserve's own research put bank lending to private credit providers at roughly $300 billion as of June 2025, a channel connecting the regulated core of the financial system to a market that core does not examine the way it examines its own loan book. The International Monetary Fund's October 2025 Global Financial Stability Report and the Financial Stability Board's May 2026 report on private credit vulnerabilities both flagged concentration, leverage, and valuation opacity as live concerns, in the hedged, conditional language international bodies use when they are worried but not yet certain. The Fifth Circuit's 2024 vacatur of the SEC's Private Fund Adviser Rule left no single American regulator with a comprehensive, standardized view of private credit fund leverage, valuation practice, and investor terms — nothing resembling what a bank examiner sees inside a bank. And in December 2025, regulators went the other direction entirely, rescinding the 2013 interagency leveraged lending guidance that had done more than any other single policy to push leveraged lending out of the banking system in the first place, on the stated grounds that the old guidance had been overly restrictive. A regulator loosening the rule that helped create this market, in the same season another regulator was still trying to figure out how much of that market it could actually see, is not proof of anything by itself. It is the kind of detail that belongs on a page a careful reader keeps returning to.

Somewhere inside all of that sits the saver this book introduced a few chapters back — the one whose account statement was traced backward, fee layer by fee layer, through a target-date fund, an alternative-assets sleeve, a fund-of-funds or business development company, and a direct-lending fund, to a single leveraged loan made to a company that saver has never heard of and never will. Nothing about that chain has changed since it was drawn. The statement still arrives quarterly. The number on it is still calm — smoother, in fact, than the number on a comparable allocation to public high-yield bonds would have been over the same stretch, because a public bond's price moves every day the market is open and a private loan's mark moves only when somebody with every incentive to be optimistic decides to move it. That saver did not choose most of those fee layers individually, did not select the valuation model, does not know whether the sleeve inside the target-date fund is five percent of the balance or fifteen, and — if the plan enrolled them by default, the way the August 2025 executive order and the Department of Labor's rulemaking that followed it were explicitly designed to make easier — may not have made an active decision to hold any of it at all. The saver's exposure to everything traced in this book runs through that one calm number, and the calm number is real in the accounting sense and conditional in every other sense that matters.

That is the picture the book has to leave the reader holding, because two of its own chapters, each argued as seriously as the evidence allowed, do not resolve into one conclusion. Chapter 18 measured today's private credit structure against Gary Gorton and Andrew Metrick's account of what actually made 2007 and 2008 a cascading panic rather than an ordinary credit downturn — short-term repo funding of long-term assets, informationally insensitive debt that abruptly became sensitive after a shock, a collateral-haircut spiral that fed on itself. Its honest finding was that the analogy is partial. Private credit funds typically carry far less fund-level leverage than the structured vehicles that collapsed in 2008. Investor capital is mostly locked up for years, not funded overnight and callable on a bad afternoon. Loans are originated directly rather than tranched and resold into a securitization chain that multiplies the same underlying risk many times over. The specific mechanical fragility that turned a housing downturn into a global panic is not present here in anything like the same form. Chapter 19, given a full chapter to make its own case on its own terms, showed that the Cliffwater Direct Lending Index has returned roughly 9.5 percent annualized since its September 2010 inception, has outperformed public high-yield bonds, leveraged loans, and investment-grade credit in thirteen of the twenty calendar years through 2024 — a span that includes 2008 and 2009 — and that realized credit losses across that index, at roughly 1.01 percent, sit close to leveraged loans' 1.02 percent and below high-yield bonds' 1.49 percent over the same period. Two full decades of data, including a real crisis, showing comparable or better realized outcomes than the public credit this market is often accused of quietly being riskier than, is not a footnote. It is close to the strongest evidence the bull case for private credit has, and this book has tried to present it as such rather than as a target.

Both chapters are honest. Neither cancels the other. The 2008 comparison being partial does not mean the valuation opacity and interconnection this book has spent nineteen chapters documenting are imaginary — they rhyme with the earlier era in ways that are real even without the repo-market fragility that made the earlier era a cascade. And the bull case's two decades of index data do not mean the next decade will look like the last two, because the market that generated that history was smaller, more institutional, slower-growing, and far less retail-facing than the market it is now being asked to describe — a limitation Chapter 19 raised against its own argument, and a February 2026 academic study comparing how different index providers construct their private credit benchmarks in the first place only sharpened. A reader who wants this book to resolve that tension into a single verdict — bubble or breakthrough, crisis waiting or premium earned — is asking it to manufacture a certainty the evidence does not currently support. The book's own falsification tests, set out before a word of it was drafted, required it to survive either outcome: to soften into "the risk was real but well managed" if the current stress episode passes without a genuine credit event, and to explain the mechanism rather than claim to have predicted the date if one eventually arrives. Both of those are still live possibilities as this book goes to press, and pretending otherwise for the sake of a tidier last chapter would undo the discipline the previous nineteen were built on.

What a reader can reasonably ask for, instead of a verdict, is a way to keep watching — a small number of things, already established earlier in this book, that a person with no access to a bank examiner's files or a fund's internal model could actually track over the years ahead, and that would move the honest answer in one direction or the other without requiring anyone to guess in advance which direction that will be. None of what follows is new. Each item is a mechanism this book has already explained at length; what changes now is only that they are gathered in one place, as a lens rather than a forecast.

The first is the frequency and size of redemption gates at evergreen and interval funds, the mechanism Chapter 8 walked through in detail. One gate, activated for the first time after years of a fund never needing it, honored in full through prorated payments rather than broken through fire sales, is a stress test a structure passed — genuinely reassuring, on its own terms. A pattern of gates recurring across successive quarters, deepening rather than resolving, or spreading from three prominent vehicles to a broader swath of the evergreen market, would be a different fact entirely: not proof of insolvency, since gating and defaulting remain distinct events, but proof that the liquidity mismatch at the center of these products is not a one-time event the market absorbed but a standing feature investors will keep encountering whenever redemption demand rises. Watch whether the caps come back down toward five percent, as Cliffwater's did, or whether managers loosen them again once the pressure passes; watch how many quarters an investor who wants out in a stressed period actually waits before receiving what they asked for.

The second is the trend in PIK-by-amendment rates that Chapter 9 traced from roughly 2.6 percent in 2021 to roughly 6.1 percent by the third quarter of 2025. A number that plateaus there, or drifts back down as rate cuts ease borrowers' cash-interest burden, would suggest the deferral wave of the mid-2020s was exactly what it looked like on its most charitable reading: a bridge across a difficult stretch, not a slow leak. A number that keeps climbing past that level, especially if it climbs fastest among the smaller, sub-$50-million-EBITDA borrowers who already account for roughly seventy percent of amendment-driven PIK and who carry no public disclosure obligations at all, would widen the gap between the reported default rate near 2 percent and the shadow default rate researchers have built from amendment data — and a widening gap between what a market reports about itself and what its own lenders' behavior implies is precisely the kind of divergence this book has argued a private market is structurally prone to hiding until it cannot.

The third is the pace of growth in bank lending to nonbank financial institutions, the channel Chapter 12 measured at roughly $300 billion as of June 2025. That figure is not, by itself, alarming; banks have always lent to other lenders, and the capital buffers built since 2008 are real. What would be worth watching is the rate at which that figure grows relative to bank capital, and how concentrated it becomes among a small number of large banks with outsized exposure to a small number of large private credit managers — the specific kind of interconnection the Federal Reserve, the IMF, and the Financial Stability Board have each flagged as the mechanism by which trouble in an ostensibly separate, lightly regulated market could reach the regulated banking system that ordinary depositors and, through deposit insurance, taxpayers ultimately stand behind.

The fourth is the substance, not just the existence, of Department of Labor rulemaking on 401(k) access to alternative assets. The August 2025 executive order and the DOL's rescission of its 2021 guidance removed barriers; they did not, by themselves, build new investor protections to replace what the removed guidance had been doing. The rulemaking process that followed into 2026 is where that gets decided — whether the final rules include meaningful disclosure requirements about valuation methodology and redemption terms specific to illiquid alternative assets inside a defined-contribution plan, whether they impose any guardrail on using these products as a default rather than an opt-in allocation, and whether plan sponsors that add a private-credit sleeve face fiduciary standards proportionate to the opacity of what they are adding. A rulemaking that arrives with real teeth on those questions would suggest the system is capable of extending access without abandoning the disciplines this book has spent its middle chapters describing as already thin. A rulemaking that arrives mostly as a green light, with disclosure and guardrails left to each plan sponsor's discretion, would suggest the opposite.

The fifth is the depth of new disclosure coming out of the NAIC's insurance-side workstream — the Private Rating Letter Rationale Reports now required within ninety days of an annual update or rating change, and the affiliated-investment reporting that Actuarial Guideline 55 put in place after its adoption in August 2025. Both were built in direct response to the growth of affiliated private credit inside insurers like Apollo-owned Athene and KKR-owned Global Atlantic, whose combined managed assets grew from roughly $72 billion in 2020 to roughly $158 billion under KKR by the mid-2020s. Whether those disclosure requirements produce reports with genuine analytical substance — the phrase state regulators themselves used when they demanded more — or become another box checked with boilerplate is not yet settled, and it is one of the more tractable things an outside observer can actually watch, because state insurance filings are public in a way that a private fund's internal model is not.

None of these five indicators, alone or together, will hand anyone a date or a verdict. That is by design, not by evasion. A reader who tracks all five for the next several years and finds gates staying rare and resolving cleanly, PIK-by-amendment leveling off, bank-to-nonbank lending growing in line with capital rather than outpacing it, DOL rules landing with real disclosure attached, and NAIC reports actually saying something — that reader would have real grounds to conclude the current stress episode strengthened the market rather than exposed it, the way Chapter 19's bull case argues it might. A reader who tracks the same five indicators and finds gates recurring and deepening, PIK-by-amendment still climbing while the reported default rate stays flat, bank exposure concentrating among a handful of large lenders, a 401(k) rule that removed barriers without adding protections, and insurance disclosures that read like compliance theater — that reader would have real grounds for the opposite conclusion, the one closer to what Dimon's remark implied before anyone had done the work of finding out whether it was true. Both outcomes remain genuinely possible from where this book stands. That is not a hedge inserted to avoid controversy. It is what nineteen chapters of evidence, honestly weighed, actually show.

Which leaves the question this book owes its most exposed reader directly: the saver whose plan now offers, or soon will offer, a private-credit sleeve, and who is not going to spend the next several years cross-referencing Federal Reserve FEDS Notes against NAIC filings the way this book has. Nothing in the preceding four hundred pages is investment advice, and nothing in this last one will pretend to be. But a reader who has come this far has earned a shorter, more practical version of the same discipline. A quarterly statement number that barely moves is not, by itself, evidence of safety; it may just be evidence that nobody has tried to sell the underlying asset recently, which is a different thing, and Chapter 7 explained at length why those two things get confused. A fund that calls itself semi-liquid, evergreen, or monthly-valued is not a mutual fund wearing a longer name; it is a different legal structure with a different, narrower promise, and the redemption terms in the risk-factor section of its prospectus, not the language on the fact sheet, are the terms that will govern the day someone actually wants their money. A plan administrator or an advisor can be asked, plainly, whether an alternative-assets allocation is the plan's default or something a participant has to elect, who values the private holdings inside it and how often, what its stated redemption terms actually allow in a stressed quarter, and how its reported return compared with a public high-yield benchmark over the same period — not because the answers will settle the argument this book has just spent two chapters refusing to settle, but because asking them is the difference between a saver who understands what kind of number they are looking at and one who has simply been told it is safe.

Jamie Dimon's remark was never really a forecast, whatever it became in the retelling. It was one very well-informed, self-interested banker's read on his own competitors, delivered half against his better judgment, on a morning when the wound was still fresh enough to talk about. He may turn out to have been right that there were more cockroaches than the two the market had already found by October 2025. He may turn out to have been describing the last of them. The honest answer, standing where this book now stands, is that nobody outside a handful of underwriting desks and valuation committees currently knows which, and that the people most exposed to the answer — the saver whose statement carries a number nobody can quite tell them how to check — are, structurally, among the last who will find out. What this book can offer that reader is not reassurance and not alarm, but the thing every chapter before this one was built to provide in miniature: a way of looking at a number closely enough to know what it is not telling you, and the patience to keep watching rather than to guess.
