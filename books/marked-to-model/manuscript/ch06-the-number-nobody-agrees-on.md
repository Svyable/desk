# The Number Nobody Agrees On

In December 2024, BlackRock announced it would pay $12 billion for HPS Investment Partners, a direct-lending shop that had spun out of Highbridge Capital Management two decades earlier and built itself into one of the largest private credit managers in the world. The deal closed the following July. When it did, BlackRock's own press release described the combined operation as a private credit franchise with roughly $190 billion to $220 billion in assets under management. Read that sentence again. This was not a stranger's guess about a rival's book. It was the acquiring company, days after taking possession of the thing it had just bought, describing the size of what it now owned — and it could not narrow the answer past a thirty-billion-dollar band. Thirty billion dollars is larger than the annual GDP of most countries. It is also, in the context of this particular disclosure, a rounding error the market absorbed without much comment, because everyone in the room already understood that private credit numbers come with slack built in.

That slack is the subject of this chapter. Ask how big the private credit market is — not what it will be worth next year, not whether it is a bubble, just how big it is right now, today, in dollars — and you will get a different answer depending on whom you ask, and the differences are not small. They run into the hundreds of billions, sometimes past a trillion, and they come from serious people using real data, not cranks with an agenda. A market that large disagreeing with itself by that much about its own basic size is not a footnote to the story this book is telling. It is close to the whole story, told in miniature, before anything has gone wrong at a single borrower.

Start with the number that gets quoted most often, because international institutions carry a kind of borrowed authority that a single data vendor does not. In April 2024, the International Monetary Fund published a blog post under the headline "Fast-Growing $2 Trillion Private Credit Market Warrants Closer Watch." The number in the title did real work. It told policymakers, journalists, and eventually the general public that private credit had grown, in a little over a decade, from a niche strategy pursued by a handful of specialist funds into something roughly the size of the entire high-yield bond market — a genuine peer to a public market that had taken generations to build. The IMF's figure counted private debt funds broadly: direct lending to companies, yes, but also distressed debt, mezzanine finance, and other strategies that fall under the private-credit umbrella in the databases the Fund drew on. It was a global number, not a U.S. number, aggregating activity across North America, Europe, and Asia. And when the IMF returned to the subject in its October 2025 Global Financial Stability Report, the language had hardened — more attention to interconnection with banks, more explicit concern about valuation opacity — but the market it was describing was still, in round terms, the same roughly two-trillion-dollar animal, grown somewhat larger, not fundamentally redefined.

Now narrow the aperture. Data providers that track only direct lending to companies — the core "unitranche" and senior-secured loans that firms like Ares, Blue Owl, and the newly enlarged BlackRock-HPS actually originate, excluding the broader universe of distressed debt, mezzanine, and other private-debt cousins — routinely put the U.S. market somewhere in the neighborhood of $1.3 trillion to $1.4 trillion. That is not a rival estimate produced by someone trying to make private credit look smaller for political reasons. It is what you get when you ask a narrower question of a narrower slice of the world. The IMF's number is global and inclusive; this number is domestic and specific. Both can be correct simultaneously, because they are not actually answers to the same question, even though they get reported in the same sentence, in the same news cycle, as though they were.

Then there is a third number, smaller in absolute terms but oddly more unsettling, because of what it measures rather than how large it is. In a research note published in June 2025, staff at the Federal Reserve Board estimated that U.S. banks had lent roughly $300 billion to private credit providers themselves — not to the underlying corporate borrowers those funds serve, but to the funds and the vehicles that hold and manage private credit assets, through subscription lines, net-asset-value loans, and other financing arrangements. Three hundred billion dollars is a modest fraction of either the IMF's two trillion or the narrower American $1.3 trillion. But it is not describing the same layer of the system as those other two numbers. It is describing the wiring underneath the market — the extent to which the banking system that private credit was supposed to have partially replaced is, in fact, still funding a meaningful share of it from behind the scenes. A saver told that her retirement account now holds a slice of "non-bank" private credit might reasonably assume the banks are out of the picture. The Fed's own accounting says they are not out of the picture; they are simply a step removed, lending to the lenders rather than to the borrowers, in a relationship that shows up on a bank's balance sheet as ordinary commercial lending and on a fund's marketing materials as evidence of its independence from the banking system. Both descriptions are technically true. Together they describe something more tangled than either implies alone.

So: two trillion, globally, broadly defined. Something over one trillion, domestically, narrowly defined. Three hundred billion, in bank exposure alone, measuring a completely different thing. None of these numbers is wrong. That is the uncomfortable part. If one estimate were simply an error — a data vendor that miscounted, an analyst who double-added a column — the fix would be obvious and the story would end here. Instead, each number reflects a defensible methodological choice, and the choices do not point in the same direction.

The first and largest source of disagreement is the oldest problem in private markets: the gap between money that has been promised and money that has actually gone out the door. Private credit funds, like private equity funds before them, raise capital in commitments. An institutional investor — a pension fund, an endowment, an insurer — agrees to provide up to a certain amount over the life of the fund, and the manager draws on that commitment gradually as it finds loans to make. The capital that has been committed but not yet deployed is called dry powder, and it sits, uninvested, waiting, sometimes for years. Some market-size estimates count only loans that have actually been made — capital at work, earning interest, exposed to an actual borrower's actual ability to pay. Others count the full committed capital, dry powder included, on the theory that money a fund has raised and is contractually obligated to deploy is a meaningful measure of the market's eventual footprint even before a single dollar has been lent. Both are legitimate ways to answer different questions — how much lending is happening right now, versus how much lending capacity exists — but they produce very different totals from the same underlying fund data, and headlines rarely specify which one they are reporting. A fund that closed on ten billion dollars of investor commitments eighteen months ago and has so far placed six billion of it with actual borrowers shows up, depending on which convention an analyst uses, as either a six-billion-dollar lender or a ten-billion-dollar one, and both figures will appear in reputable trade publications within the same news cycle, each cited as though it were simply the number.

There is a further layer underneath even that distinction, easy to miss because it sounds like a technicality: once capital is deployed, is it counted at the loan's face value — the amount actually lent, plus accrued interest — or at the fund's reported net asset value, the figure a manager arrives at after applying its own quarterly valuation process? In a market where every loan is marked internally rather than priced on an exchange, those two numbers are not guaranteed to match, and the gap between them is itself an open question this book returns to directly in the next chapter. For now it is enough to notice that "size of the private credit market" can mean the sum of what was lent, or the sum of what managers currently say it is worth, and a data provider rarely states which one it used in the same sentence as the headline figure.

The second source of disagreement is definitional, and it is in some ways more consequential than the dry-powder question, because it changes not just the total but what the total is supposed to represent. "Private credit" has become an umbrella term stretched to cover things that would have been described, ten years ago, as entirely separate businesses. Direct lending to mid-sized companies — the classic unitranche loan to a private-equity-backed borrower — sits at the narrow core of every estimate. But asset-based finance, a rapidly growing category that includes lending against pools of consumer receivables, equipment leases, or royalty streams, increasingly gets folded into the same bucket, as does real estate credit and infrastructure debt. A data provider whose definition includes asset-based finance and real estate lending will report a materially larger number than one that restricts itself to direct corporate lending, even if both are looking at overlapping sets of managers and funds. Neither choice is dishonest. Asset-based finance genuinely is private, genuinely is credit, and genuinely has grown fast enough in the past several years that excluding it understates where the industry's own growth is actually concentrated. But including it means comparing a 2024 total that sweeps in receivables financing against a 2018 total that did not, and calling the difference "growth" without acknowledging that some of it is definitional rather than real.

The third source of disagreement is the least visible and, for a book concerned with opacity, the most telling: where the data actually comes from. Some estimates are built from voluntary surveys of fund managers — firms self-report their assets under management to data providers like Preqin or PitchBook, which then aggregate the responses into an industry total. Voluntary surveys have an obvious blind spot: they only count managers who choose to participate, and participation correlates with size and sophistication in ways that can skew the total in either direction, undercounting firms too small or too private to bother responding, or overweighting the largest, most visible managers whose numbers get quoted and requoted until they harden into consensus. A manager raising its fifth flagship fund, with a marketing department and an investor-relations team built for exactly this purpose, answers a Preqin questionnaire promptly and completely. A smaller, regionally focused direct lender with three partners and no one whose job is reporting to data vendors may not answer it at all, and its loan book simply does not appear in the resulting total, not because anyone hid it but because nobody asked in a way that produced a response. Other estimates draw on aggregated fund disclosures — the filings that registered vehicles like business development companies make with the SEC, or the Form PF data that larger private fund advisers must submit. That data is more rigorous, because it is a regulatory filing rather than a marketing survey, but it only captures the subset of the industry organized into disclosing structures; a great deal of private credit activity happens inside vehicles that file nothing comparable. And still other estimates, like the Federal Reserve's $300 billion figure, come from an entirely different source altogether: bank call-report data, the standardized regulatory filings banks submit about their own loan books. That data is the most reliable of the three, in the narrow sense that a bank call report is audited, examined, and subject to real penalties for misstatement in a way that a voluntary industry survey is not. But it can only ever describe the banking system's side of the relationship. It says nothing about what happens to a dollar after it leaves a bank's balance sheet and enters a private credit fund's, because at that point the dollar has crossed into a part of the financial system that no single regulator is positioned to see whole. Three methods, three institutional vantage points, three numbers — and no referee sitting above all three with the authority, or the underlying data, to declare one of them correct and the others approximate.

Put those three sources of disagreement together and the spread between two trillion and one trillion three hundred billion stops looking like carelessness and starts looking like the predictable output of a market built, in significant part, out of exactly the kind of information asymmetry this book is investigating. Nobody is lying about the size of private credit. It is more precise, and more unsettling, to say that nobody is currently positioned to know the size of private credit with the kind of confidence regulators have about, say, the size of the U.S. Treasury market, or the deposit base of the banking system, because those markets clear through infrastructure — an exchange, a central clearinghouse, a standardized call-report regime — that was purpose-built to produce a single, reconcilable number. Private credit was not built that way. It grew, deal by deal, phone call by phone call, precisely because it operates outside that kind of infrastructure, and the price of that flexibility is that its own scale remains, to a meaningful degree, an estimate rather than a fact.

If the disagreement stopped at market size, it might be dismissed as an accounting curiosity — interesting to a policy wonk, irrelevant to anyone trying to understand whether the loans themselves are any good. It does not stop there. A comparative study published in February 2026 examined how three of the industry's most-cited benchmarks — MSCI's private credit index, PitchBook's, and Preqin's — actually construct the return and risk figures that get quoted, uncritically, as evidence of the asset class's performance. The three indexes do not select the same underlying funds; each draws its constituent list from a different subset of the managers who report to it, using different vintage-year and strategy cutoffs. They do not weight those funds the same way, so a large flagship vehicle from a top-five manager can dominate one index's return series while counting no more than any single mid-sized fund in another's. And they do not source valuations on the same schedule, which matters enormously in an asset class where every underlying position is itself marked internally by the manager rather than priced on an exchange — a mechanism this book takes up directly in the next chapter. A fund that reports its marks a full quarter behind another, folded into an index that does not adjust for the lag, will make the whole benchmark look smoother than the underlying loans actually were, simply because bad news has not yet worked its way into the number.

The study found that these methodological differences were large enough to produce meaningfully different pictures of private credit's headline selling point: its low volatility relative to public credit markets. An index that samples a different set of funds, on a different valuation lag, using a different weighting scheme, will report a different volatility number for what is nominally the same asset class over the same period — and volatility is not a cosmetic statistic here. It is the number private credit managers put on the first page of every pitch deck aimed at a pension consultant or, increasingly, at the committee that decides what goes into a target-date retirement fund's alternative-assets sleeve. A saver being told that private credit has delivered equity-like returns with bond-like volatility is being shown a specific index's version of that claim, built on specific choices about which funds count and how stale a mark is allowed to be before it stops representing the loan it is attached to. Change the index, and the volatility figure changes with it, even though the underlying loans in the real economy have not moved at all. If the index constructors — sophisticated firms with every incentive to get this right, competing on the credibility of their own data — cannot agree on how smooth the ride has actually been, the smoothness itself deserves a second look before anyone treats it as evidence of anything.

It would be a mistake, though, to read all of this as proof that something is being hidden on purpose, and the discipline of this chapter is to resist that reading even while taking the disagreement seriously. Every fast-growing, privately negotiated asset class in its early years has struggled to produce a number everyone trusts, and the struggle has generally reflected the newness of the market more than the dishonesty of the people in it. Hedge funds in the late 1990s and early 2000s were notoriously hard to size for exactly the same structural reasons: participation in industry surveys was voluntary, strategy definitions varied from one data provider to the next, and total industry assets under management were routinely quoted with hundred-billion-dollar spreads between competing estimates, long after the industry had grown large enough that regulators and the financial press assumed someone, somewhere, must have an authoritative count. Nobody did, for years. Fund managers themselves sometimes could not say with confidence how their own peers' leverage compared to theirs, because the disclosures that would have allowed the comparison simply did not exist yet. The asset-backed securities market told a similar story in its own early decades, before the Federal Reserve's flow-of-funds accounting had built out standardized categories fine enough to track structured products separately from the broader corporate bond market; analysts in the 1980s and early 1990s worked from patchwork estimates assembled by underwriters and trade groups, weighted toward whichever banks were most active in a given year, and those estimates disagreed with each other in ways that look, in retrospect, a great deal like the private credit numbers of today. In both cases, the measurement infrastructure eventually caught up with the market — but it is worth being precise about how. Hedge fund transparency did not improve gradually, on its own schedule, as the industry matured. It improved sharply after 1998, when Long-Term Capital Management's collapse forced the Federal Reserve Bank of New York to convene a private-sector bailout and made regulators, for the first time, take a hard, coordinated look at what the industry's aggregate leverage and counterparty exposure actually were. Asset-backed securities got their standardized accounting on a similarly reactive timeline, as the market grew large enough — and eventually troubled enough, well before 2008 made the point unmistakably — that regulators and trade groups had genuine incentive to build the categories that had been missing. Measurement disagreement, in other words, is often what a young, fast-growing, privately intermediated market looks like before the infrastructure to measure it exists. It is not, by itself, evidence that the underlying activity is fraudulent or even poorly run. But in neither precedent did that infrastructure arrive ahead of trouble. It arrived because of trouble, after investors, regulators, and in LTCM's case the Fed itself had already been surprised by how little the existing numbers had actually told them.

That counterargument deserves to be taken at full strength, because it complicates the book's own instinct here. If private credit's measurement problem is mainly a byproduct of youth and speed — the natural lag between a market's growth and the accounting infrastructure built to track it — then the appropriate response is patience, not alarm, and the numbers should be expected to converge over the coming years as regulators, index providers, and the industry's own disclosure practices mature, the way hedge fund sizing eventually did. That would be a real, falsifiable way for this chapter's worry to turn out overstated: if the IMF's global figure, the narrower U.S. direct-lending figure, and the index providers' return series visibly converge over the next several years as standardized reporting regimes take hold, the disagreement documented here will read, in hindsight, as an early-market growing pain rather than a structural feature of an opaque asset class. That is a fair test, and it is one worth watching rather than assuming the answer to in advance.

But the comparison cuts both ways, and it is worth sitting with the less comfortable half of it too. In neither precedent did better measurement arrive ahead of the trouble it eventually helped explain; it arrived because of the trouble, assembled afterward by regulators and trade groups who had just been surprised by how little the existing numbers had told them. Nobody gets to choose, in advance, whether private credit's own reckoning — if one comes — plays out the same way, with the infrastructure catching up gracefully as the industry professionalizes, or the disorderly way, with a genuine credit event forcing a harder look years after the disagreement was already visible to anyone reading the IMF, the Fed, and a data vendor's press release side by side. If that second pattern holds, then the current gap between two trillion, 1.3 trillion, and three hundred billion is not merely an accounting quirk to be outgrown quietly. It is closer to a warning label the market has, in effect, already placed on itself — a signal, visible right now to anyone who bothers to compare the numbers, that the infrastructure needed to see this market clearly does not yet exist, in an asset class that a growing number of ordinary retirement savers are now being asked to hold.

That is the finding this chapter wants to leave standing, without resolving it into a tidier conclusion than the evidence supports. A market of roughly private credit's scale — call it somewhere between one and two trillion dollars, because no more precise range would survive contact with the actual data — should not, by the standards applied to almost any other pool of capital that size, carry this much irreducible uncertainty about its own basic dimensions. The Treasury market does not have this problem. The U.S. banking system's total deposit base does not have this problem. Even the public high-yield bond market, private credit's closest peer in risk and yield, trades on exchanges that produce a daily, reconcilable price and a total outstanding figure nobody seriously disputes. Private credit disagrees with itself, at the level of hundreds of billions of dollars, not because the people producing these estimates are careless or dishonest, but because the market was built, deliberately, outside the mechanisms — public trading, standardized regulatory reporting, a central clearing function — that would otherwise force a single number into existence. The opacity that makes an individual loan hard to price and a fund's quarterly mark hard to verify is the same opacity that makes the aggregate market hard to size. It shows up first, and most measurably, at the top of the funnel, before anyone has even gotten to the harder question of what a single position inside that market is actually worth.

Which is, in the end, the question this chapter has been circling without quite reaching. If the IMF, the Federal Reserve, and the industry's own index providers cannot agree on how large the private credit market is in aggregate, that disagreement is really a symptom of something more fundamental: nobody outside a fund's own walls can fully verify what any single loan inside that fund is worth on a given day, because no daily market exists to check the manager's answer against. The aggregate confusion traced in this chapter is the shadow cast by millions of individual pricing decisions, made quarter after quarter, inside firms that answer, in the end, mostly to themselves. To understand where that confusion actually originates — how a loan that never trades gets a number attached to it at all, who chooses the assumptions behind that number, and what happens when the assumption turns out to be wrong — requires leaving the aggregate behind and going inside a single valuation. That is where the next chapter goes.
