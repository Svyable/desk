# The 401(k) Door Opens

On August 7, 2025, the White House released a two-page presidential document with a title that read less like a policy instrument than a campaign slogan: "Democratizing Access to Alternative Assets for 401(k) Investors." It did not, on its own, put a single dollar of private credit into a single retirement account. Executive orders of this kind rarely work by direct force; they work by pointing federal agencies at a target and telling them to clear the ground. This one told the Department of Labor, the Securities and Exchange Commission, and the Treasury Department to identify the regulatory barriers and, more pointedly, the litigation risk that had for years discouraged the people who run 401(k) plans from offering their participants anything other than publicly traded stocks, bonds, and the mutual funds built from them. The order did not create a new asset class. It aimed at the wall that had kept an existing one, mostly, on the institutional side of American finance for the better part of four decades.

The signing itself was a modest affair by White House standards — no joint session, no legislative fight to win, just a president's signature on a document that, unlike a statute, required no vote from anyone. Contemporaneous coverage treated it that way, largely: CNN Business's reporting on the order framed it primarily around what it might eventually let 401(k) plans offer rather than around any immediate change a worker would notice in her account the following Monday, and the coverage that followed in the days after the signing tracked a familiar split. Financial-industry trade press treated the order as validation of an argument asset managers had been making in Washington for years — that ordinary savers deserved the same access to private markets that institutions had enjoyed for decades. Retirement-security advocates, employee-benefits attorneys, and some consumer groups treated it with more caution, less because they doubted the illiquidity premium existed than because they doubted an executive order, on its own, could resolve the litigation exposure and valuation-transparency problems that had kept plan sponsors away from alternatives in the first place. Both reactions turned out to be reading the same two-page document correctly. The order was an instruction to go build something, not the something itself, and how much of the retirement system actually changed would depend entirely on what the agencies it instructed did next.

That wall was never a statute Congress passed to keep private equity or private credit out of ordinary retirement accounts. It was built, gradually and mostly indirectly, out of the accredited-investor rules that limit who may buy unregistered private securities in the first place, out of the practical mechanics of running a mutual-fund-like vehicle that must let a participant move money daily against assets that cannot be sold on any given day, and out of a body of ERISA fiduciary case law that made plan sponsors — the employers legally responsible for the prudence of what they offer their employees — genuinely afraid of being sued over anything unfamiliar. Pension funds, university endowments, and sovereign wealth funds had spent decades building the expertise, the legal staff, and the multi-year investment horizons that private markets require, and had captured, in exchange, a return premium that showed up reliably enough in institutional performance data that endowment consultants stopped treating it as exotic sometime around the early 2000s. A defined-contribution plan — a 401(k), the structure that has replaced the traditional pension as the primary retirement vehicle for most working Americans — was built for something else: a menu of liquid, transparent, easily priced options that an ordinary employee with no investment training could select from a dropdown list and that a plan administrator could value to the penny every business day. Private equity and private credit did not fit that design. For most of the 401(k) era, they simply were not there to choose.

The executive order's language about what counted as an "alternative asset" worth clearing the path for was deliberately capacious, and that breadth is worth sitting with before getting to the mechanics, because it tells you something about what the administration understood itself to be doing. The order did not confine itself to private equity, the category that had generated the most public debate up to that point, or even to private equity and private credit together, the pairing most naturally connected to the rest of this book's argument. It named real estate. It named infrastructure. It named commodities. It named digital assets and cryptocurrency funds, a category that had barely existed in any institutional retirement context a decade earlier and that carries a volatility and custody-risk profile almost nothing else on the list shares. It reached further still, toward what the order's drafters called emerging products, and specifically toward longevity risk-sharing pools — an arrangement, more common in some overseas retirement systems than in the United States, in which a group of retirees pool longevity risk so that the savings of those who die earlier subsidize the income of those who live longer, a genuinely different kind of financial instrument from anything else on the list, closer to insurance than to an investment fund. Reading the order's definition as a single category obscures more than it reveals. It grouped together a decades-old, extensively documented institutional asset class; a newer, faster-growing, harder-to-value one; a purely speculative digital instrument with no obvious connection to a retiree's actual spending needs; and an actuarial pooling structure that solves an entirely different problem. What unified them was not their risk profile or their suitability for a retirement saver. What unified them was that none of them, before this order, sat comfortably inside a standard 401(k) menu, and the order's job was to make all of them sit there more comfortably at once.

The follow-through arrived fast enough that it read, to the law firms whose entire client base sits inside America's retirement-plan infrastructure, as a coordinated rather than a coincidental sequence. Five days after the order was signed, on August 12, 2025, the Department of Labor formally rescinded a piece of guidance it had issued in December 2021 — a supplemental statement, in the bureaucratic language of the agency that wrote it, that had walked back an earlier and more permissive June 2020 information letter and had told plan fiduciaries, in effect, to proceed with real caution before including private equity investments inside a defined-contribution plan's core investment lineup. The 2021 guidance had not banned anything. ERISA does not work that way, and the Department of Labor's own information letters are advisory rather than binding. What the 2021 guidance did was narrower and, in practical terms, arguably more powerful: it observed that most retirement-plan fiduciaries — the human resources executives, benefits committees, and outside consultants who actually run a typical company's 401(k) plan — generally lack the specialized expertise needed to evaluate private equity investments and their unique risks properly, and it flagged the litigation exposure a fiduciary would face for offering something that complicated to a workforce that included people without the financial sophistication to evaluate it themselves. That sentence, more than any statute, functioned as the wall. A benefits consultant advising a mid-sized employer did not need a law to tell her private equity was off-limits. She needed only that one government document, sitting on the record, for her to tell a nervous general counsel that adding private equity to the plan's menu was an invitation to a lawsuit the company would probably lose even if the underlying investment performed fine. Law firms tracking the space — Seyfarth Shaw's client alert among them — flagged the 2021 guidance specifically as the single most consequential piece of paper the August 2025 rescission needed to clear before anything else in the executive order could matter in practice.

The order did not stop at rescinding one document and calling the job finished, and this is the detail easiest to lose in coverage that treated the signing itself as the whole story. It directed the Secretary of Labor to take further action on a defined timeline — action that included re-examining the Department's own fiduciary-duty guidance under ERISA Section 404, the statutory provision that sets the duty of prudence and loyalty every retirement-plan fiduciary owes to the participants whose money they are managing, with a target date of February 2026 for the Department to report back on what it had done. Section 404 is the actual legal spine of every fiduciary-liability lawsuit that has ever been filed over a 401(k) plan, and it has generated an enormous and increasingly aggressive body of case law over exactly the kind of decision an executive order cannot simply wave away: whether a specific investment option, offered to a specific workforce, at a specific fee, met the standard of a prudent expert acting solely in participants' interest. An executive order can instruct an agency to write new guidance. It cannot, by itself, rewrite forty years of accumulated ERISA litigation precedent, and it cannot make the plaintiffs' bar that has built an entire specialty around 401(k) excessive-fee and imprudent-investment suits — a specialty that has produced real settlements against real companies over ordinary index-fund fee disputes, let alone anything as unfamiliar as a private credit allocation — disappear because the White House said the word "democratizing." What the order could do, and what it explicitly directed the Department of Labor to work toward, was a form of regulatory safe harbor: clearer guidance about what a fiduciary must document and consider to meet the prudence standard when offering an alternative-asset option, of the kind that might give a benefits committee's outside counsel something firmer to stand on than the committee's own judgment call. As of this writing, that guidance is still being written. The February 2026 deadline named in the order describes when the Department was to act, not when the resulting rules, once issued, would be finalized, tested in court, or trusted by the risk-averse compliance officers who ultimately decide what actually appears on a plan's fund lineup.

The mechanism by which any of this could reach an ordinary worker's account is worth walking through slowly, because it is easy to imagine the wrong one. The natural mental image — an employee logging into a benefits portal, scrolling past index funds, and deliberately selecting a fund called something like "Ares Direct Lending" — describes something that could happen under the order's broadened permissions but almost certainly is not the main channel through which private-market exposure will actually spread through the retirement system. Most 401(k) participants do not build their own portfolio from a menu of options at all. They are auto-enrolled, by their employer, into whatever the plan designates as its qualified default investment alternative — nearly always, at this point, a target-date fund, the kind of product named for a rough retirement year (2045, 2050, 2060) that automatically shifts its mix of stocks and bonds from aggressive toward conservative as that date approaches. Target-date funds now hold the substantial majority of the money flowing into new 401(k) contributions in the United States, precisely because the overwhelming majority of participants never actively choose an allocation and never revisit the one they were defaulted into. That default status is not incidental to the private-markets question. It is the entire hinge the executive order's practical impact turns on.

Asset managers who build target-date funds — the same handful of firms that dominate the target-date business and, not coincidentally, increasingly own or partner with the same private credit and private equity franchises this book has spent its earlier chapters describing — do not need to convince an individual worker of anything to put a private-market sleeve into that worker's retirement account. They need only to convince the plan sponsor, and the target-date fund's own investment committee, to build a modest allocation to private credit or private equity into the glide path itself, the pre-set formula that governs how the fund's assets are split as the target date approaches. A participant enrolled in the plan's target-date default, who never logs in, never changes an election, and may not know the word "private credit" exists, would end up holding that sleeve automatically, the same way she already holds whatever stock and bond funds sit inside the target-date structure without ever having chosen them individually. This is the precise sense in which the order's real stakes run through default architecture rather than informed individual choice. A worker who actively decides to put five percent of her account into an illiquid, model-priced private credit fund has made an informed bet, whatever else is true about it. A worker who ends up holding the same exposure because her employer's target-date provider embedded it in the default glide path has made no such decision at all, and may not discover the allocation exists until a statement, a market downturn, or a liquidity problem forces the question.

That gap between chosen and defaulted exposure is exactly where the fiduciary-liability questions concentrate, and it is where the law firms advising plan sponsors have spent the most careful language since August 2025. Ogletree Deakins' client alert on the order was blunt about the core tension facing any benefits committee actually considering the move: the executive order and the DOL's rescission of the 2021 guidance reduce one specific kind of regulatory and reputational cover — the ability to point to the government's own prior skepticism as a reason to say no — but they do not reduce the underlying fiduciary duty itself. ERISA Section 404 still requires a fiduciary to act with the care, skill, and diligence of a prudent expert, evaluating each investment option on its own merits for the specific workforce being offered it, regardless of what the executive branch has said about alternative assets generally. A benefits committee that adds a private credit sleeve to its target-date default because the political and regulatory climate has shifted, without independently documenting why that sleeve is appropriate for a plan whose participants may need to withdraw funds on short notice, why its fees are reasonable relative to its expected net-of-fee return, and how its stated valuation and liquidity terms were actually vetted, has not satisfied Section 404 merely by citing an executive order. If anything, several of the alerts warned, the order's rescission of the DOL's earlier caution has the perverse effect of raising the bar for the fiduciary's own documented process, precisely because the "the government told us to be careful" defense that a cautious committee could once lean on is no longer available, and any future dispute over an underperforming or gated private-market sleeve will turn entirely on what the committee itself considered, in writing, at the time it made the decision.

The duty does not end at the moment of selection, either, which several of the law-firm alerts treated as the detail most likely to surprise plan sponsors moving quickly to capitalize on the new political climate. ERISA case law has been unusually consistent on this point for decades: a fiduciary who prudently selects an investment option has not thereby discharged its duty for the life of that option, but has taken on an ongoing obligation to monitor it, at reasonable intervals, against the same prudence standard that governed the original decision. For a publicly traded index fund, that monitoring is close to mechanical — a quarterly review of expense ratios and tracking error against a public benchmark. For a private credit sleeve carried at a manager's own quarterly mark, with no daily price against which to check it and no public benchmark tracking the same underlying loans, the monitoring duty becomes substantially harder to satisfy in a way a court would later recognize as adequate. A committee has to ask, and document that it asked, whether the valuation methodology behind the sleeve's reported return is sound, whether the fees layered on top of an already fee-heavy structure remain reasonable relative to what participants are actually receiving net of those fees, and whether the liquidity terms disclosed to the fund's institutional investors when it launched still match what a plan's own participants would need if a large number of them wanted out at once. None of those questions has an easy, standardized answer of the kind Section 404 case law has built up around ordinary mutual funds over forty years, and the absence of that established answer is precisely the gap the DOL's promised guidance is supposed to fill — assuming it arrives in a form specific enough to actually fill it, rather than simply repeating the general prudence standard in different words.

The liquidity mismatch sits underneath nearly every version of that concern, and it is a genuinely harder problem inside a 401(k) than inside the institutional world private credit grew up serving. A pension fund or an endowment can commit capital for a decade because its own liabilities are actuarially predictable and rarely require it to liquidate a specific position on short notice to meet an individual beneficiary's request. A 401(k) participant can take a loan against her account, request a hardship withdrawal, or roll the entire balance into an IRA the week after changing jobs, and federal law generally requires the plan to accommodate those requests within a defined window. Reconciling that participant-level liquidity requirement with an underlying asset — a direct loan to a mid-sized company, say, carried on a fund's books at a quarterly mark rather than a daily market price — that cannot itself be sold quickly without a discount is the exact structural puzzle this book's earlier chapters spent considerable time on inside evergreen and interval funds sold to accredited retail investors. Torys' analysis of the order, written with an eye toward how the secondaries market would need to evolve to absorb this new source of demand, made the mechanical version of the same point: extending private-market products into a vehicle that must remain liquid at the participant level, without the fund itself becoming genuinely liquid, requires either building semi-liquid wrapper structures robust enough to survive a real redemption run inside a much larger and more dispersed investor base than institutional private credit has ever served, or accepting that a 401(k)'s private-market sleeve will need its own gates, caps, and valuation buffers — the same tools, in other words, that Blackstone's BCRED and its peers have already needed, applied now to a saver whose entire retirement, rather than one slice of an institutional portfolio, may sit behind them.

None of this, on its own, settles whether extending this access was a mistake, and treating it as self-evidently one would be a worse error than the one this book exists to examine. The argument for the executive order, made seriously and at length by people who are not simply asset managers hunting for a new pool of fee-paying assets, deserves to be stated in its strongest form, because it is a real argument and not merely a cover story. The illiquidity premium is not an invention of the private credit industry's marketing departments. It is a documented, decades-old feature of how markets compensate investors willing to accept the inability to sell on demand, and pension funds, endowments, and wealthy individual investors who met the accredited-investor threshold have captured that premium for generations while an ordinary 401(k) participant, saving for the exact same multi-decade horizon and facing the exact same need for real returns above inflation, was shut out of it entirely — not because her investment horizon was any less suited to illiquid capital than a university endowment's, and in many cases a worker in her thirties has a longer runway before she needs the money than a pension fund managing near-term liabilities does, but purely because a regulatory classification system built around accredited-investor wealth thresholds had never been designed with defined-contribution retirement savers in mind. Freshfields' analysis of the order framed this as a genuine promise sitting alongside a genuine peril rather than picking a side, and that framing holds up under scrutiny: a worker locked out of an asset class for forty years for reasons that track her legal classification rather than her investment horizon or her capacity to benefit from patient capital has a real grievance, and closing that gap is a legitimate policy goal in its own right, distinct from and not reducible to the fact that the asset managers who stand to gain the most in fee revenue also happen to be its loudest advocates. Both things can be true, and the honest version of this chapter has to hold them at once: this is a policy change that could meaningfully close a real and long-standing access gap, and it is also a policy change engineered, lobbied for, and drafted with substantial input from the industry whose assets under management it stands to expand. Neither fact cancels the other.

It is worth resisting, with equal discipline, the opposite overstatement — the one that treats the executive order as though it had already transformed the American retirement system, because that is not what happened in August 2025 and it is not what has happened in the year since. The order removed barriers and directed further rulemaking; it did not mandate that a single plan sponsor add a single alternative-asset option to a single fund lineup, and plan sponsors, as a class, tend toward caution precisely because they are the ones who get sued when a decision goes badly. Kirkland & Ellis's April 2026 alert on the Department of Labor's proposed regulation — the rulemaking the executive order had set in motion, working its way through the process eight months past the February 2026 target the order itself had named — described a rule still in proposed form, still open to further comment and revision, still short of the final safe-harbor clarity that would give the most risk-averse benefits committees the cover they have said, consistently, that they are waiting for. Large plan sponsors with sophisticated investment staffs and outside fiduciary counsel are moving first, where they are moving at all, often through modest, capped allocations inside target-date structures rather than wholesale menu overhauls, while smaller and mid-sized employers — the ones without a dedicated benefits department, running plans through a third-party administrator on a standardized platform — are, by most accounts from the people advising them, waiting to see what the final rule actually says and what the first wave of litigation, inevitably, produces. A 401(k) door has been unlocked. Whether most Americans walk through it, and on what terms, and how quickly, remains an open question the rulemaking record had not yet answered as of this writing, and this book does not know the answer either.

What can be said with more confidence is where this leaves the reader who has followed the argument this far. Every earlier chapter of this book has described a piece of the same structure — model-based valuation standing in for a market price, gated redemptions standing in for daily liquidity, PIK interest quietly deferring the moment a loan's real condition becomes visible, insurance companies routing annuity assets into affiliated private credit funds, private ratings substituting for the scrutiny a public bond receives — and every one of those pieces, until 2025, operated substantially inside a world of institutional and accredited-investor money, people and organizations that had, at least nominally, agreed to the terms and had the staff to evaluate what they were agreeing to. The 401(k) door does not, by itself, guarantee that any particular worker's retirement account will end up holding a stake in the kind of loan this book has spent its case-study chapters examining. But if the rulemaking now underway produces the safe harbor plan sponsors are waiting for, and if even a modest share of the trillions of dollars sitting in target-date defaults ends up allocated, by design rather than by choice, into private-market sleeves built on exactly the valuation and liquidity mechanics described in the chapters before this one, then the question this book has been circling since Jamie Dimon's cockroach line stops being abstract. It stops being a question about institutional investors who signed up for the risk with open eyes, and becomes a question about a worker who never logged in, never chose, and may not learn what she is holding until the moment she most needs to know. Who, precisely, ends up bearing that risk, and how many layers of fees and intermediaries sit between her account statement and the actual underlying loan, is the question the next chapter answers.
