# The Regulators Who Can't See In

A bank examiner does not visit a bank the way an auditor visits a client once a year with a checklist and a departure date. At any large regulated bank, examiners from the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency or the Federal Reserve typically keep a standing presence on site — desks inside the building, badges that open the same doors employees use, a working relationship with credit officers that runs continuously rather than in scheduled bursts. They can pull an individual loan file and ask why it was underwritten the way it was. They can sit across from a bank's own risk committee and ask why a concentration limit was breached, or push on the assumptions inside a stress-test model the bank is required to run every year and required to justify line by line under the Federal Reserve's own capital-review process. The bank has to produce whatever the examiner asks for, on the examiner's timeline, because the alternative — a downgraded supervisory rating — touches almost everything the bank does afterward: how much capital it must hold, whether it can raise its dividend, whether a merger it wants clears review. None of this happens because a law specifically commands an examiner to sit at a particular desk; it happens because bank supervision in the United States was built, deliberately, after a long history of bank failures, around the idea that a regulator should be able to see the loan before it goes bad, not learn about it from a headline after the bank does. A bank examiner's job, at bottom, is to see in.

Nothing resembling that exists for a private credit fund. No examiner keeps a permanent desk at Ares or Blue Owl or the hundreds of smaller managers that have grown up around them, reviewing loan files as they are written, testing the assumptions behind a quarterly mark against the manager's own risk models in real time. A private fund adviser above a certain size registers with the Securities and Exchange Commission, and the SEC's examination staff can and does show up, periodically, to request documents and review a sample of the fund's practices against its own disclosures. That is meaningfully different from continuous, embedded supervision, and the difference is not an accident of enforcement priorities — it is close to the design. Private funds were built, historically, around the presumption that their investors were institutions and wealthy individuals sophisticated enough, and well-resourced enough, to negotiate their own protections directly with the manager, without needing a regulator standing over the manager's shoulder the way one stands over a bank's. That presumption is decades old. It predates a fund industry in this scale, predates the flow of ordinary savers' retirement money toward exactly these structures traced in earlier chapters, and it is the presumption the SEC tried, in 2023, to update.

On August 23, 2023, the SEC adopted a package of rules known collectively as the Private Fund Adviser Rules, on a three-to-two vote that split along the commission's usual partisan lines. The rules did not attempt to turn a private fund adviser into a bank subject to examiner-in-residence supervision; nothing proposed came close to that. What they attempted was narrower and, on paper, more modest: standardized disclosure. Advisers would have had to send investors a quarterly statement itemizing fees, expenses, and any compensation the adviser collected from the fund's portfolio companies, in a common format designed so an investor in one fund could actually compare it against a statement from a different fund managed by a different firm — a plain enough goal, and one that sounds almost bureaucratic until you notice how much of the fee-layering complexity described in earlier chapters survives specifically because no such common format currently exists. A second piece required every private fund to undergo an independent annual audit, closing a gap that had allowed some smaller or newer funds to avoid the kind of outside financial-statement review that public companies take for granted. A third piece targeted preferential treatment: the practice, well established across the industry, of larger or earlier investors negotiating a private side letter granting them rights — better redemption terms, more frequent portfolio information, advance notice before a redemption gate closes — that other investors in the same fund never see and never get to negotiate for themselves. The rule would not have banned side letters outright, but it would have required advisers to disclose certain preferential terms to all investors and prohibited a narrower category of preferential terms found to have a material negative effect on everyone else. A fourth piece addressed adviser-led secondary transactions — deals in which a manager offers investors the choice to cash out of an aging fund or roll into a new vehicle the same manager controls, a structure with an obvious conflict of interest built into its own mechanics — by requiring a fairness opinion or an independent valuation before the transaction could close. A fifth piece restricted a handful of specific practices the SEC's own examination staff had flagged repeatedly in the years leading up to the rule: charging a fund for services never actually performed, reducing an adviser's own clawback obligation by the amount of taxes it would owe on the clawback, allocating fund expenses on a non-pro-rata basis among investors without disclosure, and borrowing from the fund itself. None of it was radical. Much of it read, to anyone who had spent time inside the SEC's examination program, like a formal codification of practices examiners had already been complaining about informally for years.

It did not survive contact with the Fifth Circuit. Trade associations representing private fund managers — hedge funds, private equity firms, and the broader alternative-asset industry — challenged the rules almost immediately after adoption, filing in the Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals, a venue that had by then developed a reputation, deserved or not, as a favorable forum for parties seeking to overturn federal agency rules. On June 6, 2024, a three-judge panel ruled for the challengers and vacated the Private Fund Adviser Rules in their entirety — not narrowed, not sent back to the SEC for revision on a subset of provisions, but struck down whole, effective immediately. The law firms that spend their careers reading opinions like this one for a living — Dechert, White & Case, Debevoise & Plimpton, Morgan Lewis, and others who published client alerts within days — converged on the same basic reading of what had happened and why, even as some disagreed on the finer points of the reasoning. The court had not examined whether standardized fee disclosure was a good idea, or whether preferential side letters actually harmed the investors who did not get one, or whether an adviser-led secondary transaction genuinely needed a fairness opinion to be conducted honestly. It had examined a narrower and, in its own way, drier question: did Congress actually give the SEC the legal authority to write these specific rules under these specific statutory provisions?

The court's answer turned on two sections of the Investment Advisers Act of 1940, and it is worth walking through both plainly, because the distinction between what the court decided and what commentary about the decision sometimes implies it decided matters more than the technical language suggests. Section 211(h), added to the Advisers Act by the Dodd-Frank Act in 2010, authorizes the SEC to write rules governing the relationship between a private fund adviser and its "retail customers" — a term Congress defined to mean natural persons seeking financial advice primarily for personal, family, or household purposes. The Fifth Circuit read that language for what it says: private fund investors are, almost without exception, institutions or high-net-worth individuals who meet accreditation or qualified-purchaser standards specifically because Congress and the SEC have long treated them as not needing the same protections an ordinary retail customer needs. A pension fund allocating to a direct-lending vehicle is not a retail customer in the sense the statute uses the term, whatever it might functionally resemble by the time that pension fund's obligations reach an individual retiree decades later. If 211(h) authorizes rules protecting retail customers, and the investors these rules actually governed were not retail customers as Congress defined the phrase, the court held, then 211(h) could not be the source of authority for rules built around them. Section 206(4) is the SEC's broader, older rulemaking authority to define and prevent "fraudulent, deceptive, or manipulative" conduct by investment advisers — a real and frequently used power, the same authority underlying much of the SEC's general antifraud rulemaking. The court found that most of the Private Fund Adviser Rules were not, in any meaningful sense, aimed at fraud as that word has been understood in securities law. A rule requiring a common quarterly-statement format, or requiring an independent fairness opinion before an adviser-led secondary, is a governance and disclosure mandate; it does not require a showing that any adviser committed, attempted, or was likely to commit fraud, and the court held that 206(4) does not stretch far enough to authorize freestanding conduct regulation of that kind, however reasonable the conduct being regulated might be on its own terms. On both statutory hooks, in the court's reading, the SEC had reached past what Congress had actually given it.

That is worth stating as plainly as the case allows, because the distinction gets lost easily in casual retelling: the Fifth Circuit did not rule that fee opacity is fine, that side letters favoring large investors are harmless, or that adviser-led secondaries pose no real conflict of interest. It ruled that the SEC picked the wrong legal tools to address those problems — that Congress, when it wrote Section 211(h) to cover retail customers and left Section 206(4) tethered to fraud, did not hand the commission a general license to regulate private fund governance however it saw fit, no matter how sensible the resulting rule might be as policy. This is the kind of defeat that is genuinely easy to overstate in either direction. It is not proof that the underlying disclosure and conflict-of-interest problems the SEC identified were imaginary; a court ruling on statutory authority is not a factual finding about whether preferential side letters actually disadvantage the investors excluded from them, and nothing in the Fifth Circuit's opinion said they don't. But it is also not, whatever some of the rule's original supporters wished it had been, evidence that the SEC's diagnosis was wrong and got corrected by a court applying independent judgment to the merits. It was a ruling about who gets to decide, and through which door — a fight over institutional authority that happened to leave the substantive questions the rule was trying to answer exactly where they had been before the SEC ever voted on it, unresolved and now also unaddressed.

What the vacatur leaves behind is a specific and describable gap, not a total absence of oversight, and it is worth being precise about the shape of it rather than reaching for the easier, less accurate claim that private credit operates in a total regulatory void. No standardized quarterly fee-and-expense statement exists across the industry today; an investor comparing two funds is still comparing two documents built on each manager's own disclosure judgment, in whatever format that manager chooses, containing whatever level of itemized detail that manager decides is sufficient. No rule requires disclosure of preferential side-letter terms to the broader investor base; an early or especially large investor can still negotiate redemption rights or information access that the fund's other investors never learn exist. No rule requires an independent fairness opinion before an adviser-led secondary transaction closes, leaving that entire category of conflicted deal — a manager, in effect, selling a fund's assets to itself or to its own next vehicle — governed by whatever conflict-of-interest disclosure the adviser's own compliance program already produces, without an outside valuation check built into the rule itself. None of the vacated provisions ever took effect anywhere; the Fifth Circuit's ruling applied nationally, and the SEC did not attempt to salvage any severable piece of the rule package by appeal. A private fund adviser today operates under the same disclosure regime it operated under in July 2023, one month before the SEC ever voted.

That regime is real, and describing it honestly requires naming what it actually does provide. Investment advisers to private funds above certain size thresholds remain registered with the SEC under the Advisers Act, which was true before the Private Fund Adviser Rules and remains true after their vacatur; registration carries a fiduciary duty to clients and subjects the adviser to the SEC's general examination and enforcement authority, the same authority the commission uses across the entire registered-adviser population. The SEC's examination division publishes annual priorities that have repeatedly flagged private fund valuation practices, fee calculations, and conflicts of interest as areas warranting closer review, and examiners do, in fact, show up at private fund advisers and pull files — just not continuously, and not against a standardized template every adviser is required to follow in the same way. A separate reporting requirement, created by Dodd-Frank years before the vacated rule was ever proposed, requires the largest private fund advisers to file a confidential form with the SEC disclosing fund-level leverage, counterparty exposure, and other data used primarily to help the Financial Stability Oversight Council monitor systemic risk across the financial system as a whole. That filing is real, and it gives regulators a macro-level window that did not exist before Dodd-Frank. It is also not public, does not reach investors, does not standardize how any individual fund discloses its fees or its valuation methodology to the people actually invested in it, and was never designed to answer the questions the vacated rule was trying to answer. Weeks after the ruling, the SEC posted a short notice on its own website confirming, in the sparse and careful language a regulatory agency uses when it does not yet know what it wants to say next, that the Private Fund Adviser Rules were not in effect following the Fifth Circuit's decision and that the commission would consider its options going forward. It has not, as of this writing, proposed a replacement.

Put the pieces together and the honest description is this: no single American regulator today receives a comprehensive, standardized, continuously updated view of private credit fund leverage, valuation methodology, and investor terms comparable to what a bank examiner routinely sees inside a bank. The SEC sees pieces, unevenly, on its own examination schedule, through disclosure formats each adviser still largely controls. State insurance regulators see the slice of private credit that flows through affiliated insurers, described in an earlier chapter, through their own separate filing regime built for solvency purposes rather than credit-market surveillance. The Federal Reserve sees the exposure regulated banks carry to nonbank lenders through the lens of bank supervision, which tells a supervisor a great deal about the bank side of that relationship and comparatively little about what is happening inside the fund on the other end of a warehouse line. No one office holds all of it, in one format, on one clock, the way an OCC examiner holds a bank's loan book. The gap is not a conspiracy or an oversight anyone particularly intended; it is the residue of a regulatory architecture built around distinct institutions — banks, broker-dealers, insurers, registered investment companies — each supervised by a different agency using different tools, now applied to an industry that has grown large enough, and interconnected enough with all of those regulated sectors, that it no longer fits neatly inside any one of the boxes the architecture was built to hold.

That description should sound familiar to anyone who has read the pre-2008 accounts of how credit risk moved through the American financial system in the years before the crisis, because the structural shape of the gap, if not its specific mechanics, echoes an earlier one. In the run-up to 2008, large pools of credit risk moved through structured investment vehicles, off-balance-sheet conduits, and the short-term repo market — institutions and instruments that did not map cleanly onto any single regulator's jurisdiction, each overseen in part, none overseen in full, by agencies that were individually diligent and collectively unable to see the whole picture forming across their boundaries. No banking regulator was specifically charged with monitoring the aggregate size and interconnection of the shadow-banking system as a system; no securities regulator had comprehensive visibility into how much bank-like credit risk was accumulating outside the banks it was built to watch. That gap did not cause 2008 by itself — bad underwriting, excessive leverage, and a self-reinforcing collateral spiral did the actual damage — but the gap is a large part of why regulators were still debating the size of subprime exposure in the summer of 2007, months after the mortgage market had already begun cracking, and why the interconnections that turned a housing-market correction into a systemic panic were not fully understood by anyone until well after they had already done their damage. It is also why so much of the post-crisis reckoning came in the form of belated discovery rather than early warning: examiners and market participants alike spent much of 2008 and 2009 learning, position by position, exactly how far a single bad mortgage pool had traveled through conduits, off-balance-sheet vehicles, and counterparty chains that no one document or one agency's filings had ever laid out in full before the panic forced the mapping to happen in public, in real time, at enormous cost. The private credit gap examined in this chapter is not identical to that one; the specific mechanics of leverage, funding duration, and investor lock-up differ in ways the next chapter takes seriously and in detail. But the structural echo is not a stretch. It is the same underlying problem wearing a different decade's clothes: a form of credit intermediation that grew faster than the regulatory map built to see it, spread across institutions that no single supervisor has full authority over, valued and disclosed on terms the institutions holding the risk still substantially control.

None of that amounts to a claim that private credit today operates outside the law, and the book's own discipline requires resisting that overstatement as firmly as it resists the opposite one. Private fund advisers remain subject to the general antifraud provisions of the federal securities laws regardless of what happened to the vacated rule — Section 10(b) of the Securities Exchange Act and the SEC's own Rule 10b-5 did not go anywhere, and an adviser that lies to investors about a fund's valuation, its leverage, or its conflicts of interest remains as exposed to an SEC enforcement action and private securities litigation today as it was before August 2023. Registered advisers still owe their clients a fiduciary duty under the Advisers Act, still face periodic SEC examination, and still have to answer for material misrepresentations the same way they always did. The SEC itself, in the period since the vacatur, has continued to bring enforcement actions against private fund advisers for undisclosed fees, misallocated expenses, and valuation misconduct — using its existing antifraud authority rather than the vacated rule, because that authority was never the part of its toolkit the Fifth Circuit took away. And the door the court closed was narrower than a full prohibition on ever pursuing similar goals again. The Fifth Circuit's ruling turned on the specific statutory hooks the SEC chose in 2023, not on a categorical finding that no version of enhanced private fund disclosure could ever survive judicial review; a future rule built more carefully around authority Congress has actually granted — or a future Congress choosing to grant authority more explicitly than Dodd-Frank's retail-customer language did — remains, at least in principle, available. A court striking down one specific rule on the grounds that its authors exceeded their statutory mandate is a judgment about institutional process. It is not evidence that the goal those authors were chasing was mistaken, and it should not be read, by anyone inclined to treat the ruling as vindication for the industry it regulates, as though it were.

It is worth pausing on who, in practice, is left holding this particular gap, because the answer has shifted meaningfully since the SEC first proposed the rule in 2022. When the Private Fund Adviser Rules were drafted, the investors standing to benefit most directly from standardized fee disclosure and side-letter transparency were pension funds, endowments, and insurance companies — institutions with their own general counsel, their own due-diligence teams, and enough negotiating leverage to extract at least some of the same information a rule would have guaranteed everyone. They were not defenseless without the rule; they were simply inconvenienced by its absence, forced to negotiate individually for protections a standardized disclosure regime would have made automatic. The 401(k) door opening in the years since, traced in earlier chapters, changes who is actually exposed to this specific gap. An ordinary retirement saver defaulted into a target-date fund with a private-credit sleeve has no general counsel, no due-diligence team, and no side letter of her own; she has whatever disclosure her plan's underlying fund chooses to provide, filtered through whatever format the manager prefers, checked by whatever periodic SEC examination happens to land on that adviser in a given cycle. The vacated rule would not have solved that saver's whole problem — nothing in it addressed target-date fund construction or plan-sponsor fiduciary duty directly — but it would have guaranteed her a version of the same standardized statement institutional investors were already learning to demand for themselves. Its absence does not fall evenly. It falls hardest on precisely the investors this book has been following since the retirement account first entered the story, the ones least equipped to notice, on their own, what nobody is currently required to standardize for them.

What is left, when the legal question and the policy question are kept properly separate, is a visibility gap that nobody has actually closed, whatever its cause and whatever the eventual merits of the argument over how to close it. Earlier chapters traced that same gap from different directions — a valuation process a manager substantially controls, a ratings practice built for public markets straining to cover privately negotiated debt, an insurance channel where the entity valuing the risk and the entity bearing it for millions of policyholders now sit closer together than regulators are entirely comfortable with, a fee structure with enough intermediating layers that even a diligent saver struggles to trace where the money actually goes. This chapter adds the layer meant to catch all of the others: the regulatory apparatus itself, and what it can and cannot see when it looks. Put those five gaps side by side — valuation, ratings, insurance exposure, fee structure, and now regulatory oversight — and a single, unavoidable question follows the pattern to its logical end. The last time an under-examined form of credit intermediation grew this large, moved this much risk outside the institutions built to watch it, and depended this heavily on the assumption that nobody would need their money back all at once, was 2007. Whether that comparison holds up under real scrutiny, mechanism by mechanism rather than mood by mood, is the only question left before the book can say anything honest about what comes next. That is where the next chapter goes.
