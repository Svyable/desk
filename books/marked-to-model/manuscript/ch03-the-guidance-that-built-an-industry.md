# The Guidance That Built an Industry

On March 22, 2013, three federal banking agencies published nine pages in the Federal Register that most Americans never heard of and almost no one outside a bank's credit department ever read in full. The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, the Federal Reserve Board, and the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation jointly issued the Interagency Guidance on Leveraged Lending, an update to supervisory expectations that had first been written in 2001. It was not a law. Congress did not vote on it. It went through no formal notice-and-comment rulemaking of the kind that produces a rule a company can challenge in court before it takes effect. It was guidance — the word banking regulators use for the instructions they give examiners about what to flag, and the word banks use, with some bitterness, for the instructions that function like a rule without ever being voted into one.

What the guidance said was not exotic. It told banks that a leveraged loan — broadly, a loan to a company already carrying debt above a certain multiple of its earnings, usually one being acquired or recapitalized by a private equity sponsor — deserved a harder look than an ordinary corporate loan. It set out a marker regulators had been using informally for years and now put in writing: leverage beyond six times a borrower's total debt to EBITDA "raises concerns for most industries," a threshold that became, almost immediately, the single number every leveraged finance banker in the country learned to watch. It asked banks to stress-test leveraged portfolios against a downturn, the kind of exercise that had barely existed in any systematic form before 2008. It said a bank should not rely on the enterprise value of the borrower — essentially, what the company might fetch if sold — as the primary source of repayment, on the theory that enterprise value is itself a market judgment that can evaporate in a recession, which is exactly when a bank needs the loan to still be worth something. It wanted to see a credible path to repaying or materially reducing the debt within five to seven years, and it wanted weak covenants and aggressive repayment holidays treated as red flags rather than competitive features borrowers could shop for. None of this described a crisis. It described the kind of loan that had been getting written more and more often in the run-up to 2008, dressed in this instance not as mortgages but as buyout debt, and regulators writing in 2013 had recent enough memories of what happened when a banking system stopped asking those questions.

The guidance also did something more structural than set a single leverage number, which is part of why it proved so durable in practice even without the force of law behind it. It asked banks to evaluate leveraged lending as a portfolio, not loan by loan, meaning a bank could not simply justify each individual deal on its own facts and call the obligation satisfied; examiners wanted to see aggregate exposure tracked, limits set, and exceptions to the guidance's own benchmarks explained and approved by senior management rather than waved through by a single relationship banker eager to keep a private equity client happy. It asked banks to look hard at loans they originated with the intention of syndicating — selling down to institutional investors — and to hold themselves to the same underwriting discipline whether or not they planned to keep the loan on their own balance sheet, closing off the argument that a loan built to be resold did not need to meet the same standard as a loan a bank intended to hold. That portfolio-level, intent-agnostic framing is what made the guidance hard to engineer around from inside a bank. A single aggressive deal could be defended as an exception. A pattern of them, examined in aggregate and reported up through a bank's risk committee, was much harder to wave away, and it is that aggregate pressure — not any single rejected loan — that leveraged finance bankers spent the years after 2013 describing, with varying degrees of frustration, to anyone who would listen.

Banks did not love it, and in the years right after 2013 most of the complaints from the leveraged finance desks were the ordinary complaints any regulated industry makes about a new supervisory template: too vague, too subjective, too easily wielded by an examiner determined to find a violation regardless of the underlying credit. There was something to that. The guidance was written in a register that instructed rather than prohibited — banks "should" do this, examiners "will consider" that — and a document written to preserve examiner discretion is, by construction, a document a bank cannot fully game or fully satisfy in advance. In 2014, the Government Accountability Office weighed in on a narrower but pointed question: was this actually a "rule" for purposes of the Congressional Review Act, the law that lets Congress overturn agency rules by simple majority vote within a set window after they are issued? GAO said yes, it was a rule, which meant that in principle Congress could have voted to kill it. Congress did not. The guidance stayed in place, unreviewed by any court and unrepealed by any legislature, doing its work through the one channel that mattered most in a heavily supervised industry: examiner judgment, backed by the threat of a bank's overall regulatory rating.

That channel turned out to be more than enough. A bank subject to prudential supervision cares intensely about what an examiner thinks, because an examiner's judgment feeds directly into capital requirements, into the bank's ability to pay dividends and buy back stock, into merger approvals, into the entire apparatus by which a regulator can make a bank's life difficult without ever filing a formal enforcement action. When examiners began treating loans structured above the six-times leverage marker as something to be justified, documented, and in aggregate limited, banks responded exactly as a rational, heavily supervised institution should: they wrote fewer of those loans, or wrote them and sold them off the balance sheet faster, or structured them with tighter covenants that made them less useful to the private equity sponsors who wanted the loans in the first place. The economics of a leveraged buyout did not change. Private equity firms still needed debt to finance acquisitions, companies still needed capital to recapitalize or grow, and none of the underlying appetite for that lending went away. What changed was who was willing to supply it on the old terms.

The answer, increasingly, was not a bank. Asset managers who had spent the 2000s building private equity and mezzanine-debt franchises — Apollo, Ares, Blackstone, KKR, and a widening circle of firms behind them — were not subject to the 2013 guidance at all, because it applied only to institutions the OCC, the Fed, and the FDIC actually supervised. A nonbank direct lender raising a closed-end fund from pension plans, insurance companies, and endowments faced no examiner asking about its six-times leverage exposure, no capital charge tied to a loan's risk weighting, no supervisory rating riding on the aggregate volume of leveraged credit on its books. It could hold the loan it originated to maturity if it wanted to, rather than needing to distribute it into the syndicated market the way a bank arranger typically did, and it could write covenants closer to what the borrower and the private equity sponsor actually wanted. Direct lending funds had existed before 2013; the guidance did not invent the asset class. But it changed the relative cost of doing this kind of lending inside a bank versus outside one, at almost exactly the moment a decade of near-zero interest rates was sending institutional investors searching for any yield they could find above what Treasuries and investment-grade bonds were paying. Banks facing a specific new supervisory cost on a specific kind of loan met asset managers who had specific new investor demand for that same kind of loan and no comparable cost to bear. The lending did not disappear. It moved.

The scale of that migration is not a matter of dispute even if its precise size is — a separate argument for a separate chapter. Direct lending funds that managed well under a trillion dollars in the years just after the financial crisis were managing multiples of that by the mid-2020s. Apollo, Ares, Blackstone, and KKR built entire divisions, and in some cases entire firms-within-firms, around originating and holding exactly the kind of loan that had grown more expensive for a bank to keep. Middle-market and leveraged loan volume that would once have been syndicated through a bank arranger and sold down to institutional buyers in the broadly syndicated loan market increasingly went direct: one borrower, one fund, sometimes one phone call, no rating agency, no trading desk, no public price. Deals that in an earlier decade would have carried a public ticker on a leveraged loan index instead settled into a fund's own books, marked quarterly by the fund itself rather than priced daily by a market of buyers and sellers — a mechanical shift in how the debt gets valued that later chapters return to at length, because it turns out to matter as much as who is doing the lending.

It is worth saying plainly what this chapter is not claiming, because the temptation to overclaim here is real and the evidence does not support it: the 2013 guidance did not create private credit, and it was not the only reason lending moved. Basel III's capital rules, tightening independently of the leveraged lending guidance, made it more expensive for banks to hold any illiquid credit risk on balance sheet, not leveraged loans alone. Dodd-Frank's broader compliance and stress-testing apparatus raised the fixed cost of running a bank's lending business generally. Institutional investors wanted yield regardless of what regulators did to banks, and private equity sponsors wanted speed and certainty of close regardless of who was providing the debt — advantages the next chapter takes seriously on their own terms rather than as a byproduct of a regulatory accident. What the 2013 guidance did was narrower and more specific than "cause" the rise of private credit: it raised the supervisory cost of a bank holding exactly the loans direct lenders were best positioned, and increasingly eager, to hold instead. Multiple forces pushed in the same direction at the same time. The guidance was one of them, and it happened to be the one a regulator could someday admit to.

That admission came almost exactly twelve years later, and it came from the same two of the three agencies that had written the original guidance. On December 12, 2025, the OCC and the FDIC jointly rescinded the 2013 Interagency Guidance on Leveraged Lending, issuing a formal interagency statement — OCC Bulletin 2025-44 — that withdrew the supervisory framework in full. The rescission notice did not describe the 2013 guidance as merely outdated or superseded by newer frameworks, the usual bloodless language regulators use when they clean up old paper. It said the guidance had been "overly restrictive." Law firms that spend their careers parsing the fine distinctions between what a regulator says and what a regulator means read the same rationale and drew the same conclusion in their client memos within days: Sullivan & Cromwell's analysis and Mayer Brown's alert on the rescission both flagged the "overly restrictive" language as the agencies' own characterization of the guidance's actual effect, not merely its reputation among the banks it constrained. And in the trade press, the framing went further than "overly restrictive" alone. Banking Dive's reporting on the rescission connected it explicitly to the twelve years of market history that had unfolded since 2013, describing the guidance as a rule regulators now believed had contributed materially to the growth of private credit — the very industry that, by the time of the rescission, was drawing the kind of scrutiny this book's opening chapters describe, for reasons the regulators writing the guidance in 2013 could not have anticipated and were not trying to solve.

There is something genuinely rare in a regulator saying, in effect, that a rule it wrote worked too well at the thing it was designed to do — restrict a specific kind of bank risk-taking — and in doing so helped build an entire parallel industry operating with less supervision than the one it replaced. Regulators rescind guidance fairly often, usually because the guidance has been superseded, because the market it addressed has changed shape, or because a new administration wants to signal a different supervisory posture, all three of which were almost certainly also true here. What makes the December 2025 rescission worth pausing on is the specific admission embedded in its own stated rationale. The 2013 guidance was not repealed because leveraged lending inside banks had turned out to be safe after all — bank examiners were not, in 2025, discovering that six-times leverage loans held on bank balance sheets posed no risk. It was repealed, on the regulators' own telling, at least partly because the guidance had pushed so much of that same risk somewhere the OCC and the FDIC could no longer see it: outside the banking system, into funds these agencies do not supervise, priced by managers rather than by markets, disclosed to the investors in those funds on a schedule and in a format the funds themselves largely control. Twelve years after telling banks the leveraged loan market needed tighter discipline, two of the same three agencies were effectively conceding that the discipline had simply changed address, and asking whether that address was one anybody could actually see into. Whether rescinding the guidance now brings any of that lending back to a bank balance sheet where an examiner can once again ask questions about it, or whether it simply removes a supervisory marker from a market that has already reorganized itself around not needing one, is a question the rescission's architects did not fully answer and this book cannot answer for them. It is worth noting, too, that the rescission was not universally read as vindication rather than as a live policy dispute — arguments over the direction of bank supervision generally, and over which risks deserve more or less of it, do not resolve simply because two agencies issue a bulletin, and voices skeptical of the rescission were making the case, in the same period, that loosening bank leveraged lending standards now solves nothing about the trillion-plus dollars of loans that have already migrated to funds outside the banking system, while potentially reopening a second front of leveraged risk inside it.

The instinct to read the 2013-to-2025 arc as a clean morality tale — regulators overreach, an unintended consequence blooms into a trillion-dollar industry, regulators eventually confess the mistake — is the instinct this book has to resist, and the reason is not that the story is untrue but that it is incomplete in a way that would make a reader trust the rest of the book less if the incompleteness went unmentioned. The years after 2013 were also the years the American banking system became measurably, substantially safer than it had been in 2007. Basel III's capital requirements and Dodd-Frank's stress-testing regime, the Comprehensive Capital Analysis and Review process foremost among them, forced the largest banks to hold capital buffers far above pre-crisis levels and to demonstrate, under supervisory scrutiny every year, that they could absorb a severe recession without failing. Whatever else the leveraged lending guidance did, it was one strand inside a much larger post-2008 project of making sure that the specific kind of institution whose failure had triggered a global financial crisis — a highly leveraged bank holding risk it had underpriced — was less likely to fail the same way twice. That project largely worked, on its own narrow terms. The banks that pulled back from leveraged lending in the years after 2013 did not, as a rule, go on to fail; several of them are the same banks now writing about "cockroaches" in the market their retreat helped create. It is possible, and this book's argument requires holding both halves of it at once, for a regulatory response to a real problem to succeed at solving that problem and to also push a comparable risk into a less visible corner of the financial system, where different rules, or no comparable rules at all, now apply. The 2013 guidance did not fail. It succeeded, and the market adjusted to its success by routing around it.

Nor should the investor side of this story get lost underneath the regulatory one, because the guidance alone, however forceful, was pulling against a genuine and independent pull from the other direction. Pension funds, insurance companies, and endowments were not sitting on the sidelines in 2013 waiting for banks to become more restrictive before they discovered an appetite for private credit; they were actively searching for yield in a world where a decade of near-zero interest rates had made government bonds and investment-grade corporate debt pay next to nothing, and a direct loan to a mid-sized company, priced with a meaningful premium over what a comparable public bond might yield, looked like a rational answer to a real problem long-horizon investors actually had. Private equity sponsors, for their part, wanted something banks structured around a syndication process were not always well suited to provide quickly: a single lender who could commit to a large loan, negotiate its terms directly, and close in weeks rather than months, without the loan first needing to survive a rating agency's review and a syndication desk's judgment about what the broader market would buy. Speed and certainty of close are not regulatory artifacts. They are commercial advantages a single relationship lender can offer that a syndicated process generally cannot, and private equity sponsors were willing to pay for them well before 2013 and would very likely still be paying for them today even in a world where the interagency guidance had never been written. The 2013 guidance did not manufacture investor demand for yield or sponsor demand for speed. It arrived at a moment when both were already building, made bank-originated leveraged loans measurably more expensive to hold at the exact time an alternative supplier with fewer constraints was becoming large enough to meet both kinds of demand at scale, and the three forces reinforced each other in a way that makes it genuinely difficult, twelve years later, to say how much of private credit's growth belongs to any one of them.

There is a further complication worth sitting with before moving on, because it cuts against a version of this story that would be more satisfying than the true one. If the 2013 guidance really did push a large share of leveraged lending out of the banking system and into direct lending funds, the banking system's supervisors have had twelve years to watch what happened to the loans that stayed behind, and by most available measures those loans performed better, not worse, than the ones that left. Bank leveraged loan portfolios did not become a source of major losses in the stress episodes of the intervening years — not in 2020, when the pandemic produced the sharpest short-term economic contraction in decades, and not in the 2025 period this book otherwise spends considerable attention on. That is consistent with two different readings, and the evidence available to an outside observer does not cleanly settle which one is right. One reading is that the guidance worked exactly as designed: it kept the highest-risk leveraged loans out of insured depository institutions, where a loss ultimately touches deposit insurance and, in a severe enough case, taxpayers, and pushed that risk instead onto the balance sheets of funds whose investors — pension plans, insurers, endowments, and increasingly retail savers — had at least nominally agreed to bear it. The other reading is that the loans banks kept looked safer partly because the riskiest borrowers, and the riskiest structures, had already migrated to lenders willing to write looser terms, leaving banks with a self-selected, lower-risk remainder that would have looked good in any regulatory regime. Untangling those two readings from the outside, without access to the loan-level data both banks and direct lenders treat as proprietary, is close to impossible, and this book does not pretend otherwise.

What can be said with more confidence is narrower, and it is the claim this chapter has tried to earn rather than assert: a specific supervisory document, written by name, in March 2013, by three federal agencies now down to two who still exist to answer for it, measurably raised the cost of a specific kind of lending inside the regulated banking system, at a moment when demand for that same lending outside the regulated banking system was already growing for reasons of its own. Lending followed the lower cost, as lending generally does. Twelve years later, two of those same three agencies rescinded their own guidance and said, in language their own lawyers and the trade press covering them did not read as neutral, that it had been overly restrictive and that its restriction had helped build the industry now drawing the scrutiny this book exists to describe. Whether removing that restriction now pulls any meaningful volume of lending back inside a system regulators can supervise, or simply concedes that the lending it once tried to discipline had already found a permanent home outside that system, is not a question the rescission bulletin answers and not a question this chapter can resolve from where it sits in 2026. It is, at minimum, a rare kind of regulatory candor: an admission that a rule wrote itself into a market's history in a way its authors did not fully intend, arriving at a moment when private credit was large enough, and interconnected enough with the rest of the financial system, that the confession mattered well beyond the narrow supervisory question it was nominally about.

None of this, so far, has been an argument against direct lending as a financing tool, and it should not be read as one preemptively. A regulatory opening is not an indictment of what grew inside it. Companies that borrow from Apollo, Ares, Blackstone, KKR, and the dozens of funds that followed them are not, by that fact alone, worse off than companies that would have borrowed from a bank syndicate instead, and the lenders who built this industry did not do so by accident or by regulatory loophole alone — they built real underwriting capability, real relationships with private equity sponsors who return to them deal after deal, and a genuine case for why a borrower might prefer a single lender who can move fast and stay flexible over a syndicate that cannot. That case deserves to be made in full, by the people who believe it most, before this book turns to what can go wrong inside a market that prices its own risk. The next chapter makes it.
