# Two Ways to Lend

Picture a company that makes something unglamorous and necessary — specialty industrial coatings, the kind sprayed onto pipelines and offshore platforms to keep them from rusting apart. It has been private and profitably boring for three decades, run by a family and a bench of longtime executives, generating something like forty million dollars a year in earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization, the number lenders actually care about. Now the company wants to buy a smaller competitor, expand into a new region, and refinance debt that is coming due. Call the number it needs a hundred and fifty million dollars. The company does not have that sitting in a checking account. It has to borrow it, and the way it borrows it — which of three fundamentally different doors it walks through — will shape everything from the interest rate it pays to who finds out if the acquisition goes badly.

This is the choice sitting underneath almost every story in this book, and it rarely gets explained in plain language, because the people who live inside it every day stopped noticing it was strange a long time ago. So before going further, it is worth walking through the three doors slowly, the way a chief financial officer actually would, with a banker on one line and a private equity sponsor on another and a stack of term sheets on the desk.

The first door is the one that has existed the longest and feels the most familiar: a bank loan. The coatings company calls its relationship bank — say a large regional lender it has used for a decade — and asks to borrow the money as a term loan, alongside a smaller revolving credit line it can draw on for working capital. A team inside the bank underwrites the request. They pull the company's financial statements, model its cash flow under a few scenarios, check the value of the plant and equipment and receivables that would serve as collateral, and assign the loan an internal risk rating, the bank's own private verdict on how likely the borrower is to pay it back. If the loan is large enough, the bank may not want to hold the whole thing itself; it will syndicate pieces of it out to other banks and institutional investors, forming a group of lenders bound together by one set of loan documents, with the original bank acting as agent. Either way, once the loan is made, it sits on a balance sheet — the lending bank's, or the syndicate's collectively — as an asset.

There is a further split worth noting inside this first door, because it matters for where the story goes later. Large, well-known borrowers — companies with hundreds of millions or billions in revenue — typically borrow through what is called the broadly syndicated loan market, where a lead bank arranges the deal and then sells pieces of it down to dozens of institutional buyers, including mutual funds and collateralized loan obligations, creating something close to a secondary trading market even though the loan itself is not a security in the way a bond is. Smaller and mid-sized companies, closer to the coatings maker's size, usually cannot access that broad a syndicate. Their loans get done in what bankers call the middle market, with a handful of lenders, sometimes just one, holding the debt to maturity rather than trading it. This is the exact size of company, and the exact corner of the lending business, where direct lenders have made their deepest inroads — not by outcompeting JPMorgan for a Fortune 500 borrower's syndicated term loan, but by showing up for companies too small for that broad a syndicate and too illiquid to interest a public bond underwriter, offering a single check and a single relationship where banks increasingly offered neither.

That fact about the loan sitting, permanently, on someone's regulated balance sheet is the whole story of what a bank loan is, mechanically, and it is worth sitting with for a moment. A bank does not lend out shareholders' money in any simple sense. It lends against a pool of deposits — other people's checking and savings accounts — and against its own capital, the cushion of shareholder equity and reserves that exists precisely so the bank can absorb losses without failing outright. Regulators do not let a bank decide for itself how much of that cushion a given loan requires. Under the capital rules that grew out of Basel III and the post-2008 overhaul of American bank regulation, a loan to a company like the coatings maker — a leveraged borrower, meaning one carrying meaningful debt relative to its earnings — gets assigned a risk weight, and the bank has to hold capital against that risk-weighted amount, not just against the loan's face value. A safer loan costs the bank less capital to hold than a riskier one. That sounds abstract until you translate it into what it means for the bank's return: capital is expensive, shareholders expect a return on it, and a loan that ties up more capital has to earn more, or it drags down the bank's overall profitability. Chapter 3 goes further into how those rules actually developed and what they changed; for now, the relevant point is narrower. A bank loan is regulated capital, funded by deposits, examined by supervisors who show up periodically to look at the bank's loan files and ask hard questions about credit quality, and priced with that entire apparatus baked into the cost.

There is a second discipline layered on top of the capital rules, one examiners take seriously in its own right: the exam itself. A bank's loan portfolio gets reviewed, loan by loan in the larger cases, by supervisors from the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, the Federal Reserve, or the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation, depending on the bank's charter. Examiners can and do downgrade a bank's internal rating on a loan if they think the bank's own assessment is too rosy, which forces more capital against it or a write-down. A bank loan officer who wants to keep her job cannot simply believe a borrower's story indefinitely; eventually someone from outside the bank, with no stake in the relationship, looks at the same loan and renders an independent judgment. That is not a guarantee against bad loans — banks make plenty of them — but it is a second set of eyes that exists by law, on a schedule, whether the bank wants it or not.

The second door is a public bond. If the coatings company were larger, or further along in a strategy of tapping the capital markets directly, it could skip banks as principal lenders altogether and borrow from the investing public. It would hire an investment bank to underwrite the deal, produce a prospectus disclosing its financial condition and the risks of the business, and go on a roadshow pitching the bond to institutional investors — pension funds, insurance companies, mutual funds, hedge funds. One or more credit rating agencies, typically Moody's or S&P, would assign the bond a rating, a letter grade meant to compress a great deal of analysis into a signal the market can price off of. If the company's debt load and cash flow put it below investment grade, the bond would price as high-yield debt, what used to be called, more bluntly, junk — a name that has stuck around in market conversation even as the asset class it describes became a permanent and heavily traded part of the credit markets.

There is also a quieter mechanical difference worth flagging here, because it recurs throughout this book: most bank loans and direct loans are floating-rate instruments, priced as a spread over a short-term benchmark — today, the Secured Overnight Financing Rate, or SOFR, which replaced the old London interbank rate in the early 2020s — so the coatings company's interest payment rises and falls with the general level of interest rates. A public bond, by contrast, is far more often issued at a fixed rate, set once at issuance and unchanged until maturity regardless of what the Federal Reserve does afterward. That distinction sounds technical, but it means a floating-rate borrower's debt service can swing meaningfully within a single year for reasons that have nothing to do with how the business itself is performing, a detail that matters considerably once this book starts asking why some borrowers began falling behind.

What makes a public bond different from a bank loan has less to do with who is lending and more to do with what happens after the money changes hands. Once issued, the bond trades. Dealers make markets in it; its price moves every day, sometimes every hour, as new information arrives — a disappointing earnings report, a downgrade, a rumor, a shift in interest rates generally. That price is public. Anyone who wants to know what the market currently thinks the coatings company's debt is worth can look it up. The company itself has ongoing disclosure obligations, filing periodic financial statements that flow to every bondholder and to the public simultaneously, rather than to a single relationship lender in a private conversation. If the business deteriorates, the market usually knows before management would prefer it to, because bond prices are a running, involuntary confession. A rating agency downgrade, a bond trading down ten points in a week, a sudden widening in the spread over Treasury yields the market demands to hold the debt — these are all signals other lenders, employees, suppliers, and the company's own board can see in something close to real time. Nobody has to ask permission to look.

The third door is newer, faster-growing, and considerably less visible, and it is the one this book spends most of its pages inside. Instead of a bank or a bond market, the coatings company — or, more often in practice, the private equity firm that owns or is about to own it — goes directly to a nonbank lender: a private credit fund managed by a firm like Ares, Blue Owl, Apollo, Blackstone's credit arm, KKR, or HPS Investment Partners, among dozens of others that have built enormous lending businesses over the past decade and a half. There is no syndicate of a dozen banks and no public offering. A single fund, or a small club of two or three funds working together, negotiates the entire loan directly with the borrower, often in a matter of weeks rather than the months a syndicated bank deal or a bond issuance can take. The terms are private. Nobody outside the room — no regulator conducting a routine exam, no rating agency publishing a public grade, no retail investor watching a bond price on a screen — automatically sees what was agreed to, unless the fund or the borrower chooses to disclose it.

The instrument that has come to define this market is called a unitranche loan, and the name describes the idea well enough once it is unpacked. In a traditional bank financing, a company's debt is often split into layers — a senior secured tranche that gets paid first if things go wrong, and a subordinated or mezzanine tranche beneath it that gets paid only after the senior lenders are made whole, and that therefore charges a higher interest rate to compensate for the extra risk. Structuring and negotiating between those layers, particularly when a company is in distress and the senior and junior lenders start disagreeing about strategy, can be slow and adversarial. A unitranche loan collapses that structure into a single blended tranche, one lender, one interest rate, one set of documents, priced somewhere between what a pure senior loan and a pure subordinated loan would cost separately. For the borrower, this trades a slightly higher blended rate than the cheapest senior debt alone for enormous simplicity: one phone call to renegotiate a covenant instead of a room full of lenders with different priorities, one signature to get an amendment done, one relationship to manage through a downturn instead of several. For the private credit fund, it means holding the whole capital structure itself and pricing the entire risk, rather than splitting it with other lenders whose incentives may not match its own.

Where does the money inside these funds actually come from, and where does it sit once it is lent out? A meaningful share of it flows through a specific legal structure called a business development company, or BDC — an investment vehicle created by Congress in 1980 specifically to channel capital into growing and middle-market businesses that might struggle to access the public markets. A BDC raises capital from investors, sometimes institutions, increasingly in recent years from wealthy individuals and, as later chapters trace, retail savers through their retirement accounts. It then deploys that capital into loans like the coatings company's unitranche facility, collecting interest income and passing most of it through to its own shareholders, much like a real estate investment trust passes through rental income. Some BDCs are publicly traded and their shares do trade on an exchange with a visible market price — but that price reflects the market's view of the fund as a whole, its management, its overall portfolio quality, and general sentiment toward the sector, not a distinct, observable price for any single loan sitting inside it, the way a bond's price reflects that one company's individual credit standing. Many of the largest BDCs raised in the past decade are non-traded or only periodically traded, meaning an investor's stake is valued not by a market but by the fund itself, using its own internal models, updated once a quarter. What a loan inside a BDC is officially worth, on any given day, is essentially what the fund managing it says it is worth. That fact — a manager marking its own homework, however carefully and however governed by valuation committees and outside auditors — is the hinge this entire book turns on, and later chapters return to it at length. For now, the point is simpler: a private direct loan generates no independent, continuously updated price the way a bond does, and it sits outside the specific supervisory examination regime a bank loan sits inside. Nobody without access to the fund's own books can check its own math in real time.

BDCs are not entirely unregulated, and it would be a mistake to suggest otherwise. They are registered under the Investment Company Act of 1940, the same statute that governs mutual funds, and they answer to the Securities and Exchange Commission on questions of disclosure, custody, and governance. But the leverage rule specific to BDCs is worth understanding, because leverage is what turns an ordinary bad year into a crisis. For decades, a BDC had to maintain at least two dollars of assets for every dollar of debt it carried — an asset-coverage ratio of 200 percent, in the statute's language, limiting it to roughly a dollar of borrowing for every dollar shareholders had put in. Congress loosened that limit in 2018, allowing BDCs that met certain conditions to run at 150 percent asset coverage instead, roughly two dollars of debt for every dollar of equity if a fund chooses to use the full allowance. That is real leverage, layered on top of the leverage already sitting inside each portfolio company's own loan, and it means a decline in the value of the underlying loans hits a BDC's shareholders harder than it would a fund with no leverage at all. It is nowhere near the leverage that sat inside the mortgage-backed structures of 2008, a comparison later chapters take up directly and try to handle honestly rather than for effect — but it is not zero, either, and a reader should hold onto the number.

It is worth being honest about how large this third channel has already become, and how fast, because the scale is part of what makes the question of who is checking anyone's work worth asking at all. In December 2024, BlackRock — the world's largest asset manager, a firm that built its reputation managing index funds and public securities — announced it was acquiring HPS Investment Partners, one of the biggest direct lenders in the business, in a deal valued near twelve billion dollars. The acquisition closed the following July, and BlackRock described the combined private credit franchise it had assembled as worth somewhere between one hundred ninety and two hundred twenty billion dollars in assets. A single deal, done by a firm not previously known as a direct lender at all, created a private credit operation roughly the size of a large regional bank's entire loan book, structured almost entirely outside the banking system. That is not an isolated case; it is a signal of where the largest pools of institutional capital in the world have decided the growth is. Whatever else is true about private credit, it is no longer a boutique corner of finance that a curious reader can safely ignore.

None of this means the third door is a worse one to walk through, and it is important to say that plainly before this book spends the rest of its length worrying about what the third door leaves unchecked. Speed is a genuine advantage, not a cosmetic one: a private equity sponsor racing to close an acquisition on a tight timeline, or a company that needs to refinance quietly without spooking customers or competitors by filing a public prospectus, has real reasons to prefer a single negotiated conversation over months of syndication or a public roadshow. Confidentiality is not inherently sinister; a family-owned business, or a company navigating a sensitive restructuring, may have entirely legitimate reasons not to want its financial details broadcast to the bond market and every analyst covering it. Direct lenders can also write covenants — the contractual promises a borrower makes about maintaining certain financial ratios — that are tighter and more frequently tested than what public bond markets tend to accept today, where "covenant-lite" structures, deals with few or no maintenance tests between the loan's issuance and its maturity, became increasingly common in the years before this book was written. A direct lender checking a company's numbers every quarter against a maintenance covenant can, in principle, catch trouble earlier than a bondholder who finds out only when the company misses a payment or the rating agency finally acts. Picture what that monitoring actually looks like for the coatings company under a unitranche loan. Every quarter, its finance team sends the fund a compliance certificate: current EBITDA, current debt, the leverage ratio those two numbers produce, and confirmation that the ratio still sits below whatever ceiling the loan documents set. If a new environmental liability or a lost customer pushes the ratio close to that ceiling, the fund's deal team knows within weeks, not whenever the company next happens to file something public. What happens next is itself a choice with no single right answer. The fund can grant a waiver, renegotiate the covenant, or tighten the company's reporting further — all privately, all without a rating agency downgrade or a public bond price signaling distress to the world before management has decided what to say about it. A public bondholder in the same situation typically finds out only when the numbers are already filed, sometimes months after the trend began. Whether early, private knowledge produces better discipline or simply better-hidden problems is a fair question, and not one this chapter can settle looking only at how the mechanism is supposed to work. None of the three doors has a monopoly on either virtue or danger, and a fair accounting has to hold that thought alongside everything that follows.

It is also worth resisting the temptation to think of these three channels as competitors locked in a permanent contest for the same borrower, because in practice a single company moves between all three over the course of its life, sometimes within the same year. The coatings company might keep a modest revolving credit line with its relationship bank for everyday working capital — payroll, inventory, the ordinary cash-flow bumps of a manufacturing business — precisely because a bank facility is cheap, flexible for small draws, and does not require renegotiating a large loan agreement every time cash is tight for a month. If it grows large enough and its ownership wants permanent, tradeable capital, it might eventually issue a public bond, accepting the disclosure obligations in exchange for a broader base of lenders and, often, a lower headline interest rate than what a fund would charge for the same risk. And if a private equity firm buys the company in a leveraged transaction and wants a loan closed in three weeks with one lender to negotiate rather than a dozen, that same company might end up exactly where this chapter began, with a unitranche loan from a direct lender. These are not, for most companies, mutually exclusive identities — investment-grade bond issuer, bank-loan borrower, private-credit borrower — so much as different tools reached for at different moments, depending on size, urgency, ownership structure, and what the company most needs to keep private or make public in that particular year. Run the coatings company forward a few more years, in fact, and it might touch all three doors in a single decade: the bank revolver for everyday liquidity, sitting quietly in the background the whole time; a public bond a few years later, once the family sells a stake to outside investors and wants the lower cost and broader base that comes with public disclosure; and then, if a private equity firm eventually buys the company outright and loads it with acquisition debt, a unitranche loan from a direct lender who can close in weeks and stay on the phone with management every quarter afterward. None of those decisions would look inconsistent to a banker who has spent a career in the middle market. They would look like ordinary corporate finance, adjusted to whatever the company most needed to move fast, stay private, or minimize cost at that particular moment. A reader who comes away from this chapter believing direct lending is simply the shady cousin of bank and bond finance has misunderstood it. The honest version is closer to three different sets of trade-offs, coexisting, each one right for some borrower on some Tuesday.

What should trouble a careful reader is not that this third channel exists, but how quickly it has grown relative to the other two, and in particular how much of that growth traces back not to some obvious advantage borrowers discovered on their own, but to what changed on the other side of the table — inside the banks. Federal Reserve researchers who have studied private credit's expansion describe its growth as tightly bound up with the tightening of bank capital requirements and lending standards that followed the 2008 financial crisis, alongside the low-interest-rate years that sent institutional investors searching for yield wherever they could find it. Basel III made holding a leveraged loan more expensive in regulatory capital than it used to be. Dodd-Frank layered new supervisory expectations on top of that. A 2013 piece of interagency guidance, which the next chapter takes up in detail, told banks in fairly specific terms what kinds of highly leveraged loans supervisors would frown on. Each of those changes, on its own, sounds like a reasonable response to a financial system that had just nearly destroyed itself. Taken together, they changed the price a bank had to charge to make the exact kind of loan the coatings company needed, at exactly the moment a large and growing pool of institutional capital — pension funds, insurers, sovereign wealth funds, and eventually individual investors — was hunting for higher-yielding places to put money that public bond markets, with interest rates pinned near zero for much of the 2010s, could not offer.

So the three doors are not, in the end, equally weighted choices a borrower simply picks among on their individual merits every time, the way this chapter has mostly described them so far. Something on the supply side moved first, and borrowers and lenders both responded to it. If direct lending were simply a superior mousetrap that borrowers had always wanted and finally got, its growth would be a clean story about innovation meeting demand. But if a meaningful share of that growth is a bank loan wearing a different name because it became too expensive, in regulatory terms, for a bank to make the loan itself and hold it, then the interesting question is not just what a unitranche loan is — this chapter's job — but why, in barely more than a decade, so much lending that used to happen inside an examined, capital-regulated bank now happens instead inside a fund that marks its own book. That is a history, not just a mechanism, and it starts with a set of pages very few people outside a handful of Washington regulators ever read: the guidance that, more than any single document, built the industry this book is about.
