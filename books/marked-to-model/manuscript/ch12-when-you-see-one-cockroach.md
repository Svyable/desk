# When You See One Cockroach

Jamie Dimon did not say, on JPMorgan's October 14, 2025 third-quarter earnings call, that private credit was about to collapse. He said something narrower and, in the way of a good line delivered by a careful man, more durable: "when you see one cockroach, there are probably more." He said it after JPMorgan disclosed a $170 million write-off tied to Tricolor Holdings, the Dallas subprime auto lender whose executives federal prosecutors would go on to charge with running a scheme that double-pledged roughly $800 million in loan collateral, and after First Brands Group's own Chapter 11 filing two weeks earlier had already put a number near $10 billion in liabilities and a set of allegations about receivables pledged to more than one lender at once into the same news cycle. Two borrowers, two collapses, two sets of lenders caught holding collateral that was not what it appeared to be. By the standard the rest of this book has tried to hold to, both of those stories are, so far, borrower-level failures — one an alleged fraud on an industrial supply company's balance sheet, the other an alleged fraud charged criminally against a subprime lender's own executives. Neither, by itself, proves that private credit as an asset class is broken. Dimon's line was never really about First Brands and Tricolor specifically. It was a claim about correlation — that a visible failure in a system usually implies invisible failures nearby, related by whatever made the first one possible — and the honest way to test that claim is not to go looking for a third insect. It is to ask how the exterminator's own building is wired to the one where the roach turned up.

That is the question this chapter takes seriously, and it is a duller, more structural question than the one the financial press spent late 2025 asking. The interesting story about First Brands and Tricolor was never really whether they would turn out to be isolated — Bloomberg's reporting in the weeks after the First Brands filing already described a market where confidence was fraying well beyond the specific lenders holding First Brands paper, price marks moving on loans that had nothing to do with either bankruptcy, simply because the market had been reminded that a "low volatility" number could hide an ordinary company failing in an ordinary way. The more useful question, and the one banking regulators and international standard-setters spent 2025 and 2026 actually trying to answer, is mechanical: when a private credit borrower fails, what is the path, if any, by which that failure reaches an institution the public actually depends on — a bank holding insured deposits, backstopped by the same government whose taxpayers would be asked to absorb the cost if enough of those institutions failed at once? First Brands and Tricolor answer a version of that question directly, because in both cases the answer was yes: JPMorgan and Fifth Third Bank had real, disclosed exposure to Tricolor through warehouse lending, and a cluster of banks including Jefferies and UBS had real, disclosed exposure to First Brands through direct lending and financing arrangements that turned out to rest on collateral claims weaker than anyone had priced. But those are two loans, at two banks, to two borrowers who happened to be defrauding somebody. The question this chapter asks is what the wiring looks like underneath — not whether a bank ever lends money to a company that later turns out to be a fraud, which will always happen occasionally in any lending system, but how exposed banks are to the private credit industry as a whole, including the enormous majority of it that has done nothing wrong.

Start with the plumbing, because the word "exposure" does a lot of unexamined work in coverage of this subject and it is worth being precise about what it actually describes. A bank can be exposed to private credit in a way that has nothing to do with lending to the underlying borrower at all. When a direct-lending fund is raised — say, a vehicle that will eventually hold hundreds of individual loans to mid-sized companies — its investors do not hand over the entire committed amount on day one. They commit capital that the fund calls down over time, as deals close, and in the interval a fund frequently borrows against those uncalled commitments from a bank, a structure called a subscription line or capital-call facility. The fund draws on the line to close a deal quickly, calls capital from its investors on its own schedule weeks or months later, and repays the bank. It is a short-term loan secured not by any company the fund has lent to, but by the contractual promises of pension funds, insurers, and endowments to eventually send money when asked. Separately, and more directly relevant to the mechanics behind Tricolor's own business, a warehouse facility is a line of credit a bank extends to a vehicle that is accumulating loans — auto loans, in Tricolor's case, but the same structure recurs across consumer and commercial asset-based lending generally — before those loans are bundled, securitized, or transferred into a permanent fund. The bank is lending against a pool of assets that is still being assembled, valued in real time against collateral the bank itself has limited ability to verify loan by loan, which is precisely the vulnerability Tricolor's alleged double-pledging is reported to have exploited. And increasingly, banks lend directly against the net asset value of an entire private credit fund's portfolio — a NAV line — a newer and more contested structure that lets a fund borrow against the appraised value of loans it already holds rather than against any single asset. In none of these three structures does the bank need to have ever heard of the individual company whose loan eventually goes bad. The bank's counterparty is the fund, the warehouse vehicle, or the investor commitment behind it, and on the bank's own books the exposure typically shows up looking like ordinary secured commercial lending — a line extended to a well-capitalized financial counterparty, collateralized, revolving, renewed annually — rather than under any label that would tell an outside observer, or for that matter a bank examiner working from decades-old categories, that its ultimate performance depends on the health of loans to mid-market companies the bank itself never underwrote. The credit risk of the underlying borrower still reaches the bank's balance sheet; it simply arrives by a different door than the one the bank examiner has traditionally been trained to watch.

How much money moves through that door is the specific question the Federal Reserve tried to answer, and its own researchers' honest response is a useful corrective to any reader expecting a clean number. In a FEDS Notes research paper published in 2025, Federal Reserve Board staff estimated that US banks had extended roughly $300 billion in lending to private credit providers as of June 2025 — a figure built from the categories described above: direct loans and credit lines to business development companies and direct-lending funds, subscription-line and capital-call facilities, warehouse lending, and NAV-based facilities, aggregated across the banking system as far as existing regulatory data collection allows. The authors were careful about what that number does and does not capture, and the caveats matter as much as the total. Bank regulatory reporting was not designed with private credit in mind; a loan to a fund that itself lends to companies does not always get flagged in a way that separates it cleanly from an ordinary commercial loan, and the true figure could plausibly run higher than what existing call-report categories manage to isolate. Three hundred billion dollars is, by the scale of the American banking system, a real but not overwhelming number — smaller than total commercial and industrial lending outstanding, concentrated disproportionately at the largest banks with the balance sheet and the relationships to serve as private credit's primary financiers, and growing, according to the same research, at a pace that had outstripped growth in many other categories of bank lending over the preceding several years. The point of citing it is not that $300 billion by itself threatens the solvency of the US banking system; measured against total bank assets, it plainly does not. The point is narrower and, in its way, more useful: it is a rough, regulator-produced answer to a question that a year earlier had no official answer at all, and it establishes that the relationship between banks and private credit is not incidental. It is a real and growing line of business, sitting on bank balance sheets under labels that do not always say "private credit" on them.

What that $300 billion figure makes concrete is the mechanism Dimon's cockroach line gestures at without naming: a bank does not need to underwrite a loan to First Brands or Tricolor to be exposed to the conditions that produced First Brands and Tricolor. If a bank has extended a subscription line to a fund whose portfolio companies are quietly deteriorating — the kind of deterioration Chapter 9 of this book traced through rising payment-in-kind interest rather than outright default — the bank's collateral is the fund's uncalled capital commitments, which are only as good as the willingness and ability of the fund's own investors to honor them under stress. If a bank has extended a warehouse facility to a vehicle accumulating loans before securitization, the bank is exposed to exactly the collateral-verification risk that Tricolor's alleged scheme is reported to have exploited, at scale, across whichever other warehouse arrangements share its structural weaknesses rather than its specific fraud. None of this requires a second Tricolor or a second First Brands to matter. It requires only that the private credit funds a bank has lent to, through these indirect channels, experience the kind of correlated stress that a credit cycle eventually produces in any lending market — and that stress, transmitted through a subscription line or a warehouse facility, reaches the bank's income statement in a form that a casual reading of its loan book, organized around the century-old categories of commercial, industrial, and consumer lending, would not obviously flag as "private credit risk" at all.

The International Monetary Fund had been describing pieces of this picture for longer than the Fed's specific dollar estimate existed. As early as April 2024, IMF researchers had flagged the private credit market — then sized, on the broadest definition, at close to $2 trillion globally — as warranting "closer watch," language deliberately short of alarm but unmistakably a statement that the fund's own surveillance staff considered the sector's growth to have outpaced the visibility regulators had into it. By its October 2025 Global Financial Stability Report, published in the same month as the JPMorgan earnings call that produced Dimon's quotation, the IMF's analysis had sharpened into a more specific set of channels rather than a general caution. The report's authors pointed to valuation opacity — the same marked-to-model problem this book examined in its own title chapter, here read as a financial-stability question rather than an investor-protection one, on the theory that a valuation that lags reality does not just mislead the fund's own investors, it delays the moment at which counterparties across the system, banks included, recognize that risk has actually changed. They pointed to concentration, noting that a relatively small number of large managers account for a disproportionate and still-growing share of private credit assets, which means that a problem specific to one major manager's underwriting culture, risk appetite, or valuation practice does not stay contained to that manager the way it might in a market with thousands of small, uncorrelated lenders. They pointed to leverage, both at the fund level and embedded in the loans funds make to already-leveraged borrowers. And they pointed, most directly relevant to this chapter, to growing interconnection between banks and nonbank financial institutions — the IMF's own term of art for the funds, insurers, and other entities that sit outside prudential bank supervision — describing private credit funds as increasingly acting not merely as borrowers from banks but as market-makers and liquidity providers in their own right, stepping into roles that had historically belonged to banks and broker-dealers, in ways that blur a boundary regulators had spent decades treating as reasonably clean.

The Financial Stability Board's Report on Vulnerabilities in Private Credit, published in May 2026 and produced by the international body whose membership spans the central banks, finance ministries, and supervisory authorities of the world's major economies, arrived at substantially the same list of channels, with more granular attention to how they interact. The FSB's report described leverage, concentration, and interconnection as related rather than separate vulnerabilities: a concentrated market in which a handful of large managers account for a large share of both fund assets and bank counterparty exposure is a market in which a shock to one firm's underwriting or liquidity management can propagate through the banking system's warehouse and subscription-line exposure to that firm specifically, rather than diffusing harmlessly across an anonymous, fragmented sector. The report also picked up the IMF's observation about private credit funds acting as liquidity providers — noting, in the FSB's characteristically hedged institutional language, that as these funds grow large enough to provide financing that resembles what banks and broker-dealers historically supplied to other parts of the financial system, including to each other, the lines separating "bank" and "nonbank" risk become correspondingly harder for any single supervisor to draw, and that this interconnection is a channel meriting continued monitoring rather than a settled description of a problem already understood. That phrase, or something close to it, recurs across nearly every page of both the IMF and FSB documents, and it is worth taking at face value rather than reading past it for something more dramatic. These are not documents predicting a crisis on a timeline. They are documents produced by institutions whose entire professional culture rewards precision and penalizes overstatement, cataloguing channels through which stress could travel if it arrived, without claiming to know whether or when it will.

Concentration is worth dwelling on longer than a single clause in a multilateral report usually gets, because it is the channel that turns an abstract warning about "interconnection" into something closer to an addressable list. The direct-lending industry is not, at this point, a diffuse collection of anonymous funds; it is dominated by a short roster of firms whose names recur across this book because they recur across the market itself — Apollo, Ares, Blackstone, KKR, Blue Owl, and, since BlackRock's roughly $12 billion acquisition of HPS Investment Partners closed in July 2025, a combined BlackRock-HPS franchise managing something in the range of $190 to $220 billion in private credit assets on its own. These are also, not coincidentally, the managers large enough to be attractive counterparties for the largest banks' warehouse and subscription-line desks, because scale cuts both ways: a fund big enough to originate hundreds of loans a year is also big enough to be worth a bank's relationship-management effort, worth a dedicated credit line, worth the kind of underwriting attention a smaller, newer manager would not get. The IMF's and FSB's point about concentration is not that any one of these firms is poorly run — by most public accounts, quite the opposite, which is part of why they have grown to the size they have. It is that a banking system whose $300 billion in private credit exposure is itself concentrated among relationships with a handful of large managers has, in effect, recreated inside a new structure something like the counterparty concentration that regulators spent years after 2008 trying to reduce in other corners of the financial system: a shock to one major manager's underwriting culture, valuation practice, or liquidity management does not stay contained to that manager, because enough of the banking system's private credit exposure runs through the same small set of names.

The Federal Reserve Bank of Boston's own research note on the question, examining whether the growth of private credit poses a risk to financial system stability, reached its most useful conclusions by asking what specifically distinguishes today's structure from the ordinary background risk that any growing credit market carries. It found the interconnection channels traced by its own institution's FEDS Notes work and by the IMF and FSB to be the most concrete, evidence-based concern available — not because the Boston Fed's researchers believed a crisis was imminent, but because interconnection is the one channel that is directly measurable, through bank call reports and supervisory data, rather than inferred from indirect signals the way valuation opacity or fund-level leverage generally have to be. The regional Fed's framing was, in its own way, a kind of methodological modesty: rather than trying to adjudicate whether private credit's growth is fundamentally dangerous, a question that depends on assumptions about future credit losses nobody can verify in advance, it asked which parts of the system's plumbing could already be traced with the data on hand, and answered that the bank-to-fund lending relationship was the clearest one available. That is a narrower claim than "private credit threatens the banking system," and it is also, for exactly that reason, a claim the evidence can actually support.

None of this is a case for alarm on its own terms, and the same reports that catalogue these channels are unusually direct, for institutions of their kind, about the reasons a 2025-vintage credit event and a 2008-vintage financial crisis are not the same shape of risk. Bank capital buffers, built up over more than a decade of Basel III implementation and annual stress testing under the Federal Reserve's Comprehensive Capital Analysis and Review process — the same regime traced in this book's third chapter — are substantially larger, relative to risk-weighted assets, than anything the banking system carried into 2007. A bank absorbing losses on a warehouse facility or a subscription line today is absorbing them against a capital cushion built specifically, in the years after the last crisis, to withstand exactly this kind of unexpected credit deterioration without the bank itself failing. And the funds on the other side of these facilities generally do not carry anything close to the leverage that made 2008's structured vehicles so dangerous. The collateralized debt obligations and structured investment vehicles at the center of the last crisis routinely operated with leverage ratios of ten to one or higher, funded overnight in repo markets that could and did evaporate within days once counterparties lost confidence in the collateral backing them. A typical direct-lending fund, by contrast, is funded predominantly by investor capital locked up for years rather than overnight borrowing, and even where funds do use leverage — at the fund level, through the kinds of facilities described above, or embedded in the loans they make — the ratios involved are, across the IMF's and FSB's own data, materially lower than what characterized the pre-2008 shadow-banking complex. This is not a minor footnote to an otherwise alarming picture. It is close to the central finding of both reports, stated plainly rather than buried: the specific mechanism that turned 2008 from a series of bad loans into a cascading, self-reinforcing panic — thin capital, short-term funding, and forced fire sales feeding on each other — does not obviously exist in today's private credit structure at anything like the same intensity. A reader entitled to weigh First Brands, Tricolor, and Dimon's own quotation seriously is equally entitled to weigh this, and a book willing to describe the channels through which stress could travel is obligated, in the same breath, to describe why the specific engine that made 2007 and 2008 so violent has not, on the evidence available as of this writing, been rebuilt in the same form.

What the evidence supports, once both halves of it are held together, is closer to the framing Cambridge Associates used in its own research note on the two 2025 bankruptcies, written explicitly for the institutional investors who allocate capital to private credit funds and therefore have more reason than most to get the answer right rather than dramatic. The note asked, in effect, whether First Brands and Tricolor suggested trouble ahead for the asset class, and its own answer resisted a clean yes or no: the two failures were real, their lender losses were real, and they arrived in the same news cycle for reasons that were partly coincidental and partly diagnostic of genuine weaknesses in collateral verification and diligence that recur across the industry rather than belonging uniquely to either borrower. That is a different claim than "private credit is a systemic time bomb," and it is also a different claim than "these were isolated events with no broader lesson." It is the claim this chapter has tried to make with more mechanical specificity: two borrower-level frauds revealed, because banks happened to be exposed to both of them through ordinary lending relationships, that the channels connecting the regulated banking system to the private credit ecosystem are real, are growing, and are large enough that the Federal Reserve, the IMF, and the Financial Stability Board have all independently concluded they deserve sustained monitoring rather than the occasional academic paper. Whether that monitoring catches the next First Brands before it reaches a bank balance sheet, or whether it produces nothing more than a better-documented history of a risk nobody stopped, is not a question these reports answer, because it is not a question anyone can answer before the fact.

Dimon's line, reread against all of this, holds up better as a question than as a prophecy. He was right that a visible failure in an opaque market is reasonable grounds to suspect others exist that have not yet surfaced — that is simply what opacity means, and it is the same argument this book's chapter on marked-to-model valuation made about smoothed quarterly marks generally, restated at the scale of an entire earnings call. What the cockroach line does not answer, and was never really equipped to answer, is the question this chapter has spent its length on instead: not whether more borrowers like First Brands and Tricolor exist somewhere in the private credit market, which is close to a certainty in any lending market large enough, but how directly the health of a bank holding insured deposits depends on the health of the funds sitting one or two contractual steps away from it. The honest answer, based on what the Fed, the IMF, the FSB, and the Boston Fed have each published, is: more directly than most depositors would guess, and less directly than the loudest version of the crisis narrative assumes. That is an unsatisfying place to leave a chapter, and it is also the most accurate one available as of this writing.

There is one more channel connecting private credit to the broader financial system, and it does not run through a bank at all. It runs through an institution most people do not think of as risky in the way they think of a bank or a hedge fund — an institution whose entire cultural reputation is built on caution, actuarial tables, and promises kept over decades. It is the life insurance company that sold somebody's parents an annuity, and increasingly, it is also the private credit manager that owns it.
