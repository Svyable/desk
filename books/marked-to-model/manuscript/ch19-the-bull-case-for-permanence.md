# The Bull Case for Permanence

In the chapter on the gate, Cliffwater LLC turned up almost as an aside — the third manager, after Blackstone and Barings, to tighten its own redemption terms in early 2026, at the exact moment investors most wanted to test them. What that chapter did not stop to explain, because the moment belonged to liquidity rather than performance, is that Cliffwater is not merely another direct-lending manager with a retail-facing fund. It is also the keeper of the number the entire private credit industry reaches for whenever somebody asks whether any of this actually works: the Cliffwater Direct Lending Index, a benchmark built from the disclosed, loan-level financial statements of the business development companies that make up a large share of the direct-lending universe, tracked since the mid-2000s and formally launched as a published index in September 2010. Through the worst run of headlines the industry had faced since the financial crisis — First Brands' collapse that September, Tricolor's fraud-riddled Chapter 7 filing the same month, Jamie Dimon telling analysts a month later that where there is one cockroach there are probably more, and then, seven months after that, the gates at three prominent retail vehicles including Cliffwater's own — the index kept doing the only thing an index can do, which is report what actually happened to the loans inside it. Cliffwater put out a press release that same stretch, built almost entirely around what the index showed, under a headline that amounted to a dare: the data, it said, supported the strength of private credit. It is worth finding out whether the dare holds up, because the number behind it is real, it covers a genuine crisis, and it deserves the same rigor this book has given every uncomfortable fact that argued the other way.

Start with what the index actually says, stripped of the press release's own framing. Since its formal inception in September 2010, the Cliffwater Direct Lending Index has produced an annualized total return of roughly 9.5 percent — a figure built from the interest income, fees, and realized and unrealized gains and losses reported across the underlying loan book each quarter, not from a manager's marketing deck. That number by itself invites an obvious question: 9.5 percent compared to what? Cliffwater's own answer, drawing on a longer data series that reaches back through the underlying BDCs' disclosures to the mid-2000s, before the index existed in published form, is a comparison across the twenty calendar years ending in 2024 against three public benchmarks a fixed-income investor would recognize immediately — high-yield corporate bonds, broadly syndicated leveraged loans, and investment-grade corporate credit. Over that twenty-year span, direct lending's calendar-year return exceeded all three of those public alternatives in thirteen of the twenty years. That the comparison window includes 2008 and 2009 is not incidental. It is close to the entire point. A basket of privately originated, mostly senior-secured loans to midsize companies — the kind of borrower this book has spent several chapters arguing lacks the visibility and the continuous market pricing that public credit enjoys — went through the worst American credit crisis in eighty years and came out the other side having beaten the public markets in most of the years since, not merely in the placid ones.

The loss data underneath that return figure is, if anything, the more striking piece of evidence, because losses are harder to dress up than returns. A return figure absorbs a manager's own marks, and this book has spent an entire chapter on the discretion built into those marks. A realized credit loss is different in kind: it is what a lender actually collected, net of what it actually did not, once a loan has worked its way through default, restructuring, or payoff, and it cannot be smoothed away by a valuation committee's choice of discount rate the way an unrealized mark can. Over that same twenty-year window, the Cliffwater Direct Lending Index's realized credit losses ran to about 1.01 percent, annualized. Broadly syndicated leveraged loans — publicly traded, continuously priced, senior-secured debt of a similar seniority in the capital structure — lost about 1.02 percent over the identical period, a number close enough to direct lending's own that the two are effectively indistinguishable, which is itself a meaningful data point: a privately negotiated loan held by a single relationship lender destroyed capital, over two decades including a generational crisis, at almost exactly the same rate as a comparable loan traded in a liquid public market with continuous price discovery, a rating agency's opinion attached, and thousands of participants free to sell at the first sign of trouble. High-yield bonds, further down the capital structure and typically unsecured, lost meaningfully more — about 1.49 percent, roughly half again as much as either direct lending or leveraged loans. If the industry's critics are right that opacity quietly launders deteriorating credit into a low-volatility number, that laundering should show up somewhere in a loss statistic that cannot be marked away, and over twenty years, using the index's own reporting, it largely does not. The loans got paid back, mostly, at a rate that tracks the closest public comparison almost exactly and beats a riskier one by a real margin.

It is worth sitting with why leveraged loans, rather than high-yield bonds, are the fairer comparison to begin with, because the choice is not arbitrary and it is not private credit's own industry that gets to make it. A leveraged loan and a direct loan occupy roughly the same rung of a company's capital structure — senior, usually secured by specific assets, first in line for recovery if the borrower fails — while a high-yield bond typically sits junior to both, unsecured, and further back in the queue when there is not enough left to go around. Comparing direct lending's losses to leveraged loans' losses is, in other words, a same-seniority comparison, the kind a skeptical credit analyst would insist on before accepting any performance claim at all; comparing direct lending's losses to high-yield bonds' losses, where direct lending wins by a wider margin, is the more flattering comparison and the more forgivable one to reach for in a press release. Cliffwater's own reporting includes both, and the fact that direct lending tracks its true structural peer almost exactly, rather than merely beating a riskier and more junior instrument, is the stronger of the two claims, not the weaker one — it says the extra yield direct lenders charge for illiquidity was not simply compensation for taking on hidden extra risk relative to a fair comparison. It was closer to a clean premium for giving up a market price and a same-day exit, on debt that performed, when trouble came, about as well as its closest liquid analogue.

None of this happened by accident, and the people who have spent careers allocating patient capital would say the historical pattern is not even surprising. Long before anyone called it private credit, pension funds, university endowments, and sovereign wealth funds were paying a real, measurable cost to hold assets nobody could sell on short notice — private equity stakes, timberland, direct real estate, infrastructure concessions on toll roads and pipelines — precisely because their liabilities were long-dated too. A public pension fund does not need to cash out a position next Tuesday to make a retiree's check clear; it needs, over a horizon measured in decades, to earn enough to cover benefits it has already promised, and every dollar it does not need immediately is a dollar that can be put somewhere an ordinary saver, or a mutual fund answering to daily redemptions, cannot afford to go. That willingness to give up a market price and a same-day exit, in exchange for a documented premium over what the liquid alternative would pay, is not a theory invented to explain away private credit's opacity. It is a feature of institutional portfolio construction that predates direct lending by generations, tested across multiple market cycles by investors with every incentive to abandon it the moment it stopped working, and it has not stopped working. The illiquidity premium this book's earlier chapters described in the context of a single company's choice among three phone calls is, from the investor's side of that same transaction, the reason a pension fund or an endowment would want to be the lender on the third call in the first place.

There is a second, less obvious argument buried inside the first one, and it is worth drawing out rather than leaving implicit. A daily-traded bond fund's greatest apparent strength — the promise that any shareholder can get cash back on any given day — is also the mechanism that can force the fund to sell into exactly the moment when selling does the most damage, because enough frightened shareholders asking for their money on the same afternoon can compel a manager to dump bonds into a market where everyone else is trying to sell too, crystallizing losses that patient holders would never have had to take. A locked-up direct-lending vehicle cannot be run on in that way, not because its investors are wiser or more disciplined, but because the structure itself removes the option to panic. Capital that cannot leave on short notice cannot be the trigger for a fire sale, and a lender that is never forced to sell a performing loan at a distressed price to meet someone else's redemption request is free to do the more useful thing instead: wait out a cyclical downturn, work with a borrower through a rough quarter, and collect on a loan that would have been sold at a loss by a more liquid, more panic-prone vehicle holding the identical asset. Judged this way, illiquidity is not simply a cost patient investors accept in exchange for extra yield. In a downturn, it can be closer to a structural advantage over the liquid alternative, a claim that sounds counterintuitive only until the mechanics of a redemption run are laid out plainly, and one the twenty years of index data above did nothing to contradict.

What changed, starting with the executive order in the summer of 2025 and the Department of Labor's rescission of its own prior guidance days later, is who gets to make that same trade. For most of private credit's modern history, the ability to accept illiquidity in exchange for a premium return belonged almost exclusively to institutions large enough to run their own actuarial models, staff their own alternatives teams, and stomach a decade-long lockup without needing the money back. An ordinary 401(k) participant, saving out of a paycheck for a retirement thirty years away, has a liability schedule that in some ways more closely resembles a pension fund's than it resembles a day trader's — money that, by design, is not supposed to be touched for decades — and yet that same participant has historically had no legal channel to the asset class that pension funds themselves have used to meet exactly that kind of long-dated obligation. Freshfields' own client alert on the executive order, written for corporate clients trying to understand what had actually changed, framed the shift as carrying both promise and peril, and the peril half of that framing has occupied much of this book. The promise half deserves its own hearing, on the same terms. A capital markets system that reserves an entire category of historically well-compensated, long-duration lending for the largest and most sophisticated institutional balance sheets, while telling everyone else that their thirty-year retirement savings belong exclusively in daily-traded stocks and bonds, is not obviously protecting the ordinary saver. It may simply be an artifact of decades-old fiduciary caution, minimum-investment thresholds, and administrative complexity that never had much to do with whether the underlying asset class actually served long-horizon investors well. Extending that access is not self-evidently a con being run on people who don't know better. It may be capital markets finishing a job they started with pension funds fifty years ago and never got around to finishing for everyone else — completing a channel that was artificially restricted, not inventing a new risk that did not previously exist.

The stress episode that has occupied so much of this book's second half looks different, too, once it is read as a test of that channel rather than as its indictment. First Brands' bankruptcy revealed a company that had, according to the allegations still working their way through court, pledged the same receivables to multiple lenders through financing arrangements that sat outside any single lender's clean view of the balance sheet. Tricolor's collapse revealed federal charges of collateral fraud on a scale — roughly 800 million dollars in loans allegedly double-pledged — that embarrassed banks and direct lenders alike. Both are genuinely bad outcomes for the lenders who got it wrong, and this book has not softened either case in the telling. But it is worth asking what a market actually learns from an episode like that, and the honest answer is that markets that never encounter a bad actor do not learn anything at all; they simply have not yet met one. A lending market that had somehow made it to 2026 without a single fraud, a single gate, a single write-down, would be a market that had not yet been tested, not a market that had proven itself safe. What First Brands and Tricolor actually demonstrate is that when a borrower lies convincingly enough, some lenders — bank and nonbank alike, since Fifth Third and JPMorgan took real losses on Tricolor's warehouse lines right alongside the direct lenders — will get fooled, at least for a while. What happens next is the more important question, and the answer so far looks less like collapse and more like correction. Diligence practices around receivables financing and factoring arrangements, largely invisible to term-loan lenders before First Brands, became a live topic across the industry within weeks of the filing, with managers reportedly tightening collateral-verification requirements and pushing for direct confirmation from account debtors rather than relying on a borrower's own representations. Warehouse-line lenders facing their own version of the Tricolor lesson have reportedly done the same on auto-loan collateral verification. None of that undoes the losses First Brands' and Tricolor's lenders already took. But an industry that responds to a fraud by making the next fraud harder to pull off is doing something closer to what a functioning market is supposed to do than an industry that pretends the fraud never happened.

The gates tell a similar story, if the reader is willing to hear it on the terms the previous chapter already established rather than as a fresh alarm. Blackstone's BCRED, Barings' interval fund, and Cliffwater's own retail vehicle all did, in 2026, precisely what their governing documents said they might do under exactly the conditions those documents described: they capped redemptions, prorated the requests they could not fully honor, and kept paying everyone something rather than either ignoring the cap or dumping loans into a market with no natural buyers to raise cash they had not promised on demand in the first place. No fund discussed in this book defaulted on an obligation because of a gate. No investor lost principal because a redemption request came back prorated instead of whole. A structure that is specifically designed to prevent a liquidity panic from becoming a fire sale, and that then prevents exactly that outcome the first time real pressure arrives, has not failed a test. It has passed one, in public, in a way a decade of quiet, unstressed operation never could have demonstrated. If the alternative version of 2026 had unfolded instead — funds blowing through their own stated caps to avoid an uncomfortable headline, then being forced to sell loans at panic prices to meet redemptions they had never actually promised to meet on demand — that would be the failure worth writing a chapter of alarm about. What actually happened was closer to a fire drill that ended with everyone walking calmly out the door they had been shown in advance.

There is a historical precedent for reading a stress episode this way, and this book has already told it once, because it is close enough to press into service again here. The junk bond market Michael Milken built at Drexel Burnham Lambert did not die when Drexel did. Drexel's own guilty plea, its collapse into bankruptcy in February 1990, and Milken's own felony conviction weeks later were a real reckoning for a specific firm and a specific man, and this book has not minimized either. But the technology underneath the scandal — a diversified portfolio of properly underwritten, sub-investment-grade corporate debt, priced to compensate investors for the risk they were actually taking rather than for the stigma attached to a low rating — turned out to be sound on its own terms, separable from the firm and the individual who had first commercialized it. Other underwriters simply took over the business Drexel had built, because the underlying insight had been correct, and high-yield debt went on to become one of the largest, most liquid, most thoroughly indexed corners of American fixed income, held today inside pension portfolios and mutual funds by the trillions of dollars without anyone thinking twice about it. A reckoning that discredits a firm, or even an individual, is a different fact from a reckoning that discredits a technology. Private credit's own 2025–2026 stress episode has, so far, produced exactly that same separable pattern: specific borrowers committing what prosecutors allege was outright fraud, specific lenders whose diligence did not catch it, and a broader financing structure — origination by a single relationship lender, real maintenance covenants, capital locked up on terms that prevented the kind of forced selling that turned isolated trouble into system-wide panic in 2008 — that has not, on the evidence available as this book goes to print, itself broken. A stress test that reveals which underwriters were careless is not the same finding as proof that the underwriting technology is broken. History's closest precedent suggests the two are frequently confused with each other in the moment, and just as frequently turn out to be different things once enough time has passed to tell.

All of that deserves to stand on its own, because it is true and because the industry's defenders have earned the right to make this case without having it immediately qualified into meaninglessness. But this book has tried, in every chapter, to state plainly what evidence would weaken its own argument, and the same discipline belongs here, applied to the argument this chapter has just made. The twenty years behind the Cliffwater Direct Lending Index are real years, with real loans and real losses in them, and they are not the same twenty years, or the same market, that a saver opening a private-credit sleeve inside a target-date fund in 2026 is actually being offered. The direct-lending universe the index describes in its earliest years was a fraction of the size of today's market, dominated by institutional-only vehicles sold to pension funds and endowments through long, closed-end lockups, underwritten by managers who had, in many cases, built their credit teams inside banks before the financial crisis pushed them out. The market the index describes today, and the market a retirement saver is actually entering, is several times larger, still growing quickly, increasingly distributed through semi-liquid evergreen structures built explicitly to appeal to buyers who were never going to accept a ten-year lockup, and staffed in no small part by underwriters who came up entirely within the direct-lending boom itself rather than inside a bank's more conservative credit culture. Whether a benchmark built substantially on the earlier version of that market is still a reliable guide to how the later, larger, faster-growing, more retail-facing version will behave through its own first real downturn is an open question, not a settled one, and the index's own strong historical performance is not itself evidence that the question has been answered.

The construction of the index deserves the same scrutiny. A comparative benchmarking study published in February 2026 took up a question that sounds narrow and turns out not to be: how do the industry's own private credit indices — Cliffwater's among them, alongside benchmarks built independently by MSCI, PitchBook, and Preqin — actually differ in the loans they sample, the way they treat funds that stop reporting or wind down, and the assumptions embedded in how each provider extends its own return series backward in time. The study's finding, in short, was that these benchmarks do not agree with each other nearly as closely as an outside observer, comparing headline return figures without looking underneath them, might assume. Different sampling choices and different treatment of funds that quietly disappear from a data set can move a reported return or loss figure meaningfully, and Cliffwater's own extension of its return series back through the mid-2000s — years before the index existed in published form, built from BDC disclosures gathered after the fact — is exactly the kind of construction choice that kind of study exists to interrogate. None of this means the 9.5 percent figure, or the 1.01 percent loss rate, is wrong. Institutional Investor's own reporting on the broader argument among professional allocators, published under a headline noting that the debate over how to measure private credit returns was only just heating up, is a useful corrective on its own: this is not a settled question that only outside skeptics are raising. Serious institutional investors who want the bull case to be true are still arguing, in 2026, about the best way to measure it. That argument is itself a sign of a maturing asset class working out its own standards under real scrutiny, not proof that the underlying data is fiction. But it is also a caution worth taking at face value rather than waving away: extrapolating twenty years of one index's history, built one particular way on a smaller and more institutional market, onto a structurally different and much larger market that has not yet completed its own first full cycle, is a weaker claim than the press release headline suggested. The historical numbers are real. What they predict about the version of this market now being sold through retirement accounts is a separate question, and this chapter's own evidence cannot answer it.

Two honest, well-evidenced chapters now sit in real tension with each other, and neither one has cheated to get there. The chapter before this one took seriously the ways today's private credit structure resembles the shadow banking system that helped bring on the 2008 crisis, and concluded, on the evidence, that the resemblance is real but partial — the specific mechanism that turned isolated trouble into a cascading panic in 2008, short-term funding for long-dated assets, is largely absent from a market financed by capital locked up for years rather than funded overnight. This chapter has taken just as seriously the case that private credit is not a bubble waiting to pop but a durable, well-compensated evolution of capital markets, backed by two decades of data that include a real crisis and show real, comparably low losses. Both arguments are built from primary sources, both survive their own best counterevidence, and both cannot simply be averaged together into a comfortable middle position without losing what makes each one true. The honest response to that tension is not to pick a winner between two chapters that were never trying to fight each other in the first place. It is to ask what a reader, an investor, or a regulator could actually watch for, in the years after this book is published, that would tell them which of these two honest arguments is going to matter more. That is the question the book's final chapter takes up directly, and it is the last one this book owes its reader an answer to.
