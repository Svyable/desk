# The Case for the Phone Call

Picture a chief financial officer at a company that makes something nobody outside her industry thinks about: precision-machined seals and fittings for industrial pumps, sold mostly to other manufacturers, with about forty million dollars of annual earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization and no public equity to speak of. The founder still owns a third of it. The company has never had a bond rating, never filed anything with the SEC, and never needed to. It has a five-year-old bank loan coming due in four months, and the board has just approved a plan to refinance early and use the extra borrowing capacity to buy a smaller competitor two states over. The CFO now has, in practice, three phone calls she could make, and the choice among them will shape the next several years of the company's life more than almost any operating decision she makes in between.

The first call goes to a bank. Not necessarily to lend the whole amount itself — more likely to arrange it, meaning the bank will structure the loan, put some of its own balance sheet behind a piece of it, and then spend the next several weeks finding other banks, and increasingly collateralized loan obligations, to take the rest. That process has a name, syndication, and a rhythm that hasn't changed much in decades: a term sheet, a lender presentation, a period of "market flex" in which pricing can move against the borrower if investor appetite is thin, and a closing that, if nothing goes wrong, lands six to ten weeks out. The second call goes to an investment bank that could bring the company to the public high-yield market, but that call ends almost as soon as it starts. A forty-million-dollar EBITDA issuer with no ratings history and a name nobody in the bond market has heard of is, realistically, too small and too obscure to clear the fixed costs of a public offering — the rating agency process alone, with two committees reviewing a company that has never been rated before, can eat a month by itself, before a single investor has seen the deal.

The third call goes to a direct lender — a fund manager who will originate, hold, and monitor the entire loan itself, using capital raised from insurers, pensions, and increasingly ordinary savers through a business development company. This is the call that, for a company shaped like this one, usually wins, and it is worth taking seriously exactly why, because the reasons are not marketing. They are structural, and they follow from what a single lender can do that a syndicate or a bond market cannot.

None of these three calls would look the way they do now if banks still did what they used to do. For much of the twentieth century, a company like this one would have had essentially one option: a local or regional bank, run by people who might have known the founder personally, willing to underwrite a loan on the strength of a relationship built over years of deposit accounts, payroll processing, and the occasional site visit. That kind of banking has been consolidating away for decades, and the capital rules built after 2008 — the same regulatory shift discussed in the last chapter — made holding a loan like this one on a bank's own balance sheet a more expensive proposition than it used to be, in terms of the capital a bank has to set aside against it. Community and regional banks that once specialized in exactly this kind of middle-market relationship lending have shrunk in number and in appetite for this kind of risk; the large banks that remain increasingly prefer bigger, more standardized credits they can underwrite at scale and, where possible, sell down rather than hold to maturity. The space in between — too complex or too idiosyncratic for a plain-vanilla bank term loan, too small and unrated for the public market — did not used to be as wide as it is now. Direct lenders did not invent that gap. They walked into one that banks, for reasons that made sense from inside a bank, had already started to vacate.

In practice, the loan the manufacturer is offered rarely looks like the bank facility it is refinancing. Direct lenders often package what would otherwise be separate senior and subordinated tranches into a single loan — a unitranche, in the industry's own term — priced at a blended rate between the two, with one lender, one set of documents, and one covenant package instead of a senior bank lender and a mezzanine lender negotiating separately with each other over the same collateral. For a borrower, that collapses another layer of complexity: no intercreditor agreement to hammer out between two lenders with different priorities, no risk that a senior and a subordinated holder disagree with each other about how to handle a future amendment. For the fund providing it, the blended structure is exactly what justifies holding the whole position rather than syndicating pieces of it — the fund is being paid across the full spread of the capital structure's risk, in exchange for taking on all of it itself, and monitoring all of it itself.

Start with speed, because speed is the most visible difference and the easiest to underrate until you have needed it. A syndicated bank deal has to clear the calendars and credit committees of a dozen or more institutions, each running its own parallel diligence, each with its own internal sign-off chain. A public bond needs a rating, a prospectus or offering memorandum, and usually a roadshow — days of meetings with institutional investors who have never heard of the issuer and need to be convinced, in a compressed window, that they understand its risk. A direct loan needs one credit committee to say yes. The lender doing the underwriting is also the lender doing the diligence and the lender making the decision, which collapses a process that otherwise unfolds in stages, each waiting on the last, into something closer to a single conversation. Deals that would take two to three months through a bank syndicate or the public market routinely close in three to six weeks through a direct lender, sometimes faster when the borrower and the fund already have a relationship from a prior financing. For a company with an acquisition on a deadline, or a loan maturing on a fixed date, that difference is not a convenience. It can be the difference between winning the competitor in Ohio and losing it to someone who could move faster.

Confidentiality matters almost as much, and it is easy for anyone who has not run a mid-sized private company to underrate how much a business owner cares about keeping financial detail out of public view. A public bond, even one sold only to large institutional buyers under the rule that avoids full SEC registration, still needs a credit rating, and a credit rating comes with a published report — margins, leverage, customer concentration, the sort of granular operating detail that a competitor, a customer negotiating price, or a private equity buyer sizing up an acquisition target would all like to see and normally cannot. Ratings agency analysts talk to other analysts. Trade press covers ratings actions. A company's own suppliers can and do read them. None of that happens with a bilaterally negotiated direct loan. The lender sees everything — full financial statements, customer contracts, whatever due diligence its credit team wants — but that information stays inside one institution, under one confidentiality agreement, instead of being filtered through a rating committee and released to the market as color for other people's investment decisions. For a founder who has spent thirty years building a business specifically so as not to answer to public shareholders, this is not a minor preference. It is very often the reason he ruled out the bond market before the first phone call.

The mechanics of that exposure are worth spelling out, because "going public" with debt is a matter of degree that people outside finance tend to underestimate. Even a bond placed under Rule 144A, which lets a company avoid full SEC registration by selling only to large institutional buyers, still typically carries a credit rating, and a rating agency's published rationale — the leverage multiple, the margin trend, the customer concentration that worried the analyst — becomes a document available to anyone who wants to read it, including a private equity firm quietly building a file on an acquisition target, or a larger competitor sizing up how much room a smaller rival has to cut price in a bid. Trading data on the bond itself, once it starts changing hands, becomes visible through market data services even to firms that never bought a single bond. None of this is a defect in the public market; it is what makes that market liquid and able to discover a price in the first place, and it is exactly the trade a large public issuer accepts in return for a lower cost of capital. But it is a trade a private, owner-run manufacturer with real competitors watching its every move has good reason to decline.

Then there is the matter of covenants, which sounds like the driest part of the story and is in fact the part that does the most real work. By the middle of the 2020s, the broadly syndicated leveraged loan market — the kind of debt sold in pieces to dozens of buyers, many of them CLOs assembling diversified pools rather than analyzing any one borrower closely — had drifted overwhelmingly toward what the market calls "covenant-lite" structures. A covenant-lite loan typically carries only incurrence covenants: tests that trigger only when the company takes a specific action, like paying a dividend or issuing more debt, and that sit dormant otherwise. It does not require the borrower to maintain a given leverage ratio or interest coverage ratio quarter after quarter regardless of what it does. That structure exists for a coherent reason — with dozens of holders, no single one of whom is positioned to run ongoing credit surveillance the way a bank once did on a loan it kept on its own books, tight maintenance covenants create more friction than value, since getting a hundred dispersed lenders to agree on a waiver is its own expensive negotiation. But the effect, whatever the reason behind it, is a lending structure that often does not notice trouble until the company is already missing payments.

A direct lender, holding the entire loan on its own book rather than a sliver of a syndicated tranche, tends to insist on the opposite: real maintenance covenants, tested quarterly, calibrated tightly enough to the company's own financial plan that a leverage ratio creeping upward or a cash flow cushion thinning out will trip a covenant well before the company is in genuine danger of default. This is not generosity. It is self-interest, correctly understood. The lender that holds a hundred percent of a loan bears a hundred percent of the loss if the borrower fails, and it has no one to share the diligence burden with and no liquid market in which to sell out of a position quietly if it grows uneasy. A tight covenant, tripped early, is the mechanism by which that lender forces a conversation while the company still has options — before a missed payment, before a bankruptcy filing, while there is still room to renegotiate pricing, extend a maturity, or trade a lighter covenant package for a fee and a board observer seat. Executives who have lived through both kinds of financing describe the covenant-lite structure as a wire that doesn't trip until the fuse box is already on fire, and the tighter maintenance covenant, whatever its inconvenience day to day, as an actual early-warning system. That the industry's marketing repeats this argument does not make it false. In this case it happens to describe the mechanics correctly.

Underneath all of this sits a genuine economic idea, not an excuse invented after the fact to justify why nobody outside the lender ever sees the loan's price: the illiquidity premium. It is a simple concept dressed up in intimidating language. An investor who buys a bond that trades every day, that can be sold at a quoted price with a phone call, is holding an asset that comes with an option — the ability to exit at more or less the market's collective judgment of fair value, whenever the investor wants, at some cost in transaction fees but not much more. An investor who instead commits capital to a loan with no secondary market, locking up money for five, seven, sometimes ten years with no way to sell out early except through a privately negotiated transaction at a steep discount, has given up that option entirely. Classical finance has understood for decades that giving up an option like that is worth something, and that any rational investor will demand to be paid for it. That extra compensation, above and beyond what a comparably risky but liquid instrument would pay, is the illiquidity premium, and it shows up in nearly every corner of finance where long-dated, non-tradable commitments exist — private equity, real estate, infrastructure, and, in this case, direct loans to companies like the pump-fitting manufacturer.

The size of that premium is debated rather than fixed, and the honest answer is that estimates move around depending on who is doing the estimating and which slice of the market they are looking at. Market commentary through the mid-2020s generally put it somewhere between two and four percentage points over what a comparably risky public loan or bond would yield — a range wide enough that no single source treats it as a precise, universal number, but consistent enough across sources that it looks like a real, persistent feature of the market rather than a number someone invented to make opacity sound respectable. An insurer or a pension fund with liabilities stretching out decades does not need to sell a bond next Tuesday to meet a redemption request the way a mutual fund does; it can afford to lock money up, and in exchange for accepting that illiquidity, it is being paid more than it would earn holding the liquid alternative. That is not a subsidy for secrecy. It is the ordinary economics of patient capital, the same logic that pays a bank depositor more interest for a five-year certificate of deposit than for money left in a checking account, scaled up to institutional size and applied to corporate credit instead of consumer savings.

It is worth asking, too, whose money is actually doing the locking up. Overwhelmingly, in the years this market grew fastest, it was exactly the kind of investor classical finance would predict: life insurers matching assets against annuity liabilities that will not come due for twenty or thirty years, pension funds paying out benefits decades into the future, endowments with genuinely multi-generational time horizons. These are, almost by definition, the investors best positioned to hold something illiquid, because nothing in their own liabilities forces them to convert the holding back into cash on short notice. Matching long-dated assets to long-dated liabilities like this is one of the oldest and least controversial ideas in institutional finance — it is the same logic that leads a life insurer to hold long bonds against long policies rather than short-term paper. Extending that logic to a private loan instead of a public bond is not obviously a worse idea; if the loan is underwritten and priced honestly, it may be a better one, since the lender is being paid extra for a mismatch it was always well suited to carry. The harder question, and one this book takes up later, is what happens once that same illiquid exposure starts flowing to a different kind of investor — a saver in a retirement account, say — whose own time horizon and tolerance for a locked-up asset may not resemble an insurer's at all.

There is a second half of this argument that the book's own opening chapters raise as a genuine possibility and that deserves to be taken fully seriously here rather than dismissed: locked-up capital may not just be fairly compensated illiquidity. It may be more stable illiquidity, in a way that actually protects the borrower during a downturn better than a public market would. A mutual fund that holds corporate bonds has to meet daily redemptions from its own shareholders; if enough of those shareholders get nervous at once and ask for their money back, the fund may be forced to sell bonds into a falling market regardless of what it thinks those bonds are really worth, and that forced selling can itself become the thing that drives prices down further, hurting borrowers who did nothing wrong except share a market with panicked sellers. A bank depositor can walk up to a teller window, or these days open an app, and pull money out in minutes, which is precisely the mechanism that turns a rumor into a bank run. A direct lending fund financed by capital locked up for years does not face that pressure in the same way. Its investors cannot show up and demand cash back on a bad Tuesday, which means the fund itself is not forced to dump a borrower's loan into a market at a fire-sale price merely because sentiment has turned. It can, instead, do the thing a single relationship lender is uniquely positioned to do: pick up the phone, look at the company's actual numbers, and decide whether the underlying business is still sound enough to work with.

That last point is where the case for direct lending becomes most concrete, and where the contrast with a syndicated bond or loan is sharpest — not in the good years, but in the bad ones. When a public bond issuer runs into trouble, the people who have to agree on what happens next are not one counterparty but potentially hundreds: mutual funds that need to preserve daily liquidity for their own shareholders and may be forced sellers regardless of their view of the credit; CLOs constrained by their own rating triggers, which can require them to sell a loan the moment it is downgraded below a certain threshold, whatever they think its fundamentals actually justify; distressed-debt funds that specifically bought the bonds at a steep discount hoping for a bankruptcy fight that pays them more than a quiet amendment would; and long-only managers whose mandate simply does not allow them to hold defaulted paper at all. Getting a supermajority of a group like that to agree on anything — a covenant waiver, a maturity extension, a fresh injection of capital — is its own negotiation, often run through committees of committees, with financial and legal advisors billing by the hour on both sides, and with holdouts who have every incentive to extract a better deal for themselves at the expense of a faster resolution for everyone else. Chapter 11 exists in large part because dispersed creditors cannot always agree outside of it.

A direct lender does not have that problem, because there is no dispersion to manage. If the pump-fitting manufacturer's newly acquired Ohio subsidiary underperforms and the combined company trips its leverage covenant eighteen months after closing, the CFO does not need to coordinate a response across a syndicate of strangers with conflicting mandates. She calls the same person who underwrote the original loan, who already knows the business, already has the full financial picture, and has every incentive — since the fund holds the loan to maturity rather than trading it — to find a structure that gets repaid over time rather than one that maximizes recovery in a fire sale. That might mean an amendment, a fee, a temporary step-up in the interest rate, or a period of payment-in-kind interest that lets the company preserve cash while it works through a rough patch. None of that requires a court, a trustee, or a vote. It requires one phone call, answered by someone who was already paying attention.

The manufacturer in this story is invented, but the shape of it is not. Multiply it by the thousands of similarly sized companies scattered across the country — family-owned distributors, regional health care service providers, specialty parts makers, software companies too young to have the three years of audited financials a bank would insist on — and the gap direct lending filled starts to look less like a marketing opportunity and more like an obvious hole. These are, for the most part, ordinary businesses selling real things to real customers, generating actual cash flow, run by people who had unglamorous, entirely reasonable motives for wanting exactly what this chapter has described: money that shows up on time, doesn't require a public accounting of the business's internal workings, and comes from someone willing to answer the phone when a quarter goes sideways. None of that description depends on any of it being a bubble.

None of this means the argument for a single relationship lender is unconditional. It rests, when you follow it all the way down, on an assumption: that the lender exercising all this discretion is doing so because it genuinely believes the underlying business is sound, and not merely because acknowledging trouble on its own book would be inconvenient to admit. A tight maintenance covenant only functions as an early warning system if the lender actually enforces it rather than waiving it reflexively to avoid marking a loan down. Patient capital only protects a good company through a rough patch if the capital is being deployed on the strength of the company's prospects rather than the lender's discomfort with reporting a loss. The whole argument, in other words, depends on the quality of monitoring behind the phone call — a credit officer who actually reads the monthly financials, visits the plant, knows the difference between a bad quarter and a broken business — rather than on the existence of a covenant on paper that nobody is watching closely enough to enforce. That is a genuinely different question from the one this chapter has been answering, and it is not one this chapter is equipped to settle — it belongs to the parts of the book that examine how these loans actually get valued once the phone call is over and the relationship, rather than a market, becomes the only source of truth about what the loan is worth. For now, taken on its own terms, the case holds. A borrower like the manufacturer in this story gets something real: capital that closes fast, stays private, watches closely enough to catch trouble early, and does not have to be sold into a panic to satisfy someone else's redemption request. An investor on the other side of that loan gets paid, in the form of a documented illiquidity premium, for giving up the option to trade out. Both sides of that transaction can be telling the truth about why they prefer it.

It is also, as it happens, not a new story. Credit has migrated away from the banking system to solve a real, specific problem before, and the pattern that followed — genuine innovation, explosive growth, capital chasing a return that looked too good relative to the risk disclosed, and then a reckoning that separated the durable parts of the innovation from the parts that had been mistaken for durable — has a precedent close enough to be uncomfortable. In the 1980s, a bond salesman in Beverly Hills built an entire market for financing that banks would not touch and public investors had been taught to avoid, on an argument that was, in its own way, just as sound as the one in this chapter: that risk priced honestly and correctly, even risk everyone else called junk, was worth more than risk banks had simply declined to underwrite. That market grew explosively, financed real companies that could not otherwise have gotten capital, made a great many long-term investors a great deal of money, and did all of that for years before anyone outside it had reason to ask whether the growth and the discipline were still moving at the same speed. History does not repeat itself in the mechanical way that makes for a tidy chapter ending, and the differences between that market and this one — what traded, what didn't, who could see the price — matter as much as the similarities. But it rhymes, and the next chapter picks up precisely there.
