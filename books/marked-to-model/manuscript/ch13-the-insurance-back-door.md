# The Insurance Back Door

An annuity is one of the dullest products the American financial system sells, and that dullness is the point. A saver, usually somewhere in her fifties or sixties, hands an insurance company a lump sum — often the proceeds of a retirement account she has spent thirty years building — in exchange for a promise: a fixed stream of income, sometimes for a set number of years, sometimes for as long as she lives. The insurer takes the money into what is called its general account, invests it however its own investment policy directs, and pays out claims from the earnings and, eventually, the principal of that pool. The saver does not pick the bonds. She does not see the portfolio rebalanced. She receives a statement, a customer-service number, and a promise backed by the state guaranty system and the insurer's own claims-paying ability. For most of the history of the American annuity business, that arrangement was about as exciting as a savings account, and the industry marketed it that way on purpose: stability, predictability, an insurer whose entire institutional culture was built around the actuarial discipline of not losing money it owed to people in retirement.

That arrangement has not disappeared, but underneath it something has changed, and the change runs through two of the largest transactions in the history of the life insurance industry. In January 2022, Apollo Global Management — one of the world's largest alternative asset managers, built on leveraged buyouts, distressed debt, and increasingly on direct lending — completed a full combination with Athene, the retirement-services insurer Apollo had helped found more than a decade earlier and had already substantially controlled. Two years later, on January 2, 2024, KKR completed its own version of the same maneuver, acquiring the remaining 37 percent of Global Atlantic Financial Group it did not already own and bringing the insurer to full ownership. Two of the industry's largest private equity firms, within roughly two years of each other, each finished the job of turning a life insurer into a wholly owned subsidiary. The mechanics differ in their particulars — Apollo and Athene arrived at full combination through a stock-for-stock merger of already-affiliated companies, KKR bought out Global Atlantic's remaining minority stake in cash and stock after having taken majority control in 2021 — but the structure that resulted is, for the purposes of this chapter, functionally the same in both cases. An alternative asset manager now owns, outright, an insurance company that collects retirement savings from ordinary people and invests them.

Apollo and KKR did not arrive at these arrangements by accident, and it helps to understand what each firm was actually buying, because the logic explains why this structure has spread rather than remaining a one-off curiosity. A private equity firm's traditional business runs on closed-end funds: raise a pool of capital from pensions and endowments, invest it over several years, return it with a profit, then go raise the next fund and start the cycle again. That cycle is lucrative but exhausting, and it exposes the firm to the unpredictable moods of institutional allocators who can simply decline to commit to the next vintage. An insurance company's general account offers something a closed-end fund cannot: a large, permanent, and — because policyholders cannot easily withdraw — remarkably patient pool of capital that keeps growing on its own as new annuities are sold and existing ones are reinsured in from other carriers, all of it available to be invested, and re-invested, without ever needing to be raised again from scratch. For a manager like Apollo or KKR whose core competency is originating and structuring long-duration private credit, an insurer's liabilities — fixed annuity payments stretching out ten, twenty, thirty years — are close to a perfect match for the kind of illiquid, long-dated loans direct-lending funds specialize in holding. The insurer gets an in-house source of investment expertise and, its owners argue, better returns than a traditional bond-heavy general account would generate. The asset manager gets permanent capital, a steady base of management fees insulated from the fundraising cycle, and a captive channel for the credit its own funds originate. Athene and Global Atlantic are the two highest-profile examples of that logic playing out at scale, but they are not the only ones; a widening circle of alternative asset managers has pursued some version of the same combination since the mid-2010s, drawn by the same structural appeal.

What makes that structure worth a chapter of its own, rather than a footnote to the story of private credit's growth, is what the asset manager gets to do once it owns the insurer. Apollo manages Athene's investment portfolio and earns a fee for doing so, the same kind of management fee it earns from any other pool of capital it runs. KKR does the same for Global Atlantic. That fee arrangement alone would not be unusual — insurers have long hired outside asset managers to run pieces of their general accounts. What is different here is that the asset manager collecting the fee is also, through its ownership of the insurer, the party setting the insurer's overall investment strategy, and that strategy increasingly directs a meaningful share of the portfolio into funds the asset manager itself sponsors: its own private credit vehicles, its own direct-lending funds, its own private equity deals. Apollo does not merely advise Athene on where to invest. Apollo, in a real sense, decides where Athene invests, then charges Athene to do it, and then, when a share of that money lands in an Apollo-managed credit fund, effectively pays itself a second time on the same dollar. KKR's relationship with Global Atlantic follows the same shape. Neither arrangement is hidden — both are disclosed in regulatory filings and both companies discuss the relationship openly in investor materials, a distinction this chapter returns to — but disclosure and the absence of a conflict are not the same thing, and the conflict here is structural rather than incidental. It exists by design, because the entire commercial logic of buying an insurer in the first place was to give the asset manager a large, stable, low-cost pool of long-duration capital it could deploy into its own strategies.

The scale of that pool, and how quickly it has grown, is the clearest evidence of how significant this channel has become. Global Atlantic managed roughly $72 billion in assets in 2020, before KKR's involvement deepened into majority and then full ownership. By the mid-2020s, under KKR's ownership and the asset-management partnership that came with it, that figure had grown to roughly $158 billion — more than double in the space of a few years, a rate of growth far faster than the ordinary business of writing new annuity policies could explain on its own. Some of that growth came from new sales, as Global Atlantic's annuity products, backed by a well-capitalized parent with a strong investment track record, attracted savers and the distribution networks that sell to them. Some of it came from reinsurance transactions, in which other insurers transferred blocks of existing annuity liabilities to Global Atlantic in exchange for a premium, adding both the liabilities and the assets backing them to Global Atlantic's balance sheet in a single stroke. Whatever the mix, the effect was the same: a rapidly expanding pool of retirement savings, managed by KKR, with KKR's own funds among the eligible destinations for a portion of it. Athene's growth under Apollo tells a broadly similar story, on a still larger scale, and the two together illustrate something the rest of the private credit industry has had to build more slowly and less certainly — a captive, permanent source of investable capital that does not depend on convincing an outside institutional allocator to write a check every few years. An insurance company's premiums arrive on a schedule. They do not need to be re-raised.

The plain version of the conflict, stripped of euphemism, runs like this. When the firm that owns an insurer also sets that insurer's investment strategy and also earns a management fee on the resulting portfolio, the firm is paid — through the ownership stake, through the management fee, through the fees embedded in the affiliated funds themselves — regardless of whether the specific allocation it chooses is the best one available for the people who are eventually going to depend on that portfolio to fund their annuity payments. A manager choosing between a comparable-risk loan originated by an unaffiliated lender and a loan originated by its own private credit fund has an obvious institutional incentive to prefer its own fund, because doing so keeps the fee inside the family rather than sending it to a competitor. That incentive does not require anyone to act in bad faith. It does not require the loans chosen to be worse loans. It requires only that the person making the allocation decision and the person collecting the fee on that decision be, functionally, the same institution, and that the people bearing the ultimate risk — the annuity holders — have no seat at that table and, in the ordinary course of business, no visibility into how the decision was made. Reporting on these portfolios in recent years has put a rough number on how far that dynamic has gone in practice: at some private-equity-owned insurers, on the order of a fifth of the investment portfolio now flows into funds affiliated with the parent asset manager, a proportion large enough that it is no longer accurate to describe the affiliated relationship as one input among many. It is a structural feature of the balance sheet.

It is worth pausing on why this matters more for an insurer than it would for, say, a mutual fund with a similar affiliated-fund allocation, because the difference is not abstract. A mutual fund investor can sell her shares tomorrow if she loses confidence in the manager's judgment; an annuity holder has, in most cases, already handed over an irrevocable lump sum in exchange for a promise that will not fully mature for years or decades, with steep surrender charges standing between her and any attempt to get the money back early. She cannot vote with her feet the way a mutual fund shareholder can, which means the ordinary market discipline that might otherwise punish a manager for self-dealing — investors simply leaving — barely functions in the annuity context. What she is relying on instead is the insurer's own claims-paying ability and the state regulatory apparatus built to make sure that ability holds up, which is precisely the apparatus this chapter now turns to, because it is the honest answer to the conflict just described: not that the conflict does not exist, but that a specific regulatory structure exists to police it, and that structure has been visibly tightening.

The insurance industry's own actuarial profession has not been quiet about why this matters, and its reasoning is worth borrowing before turning to what regulators have actually done. In analysis published by the Institute and Faculty of Actuaries, practitioners examining the private credit market's own stress cases — First Brands Group's collapse chief among them, the same bankruptcy this book examined several chapters ago — have framed the exposure question specifically from the insurer's side of the ledger: when a life insurer's general account holds privately originated credit, either directly or through an affiliated fund, the same diligence failures and valuation opacity that can sink a direct lender's return on a single bad loan flow straight through to the insurer's balance sheet, and from there to the reserves supposed to be sitting behind an ordinary annuity policy. An actuary signing off on an insurer's reserve adequacy is, in effect, vouching for the quality of every privately marked loan feeding into that calculation, whether that actuary worked inside the insurer, inside its parent asset manager, or as an independent reviewer. The point is not that Athene or Global Atlantic held First Brands paper, a claim this book has no evidence for and does not make. It is that the mechanism First Brands illustrated at the level of a single collapsed borrower — a collateral picture that looked fine on a lender's books until it suddenly did not — is exactly the kind of risk an actuarial reserve calculation has to anticipate in the aggregate, across thousands of positions, years before any single position actually goes bad. That is a harder problem when a meaningful share of the positions being reserved against were originated and marked by the same institution that owns the insurer doing the reserving.

State insurance regulators, working through the National Association of Insurance Commissioners, did not discover the private-equity-ownership trend recently or reluctantly. The growth of PE-owned and PE-affiliated life insurers through the 2010s and into the 2020s drew sustained attention from state regulators specifically because the structure these deals created — an asset manager both directing and profiting from an insurer's investment portfolio — was a genuinely new variation on an old regulatory problem, related-party transactions, at a scale and level of sophistication state insurance departments had not previously had to supervise. The NAIC's response took the form of a dedicated workstream, still active as this book was being written, built around what regulators have called a set of considerations for evaluating private-equity-owned insurers: essentially a checklist of the specific risk vectors this new ownership model introduces, covering the kind of investment strategy shift a PE owner tends to bring, the scale and structure of affiliated investments and reinsurance arrangements, the complexity and illiquidity of the assets an insurer's general account increasingly holds, and the adequacy of the capital an insurer sets aside against all of it. The framework is not a single binding rule with a bright-line test attached; it functions more like the interagency leveraged lending guidance of an earlier chapter did for banks — a document that shapes what an examiner looks for and asks about, rather than a statute a company can be sued for violating. But it represents an explicit acknowledgment, from the body that coordinates state insurance regulation nationally, that PE ownership changes the risk profile of an insurer in ways ordinary solvency oversight was not originally built to catch.

Law firms that advise insurers on regulatory compliance have tracked the arc of this scrutiny closely enough to describe it as a distinct and lengthening project rather than a single reactive gesture. Analysis from Mayer Brown, summarizing the origins and expanding scope of state-level scrutiny of private-equity-owned insurers, traces the concern back to the basic observation that drove the rest of this chapter: as more of the life insurance sector's assets moved under the ownership and investment direction of firms whose primary business was originating exactly the kind of complex, illiquid, privately negotiated credit that an ordinary insurance department's examiners had little occupational experience evaluating, state regulators faced a widening gap between the sophistication of the ownership structures they were supervising and the tools their statutes had given them to look inside those structures. That gap did not close in a single legislative session or a single NAIC meeting. It has closed, to the extent it has closed at all, through a sequence of incremental additions — the considerations framework, then enhanced disclosure, then Actuarial Guideline 55, then the investment-subsidiary phase-out — each one addressing a specific piece of the problem the last one had exposed, in a pattern regulators themselves have described as necessarily iterative given how quickly the ownership structures under review kept evolving.

Alongside that framework, the NAIC has pushed for enhanced disclosure inside the Annual Statement filings every insurer submits to its regulators, specifically around affiliation and control. An insurer now has to make more visible, in its regular regulatory filings, who controls it, what related-party investment arrangements exist, and how much of its portfolio sits with affiliated managers and affiliated funds — the categories of information a regulator would need to even begin evaluating whether the conflict described earlier in this chapter is being managed responsibly in a given case. This is not a trivial addition. Regulators cannot examine what they cannot see, and for a period after the PE-ownership wave began, the ordinary Annual Statement format was not built with this ownership structure in mind; it assumed, more or less, an insurer that was independently managed rather than one whose investment strategy was set by its own parent.

The most consequential recent step, and the one most specific to the risk this chapter has been describing, is Actuarial Guideline 55, adopted by the NAIC at its 2025 Summer National Meeting in August of that year. AG 55 requires insurers to conduct deeper analysis of reinsurance collectability and counterparty risk as part of their asset adequacy testing — the actuarial process by which an insurer has to demonstrate, on a regular basis, that its assets are sufficient to pay the liabilities it has promised policyholders under a range of plausible future scenarios. The guideline matters specifically in the PE-owned insurance context because many of these companies, Athene and Global Atlantic included, have built extensive reinsurance relationships with offshore affiliates, often domiciled in Bermuda, that assume large blocks of the parent insurer's annuity liabilities and, in exchange, hold and invest the assets backing them under looser reserve and capital rules than apply onshore. Reinsurance of this kind long predates the private-equity era and serves legitimate purposes — it can free up capital, diversify risk, and let an insurer write more business than it otherwise could. But when the reinsurer sits inside the same corporate family as the ceding insurer and the asset manager directing investment strategy on both ends of the transaction, an actuary evaluating whether the arrangement will actually pay claims twenty years from now needs to ask harder questions about counterparty risk than a standard asset adequacy test, built for arm's-length reinsurance, was designed to ask. AG 55 is the NAIC's answer to that gap: a requirement that the collectability of these affiliated reinsurance arrangements, not just their nominal existence on a balance sheet, gets tested.

A parallel change, working on a slightly different piece of the same problem, is the NAIC's move to eliminate the "investment subsidiary" reporting concept, effective for filings covering the period ending December 31, 2026. Under the old approach, an insurer that held its investments through a wholly owned investment subsidiary — rather than directly on its own books — could, in effect, look through to the subsidiary's underlying assets for purposes of certain risk-based-capital calculations without regulators necessarily getting the same granular visibility into what those underlying assets actually were that they would get from investments held directly. The concept had legitimate origins as an accounting convenience for insurers organizing straightforward investment holding structures. But as PE-owned insurers built increasingly elaborate networks of affiliated investment entities, some regulators grew concerned that the investment-subsidiary treatment let an insurer capture favorable risk-based-capital outcomes — meaning the subsidiary's holdings could be treated, for capital purposes, as safer or more diversified than a direct, itemized accounting of the underlying assets might have shown — without giving examiners a correspondingly clear view into what those assets actually were, how liquid they were, or how concentrated the exposure had become. Retiring the concept forces insurers back toward more direct, transparent reporting of what they actually hold, asset by asset, rather than through a structural layer that had started to function as much as a capital-optimization tool as an accounting convenience.

None of this regulatory activity is best read as proof that the system has already caught up with the risk it is trying to address, and it would be dishonest to present it that way. Actuarial Guideline 55 was adopted in August 2025 and only recently as this book was being written; the investment-subsidiary reporting change does not take effect until the end of 2026; the "considerations" framework guiding examiner attention to PE-owned insurers remains, by its own design, closer to a set of questions than to an enforceable standard. Regulators are working through a genuinely difficult technical problem — how do you value, and how do you stress-test, a pool of privately originated, infrequently traded loans sitting inside an insurance company's general account, when the same difficulty in valuing those assets that runs through the rest of this book applies here too — and they are doing it in something close to real time, alongside an industry whose affiliated fund structures kept evolving while the rulebook was still being written.

It is equally important, though, not to let that catch-up read as absence. State insurance regulation of life insurers rests on a foundation that predates the private-equity era by decades and was built precisely to absorb the kind of asset-side losses this chapter has been describing: risk-based capital requirements that force an insurer to hold more capital against riskier or less liquid assets, and statutory reserve requirements that require conservative, regulator-defined assumptions about future claims rather than the insurer's own optimistic projections. These are not decorative rules. They are the reason state insurance regulation has, over a very long history including the 2008 financial crisis, produced far fewer large insurer insolvencies than the banking and shadow-banking sectors have produced bank and nonbank failures over the same period, and they remain fully in force, applying to Athene and Global Atlantic exactly as they apply to every other life insurer domiciled in the United States. The affiliated investment relationships at the center of this chapter are disclosed, not concealed; both companies describe their asset-management relationships with their parent firms in public filings and investor presentations, and state regulators have direct examination authority over both. And the regulatory response traced in this chapter — the considerations framework, the enhanced disclosure, Actuarial Guideline 55, the retirement of the investment-subsidiary concept — is not the behavior of a system ignoring a known risk. It is the behavior of a system that identified a structural change in how a regulated industry does business and has spent the past several years visibly, if imperfectly, building the tools to examine it. An imperfect, catching-up regulator is a different thing entirely from an absent one, and the honest claim this chapter can support is the former, not the latter. Nothing in the record assembled here suggests that any specific annuity holder's payments are currently in jeopardy; the claim is about the shape of an incentive and the pace of the oversight built to check it, not about an insurer on the verge of failing to pay what it owes.

What the regulatory record does make clear is how much of the actual analytical burden still rests on judgment calls regulators are only now developing the tools to make well. An actuary running AG 55's reinsurance-collectability test still has to form a view, using models and assumptions, about whether a Bermuda-domiciled affiliate will actually be able to pay claims decades in the future — a judgment that depends, in no small part, on the quality of the private credit and private equity assets that affiliate is holding to fund that payment. Which raises a question this chapter has been circling without fully asking. If state regulators are still assembling the disclosure requirements and the actuarial guidelines needed to evaluate an insurer's affiliated holdings at the portfolio level, who is evaluating the individual loans inside that portfolio — the specific piece of privately negotiated, unrated-by-any-public-market debt sitting at the bottom of the chain, the asset an actuary's collectability test and a regulator's risk-based-capital formula ultimately depend on being worth what someone says it is worth? That question, about who rates the underlying loans and how much rigor that rating actually carries, is where this book turns next.
