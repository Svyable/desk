# The Cockroach Call

On the morning of October 14, 2025, JPMorgan Chase released third-quarter results that were, on their face, very good news. Net income came in at $14.39 billion, up twelve percent from the year before. Revenue reached $47.12 billion, up nine percent. Earnings per share landed at $5.07, well ahead of the $4.84 that Wall Street had modeled. The bank's trading desks turned in their best third quarter on record, an $8.9 billion haul, with fixed-income trading alone up twenty-one percent to $5.6 billion. JPMorgan traditionally reports first among the big American banks each earnings season, which means its numbers set the tone for how investors read everyone else's results that follow over the next two weeks. A reader who stopped at the press release that morning would have had no particular reason to keep reading, and no reason at all to expect that anything said on the call an hour later would still be quoted a year on.

Buried a few lines down in the same release was a less flattering number: a provision for credit losses of $3.4 billion, up nine percent year over year and above the roughly $3.08 billion analysts had modeled. A bank's provision is its own estimate of how much of its current lending it expects to lose, and a provision that comes in higher than forecast is a bank quietly telling its shareholders that more of its loan book is souring than Wall Street had assumed. On the conference call that followed the release, JPMorgan's executives walked through where that provision had gone, and one name kept coming up. Tricolor Holdings, a Dallas-based subprime auto dealer and lender that packaged loans made to buyers with weak credit into securities sold to outside investors, had filed for Chapter 7 bankruptcy a month earlier, on September 10, with federal prosecutors soon alleging that its executives had pledged the same loan collateral to more than one lender at once. JPMorgan's exposure to that collapse cost the bank a $170 million charge-off. Against $14.39 billion in quarterly profit, $170 million is not a number that moves a stock. It is the kind of figure that earns a clause in an analyst's note, gets read aloud once on the call, and is forgotten by lunchtime.

It did not stay that small. Before the call ended, $170 million had become the opening line of a story that the entire private credit industry would spend the following year trying to talk its way out from under — not because of the dollar figure, but because of what the bank's chief executive said about it once an analyst pushed him past the prepared numbers.

Earnings calls follow a script, and JPMorgan's are no different in structure from any other large bank's. A company reports results before the market opens, executives read prepared remarks, and then research analysts who cover the stock for a living take turns asking questions, usually short and technical: expense guidance, net interest margin, the pace of share buybacks. Jamie Dimon has sat through hundreds of these calls since taking over as JPMorgan's chief executive in 2005, and has built, across two decades that included the 2008 financial crisis, a reputation — deserved by most accounts, resented by some of his peers — as the banker least willing to stick to the script. His annual shareholder letters run to tens of thousands of words and are read well outside JPMorgan's own investor base because he tends to editorialize about the economy, about Washington, about risks he sees building, more freely than any other sitting bank chief executive. That habit is why, when an analyst on the October 14 call pressed him on the bank's exposure to nonbank lenders — the funds and specialty finance companies, private credit shops among them, that had spent the previous decade taking market share from banks in exactly the kind of lending Tricolor had done — the answer ran longer, and said more, than a compliance-minded executive would have allowed.

Dimon told the analysts that JPMorgan had gone back through its own underwriting after the Tricolor loss and believed the rest of its loan book was sound. He said that many of the private funds now competing with JPMorgan for borrowers were large, sophisticated, and genuinely capable at assessing credit risk — not, in his own words, an industry built on sloppy standards. He said he did not see evidence of a broad systemic problem. And then, in the same answer, almost as an aside, he added something that had nothing to do with guidance, or margins, or the quarter he had just reported.

"I probably shouldn't say this," Dimon told the analysts, "but when you see one cockroach, there are probably more. And so we should — everyone should be forewarned on this one."

He was talking, in that moment, mainly about Tricolor. But Tricolor was not the only carcass on the floor that week. Eighteen days before JPMorgan's call, on September 28, First Brands Group — an auto-parts supplier that had grown rapidly through acquisitions and was carrying more than ten billion dollars in liabilities — had filed for Chapter 11, amid allegations that the same receivables and inventory had been pledged as collateral to more than one lender at once, an arrangement that had let the company look far less indebted, on paper, than it actually was. JPMorgan itself had no direct exposure to First Brands. That fact did nothing to soften how the remark landed, because the coincidence was the point: two borrowers, in two unrelated industries, financed through two different corners of the same broad and largely unregulated lending market, had both collapsed within three weeks of each other, amid remarkably similar allegations about collateral that was not what it claimed to be. The head of the largest bank in the country had just told the room full of analysts, investors, and financial journalists who set the market's mood for the trading sessions ahead that when you find one insect, you should assume there are more you have not found yet.

The quote traveled the way quotes travel now — quickly, stripped of qualification, reduced to a headline before most of the people repeating it had listened to the rest of the call. Fortune ran it as a warning within a day. CEO Today Magazine, Banking Dive, Fox Business, and a run of other outlets that do not usually cover regional subprime auto lenders followed with their own versions of the same story, each one pairing the $170 million write-off with the six-word line as though the number explained the metaphor and the metaphor explained the number. Shares of the largest publicly traded private credit managers wobbled in the following session, a market twitch that made for an easy second-day story of its own. Neither the write-off nor the stock move, on its own, was large enough to justify the attention. What actually explained the reaction was something the quote never mentioned by name at all: a trillion-dollar-plus corner of the credit markets called private credit, which had spent the better part of a decade selling itself to pension funds, insurers, and — increasingly, after a 2025 executive order this book returns to later — ordinary retirement savers, as a source of steady, dependably priced income. Dimon never said the phrase "private credit" in the sentence that made news. He did not need to. Everyone listening to that call already knew which market he meant.

It is worth pausing here, before going further, to say plainly what that market is, because the rest of this book depends on the reader carrying a working definition rather than a mood. A company that needs to borrow money has, broadly, three places it can go. It can go to a bank, which lends against its own balance sheet and answers to examiners and capital rules that make certain kinds of risky lending expensive for the bank to hold — rules built and tightened, in large part, after 2008. It can go to the public bond market, selling debt that trades on exchanges and over-the-counter desks, priced every day by buyers and sellers who do not know the borrower personally and do not need to, because the price itself carries the information: a bond trading at eighty cents on the dollar is a market telling you, in real time, that something has changed. Or it can bypass both of those channels and go directly to a private fund — a firm like Apollo, Ares, Blackstone, KKR, or Blue Owl — and negotiate a loan one-on-one, with terms set by two parties in a room rather than by a crowd of strangers on a screen. That third option is private credit: lending that happens outside a bank's balance sheet and outside any public exchange, funded by money raised from pension funds, insurance companies, wealthy individuals, and, more recently, ordinary savers investing through their retirement plans. How large that market actually is depends on who is counting and what they choose to include, and different careful institutions arrive at meaningfully different totals — a disagreement this book takes seriously later rather than papering over now. By any of those measures, though, it is large enough, and had grown fast enough in the preceding several years, for a handful of bankruptcies inside it to matter well beyond the borrowers themselves.

It had also, by October 2025, stopped being a story that belonged only to pension funds and endowments. Two months before Dimon's remark, the White House had signed an executive order directing federal agencies to clear the path for private equity, private credit, and other so-called alternative assets to be offered inside ordinary 401(k) plans, and the Department of Labor had followed almost immediately by rescinding guidance that had made plan sponsors wary of including them. None of that machinery had finished turning by the time Dimon spoke; this book returns to it in detail later, and to how gradually or quickly it actually changes what sits inside a typical retirement account. But the direction was already set, which is part of why a joke about cockroaches made at a bank earnings call landed differently than it would have five years earlier. A worker with a target-date fund as the default option in her workplace retirement plan does not read JPMorgan's earnings release. She may never read a private credit fund's quarterly valuation report, or know that one exists. If a slice of her retirement savings is ever routed into that market, the six words a bank chief executive says on a call she has never heard of become, whether she knows it or not, a comment on her own future account balance.

The feature that makes private credit different from the other two channels is not the size of the checks or the names of the firms writing them. It is the absence of a price. A loan made through private credit does not trade. There is no ticker, no daily closing quote, no crowd of buyers and sellers arguing with each other in public about what the debt is worth this week versus last week. Once a quarter, the fund that made the loan sits down, largely on its own authority, and decides what it believes that loan is worth, then reports the resulting number to its investors as fact. There are governance layers around that process — valuation committees, outside auditors, sometimes an independent valuation firm brought in to check the fund's work — and this book will look closely at how well those layers actually hold up. But the basic architecture is unusual compared with almost any other major asset class investors are used to: the entity that originated the loan, and stands to benefit from investors believing it is healthy, is also, in practice, the entity most responsible for deciding what price to put on it.

That single feature — a market that prices itself, on its own schedule, without a trading floor to check its work — is what let a $170 million write-off and a six-word aside carry more weight than either would seem to deserve on its own. In a market with a visible daily price, a bad loan tends to announce itself gradually: a bond drifts down from par to ninety, then to seventy, over weeks or months, giving analysts, journalists, and regulators time to ask questions before the borrower actually defaults. In a market that prices itself quarterly and privately, the first visible sign of trouble is too often the bankruptcy filing itself, arriving with almost no public warning in any number an outside investor could have checked in advance. Neither Tricolor nor First Brands showed up as a gradually deteriorating credit in the months before their collapses, because in the world of private credit there was no public number to deteriorate. A remark from the head of the bank that had just absorbed one of those surprises functioned, for a market with no price ticker of its own, as something close to the only public instrument available for reading the temperature of the water. That scarcity — not the size of the write-off, and not even the memorable phrasing — is the real reason six words from one earnings call became the sentence an entire industry spent the following year being measured against.

That trust in Dimon's word is worth holding up to some scrutiny before accepting the cockroach line as simple prophecy, because Dimon was not speaking from outside the private credit market looking in with clean hands. JPMorgan is a direct competitor to the funds it was being asked about. In the years before that earnings call, the bank had been building out its own direct-lending capacity, chasing many of the same corporate borrowers that Ares, Apollo, and Blue Owl pursue, and it continues to originate, warehouse, and finance loans that end up, one way or another, inside private credit funds' own portfolios through the same subscription and warehouse lines this book examines later. A bank executive warning that a rival channel might be underpricing risk is not disinterested testimony, in the way a regulator's report or an academic study aims to be. It is, at least in part, a well-positioned competitor's opinion about a business that has taken real market share away from the traditional lending JPMorgan itself would rather be doing — leveraged loans and middle-market credit that a decade earlier would more often have stayed on a bank's own books, earning a bank's own interest income, rather than migrating to a fund that does not answer to the same examiners.

None of that makes the observation wrong. Bank credit officers and syndication desks see loan performance up close, across thousands of borrowers, in a way that gives a bank chief executive genuine information most outside commentators simply do not have. But it does mean the cockroach line deserves to be read as one very well-informed, self-interested party's assessment of a rival's book, not as a neutral verdict handed down from above the fray. And the same call that produced the warning also produced Dimon's more measured comments, given in the very same answer, that he did not see evidence of a broad systemic problem and that many of the funds now competing with his bank were sophisticated, careful underwriters rather than an industry of amateurs cutting corners. Both statements came from the same man, in the same breath, on the same morning. Financial media mostly kept one of them.

None of this means the remark was empty theater, or that the two bankruptcies behind it were pure coincidence. Tricolor's executives were criminally charged with running a scheme that allegedly double-pledged roughly $800 million in loan collateral to different lenders at once. First Brands collapsed amid allegations, still being sorted out in bankruptcy court as this book went to press, that the same receivables and inventory had been pledged more than once as well. Two unrelated companies, in unrelated industries, financed through different pools of capital, had both allegedly relied on some version of the same basic trick — showing more than one lender the same collateral and letting each one believe it held the only claim. That pattern, surfacing twice within three weeks in a market that had grown large enough and fast enough to draw genuine public attention for what felt like the first time, is exactly the kind of thing that makes a seasoned banker say something he later admits he probably should not have said out loud, on the record, in front of every analyst who covers his stock. The honest position, and the one this book tries to hold from its first page to its last, is that both things can be true at once: the remark was self-interested, and it also pointed at something real. How much of each is the work of the chapters that follow, not something to settle in the first one.

What the cockroach line did, more than anything else, was put a phrase around a feeling that had already been building quietly across the financial industry for longer than three weeks. Researchers at the Federal Reserve, the International Monetary Fund, and the Financial Stability Board had already been publishing careful, heavily qualified reports asking how exposed the regulated banking system had become to the unregulated lenders growing up alongside it. Academics had already been studying whether private credit funds were marking their loans to reflect real risk or to reflect what their own investors wanted to see. None of that work had broken through to a general audience; it lived in working papers and FEDS Notes that even most finance professionals had not read. A blunt sentence from the best-known banker in the country, delivered half against his own better judgment, on a morning when the numbers behind it were still fresh, did in six words what years of careful institutional research had not managed to do at all: it gave people outside the finance industry — many of whom, without fully realizing it, already held private credit somewhere inside their own retirement accounts — a single, memorable image for a market most of them had never heard of and could not have explained if asked.

That is the trouble with the image, though, and the reason this book does not stop here. A cockroach is a fine metaphor for the feeling of hidden trouble. It explains nothing about how the trouble actually gets hidden, or why a market this large could go three weeks between borrower collapses that each landed as a surprise rather than a slow-motion event anyone could see coming. Dimon's sentence told the world that something in private credit might be wrong without telling anyone what private credit actually is beyond a name, how a loan inside it gets made, who decides what that loan is worth once it sits on a fund's books, or why an ordinary saver might now be exposed to it without ever having consciously chosen to be. Those are mechanical questions, not rhetorical ones, and they have specific, traceable answers that do not require taking Dimon's word, or anyone else's, on faith. Before this book can weigh whether he was right to sound the alarm, self-interested in sounding it, or some honest mixture of both, the reader needs to understand the thing he was pointing at with the same clarity a credit officer would bring to reading a term sheet. That begins with a question simpler than any this chapter has raised so far: when a company borrows a hundred million dollars from a fund instead of a bank, what, mechanically, actually happens — and how is it different from borrowing that same hundred million dollars the old way?
