# Marked to Model

Suppose a mid-sized industrial company has two lenders. One holds a publicly traded high-yield bond, the kind that trades on a screen alongside thousands of other securities. The other holds a directly negotiated loan to the same company, made by a private credit fund, with the same seniority in the capital structure and roughly the same exposure to the same business. On a Tuesday afternoon when a competitor issues a profit warning and the whole sector sells off, the bondholder learns what its position is worth in about four seconds. A screen updates. The price ticks down two points, then three, and settles somewhere the market has collectively decided reflects the new information. Nobody voted on it. Nobody had to. Thousands of buyers and sellers, most of whom have never heard of the company, arrived independently at a number by trying to trade at it.

The private lender's phone does not ring with a price. Nothing updates. The loan sits on the fund's books at whatever it was carried at the day before, and it will keep sitting there — through the profit warning, through the sector selloff, through whatever comes next — until somebody decides to change it. That decision typically happens once a quarter. It is made not by a market but by a small number of people employed by, or under contract to, the fund that owns the loan, and in a meaningful share of cases the fund that owns the loan is the same institution that originated it, priced it, and sold it to the very investors who will read the resulting number on their statements. This is not a scandal. It is simply what fair-value accounting for an asset with no market looks like in practice, and it is the mechanical center of everything private credit promises and everything it risks concealing. The industry has a name for the number this process produces. It calls it a mark.

Marking an illiquid loan is, at bottom, an exercise in disciplined guessing, and it is worth walking through slowly, because almost nobody outside the industry has ever seen it done. A direct-lending fund typically starts each quarter with a model already built — the same discounted-cash-flow framework used to price the loan when it was originated, updated with whatever has changed. The analyst, often someone on the same deal team that sourced the loan in the first place, pulls the borrower's latest financials: revenue, EBITDA, leverage ratios, covenant compliance, whatever the credit agreement requires the company to report. From there the real judgment begins. The analyst has to pick a discount rate — the return a hypothetical buyer would demand to hold this specific loan today — and there is no ticker to check it against. So the analyst looks for comparables: yields on syndicated loans or high-yield bonds issued by companies in a similar industry, at a similar leverage level, with a similar credit rating, if one exists. The trouble, and it is not a small one, is that comparables in private credit are never actually comparable. A single-name, privately negotiated loan to a company with no public debt and no public equity does not have a twin. It has cousins. The analyst chooses which cousins count, how much weight to give each one, and how much of a spread to add or subtract for the ways this loan differs from the ones it is being measured against — tighter covenants, a smaller company, a different industry cycle, less information available to outside observers. Every one of those adjustments is a judgment call, made by someone with a professional and often a financial stake in where the number lands. And the analyst rarely comes to the model with a blank slate. The same person, or the same small team, usually underwrote the loan in the first place, presented it to an investment committee as a good credit, and has watched it perform, mostly as expected, for a year or two since. Asking that person to now select the inputs that will determine whether the loan is worth what the fund paid for it is asking them to grade their own homework using an answer key they also get to write.

The output of that process is usually blended from two approaches. An income approach discounts the loan's projected cash flows — interest payments, principal at maturity — back to the present at the chosen discount rate. A market approach, where any data exists at all, looks at recent trades or issuances of comparable debt and tries to back into an implied yield. The two approaches are reconciled, sometimes by a formula, sometimes by consensus among the people in the room, into a single fair-value mark for that loan for that quarter. Multiply that by several hundred loans across a fund's portfolio, and multiply that fund by the hundreds of others doing the same thing across the industry, and you have the process by which a market with no continuous price discovery nonetheless produces net asset values reported to the decimal point on investor statements every ninety days.

The gap between those two clocks — a public market that reprices continuously and a private fund that reprices once a quarter — is where most of what matters in this chapter actually lives. A publicly traded bond absorbs bad news the moment the news exists: an earnings miss, a downgrade, a competitor's warning, and the price adjusts before the close of trading that day. A private loan absorbs the same bad news only when the fund's own calendar says it is time to look. In the interim, real things can happen inside the borrower's business — a late payment, a covenant waiver granted quietly to avoid triggering a formal default, two consecutive quarters of softening orders — without any of it appearing in the fund's reported value, because nothing forces an interim look. This is not necessarily negligence. Nobody expects a fund to remark four hundred loans every time one borrower has a bad month. But it means the mark, by design, is a lagging indicator dressed up as a current one, current only as of its own last quarterly snapshot, and stale for every day in between.

It would be a mistake, and an unfair one, to describe this as unsupervised. Private credit funds — particularly business development companies and the interval funds increasingly sold to individual investors — operate under real governance structures built specifically to keep any one person's judgment from becoming the fund's official value. The valuation process typically runs through a valuation committee, a standing body inside the manager that reviews the marks proposed by deal teams before they become final. In principle this is a check: a second set of eyes, often more senior, asking whether a proposed markup or markdown is supported by the underlying credit metrics. In practice the valuation committee is usually staffed by the manager's own investment professionals and executives — people whose compensation, whose fund's reported performance, and whose firm's next fundraise are all, to varying degrees, affected by the number the committee approves. That is not evidence of misconduct. It is simply a structural fact worth sitting with: the body reviewing the mark and the institution that benefits from the mark are, more often than investors realize, the same institution wearing two hats.

The second layer of governance sits outside the manager, at least nominally. Most funds retain an independent third-party valuation firm to provide an outside opinion on some portion of the portfolio each quarter — typically a rotating subset rather than every position every time, since valuing hundreds of illiquid loans from scratch each quarter would be prohibitively expensive and slow. These firms are genuine specialists, and their involvement is a real constraint, not a rubber stamp for its own sake: a valuation adviser that consistently blesses aggressive marks risks its own reputation and its next engagement. But it is worth being precise about what the outside opinion actually certifies. The third-party firm is typically asked to confirm that a value falls within a reasonable range given a set of inputs — many of which, including the comparable universe and key assumptions about the borrower, are supplied by the manager itself. An independent valuation firm checking whether a manager's assumptions produce a defensible number is a meaningfully different exercise from an independent market checking whether a security can actually be sold for that number. The first tests internal consistency. The second tests reality.

The third layer is the annual audit, and it is real too, though it arrives on a slower clock than the quarterly marks it eventually reviews. Under the fair-value accounting rules that govern these funds, an illiquid loan with no observable market price is classified at the bottom of a three-tier hierarchy — the tier reserved for assets valued using assumptions the reporting entity itself must supply, because no one else has any to offer. An auditor signing off on a fund's annual financial statements is testing whether the valuation process was followed consistently and whether the resulting marks are reasonable given the inputs, generally on a sample basis across the portfolio rather than a full re-derivation of every position — re-performing the discounted-cash-flow math on a handful of the largest or most judgmental holdings, confirming that the comparables cited actually exist and were applied the way the policy describes, checking that a markdown flagged by the valuation committee earlier in the year eventually shows up in the numbers. That is a legitimate and useful discipline. It is not the same thing as an auditor independently discovering what four hundred private loans would actually sell for in a market that, by definition, does not exist to test them against. Valuation committees, outside valuation firms, and auditors are three real checks, stacked on top of one another, and dismissing them as theater would be both inaccurate and unfair to the professionals who take the work seriously. But all three checks operate on a foundation supplied, ultimately, by the same party whose fund benefits from the answer.

This is roughly the terrain that the Oxford economist Ludovic Phalippou and a broader body of academic and practitioner research on private markets have spent years mapping, and their findings, taken together, do not allege that fund managers are lying. They describe something closer to an information problem baked into the structure itself. Investors in private credit funds are, in the language this research uses, on one side of a persistent asymmetry: the manager sees the borrower's monthly financials, sits on the credit committee, negotiated the covenants, and controls the valuation model; the investor sees a quarterly NAV and has to take a great deal on faith. Combine that asymmetry with what the literature calls valuation smoothing — the well-documented tendency of appraisal-based and model-based marks across illiquid asset classes to move less, and later, than the true economic value of the underlying assets actually changes — and you get a specific, testable prediction: private credit returns, as reported, should look calmer than the credit risk they actually contain. Not because the risk isn't there. Because the pricing mechanism is built to notice it slowly.

The asymmetry Phalippou's work describes matters differently depending on who is standing on the other side of it. A large pension fund or endowment allocating to private credit typically employs its own staff of analysts, has read a decade of academic literature on smoothing, negotiates side-letter reporting rights, and treats a fund's quarterly NAV as one input among several rather than as gospel; institutions of that size have spent years learning to discount a private manager's marks the way a buyer learns to discount a dealer's asking price. A retail saver evaluating a private credit fund inside a brokerage account, or increasingly inside a retirement plan, generally has none of that infrastructure. The saver has the fact sheet, the fund's own quarterly letter, and whatever a financial adviser chooses to explain about how the number on it was built. The mechanism described in this chapter does not change based on who is looking at the output — the valuation committee, the third-party firm, and the auditor do the same work whether the fund's investors are a handful of sophisticated institutions or tens of thousands of individual retirement accounts. What changes is how much scrutiny meets the number on the other end, and how much room that leaves for an optimistic mark to travel unchallenged all the way to someone's retirement statement.

None of this requires anyone to be dishonest, and the honest version of the story is the more useful one to understand, because it is the one that survives scrutiny. A manager marking its own loans is not typically choosing between an honest number and a fraudulent one. It is choosing among several defensible discount rates, several plausible sets of comparables, several reasonable adjustments for a covenant package or a management change — and in a world of reasonable ranges, the incentives on the table are rarely neutral. A fund's fee base is frequently calculated as a percentage of reported net asset value, so a lower mark can mean a lower fee that quarter. A fund raising its next vintage wants a strong, stable track record on the marketing deck, and a portfolio full of markdowns does not build one. A manager's professional reputation — the thing that gets a fund's calls returned by borrowers, lenders, and other investors — is also, quietly, riding on every mark; a manager known around the market for writing down loans aggressively earns a reputation for caution that can cost it the next deal as easily as it wins it credibility. A deal team that originated a loan has already told its investment committee, its investors, and often itself that the credit is sound; marking it down is an admission that the original judgment may have been wrong, and that is a harder thing to write into a model than a spreadsheet cell suggests. None of these pressures has to be conscious or cynical to matter. A discount rate chosen at the generous end of a defensible range, quarter after quarter, by professionals who genuinely believe in their own credit work, produces exactly the same smoothing effect as bad faith would — slower, and far more durable, because there is no single moment anyone could point to and call it wrong.

The consequence shows up most vividly in what a private credit fund's return stream looks like next to a public one holding genuinely comparable risk. Picture two charts side by side. The first tracks a high-yield bond fund or a leveraged loan fund through a stretch of market stress — a rate scare, a credit-spread widening, a sector selloff triggered by one large borrower's bad quarter. The line moves. It drops sharply as the selloff hits, sits lower for a while as the market digests the news, and eventually recovers or doesn't, in full view, in real time, because every holding in that fund is priced continuously by people trying to trade it. The second chart tracks a private direct-lending fund holding loans to companies in the same industries, carrying comparable leverage, exposed to the same macro conditions. The line barely moves. It ticks up a fraction each quarter, smooth as a savings account, through the same stretch of market stress that visibly rattled its public cousin. An investor looking at both charts side by side, as many financial advisers do when building a pitch for a client's portfolio, sees an asset class that appears to have solved a problem the rest of the market still has: real credit exposure, without the stomach-churning volatility.

Some of that smoothness is genuinely earned, and the book's own thesis would be dishonest to pretend otherwise. A direct loan really is held differently than a traded bond. Nobody is forced to sell it into a panicked market at a fire-sale price, because there is no daily market demanding an exit; the lender committed capital for years, not for as long as it takes to hit a sell button, and that structural patience is a real form of protection against the kind of self-fulfilling liquidity spiral that can savage a mutual fund during a run. Floating-rate structures and maintenance covenants, the subject of an earlier chapter's argument for direct lending's genuine advantages, also mean these loans behave differently — often better — than a fixed-rate public bond when rates move or credit quality wobbles. But some of that smoothness is not earned by the underlying economics at all. It is manufactured, unintentionally, by a pricing process that only checks in once every ninety days, run by people with every professional incentive to find the top of a reasonable range rather than the bottom, using inputs nobody outside the fund can independently verify. The two effects are tangled together in every reported number, and there is no clean way, from outside the fund, to say how much of the calm belongs to each.

That tangle is precisely why low volatility has become one of the more effective selling points in private credit's marketing to date, and it is worth being plain about how directly the pitch trades on it. Fact sheets for private credit funds routinely feature a chart much like the smooth one just described, often alongside a Sharpe ratio — a standard measure of return per unit of risk — that looks remarkably attractive next to a comparable public high-yield or leveraged loan benchmark, precisely because the risk half of that ratio is calculated from a return series that barely moves. Financial advisers building retirement portfolios have been told, correctly as far as the marketing materials go, that adding private credit can lower a portfolio's measured volatility while maintaining or improving its yield. That claim is not fabricated. It follows mechanically from the numbers the funds report. What it leaves out is that the volatility being measured is the volatility of a quarterly, manager-produced mark, not the volatility of the underlying credit risk itself, and those two things are not the same measurement even when they are describing the same loan.

The pitch built on that chart tends to follow a familiar shape, whether it arrives in a wholesaler's slide deck or a quarterly letter mailed to existing investors. Private credit gets framed as offering equity-like or high-yield-like income with a fraction of the swings, a low correlation to public stocks and bonds that makes it a natural diversifier, a way to collect a steady coupon while everyone else white-knuckles their brokerage app during a selloff. During exactly the stretches when public credit markets are visibly nervous, investor letters from private funds tend to strike a calm, almost reassuring tone — performance was stable, the portfolio is well positioned, nothing here requires your attention — and the reassurance is not manufactured out of nothing. It reflects a number that, quarter after quarter, really did come in smooth. The letter is not lying about what the model produced. It is simply not the letter's job to explain that a model, not a market, produced it.

It is worth pausing on one more piece of honest counterevidence before pressing further into the implications, because private credit did not invent this practice and does not deserve sole credit, or sole blame, for it. Quarterly marking with a built-in lag between an event and its reflection in reported value is standard across illiquid asset classes generally. Commercial real estate funds have relied on appraisal-based indices for decades — the widely cited NCREIF Property Index is built the same way, from periodic appraisals of buildings nobody is actively trying to sell — and researchers have documented the same smoothing pattern in property values that shows up here — a pattern well known enough inside real estate that industry professionals routinely discount reported appraisal returns when comparing them to publicly traded real estate securities, such as real estate investment trusts, whose share prices move every day for exactly the reason a private fund's marks do not. Infrastructure funds, timberland funds, and private equity funds all mark to model on similarly infrequent schedules, using similarly manager-supplied inputs, reviewed by similarly structured valuation committees. None of this is new, and none of it was invented by direct lenders looking for a marketing edge. What has changed is the audience. Institutional investors — pension funds, endowments, insurance companies — have spent decades learning, sometimes the hard way, how to discount a smooth private-market return series before comparing it to a public one. A retail saver looking at a fact sheet for the first time, choosing among options in a retirement account, generally has not.

The claim this chapter wants to leave standing, then, is a narrower and more defensible one than the version that shows up in the more excitable corners of financial commentary. It is not that private credit marks are fraudulent, or that the managers producing them are lying to their investors. Most of them, most of the time, almost certainly are not. It is that the entire apparatus — the manager-built model, the manager-selected comparables, the valuation committee staffed by the manager's own people, the third-party firm testing internal consistency rather than market reality, the auditor sampling a process rather than rediscovering a price — is structurally built to make optimistic marking easy, comfortable, and defensible for honest people acting in good faith, one quarter at a time, for years, without anyone involved ever having to make a single indefensible decision. A mark does not have to be wrong to be a guess dressed as a fact. It only has to sit unchallenged long enough for everyone reading it to forget that a guess is what it was.

A guess like that can sit unchallenged indefinitely, as long as nobody needs the cash. That is the condition under which private credit marks have mostly operated so far — a growing market, patient capital, redemptions that could be met out of ordinary cash flow or new investor money coming in the door faster than existing investors wanted theirs back. Under those conditions, a mark rarely has to answer to anything harder than the next quarter's valuation committee meeting, and a manager whose model consistently finds the generous end of a reasonable range can go years without a single external event forcing the question of whether that range was reasonable at all.

The mark on a statement is, in the end, only a number until somebody tries to convert it back into dollars they can actually spend. It is at that exact moment — not when the loan is made, not when the quarterly valuation committee meets, but when an investor asks for their money back and the fund has to decide whether it can, or will, hand it over on the terms it originally promised — that a model-based mark finally meets something resembling a market test. A fund can carry a loan at ninety-eight cents on the dollar for eight consecutive quarters with no one able to prove the number wrong, precisely because no one was asking it to prove anything by actually selling. The test arrives only when redemption requests do. What happens when a large number of investors ask for that test at the same time, in a fund built around promises of periodic liquidity that were always narrower than they sounded, is the subject the next chapter takes up directly.
