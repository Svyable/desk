# Rating the Unratable

A public bond downgrade is, among other things, a broadcast event. When Moody's or S&P moves a rating on a widely held corporate bond, the action goes out over the same wire services that carry earnings releases and central bank statements, lands on every trading desk's screen within seconds, and starts repricing the security before most of the humans involved have finished reading the press release. The rating is published. It sits on the agency's public website, indexed by CUSIP, available to a competitor analyst, a journalist, a retail investor with a brokerage login, or a regulator with no relationship to the deal at all. Anyone who thinks the agency got it wrong can say so, and more usefully, can act on that opinion by buying or selling the bond, which is exactly what happens: a bond's market price is, among other things, a continuous referendum on whether the rating attached to it still makes sense. A rating agency that persistently rates too generously on the public side eventually shows up in its own data, because the spread at which its investment-grade bonds actually trade drifts away from the spread investment-grade bonds are supposed to command, and everyone with a Bloomberg terminal can watch it happen in real time.

A private letter rating is a different kind of document, and the difference starts with the noun. It is, in the most literal sense, a letter — typically a handful of pages, addressed by name to the single institution that requested and paid for it, marked confidential, and never filed anywhere a stranger could stumble across it. The loan it describes is not a bond with a CUSIP trading in a market of buyers and sellers who have priced in everything they know; it is a bespoke instrument negotiated bilaterally between a borrower and a lender, or a small handful of lenders, with terms — covenants, pricing, maturity, the specific carve-outs a private equity sponsor's lawyers fought for on a Tuesday night — that exist nowhere else and were built for this one deal alone. There is no comparable security trading anywhere for an outside observer to check the letter against, because there is nothing else quite like the thing it rates. The rating agency's analysts do real work to produce it: they build a credit model, review the borrower's financials, apply the same fundamental frameworks — leverage multiples, interest coverage, industry risk, management quality — that inform a public rating. But the finished product goes to one reader. It does not get picked up by an index. It does not move a market, because there is no market for it to move. Nobody outside the room in which it was requested has any occasion to ever see it, unless a regulator specifically asks. Ask a private credit executive why the letter stays private and the answer is rarely evasive: the borrower is often a family-owned or sponsor-backed company that would never agree to have its financials, its covenant package, or its management's internal projections made public in the first place, and a privately negotiated loan is, almost by definition, a transaction both sides wanted kept out of general view. That is a legitimate business reason, not a cover story invented after the fact. It is also, whatever its legitimacy, the reason nobody but the requesting party and the agency that wrote the letter has any way to know whether the number on the last page holds up.

That last fact is not a technicality. It is the whole difference. A public rating is checked, continuously and by construction, by thousands of people with money at stake in whether it is right. A private letter rating is checked by nobody except the rating agency's own internal review process and, now, an insurance regulator paging through a filing months or years later. The instrument being rated has no trading history to fall back on if a reader wants a second opinion, because it was never designed to trade. It was designed to be held — often to maturity, often by the single institution that financed it in the first place — which means the rating attached to it is not really being tested by markets so much as it is being trusted, for as long as the loan exists, by whichever institution decided to rely on it when the loan first went on its books.

The reason any of this matters beyond the abstract question of epistemics is that a rating on an insurance company's balance sheet is not a badge. It is a number with a dollar figure attached. State insurance regulation ties the capital an insurer must hold against a bond investment directly to that bond's credit quality: an asset that clears the investment-grade threshold requires meaningfully less capital sitting behind it than one that does not, freeing the insurer to deploy the difference elsewhere — into new business, into distributions, into further investment — rather than leaving it parked against a risk regulators judge unlikely to materialize. For an insurer holding a privately placed loan with no public rating of its own, the private letter rating is frequently the entire basis on which that capital determination gets made. A rating that lands on the investment-grade side of the line is not merely an opinion about credit quality. It is money, released the moment the letter is filed, for as long as nobody looks closely enough to ask whether the letter earned it.

The mechanism by which that happens runs through an office most policyholders have never heard of and most reporters covering the industry rarely name. State insurance regulation, coordinated through the NAIC, sorts fixed-income holdings into a small set of credit-quality designations, and an insurer's required capital scales sharply as a holding moves down that scale — an investment-grade designation at the top of the range costs an insurer a small fraction of the capital that a designation near the bottom would require against the identical dollar amount invested. For a bond with a public rating from a recognized agency, the designation mapping is close to mechanical: the public rating translates almost directly into the NAIC category. For a privately placed loan with no public rating, an insurer can instead submit the private letter rating itself to justify the designation it wants to use, which means the letter is not simply an opinion sitting in a file somewhere. It is the direct input into a formula that tells the insurer how much of its own capital it gets to keep working somewhere else.

The previous chapter traced how much of that dynamic now runs through insurers built, in substantial part, by the same private equity firms that also manage the credit those insurers hold — Apollo standing behind Athene, KKR standing behind Global Atlantic, an arrangement in which the asset manager earns fees on capital it also has real influence over directing. Layer the private rating letter into that picture and the incentive structure gets one degree more pointed. The loan in question may have been originated or arranged, in whole or in part, by an affiliate of the very insurer whose balance sheet is asking what capital charge it deserves. The rating that answers that question is produced by an agency the insurer or its manager is paying, on a bespoke instrument no outside party can independently price, and delivered in a document nobody but the requesting institution and, eventually, a regulator will read. None of that makes the resulting rating wrong. It does mean the private letter rating sits at the exact point in the private credit machine — insurer capital treatment, affiliated origination, no market check — where the book's central worry about self-dealing without correction is most concentrated, which is presumably why regulators eventually turned their attention there.

They did, and in fairly specific terms. The National Association of Insurance Commissioners now requires insurers holding assets that carry a private letter rating to file what it calls a Private Rating Letter Rationale Report, due within ninety days of either the annual update to an existing private rating or any change to the rating itself. The requirement is not a request for the rating — insurers were already reporting the rating designation on their annual statements. It is a request for the reasoning underneath the rating, filed separately, on its own schedule, specifically so that a regulator reviewing an insurer's book can see not just what number an agency assigned but why, in enough detail to evaluate whether the analysis holds up. And the NAIC did not leave the bar for that reasoning to the reader's imagination. Its own language is explicit: the rationale report must, in its phrasing, "possess analytical substance." That is an unusual thing for a regulator to feel compelled to specify. Nobody writes a rule demanding that documentation contain actual analysis unless the documentation regulators had been seeing did not reliably contain enough of it.

It is worth sitting with what that requirement implies, because the implication is more interesting than the rule's bureaucratic phrasing suggests. Regulators do not typically legislate against a problem they have not observed some version of. A rule demanding "analytical substance" behind a rating letter is not, on its face, an accusation that any specific private rating was fraudulent or captured; it is a judgment, made by people who review these filings for a living, that the rationale documentation attached to private ratings had not been uniformly demonstrating the kind of rigor the capital relief riding on those ratings would seem to require. Picture the range of what a private letter rating can actually look like in practice, because the range is wide in a way a public rating's is not. A public investment-grade rating on a large corporate bond typically reflects years of the agency's own methodology, a rating committee process, ongoing surveillance, and a track record the agency has to defend against the whole market's scrutiny every time the bond trades. A private letter rating on a single unitranche loan to a portfolio company nobody outside the deal has ever heard of might be the analyst's first and only look at that borrower, completed on a timeline set by the closing date the private equity sponsor wants, for a fee paid by a party with an obvious interest in the number coming back above the investment-grade line. Both processes can be done well. Nothing about the private version is inherently less rigorous. But nothing about it was previously required to prove, in writing, that it had been.

There is also a narrower, more mundane version of the incentive problem that any private-ratings market has to manage, and it is worth naming because it is the one closest to what public-market critics usually mean when they invoke "ratings shopping." A borrower or an arranger seeking a private letter rating is not obligated to use only one agency, and nothing prevents a party unhappy with an indicative number from one shop from quietly approaching another before committing to a request that becomes part of the permanent record. Public ratings face a version of this same dynamic — issuers of public bonds have long been known to solicit more than one agency and favor the friendlier outcome — but a public rating that eventually gets published is at least visible for a market to react to regardless of which agency wrote it. A private letter rating that never gets requested in the first place, because an informal conversation suggested the number would land somewhere the insurer did not want, leaves no public trace of the road not taken. That is not evidence any specific insurer has done this. It is evidence that the discipline preventing it — reputation, professional norms, the NAIC's own review of what gets filed — is doing more of the work than a visible market check would otherwise have to do, and the rationale-report requirement is, among other things, an attempt to make that hidden work checkable after the fact.

None of this should collapse into an indictment of the agencies doing the rating, and the book's own discipline requires saying plainly why not. Moody's, S&P Global, KBRA, and Fitch built dedicated private ratings groups because insurers, pension funds, and other sophisticated institutional buyers wanted an independent read on credit quality for instruments that would never trade publicly, and those groups are staffed largely by the same analysts, trained in the same methodologies, that produce the agencies' public ratings. They have decades of default and recovery data to calibrate against, genuine sector expertise, and their own reputational capital riding on getting it right — an agency whose private ratings prove systematically too generous eventually loses the very insurers and asset managers who pay for its opinion, because those institutions answer to their own regulators and auditors, and a rating that keeps producing surprises stops being worth paying for. The NAIC's new filing requirement is best read as evidence of a system correcting itself in response to a real gap, not as a finding that the gap had already produced bad outcomes. If private ratings, once this documentation requirement has been in force for a few annual cycles, turn out to show default and recovery experience broadly in line with what comparable public ratings would have predicted, that would be real evidence against this chapter's worry, and the book should say so plainly when that evidence exists. It does not exist yet. The requirement is too new, and the industry's response to it — whether the rationale reports substantively deepen the analysis behind these ratings or simply generate more paper that says the same thing at greater length — is one of the more concrete, checkable things a reader of this book could watch for in the NAIC's own future filings.

The rating-letter requirement did not arrive in isolation. It landed inside a broader 2024–2026 period in which the machinery for overseeing privately negotiated credit was visibly still being assembled, with some pieces going up and others coming down in the same rough window. In June 2024, the Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals vacated the SEC's Private Fund Adviser Rule in its entirety, ruling that the Commission had exceeded the rulemaking authority Congress actually gave it under the Investment Advisers Act. The rule, adopted less than a year earlier, would have required private fund advisers to give investors detailed quarterly disclosures of fees and expenses, obtain annual audits, and disclose or restrict certain preferential treatment given to some investors over others — the side letters and priority arrangements that let a fund's largest or earliest backers negotiate terms smaller investors never see. The court's decision turned on the SEC's statutory authority to write that particular rule, not on a judicial finding that the underlying disclosure goals were bad policy; the vacatur was a separation-of-powers ruling, not a verdict on transparency. But the practical effect was the same either way. A disclosure regime aimed at exactly the kind of privately negotiated fund structure this book has spent a dozen chapters inside never took effect, and as of this writing no comparable replacement has. Regulators trying to build oversight infrastructure for private credit are working, in other words, against a backdrop where one significant attempt at it was struck down on procedural grounds within a year of being written, even as another regulator, working a different corner of the same market from the insurance side, was concluding it needed more documentation, not less.

Taken together, the pattern that emerges is not one of a mature oversight system with settled rules everyone has learned to work within. It is a system still finding out, piece by piece and mostly after the fact, where its blind spots are. Bank lending has two disciplines that reliably surface credit deterioration early: an examiner who can walk into a bank and ask hard questions about a loan's underwriting, backed by the threat of a worse supervisory rating, and a public market that prices bonds every day and moves the price before most participants have consciously updated their view. Private credit was built, in large part, to route around both — that is a real part of its value proposition to borrowers who want speed and confidentiality, not merely a loophole its practitioners stumbled into. But routing around both disciplines means whatever replaces them has to actually do the work those disciplines used to do, and what has replaced them — valuation committees marking positions quarterly using inputs the manager itself selects, redemption gates that only activate once investors are already trying to leave, NAIC capital rules and now rationale reports arriving years after the assets they govern were already on insurers' books — is newer, thinner, and still visibly under construction while trillions of dollars already sit inside the structure it is meant to police. None of the individual pieces going up — a rationale-report requirement here, an actuarial guideline there, a designation methodology under periodic review — is trivial, and taken as a trend line they describe regulators moving in a consistent direction: toward more documentation, more standardized review, more places where an outside party can eventually ask a private credit institution to show its work. The honest complaint is not that nothing is being built. It is that the building is happening years after the money it is meant to govern already moved in, on a schedule set by regulators discovering gaps rather than by anyone anticipating them in advance.

It is tempting, at this point in the argument, to reach for 2008, and the temptation deserves a direct answer rather than a vague gesture in either direction. The rating failures that sit at the center of the last financial crisis were not, in the main, failures of individual analysts misjudging individual borrowers. They were a structural failure specific to pooled, tranched securitization: collateralized debt obligations built from thousands of anonymous subprime mortgages, sliced into tranches whose ratings depended on assumptions about how correlated all those individual defaults would turn out to be, rated at industrial scale by agencies whose fee depended on the issuer that had assembled the pool in the first place — and issuers could and did shop the same pool of loans among multiple agencies until they found the rating each agency's model would tolerate, then structured the deal to that threshold. When the correlation assumption broke down and mortgages across the pool started defaulting together rather than independently, the failure did not stay contained to one tranche or one deal; it cascaded through every structure built on the same assumption at once, because the assumption itself, not any single credit judgment, was the load-bearing element. Most private credit today is a genuinely different animal. A unitranche loan to a single mid-market company is single-name, individually underwritten corporate credit — one borrower, one balance sheet, one business to actually understand — not a pool of thousands of obligations whose rating depends on a correlation model nobody can fully test until the correlations actually show up. There is no tranching arbitrage in a bilateral loan, no assembly line rating thousands of securities a quarter, no mechanism by which one bad assumption breaks dozens of structures simultaneously in the specific way it did in 2008. Collapsing the two stories into a single "ratings agencies were captured, again" narrative would erase a structural difference the evidence plainly supports and the book owes its reader.

That distinction should not do more work than it has earned, though, and the honest version of this chapter has to hold both halves at once. The conflict that made 2008's rating failures possible in the first place — the issuer, or the party who most benefits from a favorable rating, is also the one paying the agency for it — is fully present in the private letter rating market too; nothing about moving from pooled structured finance to single-name corporate credit removes the fact that someone with a direct financial stake in the outcome is the customer. And the absence of a public trading history to check a rating against, which this chapter has treated as the private letter rating's defining feature, is if anything a more severe version of the vulnerability 2008 exposed, not a milder one. A mispriced CDO tranche at least existed inside a broader structured-finance market where comparable tranches, however illiquid, eventually traded somewhere and generated some signal. A bespoke unitranche loan to a single borrower has no comparable instrument anywhere, which means a private letter rating that drifts from reality has essentially nothing external to correct it before the borrower itself either pays or does not. The 2008 mechanism — tranching, correlation modeling, rating shopping at industrial scale — is not what is happening here, and precision requires saying so. But the deeper vulnerability underneath that mechanism, a rating insulated from any market able to contradict it, is arguably more acute in a market built almost entirely out of instruments that were never meant to trade at all.

Play that vulnerability forward and the practical asymmetry becomes clear. When a public bond's rating turns out to have been too generous, the market usually finds out before the agency's next scheduled review, because the bond's price starts drifting on its own as buyers grow uneasy, spreads widen, and a downgrade often arrives already priced in rather than as a surprise. A private letter rating has no equivalent early-warning channel. The loan sits on the insurer's books, paying cash interest or, increasingly in a stressed credit, deferring some of it as payment-in-kind rather than defaulting outright — a mechanism this book has already examined for what it can quietly hide — and the rating attached to it simply holds, unchallenged by anything except the next scheduled annual review, until the day the borrower genuinely cannot pay. By that point the rating has already done its most consequential work: it set the capital charge the insurer held, or did not hold, for however many years the loan sat there looking fine. The NAIC's rationale-report requirement is aimed precisely at that gap — forcing a documented re-examination at least once a year, and immediately whenever a rating changes, rather than letting a private rating coast on inertia between look-ins. Whether an annual paper review closes a gap that a live market used to close every trading day is exactly the kind of question this book cannot answer in advance and does not pretend to.

All of this — the models behind a quarterly mark, the gates that activate when too many investors try to leave at once, the private equity-owned insurer buying its own affiliate's credit, and now the private letter rating that sets how much capital sits behind it — has, up to this point in the story, still been substantially a tale about institutional money: pension funds, insurance companies, endowments, and the sliver of individually wealthy investors sophisticated or well-advised enough to be let in as accredited buyers. Whatever the flaws traced across the last several chapters, the people bearing the direct consequences of a bad private credit surprise were, through 2024, professional investors with staff paid to read a rationale report, or none at all, and decide whether to trust it. A pension fund's investment committee can ask an asset manager pointed questions about how a private letter rating was derived. An ordinary saver looking at a quarterly 401(k) statement cannot, and until 2025 had no occasion to, because nothing in a standard employer-sponsored retirement plan put a privately rated loan anywhere near that saver's account in the first place.

That changed in 2025. In August of that year, the White House and the Department of Labor opened the door that had kept ordinary retirement savers on the other side of all of this — valuation practices built for institutions, redemption terms institutions understood, and rating letters institutions had at least the standing to question. The chapter that follows walks through exactly how, and how quietly, that door came open.
