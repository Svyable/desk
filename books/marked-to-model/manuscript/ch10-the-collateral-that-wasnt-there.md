# The Collateral That Wasn't There

The statistics in the last chapter described a pattern in the aggregate — a rising share of direct-lending investments quietly converting cash interest into more debt, a slow leak that regulators and researchers could only see by pooling thousands of loans together and watching the percentages creep. PIK data can tell you that something in the system is under strain. It cannot tell you which company, or what the strain will look like when it finally reaches the surface. On September 28, 2025, that question got a specific answer. The pattern stopped being an aggregate. It became a company, a bankruptcy court docket, and a number: more than $10 billion.

That figure put First Brands Group among the largest corporate bankruptcy filings of 2025, in the company of names far better known to the general public. It arrived with none of the advance warning that usually accompanies a failure of that size — no drawn-out ratings downgrade cycle, no quarter of missed earnings calls, no public bond price sliding for months while analysts wrote cautious notes. One week First Brands was a private company servicing a syndicate of lenders who, by every account available before the filing, believed they held a reasonably secured position in a durable, cash-generative industrial business. The next week it was in Chapter 11, and the lenders were trying to work out what, exactly, they actually owned.

Almost nobody outside the auto-parts trade and the small world of private credit underwriting had heard the name First Brands Group before that week, which was itself part of the point. It was not a household brand. It was a holding company, built over roughly a decade through a long string of acquisitions, that had stitched together dozens of smaller manufacturers and distributors into one enterprise making and moving the unglamorous parts that keep an aging vehicle fleet on the road — brakes, filters, belts, hoses, fuel and water pumps, the aisle of a parts store nobody browses for pleasure. It was exactly the kind of business direct lenders had spent the previous decade learning to love: asset-heavy, demand that does not evaporate in a downturn because people keep driving old cars rather than buying new ones, steady free cash flow, and an owner who preferred a confidential loan from a handful of relationship lenders to the disclosure obligations of a public bond. Chapter 4 made the case for exactly this kind of borrower. First Brands, on paper, was the case.

Roll-ups like this are built by buying, not by growing organically — one manufacturer this year, a distributor with an overlapping customer list the next, a supplier of some component the combined company used to buy from someone else. Each acquisition usually arrives with its own legal entity, its own historical financing relationships, and sometimes its own pre-existing lender still owed something on assets the parent company now considers part of one integrated enterprise. Consolidating all of that into a single set of audited financials that a term-loan syndicate can underwrite is genuinely difficult accounting work even when everyone involved is acting in good faith. It is also, as the allegations against First Brands would eventually suggest, work that creates room to hide things, because a structure with dozens of subsidiaries has dozens of places a financing arrangement can live without ever crossing the desk of a lender focused on the consolidated parent.

What the filing revealed, and what a widening set of lender lawsuits and forensic accounting since have alleged, is that the balance sheet lenders thought they were underwriting was not the whole picture. The core allegation, still being tested in bankruptcy court and in litigation that had not resolved as this book went to press, is that First Brands financed a significant part of its working capital through receivables factoring and related off-balance-sheet arrangements — and that some of the same receivables and inventory pledged as collateral in those arrangements were also pledged, separately, to the term-loan lenders who believed they held a senior claim on those assets. If true, it means two or more lenders each held a security interest in the same invoice, or the same pallet of brake pads sitting in the same warehouse, each unaware the other's claim existed. That is the phrase that gives this chapter its title: collateral that showed up on more than one lender's books and, in the arithmetic that matters in a bankruptcy, could not actually cover both.

Nobody in the lender group appears to have suspected any of this before the filing. What changed their understanding was the ordinary, unglamorous process that follows almost every large bankruptcy: restructuring advisers and forensic accountants, hired by the company and by competing creditor committees, sat down to reconcile what the borrowing-base certificates said was pledged against what a physical and documentary count of the assets actually supported. That reconciliation is where the discrepancies reportedly surfaced — inventory levels and receivables balances that did not match what multiple lenders had each been told, separately, was theirs alone. It is worth being precise about the sequence, because it matters for how the reader should weigh the allegations: this was not a regulator catching the company in the act, and it was not a lender's routine periodic audit flagging the problem before it became urgent. It was bankruptcy itself, and the adversarial scrutiny bankruptcy invites, that produced the first outside look detailed enough to notice the overlap.

It is worth pausing on what receivables factoring actually is, because the word does a lot of quiet work in this story and most readers have never had a reason to learn it. A company that manufactures and ships goods does not get paid the moment the truck leaves the loading dock. It sends an invoice — to an auto parts retailer, to a repair-shop chain, to another manufacturer — and waits thirty, sixty, sometimes ninety days to collect. That gap between shipping the goods and receiving the cash is where a lot of ordinary businesses run into trouble, because payroll and suppliers do not wait ninety days. Factoring solves that gap by selling the invoice, or borrowing against it, to a specialized lender who advances most of its face value today in exchange for a fee, then collects the full amount from the customer later. It is a legitimate, widely used tool, older than modern banking, and by itself it is not a red flag. What makes it relevant here is structural: a factoring facility is frequently arranged through a separate financing subsidiary, sometimes an entity that exists only to buy and hold receivables, governed by its own agreement with its own lender, and it does not necessarily appear as debt on the parent company's audited balance sheet the way a term loan does. Depending on how the transaction is structured, the receivables can be treated as sold rather than borrowed against, which changes how — or whether — they show up in the consolidated financial statements a term-loan lender reviews before signing a credit agreement.

Picture one invoice. First Brands ships a pallet of brake pads to a regional auto parts chain and bills it $50,000, due in sixty days. Under one arrangement, First Brands could pledge that same $50,000 receivable to its term-loan lenders as part of the collateral securing their loan — the receivable sits on a schedule, gets counted toward the borrowing base, and the lender's credit agreement treats it as an asset backing their claim. Under a separate factoring arrangement, arranged perhaps through a different subsidiary and a different financing counterparty, First Brands could also receive an advance today against that identical $50,000 invoice, in exchange for assigning the right to collect it once the sixty days are up. Each lender, reviewing its own paperwork, sees a receivable it believes is pledged to it. Neither necessarily sees the other transaction, because the two arrangements were never designed to be cross-checked against each other by anyone outside the company. Multiply that single invoice by the receivables and inventory of a manufacturer doing billions of dollars in annual revenue across dozens of subsidiaries, and the scale of what the allegations describe comes into focus: not one invoice pledged twice, but a systemic practice of doing so, allegedly, across a large share of the working-capital base the company used to run its business.

That is the diligence gap the First Brands case put on display. A term-loan lender doing standard underwriting reviews audited financial statements, management projections, and a borrowing base built from the assets pledged specifically to that loan. What that lender does not automatically see is every other financing arrangement operating inside the borrower's corporate family — every factoring facility, every supply-chain-finance program, every subsidiary-level credit line — unless the borrower discloses it, or unless the lender independently searches every relevant public lien registry for every entity in a sprawling, multi-subsidiary structure and cross-references what it finds against its own collateral schedule. First Brands, like many roll-up companies built through years of acquisitions, had exactly that kind of structure: a parent company sitting atop a large number of operating subsidiaries, each with its own legal identity, any one of which could in principle be the counterparty to a separate financing arrangement invisible to a lender focused on the consolidated whole. A determined, well-resourced lender can search Uniform Commercial Code filings against a borrower's various entities and catch some of this. In principle, a UCC lien search is exactly the tool designed to reveal a prior or competing claim on an asset — any lender who files a financing statement against specific collateral is, by law, putting the world on notice. In practice, the tool works only as well as the lender's ability to know which entities to search. A borrower with dozens of subsidiaries, some created only recently through acquisition, some structured specifically to hold receivables or inventory for financing purposes, presents an enormous surface area to search exhaustively, and lien filings can lag the transactions they describe, be filed against a slightly different legal name, or simply not be filed at all if a counterparty is willing to cut corners. What is much harder to catch, absent a whistleblower, a forensic audit, or the borrower's own disclosure, is a counterparty who is actively concealing the overlap — pledging the same receivable twice on purpose, giving each lender a borrowing-base certificate that looks clean in isolation, rather than an honest structural oversight two lenders might have stumbled onto by comparing notes. That is the distinction the case turns on, and it is the distinction this book keeps returning to: a lender's ordinary process can be diligent, can involve real people asking real questions and checking real filings, and still miss a borrower who is determined to lie to it.

The lender exposure, once the filing forced it into the open, was substantial and widely distributed across some of the most sophisticated names in structured finance. Bloomberg reported that First Brands' collapse rattled confidence across the private credit market broadly, not just among the specific lenders directly holding its debt. CNBC's reporting on the fallout named Jefferies, UBS, and Morgan Stanley among the firms scrambling to assess their exposure, alongside joint-venture lending vehicles involving major Japanese banks that had partnered with US asset managers to fund exactly this kind of direct loan. One firm's exposure was reported as high as $1.75 billion — a striking number for a single credit relationship with a company most of its own investors, and likely many of its own employees outside the deal team, had never heard of. Jefferies in particular had marketed funds built substantially around First Brands-related receivables financing to its own investor base, which meant the exposure was not confined to a single balance sheet but had already been distributed downstream to people who believed they were buying a diversified, short-duration credit product.

The names on that list are worth sitting with for a moment, because they are not the small, thinly capitalized shops a skeptic might expect to get caught out by a sophisticated fraud. Jefferies and Morgan Stanley are among the oldest names on Wall Street; UBS is one of the largest banks in the world; the Japanese joint-venture lenders involved had partnered with established US managers specifically to get exposure to direct lending they judged safer and more diversified than what they could originate on their own. This is precisely the syndicate structure the earlier chapters described as one of private credit's genuine strengths — risk spread across several experienced underwriters rather than concentrated in one, each of whom presumably ran its own diligence before committing capital. That the structure still left a multi-billion-dollar collective exposure to allegedly double-pledged collateral is not, by itself, proof the underwriters were careless. It is evidence that the specific failure mode at issue here — a borrower lying about what else it had pledged — can defeat diligence performed by more than one careful institution independently, precisely because each institution's diligence stopped at the edge of what it could see, and none of them were comparing notes with each other before the filing forced them to.

The market reaction, once real price discovery arrived in the form of a bankruptcy filing, was the more vivid illustration — the kind of data point this book has been building toward since the title chapter. First Brands' first-lien term loan, the senior-most piece of its capital structure, the tranche a lender would ordinarily expect to be made close to whole even in a bad outcome, was reported by CNBC to have fallen by roughly 83 percent over about three weeks, with some of the debt trading around 36 cents on the dollar. A loan that had likely been marked on lender and fund books at or near par only weeks earlier — the ordinary, quiet, low-volatility number described in Chapter 7 — was suddenly worth barely a third of its face value, not because the underlying business had gradually deteriorated in a way anyone was tracking, but because a filing forced a market to actually price a security that had not traded, in any meaningful sense, in years. This is the mechanism the book keeps circling. It is not that private credit marks are always wrong. It is that nobody outside the lender group has a way to check whether they are right until an event — a bankruptcy, a fraud allegation, a forced sale — makes someone try to sell the asset at whatever the market will actually pay. First Brands is the cleanest data point available for what that repricing can look like when it finally happens: not a gradual drift, but a cliff.

Put the number in context. An 83 percent decline in three weeks is a move associated with equities in a genuine panic, not with a senior secured loan that a lender group had, by definition, agreed was worth close to its face value when the deal was struck. Public high-yield bonds, even in the worst weeks of 2008 or the initial COVID shock of March 2020, rarely moved that fast on that scale for a single issuer without an active, continuously quoted market absorbing the news in smaller increments along the way. Part of what made First Brands' repricing so violent was the very illiquidity that private credit's defenders, correctly, cite as a source of return: there was no continuous market gradually digesting bad news in the weeks before the filing, because there was effectively no market at all. The loan traded, if it traded, by phone call between a small number of counterparties who mostly agreed to leave it marked near par because nothing had forced anyone to test that number. The day something did force the test, three weeks of trading did the work that a liquid public market would ordinarily have spread across months. Investors in funds holding First Brands exposure — some of them institutional, some of them, by the time this book was written, participants in retail-facing vehicles of the kind described in Chapter 8 — found out what they actually owned only when the fund's own quarterly mark caught up to a price the market outside had already discovered.

None of this, it is important to say plainly, has been established in a court of law as proven fraud, and the surrounding litigation was still unresolved as this book went to press. What has happened, industry-wide, is a rapid and fairly unified reframing of First Brands as a case of alleged borrower deception rather than evidence that direct lending's underwriting process is inherently broken. Cambridge Associates, the institutional investment consultant whose job is to vet exactly this kind of exposure on behalf of pension funds and endowments, published a note asking whether First Brands and Tricolor — the subprime auto lender whose own collapse is the subject of the next chapter — together suggested trouble ahead for private credit generally, and its answer was measured rather than alarmed: these look like company-specific instances of alleged misconduct, the note argued, not proof of a systemic underwriting failure across an asset class that had, by most measures, performed reasonably well through prior stress. T. Rowe Price's OCredit team, in a practitioner note published shortly after the filing and titled with the same understated phrase this book has borrowed for its own inquiry — looking under the hood — walked through the mechanics of the alleged double-pledging in granular detail and drew a similar distinction: the failure here, if the allegations hold up, was concealment by the borrower, not a defect in the concept of direct lending itself. The law firm McKool Smith, writing a client alert for lenders navigating exactly this kind of collateral dispute, was more pointed about what the case exposes procedurally — that in a complex, multi-entity borrower structure, a senior lender's collateral package is only as reliable as the borrower's own representations about what else has been pledged, and that existing diligence practice generally assumes good faith on that specific question because verifying it exhaustively, across every subsidiary and every financing counterparty, is neither standard practice nor, in most deals, commercially realistic to demand.

This is also the place to say plainly what would weaken this book's own argument, since the research discipline behind it requires that admission in every chapter. If it turns out, as the litigation proceeds, that First Brands' lenders had clear disclosure of the factoring arrangements and simply failed to act on red flags sitting in plain sight, the case becomes a story about lender complacency rather than about a structural blind spot in how private credit is underwritten — a less interesting story for this book's thesis, and a more damning one for the specific institutions involved. If, alternatively, it turns out that only a handful of individuals inside First Brands orchestrated the concealment through conduct no ordinary diligence process could reasonably have been expected to detect, that also cuts against reading this case as representative of anything systemic about direct lending, and toward reading it as what most of the industry has already called it: an outlier fraud.

That is the honest, unresolved center of the chapter, and the book's own discipline requires stating it without smoothing it into a tidier verdict than the evidence supports. Standard private credit diligence — audited financials, a borrowing-base certificate, management interviews, third-party quality-of-earnings review, UCC lien searches against the specific entities pledging collateral — is a real process, not a fiction, and it catches a great deal of ordinary credit deterioration and a fair amount of ordinary overstatement. What it is not well designed to catch is a determined counterparty deliberately pledging the same asset to more than one lender across a structure large enough that no single lender sees the whole of it. The Institute and Faculty of Actuaries, writing for an insurance-industry audience increasingly exposed to private credit through affiliated annuity portfolios — the subject taken up later in this book — used First Brands as a case study for precisely that reason: not because the mechanism is exotic, but because it is a fairly ordinary vulnerability, hiding inside an unglamorous industrial company, that a market moving trillions of dollars through confidential, individually negotiated loans has limited structural means of self-detecting until something forces the collateral into daylight. A public bond market has thousands of participants pricing a security every day, any one of whom might notice something wrong. A private loan has a handful of lenders and the borrower's own word.

None of the practitioner voices surveyed here argue that lenders should have caught this before it happened, and none argue that they definitely could not have. The more useful, less satisfying conclusion sitting underneath their notes is that direct lending's diligence process was built to price ordinary credit risk — the chance a decent business has a bad year, misses a covenant, needs an amendment — and was never seriously stress-tested against a borrower willing to falsify the collateral schedule itself. Public markets have that stress test running constantly, in the form of short sellers, forensic analysts, and journalists who make a living finding exactly this kind of discrepancy in a company with a stock price to move. A confidential loan to a private company has none of that scrutiny by design, because confidentiality was the selling point. First Brands is what it can look like when the thing being kept confidential is not the company's competitive strategy, but its books.

First Brands was, whatever the litigation ultimately determines, an industrial company — a maker of brake pads and fuel pumps undone, allegedly, by collateral that existed on paper in more than one place at once. Its lenders were sophisticated firms with real diligence processes, apparently defeated less by their own carelessness than by a borrower, if the allegations hold, determined enough to keep two sets of books straight in its own head while showing each lender only the set that made sense to them. The loss, once the filing forced a real price on the debt, was immediate and large, and it was the kind of loss no valuation model back at the lenders' own offices had been built to anticipate, because the model had never been asked to price deliberate deception. That is the same fragility this book found waiting inside a quarterly mark in Chapter 7 and inside a PIK amendment in Chapter 9 — a number that looks calm until an outside event forces it to answer for itself.

Weeks earlier, on September 10, 2025, a very different kind of borrower had already collapsed through a different failure mode inside the same broad asset class, and its failure would turn out to be the one that put Jamie Dimon's cockroach line into every subsequent conversation about private credit. Tricolor Holdings was not an auto-parts manufacturer financing working capital against invoices; it was a subprime auto lender in Dallas, financing loans to car buyers with poor credit and then pledging those loans, in turn, as collateral for its own borrowing from banks and private lenders. Its bankruptcy, a Chapter 7 liquidation rather than a Chapter 11 reorganization, arrived with federal prosecutors alleging a scheme of collateral fraud on an even larger scale than what First Brands' lenders have alleged — and with one of the country's largest banks, not a private credit fund at all, sitting among the parties left explaining, in its own chief executive's words, what its own underwriting had missed.
