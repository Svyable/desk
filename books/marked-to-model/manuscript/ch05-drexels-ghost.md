# Drexel's Ghost

In 1970, a Wharton MBA student named Michael Milken spent a great deal of time with a book almost nobody else in his class had opened: W. Braddock Hickman's decades-old statistical study of corporate bond performance, later extended by a Federal Reserve economist named T.R. Atkinson. Hickman had tracked the actual, realized returns on low-grade corporate bonds going back to the turn of the century, and his numbers said something that did not match what most of Wall Street believed. A diversified basket of low-rated bonds, held to maturity, defaults and all, had historically returned more than a portfolio of the safest, most conservatively rated debt — more, in fact, than the extra yield alone would suggest was fair compensation for the added risk of default. The market, in other words, was not pricing sub-investment-grade debt on the merits. It was pricing the stigma of holding it.

That distinction — between a company's actual credit risk and the reputational penalty attached to a low rating — became the idea Milken built a career on, and eventually the idea private credit inherited, two generations later, in a very different form. The problem Milken identified in the late 1960s was structural, not moral. Public bond markets ran almost entirely on ratings issued by Moody's and Standard & Poor's, and by the postwar era those ratings had become more than an opinion; they were embedded, by law and by charter, into what regulated institutions were permitted to buy. Pension funds, insurance companies, and many bank trust departments operated under rules that barred them from holding bonds rated below investment grade, or made doing so prohibitively expensive in capital charges. A young, fast-growing, or heavily leveraged company — the kind of business with real cash flow but no decades-long record of conservative balance-sheet management — usually could not get an investment-grade rating no matter how sound its underlying economics. That shut it out of the public bond market almost entirely. Its remaining option was a bank, and banks in the 1970s were themselves conservative lenders to exactly this kind of borrower, wary of anything that looked like a credit risk a regulator might later criticize on examination. A wide category of American business — smaller, newer, or simply more indebted than blue-chip norms allowed — was effectively unbanked by both channels at once. This was not, on its own, evidence of a broken system. Bank examiners and ratings committees existed because earlier decades had shown what happened when lenders extended credit on optimism rather than analysis, and the caution built into both institutions after the Depression had real value. The tension Milken exploited was not that conservatism was wrong; it was that conservatism, applied uniformly, also excluded borrowers whose risk was simply being mispriced rather than genuinely uninvestable, and that a market existed for anyone patient enough to tell the two apart correctly and often enough to profit from it.

One example of that gap, cited often enough in later accounts of Milken's career to be worth repeating here, is MCI Communications. In the 1970s, MCI was trying to build a long-distance telephone network to compete against AT&T, a monopoly with an investment-grade credit rating and decades of regulated cash flow behind it. MCI had neither. It had promising technology, an antitrust case working its way through the courts, and years of capital-intensive network construction ahead of it before it would generate the kind of steady earnings a bank or a ratings committee could comfortably underwrite. Conventional lenders were in no position to fund that bet, and MCI could not have issued investment-grade bonds against a business plan that consisted largely of a lawsuit and a construction schedule. Milken's high-yield financing helped MCI build the network anyway — a case, still cited by defenders of the junk bond market, of below-investment-grade debt funding exactly the kind of disruptive, capital-hungry young company that conservative lending was structurally unable to serve, years before that company's success helped force the breakup of AT&T's monopoly altogether.

Milken's answer, more broadly, was to build a market for newly issued high-yield bonds rather than wait for the market that already existed, the so-called fallen angels — investment-grade issuers that had been downgraded after the fact. He took the idea to Drexel Firestone, later Drexel Burnham Lambert, which was itself a fitting home for a contrarian bet: the firm was a product of mergers among smaller, second-tier houses, without the blue-chip corporate relationships of Morgan Stanley or Goldman Sachs, and it had less to lose by specializing in a corner of the market the white-shoe firms considered beneath them. Milken set about doing two things simultaneously: persuading below-investment-grade companies that they could raise capital directly from the public markets instead of begging a loan officer, and cultivating a dedicated base of buyers willing to hold that paper on the strength of his own credit judgment rather than a rating. Fred Carr's First Executive Life and a scattering of savings and loans — several of them, including Charles Keating's Lincoln Savings, institutions that would later become notorious for other reasons — bought heavily, along with mutual funds built specifically to hold high-yield debt. By the early 1980s Drexel's trading operation, run out of a distinctive X-shaped desk in Beverly Hills rather than New York, had become the dominant underwriter of original-issue junk bonds in the country, and its annual gathering of issuers, buyers, and raiders — the event the press nicknamed the Predators' Ball — had become one of the more consequential rooms in American finance, drawing chief executives and dealmakers who a decade earlier would not have taken a meeting with a below-investment-grade underwriter. Milken's own compensation tracked the market's growth just as steeply: by 1987, the year before the guilty plea that ended his career at the firm, his reported pay for a single year ran into the hundreds of millions of dollars, a sum that startled even a Wall Street accustomed to large bonuses and became, in its own right, a symbol of how much value the market believed Milken's judgment about credit risk was creating.

Drexel did not simply arrange bond sales. Through its "highly confident letters," it told corporate acquirers it could raise financing for a takeover before a single buyer had actually committed capital, on the strength of Milken's relationships and the firm's placement power alone — a promise no bank credit committee, bound by its own loan limits and examiners, could plausibly make. That confidence, extended to men like Carl Icahn, T. Boone Pickens, and Ronald Perelman, whose 1985 junk-bond-financed pursuit of Revlon became one of the decade's defining hostile takeovers, financed a wave of leveraged buyouts and corporate restructurings that traditional lenders would not touch. The leverage those deals carried was itself new: a bank-financed buyout of the 1970s had typically kept debt to a conservative multiple of cash flow, with the bank retaining a senior, secured, and closely monitored position. A junk-bond-financed buyout could push leverage far higher, because the risk was no longer concentrated on one lender's balance sheet under a regulator's eye — it was distributed across thousands of bondholders who had bought the debt precisely because Milken had priced it to compensate them for taking it. Whether that distribution made the system safer, by spreading risk widely, or simply made it harder to see building leverage clearly, was an argument contemporaries had at the time and historians still have today.

By the late 1980s the junk bond market Milken had built from a standing start had grown to roughly $150 billion in outstanding issuance, and Drexel controlled something close to half of the underwriting business in it. That is not a small market cornered by one firm; it is close to a majority of an entire asset class run through a single trading desk and a single man's rolodex of buyers, in a period of a little over a decade. The shape of that growth — a category of borrower locked out of conservative, heavily regulated capital by the very rules meant to keep the system safe, met by a nonbank intermediary willing to underwrite that risk directly and hold relationships with the specific investors patient enough to fund it — is the same shape private credit's own growth has taken since 2008. The details differ: today's locked-out borrower is more often a private-equity-owned, non-investment-grade middle-market company facing tighter bank capital rules and stricter leveraged-lending guidance rather than a corporate raider financing a hostile bid, and today's dedicated buyers are pension funds and insurers seeking an illiquidity premium rather than thrifts reaching for yield. But the underlying mechanism — credit that public markets and regulated banks will not price moving to an intermediary willing to underwrite it on its own judgment, growing explosively because the need was real — is close enough that anyone studying private credit's rise has an obligation to look at what happened the last time American finance ran this experiment at scale.

The reader who has come this far already has the modern version of that mechanism in hand. The 2013 interagency leveraged lending guidance, the post-2008 Basel capital rules that made banks hold more capital against a leveraged loan than a direct lender ever had to, and the general retreat of regulated banks from anything a supervisor might flag on examination did to the current generation of non-investment-grade borrowers roughly what conservative bank underwriting and ratings-agency gatekeeping did to Milken's borrowers in the 1970s: they narrowed the path to capital for exactly the companies with the least conservative balance sheets, and left a gap for a nonbank lender to fill. Direct lenders filled it the way Drexel filled its own gap, by building relationships with a dedicated pool of patient capital — pension funds, insurers, and endowments willing to accept illiquidity for extra yield — and by underwriting on their own judgment rather than a public rating. The instrument changed from a tradable bond to a negotiated loan held to maturity on a single lender's books, but the economic function performed for the borrower, and the market gap it plugged, would have been recognizable to anyone who worked a Drexel trading desk in 1985.

What happened started, publicly, with a man who was not Milken. On November 14, 1986, the arbitrageur Ivan Boesky pleaded guilty to a single felony count of conspiracy tied to insider trading, agreed to pay a hundred million dollars in disgorgement and penalties, and — critically for Drexel — became a cooperating witness for the Securities and Exchange Commission and the United States Attorney's office in Manhattan, then run by Rudolph Giuliani. Boesky had done business with Milken for years, including trades investigators would later characterize as stock parking, in which one party holds securities on another's behalf to disguise true ownership or manipulate reported positions. Boesky's cooperation, which reportedly included recorded conversations, turned a federal insider-trading investigation that had begun elsewhere into a direct examination of Drexel's own trading practices and of Milken personally. What had been an isolated arbitrage scandal became, over the following two years, an inquiry into the machinery that had built the junk bond market itself — grand jury subpoenas, congressional hearings, and a slow public erosion of the assumption that Drexel's dominance rested purely on superior credit judgment rather than, in part, on trading arrangements investigators believed had crossed into fraud.

Drexel fought the resulting investigation for two years before it stopped fighting. Federal prosecutors had the option of indicting the firm under the Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations statute, which carried the power to freeze a defendant's assets before trial — a threat that, for a securities firm entirely dependent on continuous, unimpeded access to capital markets and counterparty trust, amounted to a potential death sentence regardless of the eventual verdict. Facing that exposure, Drexel Burnham Lambert pleaded guilty in December 1988 to six felony counts of securities and mail fraud and agreed to pay $650 million in fines and restitution, at the time the largest such penalty in Wall Street history. The firm did not admit that Milken personally had committed the underlying violations, and it continued, for a while, to operate. But a securities firm's most important asset is the market's willingness to keep lending it money overnight, and a felony plea of that size is not the kind of news that leaves confidence undisturbed.

Not everyone at the time, or since, has agreed on how much of what followed Drexel actually deserved. Some of Milken's contemporaries and later chroniclers of the case have argued that Giuliani's office used the RICO asset-freeze provision coercively, extracting a guilty plea from a firm that might have prevailed, or at least survived, had it gone to trial and forced prosecutors to prove the underlying charges; others have argued the plea reflected a firm that knew exactly what a jury would have found. That argument has never fully settled, and this book does not need to settle it either. What is not disputed is the sequence of consequences that followed the plea, regardless of which reading of the prosecution's fairness one accepts.

The funding pressure that killed Drexel did not come only from its own legal troubles. Congress had already begun forcing a separate reckoning on one of the junk bond market's most important buyer bases. The savings and loan crisis of the late 1980s, driven by a wave of failed thrifts that had chased yield into real estate and high-yield debt, produced the Financial Institutions Reform, Recovery, and Enforcement Act of 1989, which required federally insured thrifts to divest their junk bond holdings entirely within five years. Institutions that had been among Milken's most loyal buyers became forced sellers almost overnight, adding real selling pressure to a market already turning down as a recession approached, and pushing prices for lower-quality issues lower still. Drexel, still holding its own retained inventory of underwritten bonds, watched the value of what it could not sell decline for reasons that had comparatively little to do with Milken's guilty plea and everything to do with a separate, self-reinforcing wave of forced liquidation moving through the same market at the same time.

Milken's own reckoning came seventeen months later. In April 1990, after his indictment on a far longer list of charges the previous year, he pleaded guilty to six felony counts, including securities and reporting violations connected to the trading relationships investigators had traced back to Boesky's cooperation, and left Drexel for good. He would go on to pay a substantial fine, serve time in federal prison, and eventually rebuild a public reputation as a philanthropist and prostate-cancer research funder — a second act few of Drexel's other veterans got the chance to attempt. But by the time he pleaded guilty, the firm that had made him was already dying. Drexel Burnham Lambert filed for bankruptcy on February 13, 1990, less than fifteen months after its own guilty plea and barely two months before Milken's. The proximate cause was not a court judgment; it was a run. Once the firm's short-term commercial paper lenders and repo counterparties concluded Drexel's capital position could not be trusted, they simply stopped rolling over its funding, and a securities firm without overnight funding cannot survive to see whether the underlying business was actually sound.

Here the historical parallel to private credit reaches its most important limit, and it is worth being precise about exactly where that limit sits. Junk bonds, for all the scandal attached to the man who popularized them, were public securities. They traded on exchanges and over the counter, they carried continuously observable prices, and — this is the part that matters most for a book about private credit — when the market turned against Drexel's positions in 1989 and 1990, as a recession approached and a wave of junk-financed deals began to look overleveraged, that judgment showed up immediately and visibly in falling bond prices that Drexel, like every other holder, had to mark against. Drexel had accumulated its own retained inventory of bonds it had underwritten but not yet placed with buyers, and as prices for lower-quality paper fell through 1989, that inventory lost real, observable value — value the market recorded daily, not value a valuation committee estimated once a quarter using a model built on comparable-yield assumptions the committee itself selected. Nobody at Drexel got to decide, internally, what its junk bond portfolio was worth that week. The market decided, the same way it decides the worth of a share of stock or a Treasury bond, by clearing a price between willing buyers and sellers in daylight. It was ultimately that public, continuous market price — not a regulator's exam, not a court's verdict, not even the firm's own guilty plea by itself — that translated Drexel's declining fortunes into a number its lenders could see and act on before the firm could recover. Most of today's private direct loans have no equivalent mechanism. They are held at a lender's own internally modeled fair value, updated on a quarterly cycle, using inputs the lender itself selects and rarely tests against an outside transaction. If a twenty-first-century direct-lending portfolio were quietly deteriorating the way Drexel's junk bond inventory deteriorated in 1989, there is no guarantee anyone outside the fund would see it happen in anything like real time. That gap — a public, continuously priced market on one side of the analogy, and a privately modeled, quarterly-marked one on the other — is the single most important place where the Drexel story stops being a clean predictor of what comes next. It is also the gap this book keeps returning to, under different names, in nearly every chapter that follows: a later chapter is titled, quite literally, after the practice of holding a position at a value the holder itself computed rather than a value the market set. Drexel's downfall is proof of what a functioning, continuously priced market can do when a lender's fortunes turn — it forces the truth out, however unwelcome, well before the lender's own choices about disclosure catch up to it. Whether today's private credit funds enjoy anything like that same discipline, or merely appear to because nobody outside the fund can see the underlying loans clearly enough to test the manager's marks against, is a question this book keeps asking because Drexel's history keeps insisting it is the right one to ask.

It is also worth resisting the temptation to let Drexel's ending stand in for the ending of junk bonds themselves, because that is not what happened. The market Milken built did not die with his firm. High-yield corporate debt survived the scandal, survived the 1990 recession that followed close behind it, and went on to become one of the most ordinary, liquid, heavily indexed corners of American capital markets — held today inside mutual funds, exchange-traded funds, and pension portfolios by the trillions of dollars, priced every day, rated by the same agencies that once refused to touch it, and treated by corporate treasurers as a completely unremarkable way to raise money outside investment grade. Other firms — including, in time, some of the largest banks on Wall Street — simply took over the underwriting business Drexel had built and continued running it, because the underlying insight that had started the whole enterprise, that a diversified portfolio of properly priced sub-investment-grade debt could be a sound and profitable thing to own, had turned out to be correct. Many of the bankers and traders who had worked under Milken went on to build or lead high-yield and leveraged-finance operations elsewhere, carrying the discipline of that market with them into firms that, a decade earlier, would not have touched sub-investment-grade underwriting at all. That durability matters, and a book inclined to treat private credit's boom with suspicion owes it honest weight. Financial innovations that solve a genuine problem sometimes do outlive the excesses, and occasionally the crimes, of the people who first commercialized them. The technology was not the fraud. The fraud was what one firm and one trader did on top of a technology that, stripped of Milken's own conduct, went on to work. A saver today who owns a broad bond index fund, or a target-date retirement fund with a high-yield sleeve, very likely owns some of that same technology without ever having heard Milken's name — a fact worth sitting with before concluding that everything built outside the traditional banking system in a hurry is destined to end the way Drexel did.

That distinction is worth stating plainly, because it is easy to let a story this dramatic flatten into an accusation it does not actually support. Michael Milken was convicted of specific federal securities violations connected to concealed trading relationships and reporting failures uncovered through Ivan Boesky's cooperation — a matter of what he and others disclosed and to whom, not a claim that the underlying business of underwriting below-investment-grade debt was itself illegitimate. Nothing in the record of today's direct-lending managers — Ares, Blackstone, Blue Owl, Apollo, and the rest of the firms that will populate the rest of this book — alleges anything resembling that conduct, and this book makes no such claim on their behalf. The parallel worth drawing is narrower and, in a way, more useful than a fraud story would be: it is structural, in that credit intermediation migrated outside the banking system and outside continuous public pricing to solve a real financing gap, in both eras; and it is cyclical, in that an industry built and scaled during years of easy credit and rising asset values had not yet, by Milken's own telling, been tested by a real downturn when the reckoning arrived. Private credit's managers are not accused of Milken's crimes. What they share with Drexel is a market position — indispensable financier to borrowers the traditional system would not serve — that has so far mostly been observed in good times.

History does not resolve that question on its own; it only offers a shape to watch for. Drexel's story establishes that credit can migrate outside banks for entirely legitimate reasons, grow explosively while it is solving a real problem, and still eventually meet a test that separates a durable innovation from an overextended one — and that the two outcomes are not mutually exclusive, since junk bonds managed to be both. It also establishes, more specifically, that the presence or absence of a public market price is not incidental detail; it is close to the whole story of how the 1990 reckoning actually arrived, and how fast. Where today's private credit market sits inside that pattern — whether it more closely resembles the junk bond market on its way up, on its way down, or in its long and largely uneventful afterlife as an established asset class — is not a question history can answer by itself. It requires current facts: how large the market actually is, how its loans are actually valued, and what happens when investors or borrowers under stress try to test either number. None of those facts were available to Milken's contemporaries in anything like real time, which is part of why the 1990 reckoning arrived as suddenly as it did; the honest ambition of this book is to gather what current facts do exist and lay them out before the reckoning, if there is one, rather than after. The obvious place to start is the most basic question of all, and, as the next chapter shows, one that a market this large has somehow still not managed to answer consistently. How big is private credit, really?
