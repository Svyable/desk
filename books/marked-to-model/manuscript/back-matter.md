## Epilogue

A book about private credit is a book about a moving target. Between the first page of research and the last page of this manuscript, a fund gated redemptions for the first time in its history, a rescinded regulation quietly reversed a decade of guidance, and a subprime auto lender's fraud case moved from newspaper allegation toward a federal docket. Some of the numbers in this book will be stale before a reader opens it, and a few of the open questions it leaves standing will already have been answered one way or another. That is not a flaw to apologize for. It is the condition of writing about anything still happening.

What should not go stale is the mechanism. A loan that only its own lender prices, marked quarterly by a model whose assumptions the lender chooses, sold onward to investors who cannot easily verify those assumptions and increasingly cannot be sophisticated enough to try — that arrangement will keep producing the same basic tension regardless of which fund, which borrower, or which regulator happens to be in the headlines when you read this. The industry may weather 2025 and 2026 without a single systemic event. It may not. Either outcome is compatible with the more modest claim this book actually makes: that an asset class built on the deliberate absence of a daily price deserves more scrutiny than it has received, precisely because it is now being sold to people who have the least capacity to supply that scrutiny themselves.

If there is a single image worth carrying out of this book, it is not Jamie Dimon's cockroach. It is the retirement account statement — smooth, quarterly, reassuring — sitting on top of a debt instrument that nobody watching that statement has ever actually priced.

## A Note on Sources and Method

Every factual claim in this manuscript is drawn from named, dated, publicly available sources: company and regulatory filings, court dockets, central bank and multilateral publications, law-firm client alerts written for a professional audience that could not afford to get the underlying facts wrong, trade-press and financial-journalism reporting, and peer-reviewed or working-paper academic research. The complete source ledger, organized by chapter, is available in this book's research folder for any reader who wants to check a claim against its original source rather than take the author's word for it. Where sources disagreed — on the total size of the private credit market, on how dangerous the parallel to 2008 really is, on whether a redemption gate is evidence of prudence or evidence of a broken promise — the manuscript tries to show the disagreement rather than resolve it by fiat. Litigation referenced in this book, including the bankruptcy proceedings of First Brands Group and Tricolor Holdings, was ongoing as of this writing; allegations are described as allegations, and readers following either case afterward should expect the record to have moved.

## Acknowledgments

This manuscript exists because a small industry of financial journalists, law-firm analysts, central bank researchers, and academic economists spent 2025 and 2026 doing the unglamorous work of writing down, carefully and with dates attached, what was actually happening inside a market built to be hard to see into. None of them asked to be a source for a book like this one; their bylines are preserved in the research ledger because the debt this manuscript owes them is real.

## About the Author

Sven Hardy Benson writes narrative nonfiction about the financial and technological systems that quietly reorganize how risk moves through ordinary lives, generally arriving at the subject a few years after the professionals and a few years before everyone else has to care.
