# Who Actually Bears the Risk

Picture a quarterly statement, the kind that arrives by email now instead of paper, with a link that opens onto a single pie chart and a balance that is either bigger or smaller than it was three months ago. Call the account holder Denise. She is not a real person; no fund in this chapter is a real fund, and no dollar figure attached to her is a real dollar figure. She is a composite, built to make a structure visible that is otherwise almost impossible to see in one place, because the structure was not designed to be seen in one place. Denise is forty-six, has worked at the same mid-sized logistics company for eleven years, and has never once logged into her 401(k) provider's website to change an allocation. She did not choose her investment. It chose her, the way most retirement savings in this country get chosen — by default, on her first day of eligibility, into whatever fund her plan's administrator had designated as the option for people who make no active decision at all. In her case that fund carries a name like "Horizon Target 2045," built around the year she is expected to retire, and it is the only line on her statement she has ever really looked at.

That single line is the top of a chain this book has spent fifteen chapters describing from the bottom up — the loan, the fund, the valuation, the insurer, the executive order — without yet walking it end to end, from a saver's screen down to an actual company's actual debt. It is worth doing once, slowly, because the chain has a feature that matters more than any single link in it: at every joint, somebody takes a fee, and almost nobody along the way, including Denise, including in some cases the professionals paid to represent her interest, can see the whole thing at once.

Start where Denise starts, with the target-date fund itself. A target-date fund is not one investment but a moving blend of many, rebalanced automatically as the target year approaches — mostly equities decades out, shifting gradually toward bonds and capital preservation as retirement nears — wrapped in a single ticker so that a plan participant with no interest in asset allocation still ends up reasonably diversified. It exists because behavioral research on 401(k) plans found, repeatedly, that employees left to build their own portfolios from a long fund menu tended to do it badly or not at all, and Congress responded by giving these bundled, professionally managed defaults a legal blessing as a "qualified default investment alternative," the designation that let plan sponsors auto-enroll workers into them without taking on extra fiduciary exposure for the choice. Actively managed target-date series typically charge something on the order of a third to two-thirds of one percent of assets a year — an expense ratio that funds the asset manager's own overhead, trading, and the layer of portfolio construction that decides how much goes to stocks versus bonds at any given age. That fee is visible. It is printed in the fund's prospectus and, since a 2012 Department of Labor disclosure rule, itemized on the summary a plan is required to send participants once a year. Denise's fee, at this layer, is a known number.

It is the next layer that the August 2025 executive order and the Department of Labor's rescission of its earlier guidance were built to make routine rather than exceptional. Before that order, a target-date fund's alternative-assets exposure — private equity, private credit, real estate, the categories this book has been tracing since Chapter 2 — was rare and, where it existed at all, was usually confined to funds sold to large institutional plans with in-house investment staff able to evaluate it directly. After it, the practical mechanism law firms spent the fall of 2025 explaining to their plan-sponsor clients was straightforward: a target-date fund provider carves out a sleeve, typically capped at some single-digit-to-low-double-digit share of the total portfolio, and hands that sleeve to a specialist manager under a sub-advisory agreement, while the target-date fund's own name and ticker stay exactly the same on Denise's statement. One of the client alerts circulated to retirement-plan fiduciaries in the weeks after the order — from Ogletree Deakins, a firm whose practice is advising employers on exactly this kind of plan-design decision — walked through the fiduciary questions this raises before a single dollar moves: how does a plan committee, most of whose members are not investment professionals, actually diligence an illiquid sleeve it cannot itself value day to day, and what does "prudent selection" under ERISA mean for an asset class that by its own design has no daily price to check the committee's judgment against? Nobody has fully answered that question yet, which is itself a finding. The sleeve carries its own additional management fee on top of the target-date fund's existing expense ratio — often somewhere in the range of half a point to a full point of additional annual cost, depending on the manager and the structure — and that fee is where the statement's transparency starts to thin out, because a single blended expense ratio on Denise's account summary does not, in most current disclosure formats, separately itemize what the alternatives sleeve costs versus what the traditional stock-and-bond portion costs.

Follow the sleeve's money one step further and it does not go directly into a loan. It goes into a vehicle built to hold private credit at semi-liquid, retail-compatible scale — a fund-of-funds structure, or in some designs a business development company, of the kind this book examined closely in the chapters on marking and gating. This is the layer where an investor's dollar is pooled with thousands of others and allocated, at the vehicle manager's discretion, across a basket of underlying direct-lending funds chosen for diversification rather than any single loan Denise or her plan committee ever sees named. That discretion is a service — genuine portfolio construction, genuine due diligence on which managers to trust with the money — and it is priced like one: another management fee, commonly in the neighborhood of a point to a point and a half annually, sometimes paired with a modest fee on the gains the vehicle eventually passes through. Some of these vehicles are run independently of the funds they invest in. A meaningful number are not — they are managed by the same firm, or an affiliate of the same firm, that also runs one or more of the underlying direct-lending funds the vehicle allocates to, which means a single organization can be collecting a fee for choosing itself. That is not illegal, and it is disclosed in the fine print of the vehicle's offering documents. It is also precisely the shape of the affiliated-transaction conflict this book documented at the insurance layer in Chapter 13 and at the ratings layer in Chapter 14, now reproduced one rung further down the chain, closer to a saver with far less capacity to read an offering document than a state insurance regulator has to read a Schedule D filing.

Only now, four layers down from Denise's screen, does the money reach a fund that actually originates and holds loans — a direct-lending fund of the kind Chapters 2 and 4 described in detail, run by one of the specialist credit managers that built this industry out of the space banks vacated after 2013. This fund's compensation is the one most people mean when they talk about private credit's fee structure, and it follows a template that has held steady across the industry for years: a management fee, typically in a band of roughly one to one and three-quarters percent of committed capital annually, charged whether or not that capital has actually been deployed into loans yet — the dry-powder question Chapter 6 raised in a different context returns here in its most concrete form, since a fund earning a fee on committed-but-undeployed capital is being paid before it has taken any credit risk at all. On top of the management fee sits an incentive fee, often structured as fifteen to twenty percent of profits above a preferred return or "hurdle," commonly somewhere around six to eight percent annually, sometimes subject to a high-water mark or a clawback provision that claws back incentive fees already paid if later losses erase the gains that generated them. This fund is the one that actually negotiates covenants, actually prices the loan, actually decides whether to let a struggling borrower pay in kind rather than declare default — the decisions Chapter 9 examined from the outside. It is, in the chain Denise's dollar has now traveled through, the first layer where the fee is explicitly and contractually tied to whether the underlying credit performs.

And at the very bottom sits the actual borrower: in this composite, a private-equity-owned manufacturer of mid-sized industrial components, the kind of company too small for a public bond offering and too leveraged, after its buyout, for a conservative bank's credit committee, borrowing at a floating rate several points over a reference rate to refinance existing debt and fund working capital. Its quarterly interest payment is the actual cash that, once it clears every layer above it, is supposed to become the return that shows up, eventually, compounding quietly, in the balance on Denise's statement. It is worth sitting with how far removed that company is from the language on her screen. Denise does not know its name, its industry, its leverage ratio, or the covenant package protecting the fund that lent to it. She knows "Horizon Target 2045," and beneath that, if she clicks far enough, an "alternative assets sleeve," and nothing below that at all.

Add the layers up and the exercise stops being about any single fee and becomes about what stacking does. A third of a point here, a point of sleeve overhead there, a point and a half at the fund-of-funds, one to one and three-quarters plus a performance cut at the direct-lending fund itself — none of these numbers is scandalous in isolation, and none of them is invented to shock; each sits comfortably inside the range an industry professional would recognize as ordinary for the type of vehicle in question. But they compound across four or five discretionary layers, each charged on some version of assets rather than strictly on results, before a single dollar of Denise's return clears the whole structure. The precise total is not the point, and this book has spent a full chapter already warning against false precision dressed up as rigor; the honest statement is that the drag is real, that it is larger than the single expense-ratio number on Denise's summary suggests, and that almost no publicly available disclosure adds all of it up for her in one place.

Some of what happens inside that stack genuinely does align the people managing Denise's money with Denise. Direct-lending managers commonly commit their own capital, or their firm's, alongside the fund's outside investors — co-investment that means a bad quarter for the fund is also a bad quarter for the people who run it, not merely for the people who bought into it. Incentive fees, when properly structured with a real hurdle and a genuine clawback, pay the manager only after the saver has already cleared a minimum return, which is a meaningfully different bargain than a flat fee collected regardless of outcome. Lockups and multi-year commitment periods, the very illiquidity this book examined skeptically in the chapter on gates, cut the other way here: a manager who cannot be redeemed out of a position in a panic has less reason to chase short-term marks and more reason to underwrite patiently, the way Chapter 4's steelman argument for direct lending predicted. None of this is theater. It is the strongest honest case that a well-structured fee arrangement, imperfect as it is, still points the manager's incentives roughly where the investor's incentives point.

The part that does not align sits mostly at the layer just above the loan itself, and it is a simple, well-understood problem in finance rather than a novel one: a management fee charged on assets under management, or on committed capital, rewards raising more money almost independent of what happens to the money already raised. A direct-lending fund earning one and a half percent on ten billion dollars of committed capital collects roughly the same fee whether that capital is deployed into disciplined, well-covenanted loans or into looser credits chased late in a cycle to keep growth going, right up until the loans actually default — at which point the incentive fee disappears but the management fee, largely, does not. Layer that structural pull toward asset-gathering on top of the affiliated-transaction incentive already documented at the insurance layer in Chapter 13 and the ratings layer in Chapter 14 — a single manager or its affiliates collecting a fee at the sleeve, the fund-of-funds, and the underlying fund simultaneously — and the saver's interest is no longer simply one interest among several the structure is balancing. It is one interest among several, competing for the same dollar of return, against parties who get paid substantially regardless of how that competition resolves.

The economist most associated with making this argument in a way institutional investors themselves have had to reckon with is Ludovic Phalippou, whose research has spent years pressing on a specific and uncomfortable claim: that even large, professionally staffed limited partners — pension funds and endowments with dedicated teams whose entire job is evaluating exactly these fund structures — routinely struggle to reconstruct a fund's actual net-of-fee, net-of-carry performance from what the fund itself discloses. Different managers report returns on different bases, gross versus net, internal rate of return versus a multiple on invested capital, before or after the layered fees this chapter has just walked through, and reconciling those disclosures into a single honest number that says what a given institutional investor actually earned, after everything, is harder than it should be for people whose entire professional mandate is doing exactly that reconciliation. If a pension fund's own investment staff — people who read fund documents for a living, who can pick up the phone and ask a general partner a pointed question, who negotiate side letters and most-favored-nation clauses that guarantee they get any better fee terms extended to other investors — cannot always fully see through a single fund's fee structure, the honest question this chapter has to ask is what chance an ordinary saver has of seeing through four or five of them stacked on top of each other, with no staff, no side letter, no phone call to place, and nothing to read but a summary plan description and a one-line expense ratio.

That gap in capability is the compounding problem this chapter's argument turns on, and it is worth being precise about what compounds. It is not only that Denise's fees are higher than they look; a sophisticated institutional investor faces some version of the same opacity Phalippou describes and still, on average, manages to negotiate better terms than a retail saver ever will, because a nine-figure commitment from a public pension fund buys real leverage a retirement plan's individual participant simply does not have. The saver's disadvantage is not new in kind. It is new in degree, extended for the first time into a product designed for people who will never read a private placement memorandum, delivered inside a wrapper — the target-date default fund — explicitly built to require no active decision at all.

None of this is a claim that fee layering and misaligned incentives are inventions of private credit, or that the rest of the investment industry has solved the problem this chapter is describing. Ordinary actively managed mutual funds have run on layered, asset-based fee structures for decades, some of them extracting revenue-sharing arrangements from the very fund families a broker recommends, a conflict retail investors have litigated and regulators have chased since long before anyone had heard of a unitranche loan. Target-date funds generally, not only the ones carrying an alternatives sleeve, have drawn their own lawsuits over plan sponsors defaulting employees into a provider's proprietary in-house fund lineup rather than shopping the market for the cheapest comparable option — a bare-metal version of the same asset-gathering incentive traced above, playing out inside plain-vanilla stocks and bonds. Even index funds, marketed as the low-cost, conflict-free answer to all of this, are not free of it: the handful of asset managers that dominate index investing collect real fees at enormous scale, earn additional undisclosed-to-most-investors revenue lending out the underlying securities, and wield the voting power that comes with owning a meaningful slice of nearly every public company in America, a governance concentration that has become its own subject of serious academic and regulatory debate. The honest way to state this chapter's claim, then, is not that private credit invented the principal-agent problem in asset management. It is that private credit's structure — illiquid, unpriced daily, valued by the manager's own model, now stacked several layers deep between saver and loan — makes an old and industry-wide problem harder to see through than it is in a fund that at least trades on an exchange every day and reports a single, unambiguous, market-verified price.

Nor is Denise as unprotected as this chain, described cold, might suggest. Her plan sponsor operates under a fiduciary duty, imposed by ERISA, to select and monitor investment options prudently and to act solely in participants' interest — a legal standard that has produced real litigation, including a wave of excessive-fee lawsuits against employers over the past decade that has meaningfully reshaped how plan committees benchmark costs and negotiate share classes. Federal disclosure rules require that she receive, at minimum, an annual accounting of her plan's fee structure, imperfect and incomplete as this chapter has argued that accounting currently is for an alternatives sleeve. The Department of Labor's own rulemaking, still in progress as this book was written, is the process by which whatever comes next for alternative assets inside 401(k) plans gets defined with more specificity than the original executive order provided. None of these checks is airtight, and this book has not found reason to believe any of them is being enforced with the rigor a structure this layered deserves. But a saver whose plan adds a private-credit sleeve is not, legally, standing alone in front of it; somebody with a fiduciary obligation is supposed to be standing between her and the fund manager's incentives, even if that somebody cannot see all the way down the chain any better than she can.

Which is, in the end, the honest place this chapter has to leave the reader. The fee stack from Denise's screen down to the manufacturer's loan is not a conspiracy; every layer of it does something a reasonable investor might actually want — professional asset allocation, specialist credit selection, diversification across managers, underwriting discipline on an individual loan. It is also not free, not fully visible on the one page Denise will ever actually read, and not perfectly aligned with her interest at every rung, particularly where a manager gets paid for raising money rather than for growing it well, or where the same firm sits on more than one side of the same transaction. The people whose job is supposed to be catching that kind of misalignment before it reaches a saver who will never read a fund prospectus are not plan committees, who mostly cannot see inside a fund's own books, and not academics like Phalippou, whose research describes the problem without the power to fix it. They are financial regulators, the agencies charged with seeing what a private fund's own investors cannot. The next chapter asks the plainest possible version of that question: what, exactly, can those regulators actually see?
