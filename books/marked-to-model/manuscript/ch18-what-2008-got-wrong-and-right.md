# What 2008 Got Wrong, and Right

Every night, long after most of the country has gone home, a large and almost entirely invisible market opens for business in Lower Manhattan and a handful of other financial centers, and closes again the next morning. A bond dealer holding an inventory of securities needs cash to fund its book overnight. A money market fund, a securities lender, a corporate treasury desk, has cash sitting idle and wants a safe place to park it that pays a little interest. The two sides strike what is called a repurchase agreement, or repo: the dealer sells its securities to the cash lender for one night, with a contractual promise to buy them back the next morning at a slightly higher price, the difference standing in for interest. If the collateral is a U.S. Treasury bond, the whole transaction is close to riskless and close to automatic; nobody spends much time wondering whether the Treasury will still be worth roughly the same amount tomorrow morning, so almost nobody bothers to check. Trillions of dollars move through this market on an ordinary night, unremarked, because the collateral behind it is the kind of asset nobody needs to investigate closely before accepting it. Now hold that same transaction in your head and swap the Treasury bond for a AAA-rated slice of a mortgage-backed security. Nothing about the mechanics changes. The dealer still posts collateral, the lender still advances cash overnight, the paperwork still rolls over every morning like clockwork. What changes, when the underlying collateral is no longer a government obligation but a privately assembled pool of mortgage risk repackaged into a bond that happens to carry the same rating as a Treasury, is how much anyone can actually trust that nothing has changed about what the collateral is worth. That gap — between how a AAA tranche of a securitization was treated by the market and what it actually was — is where the economists Gary Gorton and Andrew Metrick located the real mechanism of the 2007–2008 financial crisis, in an argument that reframes the popular story of subprime mortgages blowing up the world into something more precise, and considerably more useful for judging whether the same thing could happen again.

Gorton and Metrick's framework, developed across a series of papers including "Securitized Banking and the Run on Repo" and "The Run on Repo and the Fed's Response," makes a claim that sounds almost too simple until you sit with its implications: 2007–2008 is best understood not as a slow-moving deterioration in mortgage credit quality but as a banking panic — a classic, old-fashioned run — that happened to occur in a market almost nobody outside Wall Street trading floors had ever heard of. The dollar losses from actual subprime mortgage defaults, large as they were, were nowhere near large enough on their own to explain a crisis that wiped out Bear Stearns and Lehman Brothers, forced the rescue of AIG, and froze credit markets worldwide within a matter of months. Something amplified those losses into a systemic event, and Gorton and Metrick's answer is that the amplifier was the repo market itself — specifically, the funding structure that had grown up around it over the preceding two decades, which they call securitized banking. Banks, dealers, and the off-balance-sheet vehicles connected to them had built an enormous financing apparatus in which long-term, illiquid mortgage credit was funded not by patient deposits or long-term bonds but by short-term, overnight or near-overnight repo borrowing, rolled over daily, collateralized by the securitized tranches that credit rating agencies had blessed as safe enough to trade like cash. It functioned, for years, exactly like a bank — taking in short-term funding, holding long-term assets — without deposit insurance, without a discount window, and largely without the examination regime that governs an actual bank. When it ran, it ran the way banks have always run: not because every dollar of underlying collateral was worthless, but because everyone holding short-term claims against that collateral decided, more or less simultaneously, that they could no longer be sure which of it was.

The concept doing the real work in that account is what Gorton calls informationally insensitive debt, and it is worth translating out of the economics literature because the plain-English version explains almost everything that follows. Some kinds of debt circulate in an economy the way cash does: nobody checks a twenty-dollar bill's serial number against a database before accepting it in exchange for a cup of coffee, because the twenty is worth the same twenty dollars whether or not the barista investigates it. Bank deposits have historically occupied roughly the same status, at least up to the deposit insurance limit — a depositor does not perform credit analysis on the bank before trusting that a hundred dollars in a checking account is worth a hundred dollars, because deposit insurance and bank regulation have made that trust largely unnecessary. Gorton's argument is that, in the wholesale, largely uninsured financial system that grew up outside retail banking over the 1990s and 2000s, the senior and super-senior tranches of mortgage securitizations came to occupy that same no-questions-asked status. A triple-A rating, backed by decades of historically low default rates on the kind of mortgage pools the ratings were originally calibrated against, let these instruments circulate through the repo market the way cash circulates through a register — accepted at face value, posted as collateral without anyone on the other side of the trade feeling the need to open the prospectus and read what was actually inside the pool. That is not laziness. It is, under ordinary conditions, an efficient and entirely rational way to run a funding market: checking the contents of every bond every time it changes hands would make the whole system too slow and too expensive to function, and for a long stretch of time nobody needed to check, because the debt really did behave the way cash behaves.

The trouble with informationally insensitive debt is that its safety is a belief the market holds about the debt, not a property built permanently into the debt itself, and beliefs can change quickly when the right shock arrives. That shock, in Gorton and Metrick's telling, was the rise in subprime mortgage delinquencies that became unmistakable through 2007. The delinquencies themselves were concentrated in a specific, identifiable slice of the mortgage market — loans originated with weak underwriting standards during the boom's final years, disproportionately packaged into specific vintages of subprime securitizations. But here is the mechanism that turned a contained problem into a systemic one: from outside any given securitization, a repo lender holding a AAA tranche as collateral had no fast, reliable way to know whether the specific pool behind that specific bond was one of the contaminated ones or one of the clean ones. The underlying loans, the servicing records, the exact composition of any given pool, were not things a repo desk could evaluate overnight, or in most cases at all. Faced with genuine uncertainty about which bonds were safe and which were not, and unable to tell the difference quickly enough to matter, rational lenders did the only thing available to them: they stopped trusting the entire asset class, good pools and bad pools alike, and either demanded a much larger haircut — extra collateral posted above the loan amount, as insurance against being wrong — or simply refused to lend against that kind of collateral at all. Gorton and Metrick's own reconstruction of the data shows haircuts on securitized collateral moving from something close to negligible in early 2007 to levels, on some classes of structured product, that made borrowing against them prohibitively expensive within about a year — a shift few repo desks would have thought possible when the market opened for business on an ordinary morning in 2006.

That is the specific move that makes a Gorton-style panic different from an ordinary, if painful, credit downturn, and the difference matters enormously for judging any later market against it. An ordinary downturn discriminates: bad borrowers get marked down, good borrowers do not, and a careful analyst willing to do the work can, in principle, tell the two apart and price them differently. A run on informationally insensitive debt does not discriminate, at least not at first, because the entire point of the panic is that nobody can do the discriminating fast enough. Every bond that shares the collateral type, the ratings category, the general shape of the instruments now under suspicion, gets treated as potentially contaminated, because verifying otherwise takes longer than the funding market is willing to wait. That indiscriminate quality is what made the 2008 panic self-reinforcing rather than self-correcting. Rising haircuts forced holders of securitized collateral to post more collateral or find other funding, which some could not do, which forced asset sales into an already nervous market, which pushed prices down further, which made the case for higher haircuts look more justified rather than less, which pushed haircuts higher still. Money market funds, which had themselves issued shares treated as close to cash-equivalent, discovered they held commercial paper backed by exactly the kind of collateral now under suspicion, and one of them, the Reserve Primary Fund, famously broke the buck — reported a net asset value below a dollar a share — a shock to a corner of the financial system that had never been expected to lose money at all. None of that story required every mortgage in America to default. It required a large, short-funded, previously unquestioned market to lose confidence in what it was holding, all at roughly the same moment, with no time for anyone to sort the good collateral from the bad before the funding evaporated underneath both.

That is the ruler this chapter means to lay against today's private credit market, mechanism by mechanism rather than mood by mood, because the resemblance between the two eras is real enough at a glance to deserve the comparison and specific enough, once you look closely, to need more precision than a glance provides. Start with the funding side, because it is where the analogy is strongest at first sight and weakest on inspection. Pre-2008 securitized banking was funded overnight or close to it — repo agreements rolled daily, asset-backed commercial paper reissued every few weeks, money market fund shares redeemable on a moment's notice — against assets that could not actually be liquidated on anything like that timescale. Private credit, as earlier chapters of this book have described in detail, is funded almost the opposite way. The dominant vehicle in the industry remains the closed-end drawdown fund, in which an institutional investor commits capital for a term typically measured in seven to ten years or longer and cannot simply ask for it back because the market turned nervous on a Tuesday. Even the retail-facing evergreen and interval funds built to offer some periodic liquidity, the kind this book has already followed through a redemption gate, are explicitly structured around quarterly windows, percentage caps, and disclosed gating mechanics rather than daily, on-demand redemption funded overnight. An investor in one of these vehicles who wants cash back in a panic runs into a wall built into the fund's own governing documents, not a market that has simply stopped taking calls. That is not a small difference. It is close to the entire difference between a structure that can be forced to sell into a falling market at two in the morning to meet an overnight obligation, and one that, whatever its other flaws, was never funded on a clock fast enough to force that kind of sale in the first place.

The Federal Reserve, the International Monetary Fund, and the Financial Stability Board — the same institutions whose interconnection data appeared earlier in this book — have each made versions of this same point in their own recent assessments of private credit, generally in more hedged and technical language than a book is free to use, but pointing the same direction. The Federal Reserve's own research on bank lending to private credit providers, alongside a separate Federal Reserve Bank of Boston study of the systemic-risk channels running through the sector, characterizes private credit funds as typically employing materially lower leverage at the fund level than the structured investment vehicles and conduits that sat at the center of the 2008 panic, many of which ran on leverage ratios so extreme — assets many multiples of the sliver of equity actually absorbing losses — that a relatively small decline in asset values could wipe out the entire equity cushion in days. Direct-lending funds do use leverage, through subscription credit facilities that borrow against uncalled investor commitments and, increasingly, through loans secured against the fund's net asset value rather than against specific underlying loans. Both tools have grown, and both draw real scrutiny from the Financial Stability Board's May 2026 report on private credit vulnerabilities, which names leverage growth as a trend worth continued monitoring rather than dismissing. But growing scrutiny of a trend is a different finding from evidence that the trend has already reproduced pre-2008 leverage multiples, and as of the reports gathered for this book, it has not. The gap between "some leverage, drawing regulatory attention" and "leverage so extreme that a modest asset decline erases the equity cushion in days" is not a rhetorical distinction. It is close to the entire reason a 2008-style cascade requires the specific funding structure it requires, and not merely the presence of risk somewhere in a system.

The second mechanism worth testing directly against the 2008 framework is the shape of the credit itself — not how much risk it carries, but how many hands it passes through and how many times that risk gets re-leveraged along the way. Pre-crisis securitized banking was, definitionally, a daisy chain: a mortgage originator sold loans to an issuer, who pooled them into a trust, who sliced the trust into tranches sold to a wide range of investors, some of whom then repackaged their own tranches into new structured products — collateralized debt obligations built substantially out of other securitizations' mezzanine tranches — that were rated and sold again, and some of those new instruments were themselves financed through repo by yet another set of institutions, each layer adding its own funding leverage on top of the leverage already embedded in the layer below. The same underlying pool of mortgage risk could end up supporting several multiples of its own value in claims scattered across different balance sheets, each one believing it held a safe, diversified, professionally rated instrument. Direct lending, for all the real problems traced elsewhere in this book, mostly does not work that way. A private credit fund that lends to a mid-market industrial company or a software business typically originates that loan, holds it on its own books through maturity or refinancing, and reports it as a single asset on a single balance sheet rather than slicing it into tranches resold to third parties who then finance their slice through a separate, shorter-term funding chain of their own. There are meaningful exceptions worth naming rather than glossing over — collateralized loan obligations built from broadly syndicated leveraged loans remain a real, tranched, actively traded corner of the credit markets, and some direct lenders do participate in club deals and secondary sales that create their own, smaller versions of multi-party exposure. But the dominant private credit structure this book has spent its chapters describing — a fund originating and holding a loan directly, funded by locked-up limited partner capital — lacks the multiplicative re-leveraging chain that let a finite pool of actual mortgage losses fund a crisis many times that size.

Where the analogy earns its keep, and where this book's own earlier chapters have already done the work of establishing it, is in the two places Gorton's framework treats as necessary but not sufficient conditions for a panic: opacity about what an asset actually contains, and interconnection between the shadow structure and the regulated banking system it depends on. Both are real, and both rhyme. The chapter of this book built around fair-value accounting described in detail how a private credit fund's quarterly, manager-produced mark substitutes for the continuous, independently discovered price that a public bond or a repo-eligible security would carry — a substitution that, at least on its surface, recalls how AAA-rated tranches came to circulate as cash-equivalent collateral before anyone had reason to doubt the rating behind them. The interconnection data are just as concrete. The Federal Reserve's own research estimated bank lending to private credit providers at roughly three hundred billion dollars as of June 2025, extended through the warehouse lines, subscription facilities, and direct credit arrangements that let banks fund the nonbank lenders now competing with them for the same borrowers — a structure that, in shape if not in scale, echoes the liquidity backstops banks quietly extended to their own off-balance-sheet conduits and structured investment vehicles before 2008. Both parallels deserve to be taken seriously rather than waved away, and both appear, in the regulators' own reports, as genuine reasons for continued monitoring rather than complacency. But it is worth sitting with what neither parallel establishes on its own. Opacity about an asset's contents matters enormously for how badly a downturn's losses get distributed and how slowly they surface. It is not, by itself, the mechanism that turns a downturn into an overnight, system-wide funding panic. That mechanism, in Gorton's own account, required short-term funding that could be pulled all at once — and that is precisely the piece missing, or at minimum far smaller and far slower, from the structure this book has spent seventeen chapters describing.

It is worth adding one more genuine rhyme before drawing a conclusion, because a fair comparison does not get to ignore the evidence that complicates it. Private credit today is concentrated among a small number of very large managers, whose combined share of origination and assets under management is large enough that their distress would not stay neatly contained to their own funds, given how much bank lending, insurance-company capital, and retirement savings now run through the same handful of names traced across this book's earlier chapters on insurance and retirement access. That concentration draws its own comparison to the small handful of large dealers and monoline insurers whose distress in 2008 turned out to matter far beyond their own balance sheets, because so much of the system's plumbing ran through them. Concentration of this kind is a legitimate systemic concern independent of funding structure, and it is one the Financial Stability Board's own report flags directly, alongside leverage and interconnection, as a factor worth continued attention. But concentration of ownership and concentration of short-term funding reliance are different risks, with different failure speeds. A manager whose fund faces rising losses across a concentrated book of exposures can, and most likely would, respond with slower write-downs, tightened gates, and a multi-year workout — the mechanism this book has already described in its chapters on valuation and redemption gates — rather than an overnight collapse in the value of collateral being financed hour to hour across dozens of counterparties simultaneously. The size of an eventual loss and the speed at which it arrives are not the same question, and Gorton's framework is fundamentally a theory about speed.

Put the pieces together honestly, and the conclusion this chapter can defend is narrower than the one a reader primed by a year of cockroach headlines might expect, and it is worth stating plainly rather than building toward it as a surprise: private credit today appears to lack the specific overnight funding fragility that turned 2007's mortgage losses into 2008's cascading, self-reinforcing panic, even in the places — valuation opacity, bank interconnection, manager concentration — where the resemblance to the earlier era is genuine and worth continued attention. That is not the same claim as private credit being safe. It is a claim about which failure mode is plausible and which one is not. A structure funded by multi-year lockups and disclosed redemption gates, holding loans directly rather than re-leveraging them through a chain of tranched resales, using materially less leverage at the fund level than the vehicles Gorton and Metrick studied, is not built to produce a Lehman weekend. It is built, if the underlying credit turns out to have been underpriced across enough of the industry at once, to produce something slower and less cinematic: rising PIK interest quietly substituting for cash payments, spreading redemption gates as investors who can ask for money back do so, gradual markdowns arriving over quarters rather than the space of a single trading session, and losses landing hardest on whichever specific banks, insurers, and warehouse lines happen to be exposed to the specific managers and specific borrowers that go wrong — much closer, in shape, to what this book has already traced through the First Brands and Tricolor bankruptcies than to what happened at Bear Stearns or Lehman Brothers.

None of this should read as an all-clear, and the honest version of this chapter's argument has to resist that temptation as hard as it resists the opposite one. A slower failure mode is not a smaller one, and it is not automatically a safer one for the people at the end of the chain. A credit cycle that unwinds over eight quarters instead of eight days can still destroy real capital, still gate real retirees out of money they believed they could access, still leave banks holding real losses on warehouse lines they underwrote with the same overconfidence Jamie Dimon later called, about his own bank's Tricolor exposure, not its finest moment. The absence of a repo-style funding run does not mean the absence of loss; it means that any loss, if it arrives, will very likely look different from the version of 2008 most readers already carry around in their heads, arrive on a different clock, and concentrate in different places than a mortgage-securitization crisis did. It is also worth naming, rather than hiding, what could weaken this chapter's own conclusion over time: fund-level leverage through subscription lines and net-asset-value-based borrowing has been growing, not shrinking, and regulators at the Federal Reserve, the IMF, and the FSB are watching that growth specifically because leverage is the one variable in Gorton's framework that private credit could, in principle, still import in greater force than it currently carries. If subscription-facility and NAV-loan leverage continue expanding faster than regulators currently expect, or if some future retail-facing structure quietly reintroduces daily liquidity funded against genuinely illiquid loans, the specific asymmetry this chapter rests on would narrow. As of the evidence gathered here, it has not narrowed yet.

Chapter after chapter, this book has tried to hold the harder, more disciplined version of its own worry rather than the version that makes for a better headline, and this chapter's job was to take that discipline to its most demanding test: the single best-documented, most rigorously modeled financial panic in a century, examined by two economists who did the empirical work of tracing how a shadow banking system's funding structure turned a contained mortgage problem into a global crisis. Held up against that specific, well-evidenced mechanism rather than against a general feeling that private credit resembles 2008 because both involve opaque, fast-growing, nonbank credit, the resemblance holds in places and breaks in others, and a book that claimed otherwise in either direction would be less honest than the evidence allows it to be. The valuation opacity is real. The bank interconnection is real, if smaller in scale than its pre-2008 counterpart. The concentration among a handful of dominant managers is real. The overnight, self-reinforcing funding run that made those earlier problems catastrophic rather than merely serious — the mechanism Gorton and Metrick spent their careers documenting — is, on the evidence available as this book goes to press, largely absent from the structure private credit actually uses today.

Having taken the systemic worry as seriously as the evidence allows, and having found it partial rather than total, the book owes its own argument something it has not yet given it: a full, serious hearing for the other side, on its own terms rather than as a rebuttal squeezed into the margins of a chapter built to worry. The next chapter makes that case directly — not as a counterweight inserted for the sake of balance, but as an argument with two decades of its own data behind it, including a return stream that lived through 2008 itself and came out the other side with numbers worth taking as seriously as anything in this chapter.
