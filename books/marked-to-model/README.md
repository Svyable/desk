# Marked to Model

*Private Credit, Retail Retirement, and the Debt Nobody Prices*

Banks used to make most of the loans that kept mid-sized companies running. Over the last fifteen years, a growing share of that lending moved to a different kind of institution: unregulated, privately negotiated, and largely invisible to the public markets that once priced credit risk every day. By 2025 this private credit industry had grown to roughly two trillion dollars, financing everything from leveraged buyouts to auto dealerships to data centers, valued not by a ticking market price but by the lender's own model, marked quarterly, mostly out of public view.

*Marked to Model* asks what happens when an asset class built on the absence of a daily price collides with two things at once: a stress test it did not expect, and a retail audience it did not have before. In September and October of 2025, two mid-sized private-credit borrowers — an auto-parts supplier and a subprime auto lender — collapsed within weeks of each other amid allegations of double-pledged collateral, prompting JPMorgan's Jamie Dimon to warn that "when you see one cockroach, there are probably more." The same year, an executive order began opening 401(k) plans, for the first time, to exactly the kind of illiquid, privately valued assets that professional investors had spent a decade learning to price with extraordinary caution.

The book is not a bet that private credit is a bubble that must burst on a schedule. It traces the real history of why lending left the banking system, gives serious voice to the case that direct lending is a durable, valuable evolution of capital markets, and follows the money through insurance companies, business development companies, evergreen retail funds, and eventually toward ordinary retirement accounts — asking, at each stop, who is actually pricing the risk, and who is merely marking it.
