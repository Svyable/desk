# A Sphere With a Bad Reputation

Ball lightning has spent most of its scientific life in the worst possible evidentiary neighborhood.

The reports are vivid. A luminous sphere appears during or after a thunderstorm. It may hover, drift, enter a building, travel along a wire, cross an aircraft cabin, pass near a window, vanish silently or end with a bang. Durations are often longer than an ordinary lightning flash. Colors vary. Size estimates cluster around the human scale: not kilometers, not sparks, but something one can imagine occupying a room.

That richness is exactly the problem.

A literature made primarily of reports can accumulate characteristics faster than nature can be shown to possess them. Once “ball lightning” becomes a bucket, every spherical or roughly spherical luminous event near a storm risks entering it. The resulting composite creature can pass through windows, explode walls, float against wind, roll down aisles, smell of sulfur, melt conductors and remain cool enough to approach. A theory that explains all of that may be explaining a folklore aggregate rather than one physical phenomenon.

The scientific question is therefore not simply, “What causes ball lightning?” It is, “How many physically distinct things have been called ball lightning?”

That is a harder question and a better one.

Some reports likely describe electrical arcs or power-line failures. Some may be afterimages or ordinary lightning seen through unusual geometry. Some involve combustion. Some may be compact luminous plasma or aerosol systems. Some cannot be classified from the surviving information. The category may still contain a coherent natural phenomenon; the 2012 Qinghai observation is powerful evidence that at least one ball-lightning-like event can be instrumented as a real atmospheric occurrence associated with a cloud-to-ground strike.

But one good event cannot retroactively authenticate the whole archive.

This becomes directly relevant to UAP. The modern UAP category is even broader. It is administrative before it is physical. A case enters because something observed in air, sea, space, or across domains remains unidentified at some stage of analysis. The category therefore contains ordinary objects not yet resolved, sensor artifacts, classified systems, unusual natural phenomena, poor data, hoaxes, and perhaps genuinely novel phenomena. Treating “UAP” as if it were one thing repeats the ball-lightning error at a larger scale.

The right move is decomposition.

Ball-lightning reports can be separated by proximity to thunderstorms, relation to cloud-to-ground strikes, lifetime, spectrum, physical interaction, indoor versus outdoor formation, trajectory, acoustic termination and electromagnetic environment. UAP cases can be separated by sensor suite, range certainty, kinematics, persistence, environmental context, morphology, thermal behavior and independent corroboration. The purpose is not bureaucratic tidiness. It is to discover whether one mechanism is being asked to explain incompatible clusters.

A sphere is particularly treacherous because optics likes circles. A defocused point source becomes a disk. Blooming makes bright compact objects larger. Aperture effects create structured bokeh. Thresholding rounds diffuse lights. A saturated source can lose internal detail and become a clean geometric object precisely when the sensor is providing less information, not more.

That does not mean reported spheres are artifacts. It means “spherical” is the beginning of measurement, not the end.

The same applies to silent motion. A distant object can be acoustically silent at the observer. A low-energy luminous event can have no engine to hear. A moving image can be caused by observer motion. A real nearby solid object moving quickly and silently imposes a different physical burden. Those cases should not be blended.

Ball lightning earned its bad reputation partly because science was right to distrust a heterogeneous testimony record and partly because the heterogeneity made it easy to dismiss the possibility of a real core. That combination is common around anomalies. Skepticism notices contamination and then quietly treats contamination as nonexistence. Enthusiasm notices persistent reports and quietly treats persistence as validation.

The better posture is more laborious. Preserve the report. Grade the data. Identify what was actually measured. Build subclasses. Seek events where several independent physical channels were recorded. Do not let the weakest stories inflate the strongest case, and do not let the weakest stories poison it either.

A sphere with a bad reputation can still be a real thing.

It just has to earn its properties one at a time.
