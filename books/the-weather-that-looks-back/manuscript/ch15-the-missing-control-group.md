# The Missing Control Group

Most anomaly archives have cases and almost no controls.

That is backwards.

If a naval aviator reports an unusual infrared target, the comparison set should include thousands of ordinary infrared tracks from the same sensor, modes, altitudes, backgrounds and maneuvers. If a civilian camera captures a luminous sphere, investigators need examples of insects, raindrops, defocused stars, distant aircraft, power-line arcs and lightning recorded by the same model under similar settings. If radar shows a transient track, the system's clutter and anomalous-propagation behavior should be known before the track becomes exotic.

Without controls, analysts learn the event by staring at it. Human perception is very good at manufacturing significance under those conditions.

Atmospheric electricity offers a ready-made control library because many phenomena are visually uncanny and physically ordinary. Sprites can be enormous and brief. Elves can expand across hundreds of kilometers. Meteors leave ionized trails that radar detects after visible emission changes. Lightning can generate radio bursts, x-rays and gamma rays. Power systems produce arcs and luminous debris. Temperature inversions distort distant lights. None of these should be shoved into UAP databases indiscriminately. They should be measured deliberately as known classes.

The missing experiment is simple in concept: expose UAP sensor systems to a zoo of known weird things.

Do it blind. Give analysts and algorithms the same data products they would receive from an unknown event. Ask for classification and confidence. Include aircraft and balloons, atmospheric plasmas, astronomical sources, sensor artifacts and deliberately ambiguous cases. Preserve the failure modes.

The resulting confusion matrix would tell us more about UAP evidence than another year of arguments over labels.

If a particular radar-infrared combination almost never confuses known natural phenomena with maneuvering targets, then a future anomaly on that system becomes more interesting. If the system frequently manufactures apparent accelerations or loses range on diffuse sources, those failure modes should enter the prior.

This also creates a way to compare institutions. A military sensor suite may have extraordinary sensitivity but opaque processing. A scientific observatory may have modest sensitivity but calibrated raw data. Citizen camera networks may have broad coverage but poor metadata. None is universally superior.

Controls let each system reveal what it is actually good at.

The strange status of ball lightning makes it especially valuable. It can occupy both sides of the experiment. The Qinghai event is a partly characterized natural anomalous event. A 2025 analysis by K. Stephan, R. Sonnenfeld and J. Bertoloni examined a close-range security-camera video of glowing objects during a thunderstorm and treated both ball lightning and nearby power-line arcing as live alternatives. The paper's value lies less in declaring a winner than in reconstructing geometry, brightness, motion and weather while preserving a conventional competitor.

That is the habit UAP work needs.

A case should not become stronger because analysts ran out of ideas. It should become stronger because specific competitors were tested and failed.

Controls turn ignorance into an experiment.
