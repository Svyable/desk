# Build a Weather Zoo

The proposal is embarrassingly concrete.

Take the sensors we hope will discover something new and point them first at things we already know are strange.

Sprites. Elves. Blue jets. Gigantic jets. Ordinary lightning at several ranges. Triggered lightning. Meteors. Persistent meteor trails. Aurora. Balloons. Aircraft. Drones. Birds. Insects near optics. Lens flare. Internal reflections. Power-line arcs. Rocket plumes. Reentry debris. Searchlights on cloud. Superior mirages. Temperature inversions over water. Ball-lightning candidates when fortune permits.

Record them with synchronized visible, infrared, radar, RF, acoustic, electric-field and weather instrumentation. Publish the raw or suitably reduced reference data where security permits. Create adversarial subsets that look object-like.

Then stop calling the database a list of false positives. It is a natural phenomenology library.

The distinction matters. “False positive” defines a sprite by its failure to be a vehicle. “Atmospheric electrical event” defines it by what it is. A good reference zoo should contain enough physical metadata that an unknown can be compared mechanistically, not merely visually.

A spectrum can separate incandescent material from line-dominated plasma emission. Polarization can constrain scattering. Radar frequency dependence can distinguish target types. Stereo imaging can settle range. Broadband RF can tie an optical event to lightning or electrical discharge. Infrasound can expose energetic events invisible to a camera. Weather profiles can identify refractive layers.

Machine-learning systems trained on such a zoo would still require caution. A classifier can become very confident outside its training distribution. The point is not to automate mystery away. It is to quantify where known classes overlap the observations.

The reference library also needs ugly data. Compressed video. Dirty windows. Saturated targets. Sensor slews. Missing frames. Tracking loss. Those are not defects to remove from training; they are part of the operational world where anomalies occur.

An especially useful program would use mobile instrument packages around regions with recurring reports. Hessdalen is obvious. So are high-lightning regions where ball-lightning reports can be correlated with modern lightning-location networks. Coastal zones with strong inversion layers offer another control. Meteor showers provide predictable ionization events.

This approach changes the tone of UAP research. Instead of beginning with the most famous cases and arguing backward, it builds a forward model of the sky.

What does nature generate? How often? Under what conditions? What signatures reach our sensors? Which known classes are systematically misclassified? What remains after the library gets good?

The residue then means something.

A weather zoo will not solve the political question of classified aerospace programs. It will not authenticate crash-retrieval testimony. It will not tell us whether intelligence exists elsewhere in the universe. It does something narrower that all those questions need: it removes avoidable ignorance about our own atmosphere.

Before asking whether something came from somewhere else, we should become unusually good at recognizing what the Earth can make by itself.
