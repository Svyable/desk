# 18. What Would Change My Mind?

A book about evidence should end with a way to lose.

Here is mine.

If returned material from a well-characterized extraterrestrial environment produced complex molecular systems with strong non-random sequence structure, a coherent and hard-to-reproduce chiral architecture, independent isotopic evidence, spatial organization in primary geological context, and no plausible contamination route—and if separate laboratories reproduced the result on independently allocated samples—I would move far beyond 'interesting organics.'

If investigators then demonstrated active chemistry resembling metabolism, templated copying, growth, repair or heritable variation under conditions that exclude terrestrial organisms and laboratory artifacts, the biological hypothesis would become difficult to avoid.

If a clean extraterrestrial sample yielded an organism whose molecular machinery was fundamentally unrelated to terrestrial life—different informational polymers, different coding logic, different core biochemistry—the case for an independent origin would be extraordinary and testable. If instead it shared terrestrial biochemistry in exquisite detail, panspermia or contamination would become competing hypotheses and provenance would matter even more.

What would weaken the central thesis of this book?

If future work showed that the major Bennu and Ryugu organics attributed to extraterrestrial chemistry were actually dominated by terrestrial contamination despite the documented controls, then the claimed strength of the abiotic inventory would fall. If supposedly abiotic pathways repeatedly failed to reproduce key compound distributions under realistic parent-body conditions, biological alternatives might deserve more weight. If a robust biosignature emerged that was both common to life and genuinely inaccessible to relevant abiotic chemistry, the evidentiary ladder could become shorter.

I would welcome any of those outcomes.

The universe does not owe skepticism a victory any more than it owes believers aliens.

The lesson from Bennu is not that dead rocks explain everything. It is that dead rocks have earned the right to be taken seriously.

We brought one home, protected it from ourselves, opened it carefully, and found a chemical world that used many of life's words without ever writing the sentence.

That is not disappointing.

That is the clue.
