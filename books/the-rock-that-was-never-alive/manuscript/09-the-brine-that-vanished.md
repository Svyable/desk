# 9. The Brine That Vanished

Some of Bennu's most consequential evidence is fragile enough that Earth's air can erase it.

Returned samples contain an evaporite sequence: sodium-bearing phosphates, carbonates, sulfates, chlorides and fluorides interpreted as products of ancient brines in the parent body. Nature's 2025 work emphasized that careful sample return and curation made it possible to preserve salts that prolonged terrestrial atmospheric exposure could compromise.

This is the chapter where chain of custody stops looking administrative and starts looking geological.

If exposure after collection changes a mineral, then provenance is not merely about whether a lab technician sneezed on the sample. It is about whether the thing being measured still exists in the form it had before Earth.

The inferred brines widen the chemical stage. Water existed long enough, under the right conditions, to move ions and precipitate minerals in sequence. Phosphates matter because phosphate is central to terrestrial biochemistry. Carbonates matter because they encode fluid history. Salts matter because concentration and phase behavior can reshape reaction environments.

But a habitable chemistry is not an inhabited chemistry.

Earth teaches the opposite lesson too often. We see water and mentally add microbes. We see organics and add metabolism. We see phosphate and add RNA. The proper sequence is slower: characterize environment; model abiotic processes; identify disequilibria; search for structures or distributions that exceed those processes; close contamination pathways; seek replication across independent methods.

The brines are thrilling because they show that small bodies could host chemically productive aqueous episodes. Similar mineralogical clues connect outward to Ceres and icy worlds. They make parent bodies less like frozen warehouses and more like transient reactors.

The vanished brine also offers a useful metaphor for evidence itself. Some facts survive only if the method was designed before the sample arrived.

You cannot reconstruct a clean-room history after the molecule becomes famous.
