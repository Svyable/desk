# 14. Panspermia's Missing Middle

Panspermia has a branding advantage over prebiotic delivery because it gives the traveler a passport.

Not molecules. Life.

The modest version says viable organisms or propagules can move between worlds under some circumstances. Lithopanspermia models ask whether impact ejecta can launch rocks, whether organisms shielded inside could survive shock, vacuum, radiation and time, and whether material could land gently enough to seed another environment. Some steps are experimentally testable. Microbes and spores can survive extremes that once seemed absurd. Rocks do move between Mars and Earth. None of that proves a natural transfer event carried living organisms that founded terrestrial life.

The strong version often skips the middle.

We find organics in meteorites. Microbes can survive space exposure for bounded periods. Therefore life came from elsewhere.

Between those statements lies the entire case.

A credible natural panspermia claim would need a source biosphere, a transfer vehicle, survival through launch, transit and entry, and evidence linking the resulting lineage to the recipient world. Ancient transfers are especially difficult because successful seeding erases its travel label. Once descendants evolve for billions of years, what observation distinguishes imported life from locally originated life if both share the same chemistry?

Directed panspermia adds agency and therefore another evidentiary burden: not only did life travel, someone intended the trip.

Bennu does not solve panspermia. It makes a weaker and better-supported proposition stronger: prebiotic molecules are not confined to planets.

That may turn out to be more important. If many young worlds are chemically provisioned before biology, then the universe does not need microbes to ride every rock in order for worlds to share a prebiotic inheritance.

We may be cousins at the level of chemistry without sharing an ancestor.
