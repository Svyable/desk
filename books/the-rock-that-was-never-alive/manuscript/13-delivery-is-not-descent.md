# 13. Delivery Is Not Descent

Suppose an asteroid delivered ribose to early Earth.

What follows?

Less than the sentence wants.

Delivery hypotheses operate at several layers that are routinely compressed into one. First, a compound must exist on the source body. Second, material containing it must be liberated. Third, the compound or a useful precursor must survive transport and atmospheric entry. Fourth, it must arrive at a terrestrial environment where it is not immediately destroyed or diluted beyond relevance. Fifth, it must participate in a network that advances prebiotic chemistry. Sixth, that network must cross whatever thresholds separate chemistry from evolution.

Bennu strengthens the first layer for an impressive inventory. It contributes to the plausibility of the second because asteroid material obviously reaches planets. It does not complete the rest.

The quantitative problem matters. Early Earth synthesized organics endogenously through atmospheric, hydrothermal, surface, lightning, photochemical and impact-related processes. Exogenous delivery may have supplemented those inventories, dominated certain molecules, mattered only locally, or been largely irrelevant to the decisive transitions. Different models can fit different compounds.

The phrase 'delivered the building blocks of life' is therefore both reasonable and dangerously complete-sounding.

A truck can deliver lumber without building a house.

Even the lumber metaphor cheats because prebiotic molecules are consumed, transformed and selected by environment. There is no fixed blueprint waiting for adenine to arrive. Chemistry explores.

The real scientific opportunity is not to choose between 'Earth made life' and 'space made life.' That binary belongs to mythology. Solar-system chemistry and terrestrial chemistry were coupled. Material crossed boundaries. The origin problem is distributed across reservoirs.

Delivery is part of that history.

Descent—with modification—is a later word.
