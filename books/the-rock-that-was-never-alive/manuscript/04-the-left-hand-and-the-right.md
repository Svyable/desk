# 4. The Left Hand and the Right

Life on Earth has preferences.

Proteins are built overwhelmingly from left-handed amino acids. Sugars in nucleic acids have their own handedness. This asymmetry is so deep in biology that chirality has long tempted astrobiologists as a possible signature of life: perhaps a strong enantiomeric excess, especially in a biologically meaningful family of molecules, could indicate selection by living chemistry.

Then the rocks complicate the romance.

Ryugu's returned samples contained amino acids with left and right forms present at roughly equal abundance. Bennu's chiral non-protein amino acids were likewise reported as racemic or nearly so. That is exactly the sort of result a careful field should love because it refuses to flatter the desired conclusion. The samples contain life's components while withholding life's familiar asymmetry.

Racemic mixtures do not prove every molecule was formed abiotically in every possible case. They do something narrower and more useful: they fit well with known non-biological synthesis and weaken the idea that simply finding those amino acids is a fingerprint of organisms.

Murchison complicates the story further because some meteorite amino acids have shown enantiomeric excesses. The literature around those excesses matters because it demonstrates how fast chirality can become metaphysics. A small asymmetry may arise from astrophysical processes, aqueous alteration, amplification, analytical effects, contamination or some combination. If a rock contains a left-handed excess, that does not entitle us to smuggle in a microbe.

The real question is not whether chirality matters. It is what distribution would be difficult for nonliving chemistry to reproduce, under the exact environment being studied.

That standard sounds annoyingly conditional because it is. Biosignatures are rarely universal passwords. Methane can be biological or geological. Oxygen can accumulate abiotically on some planets. Organics can arrive from space. Isotopic fractionation can have multiple causes. Morphology can deceive. Even complex molecules may emerge in prebiotic networks.

Life is not obliged to leave one clue at a time.

A strong case will probably look less like finding a magic molecule and more like catching a system in the act of violating its geochemical alternatives: coupled disequilibria, structured distributions, selective chemistry, spatial organization, replication-associated patterns, or multiple independent signatures that converge on the same biological history.

Handedness may be part of that convergence. It is not a shortcut around it.
