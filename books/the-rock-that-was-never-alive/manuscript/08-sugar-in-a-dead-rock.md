# 8. Sugar in a Dead Rock

In late 2025 researchers reported ribose and glucose in Bennu samples.

The finding was almost designed for headlines. Ribose is part of RNA's backbone. Glucose is central to terrestrial metabolism. Put either molecule beside the phrase asteroid sample and a person can be forgiven for imagining a fossilized kitchen.

The actual result is better.

Ribose in pristine returned material removes some of the ambiguity that hangs over sugar detections in meteorites exposed to Earth. The absence of detected deoxyribose in the same work is also interesting because it gives the chemistry shape rather than merely adding one more item to a list. The authors connected the distribution to plausible parent-body chemistry involving formaldehyde and aqueous alteration.

Again: plausible chemistry, not biology.

The broader lesson is that life's molecular infrastructure may be assembled from components whose individual synthesis is not rare. Amino acids, bases, phosphate and ribose together sound almost scandalously close to RNA until one asks for RNA.

There is no reported Bennu genome. No ribozyme. No self-replicating polymer population. No evidence of Darwinian history.

This is where origin-of-life discussions often commit a kind of scale error. If the gap from simple carbon chemistry to a sugar once seemed enormous, then closing that gap feels like closing the problem. But the remaining gap may contain the real difficulty. Polymerization in water has constraints. Monomers compete. Side reactions proliferate. Concentrations matter. Cycles matter. Stability matters. Compartments matter. Copying fidelity matters. Selection needs heritable variation, not merely interesting molecules.

The fact that a dead asteroid can supply the parts makes those system-level transitions more, not less, important.

Bennu is not telling us life is easy.

It is telling us we were probably giving the easy part too much credit.
