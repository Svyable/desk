# 10. The Contamination Budget

Every extraterrestrial-life claim should come with a contamination budget.

Not a paragraph saying contamination was considered. A budget.

What touched the sample? What outgassed near it? Which amino acids were present on the hardware? Which solvents were used? What was in the clean-room air? Which microbes were found on surfaces? Which blanks traveled through the same procedure? Which witness plates were exposed during assembly? Which compounds can the spacecraft itself contribute? What changed after landing? Which laboratory standards could bleed into the measurement?

OSIRIS-REx is valuable because contamination knowledge was engineered into the mission. Published plans specified cleanliness levels and limits for amino acids and hydrazine on the sampler head. Witness materials monitored assembly and flight environments. Archived hardware could be compared with returned material. The curation system used high-purity nitrogen and an ISO-5-equivalent environment, with monitoring for particles, organics, gases and microbes.

None of this sounds as glamorous as an alien bacterium.

That is why it is trustworthy.

Contamination control has an asymmetry. When it succeeds, nothing happens. No terrestrial cell colonizes the grain. No fingerprint contributes fatty acids. No bag leaks plasticizers into an extract. Success produces boring provenance. Failure produces exciting molecules.

The incentive structure is therefore perverse. Weak claims benefit rhetorically from contamination being invisible, while strong programs spend years making contamination visible enough to subtract.

This problem becomes vicious with low-biomass samples. If the true extraterrestrial signal is tiny, a trivial amount of terrestrial material can dominate. DNA sequencing is especially unforgiving because amplification turns rare contamination into abundant data. A clean negative control is not an accessory to such experiments. It is part of the claim.

The phrase *extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence* has become decorative from overuse. A contamination budget is what the phrase looks like after an engineer gets hold of it.
