# 1. The Capsule in Utah

On September 24, 2023, a small capsule crossed into Earth's atmosphere and landed in Utah carrying material collected from asteroid Bennu. The drama was backwards. Nothing inside moved. Nothing inside was supposed to move. The point was to keep Earth's moving, breathing, shedding biology away from the specimen.

That distinction is the book.

Meteorites have always come with a customs problem. A stone can fall from space and still spend hours in mud, days in rain, years in a field, decades in a drawer. It can be handled by the finder, the dealer, the curator, the graduate student, the instrument technician. The molecule detected at the end may be extraterrestrial, terrestrial, laboratory, or a mixture whose history is impossible to reconstruct.

OSIRIS-REx treated contamination as a first-class engineering problem before launch. NASA's published contamination-control strategy specified cleanliness requirements for the sampling hardware, monitored amino acids and hydrazine, used witness materials, archived relevant spacecraft components, and built a record of the environmental background. After return, the canister was protected with nitrogen and moved into curation designed to reduce oxygen, organic and biological exposure.

This was not bureaucratic tidiness. It changed what could be known.

Bennu's samples later yielded amino acids, nucleobases, ammonia, carboxylic acids, fragile salts and other organics. In 2025 researchers reported all five nucleobases used in terrestrial DNA and RNA, fourteen of the twenty amino acids used by terrestrial life to build proteins, and a very large inventory of nitrogen-bearing compounds. Later work reported ribose and glucose. Those discoveries mattered precisely because the chain of custody was better than the chain of custody of a rock picked up in a pasture.

But pristine is not magical. A clean chain does not make a molecule biological. It makes origin claims about the molecule better constrained.

The capsule therefore carried two samples home. One was asteroid regolith. The other was a new standard of embarrassment for weak astrobiological claims. If a result from a returned sample can survive witness plates, clean rooms, archived controls, isotopes, chirality, mineral context and multiple instruments, then a future claim of life from a loose stone has to explain why its evidence deserves the same confidence without the same provenance.

We used to ask whether chemistry from space could resemble life's chemistry. Bennu makes that question feel almost antique. The sharper question is how much resemblance dead chemistry can generate before resemblance stops being evidence.
