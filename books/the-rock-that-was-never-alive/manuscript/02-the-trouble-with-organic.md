# 2. The Trouble with the Word Organic

A molecule does not become a fossil because it contains carbon.

That should be too obvious to need a chapter, but entire cycles of extraterrestrial-life enthusiasm depend on forgetting it for forty-eight hours at a time. In ordinary speech, organic means alive, natural, wholesome, expensive, pesticide-adjacent, or printed in green ink. In chemistry it describes a family of carbon compounds. Carbon compounds can be made by organisms. They can also be made without organisms in molecular clouds, meteorite parent bodies, hydrothermal systems, atmospheres, laboratory flasks and probably more places than we have yet sampled cleanly.

The mistake is not merely semantic. It changes priors.

If a rover finds an organic molecule on Mars, one story says: life may have made this. Another says: geochemistry, photochemistry or delivery may have made this. Neither story wins by existing. Evidence comes from distributions, isotopes, structures, associations, spatial context, degradation pathways, chirality, mineral relationships and the exclusion of known abiotic routes. Context is not garnish around a biosignature. Context is the biosignature.

The Bennu results are useful because they make the abiotic baseline richer. Amino acids sound biologically charged until a pristine asteroid sample produces many of them with near-racemic handedness. Nucleobases sound like genetic evidence until all five canonical bases appear in material with no genome, no cell, no metabolism and no known lineage. Ribose sounds like an RNA whisper until a dead parent body gives chemistry enough time and water to make it.

This is not deflation. It is a promotion of the chemistry.

For decades, popular accounts of life's origin treated the ingredients as the improbable part. Sample-return science is pushing toward a stranger possibility: ingredients may be relatively ordinary, while organization is the miracle. The hard transition is not carbon to glycine. It may be mixtures to selection, selection to autocatalysis, autocatalysis to heredity, heredity to open-ended evolution.

A telescope cannot see that ladder in one spectral line. A mass spectrometer cannot announce the top rung because it detected a lower one.

The word *organic* survives because it is useful. The confusion survives because it is useful too. It is a narrative accelerant. It turns chemistry into creature in a headline-sized step.

The discipline required here is almost comically small: stop at the noun you actually measured.
