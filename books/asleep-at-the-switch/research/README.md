# Research Brief

## Thesis under test

*Asleep at the Switch* asks whether the single, consolidated, roughly eight-hour night that industrial societies treat as biological baseline is actually a historical artifact — an engineering decision made by railroads, factories, electric utilities, and the culture that grew up around them, rather than a discovery about what human bodies need.

The strong version of the argument — that pre-industrial "natural" sleep was healthier and modern sleep is a pure decline — is too simple to survive the evidence and is not the version the book makes. The version worth testing is more durable:

> Artificial light and industrial scheduling did not just shift when people slept; they compressed a historically flexible, often segmented pattern into a single nightly block, removed most of the slack that pattern used to hold, and left almost no institutional mechanism — legal, medical, or economic — to price the resulting deficit until it showed up in a control room, a cockpit, a tanker helm, or a cancer registry.

The book should be able to survive counter-evidence. Some industrial-era sleep science is itself overstated or contested — the book's own chapter on the "Why We Sleep" controversy exists specifically to hold the book to the same standard it applies to its subjects. Some disasters attributed heavily to fatigue had other, larger causes. Some modern "solutions" (trackers, pills, nap pods) are commercial products with their own incentive to dramatize the problem they treat.

## Core empirical tests

1. **The segmentation claim:** How well documented is pre-industrial biphasic ("first sleep"/"second sleep") sleep, and when and why did it disappear?
2. **The electrification mechanism:** What is the actual, traceable link between artificial lighting, industrial shift scheduling, and the compression of sleep into one block?
3. **The vigilance-failure claim:** In which well-investigated disasters does fatigue appear as a documented, investigator-cited causal factor, as opposed to a claim added later by popularizers?
4. **The institutional-response claim:** Where have courts, regulators, or medical bodies (IARC, NHTSA, state legislatures, the FDA) actually created binding rules or classifications tied to sleep or circadian disruption, and how strong is the underlying evidence they cite?
5. **The genetics counter-claim:** How rare and well-documented are natural short-sleep mutations (DEC2 and related genes), and what does their rarity imply about "sleep less, achieve more" folklore built around specific historical figures?
6. **The commercialization claim:** How large and how evidence-based is the current market response (wearables, pharmaceuticals, mattresses, corporate wellness), and where does that market's own incentive to dramatize sleep loss need to be flagged?
7. **The inequality claim:** How unevenly is sleep actually distributed across income, employment status, parental status, and historical housing patterns, and what is the quality of evidence behind each disparity?

## Falsification and counter-evidence

- Ekirch's segmented-sleep thesis, while well documented in historical records, describes a *pattern*, not a universal human requirement; the book should not claim ancestral sleep was superior, only that it was more flexible and less singularly enforced.
- Not every fatigue-linked disaster narrative holds up to scrutiny at the same strength; the Chernobyl fatigue claim specifically has been challenged as overstated by at least one serious critic, and the book should say so rather than repeating the popularized version uncritically.
- IARC's 2007 and 2019 classifications of night shift work as "probably carcinogenic" (Group 2A) are explicitly built on *limited* human evidence: the agency itself notes chance, bias, and confounding could not be ruled out with confidence. The book must preserve that qualification rather than reporting the classification as a settled causal finding.
- A large, well-evidenced sleep-science literature can coexist with a bestselling popularization of it being partly wrong; the book uses the Walker/Guzey episode as a case study in how a broadly correct field can still be misrepresented by its most successful communicator, not as a reason to distrust sleep science generally.
- Commercial sleep-technology and pharmaceutical markets have a direct financial incentive to frame ordinary variation as pathology; market-size figures from research-firm reports vary considerably between vendors and should be presented as estimates, not precise facts.
- Genetic short-sleep mutations are documented but rare; most self-reported short sleepers, including historical figures associated with the trait, are far more likely to be chronically sleep-deprived than genetically exceptional.

## Source discipline

- Prefer investigative-body findings (NTSB probable-cause reports), peer-reviewed journal articles, first-party regulatory or agency material (IARC, NHTSA, FDA, CDC, AASM), university and hospital communications describing named researchers' published work, and long-form journalism from outlets with a named byline and correction record.
- Treat market-size and forecast figures from industry research firms as vendor estimates, not settled facts, and say so in the text where they appear.
- Never quote a historical figure without tracing the quotation to a documented interview, publication, or record cited by a credible secondary source; where a widely repeated quotation's exact provenance is uncertain, say so rather than presenting it as verbatim and unimpeachable.
- Distinguish "probably carcinogenic" (IARC Group 2A, built on limited human evidence) from a proven causal mechanism; distinguish a contributing factor an investigator names from the sole cause of a disaster; distinguish a rare, confirmed genetic mutation from a lifestyle claim.
- Preserve real disagreement in the record — including scholarly pushback on the book's own genre of popular sleep-science claims — rather than smoothing it into a single tidy narrative.
- For every major claim, keep a note titled "What would make this false?"
