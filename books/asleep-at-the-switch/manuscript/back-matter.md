# Back Matter

## A Note on Evidence

This book rests on federal accident investigations, peer-reviewed journal articles, agency classifications and regulatory filings, university research communications, and reporting from established news organizations, rather than on anonymous sources or private access. Every specific statistic, study, and case examined in these pages is attributed to a named institution, researcher, or investigator in the text itself, and the detailed research trail — organized chapter by chapter, with source URLs and explicit notes on what each chapter does and does not claim — is kept separately in `research/README.md` and `research/source-ledger.csv`, so the narrative can read cleanly while the claims underneath it remain checkable.

Several boundaries are worth stating directly, because this book has tried to name its own limits throughout rather than only at the end. The World Health Organization's cancer agency has classified night shift work as "probably carcinogenic to humans" on the strength of limited human evidence that the agency itself says cannot fully rule out chance, bias, or confounding; this book has tried to report that qualified finding rather than the flatter, more alarming version of it that circulates informally. Fatigue is a documented contributing factor in several major industrial disasters examined here, but "contributing factor" and "primary cause" are not the same claim, and at least one of the four disasters discussed — Chernobyl — has had its popularized fatigue narrative directly challenged by a serious critic, a challenge this book has tried to preserve rather than argue around. Margaret Thatcher's association with a rare short-sleep genetic trait is a media nickname built on her public reputation, not a claim that she was ever tested for or confirmed to carry any such mutation. Market-size figures for the sleep-technology and sleep-economy industries come from commercial research firms whose estimates vary considerably from one report to the next, and are presented here as estimates rather than settled facts. And this book's own chapter on the *Why We Sleep* controversy exists because the field it covers has already demonstrated, in public, that even a credentialed expert writing in good faith can outrun the evidence — a caution this book has tried to take seriously about itself, rather than only about its subjects.

## Acknowledgments

This book owes its existence to the investigators, scientists, and public-health officials who did the work of measuring a phenomenon most institutions spent a century treating as unmeasurable — the National Transportation Safety Board investigators who traced a Bronx derailment back to an undiagnosed sleep disorder, the historian who spent decades reading old diaries and court records closely enough to notice a vanished pattern of two sleeps a night, the physiologist and his graduate student who first watched a sleeping eye move, the epidemiologists who built the case files behind a cancer agency's cautious classification, the geneticists who traced a family's unusual mornings to a single mutated gene, and the independent critic who was willing to spend a hundred and thirty hours checking one chapter of a bestselling book against its own footnotes. None of them are responsible for how their work has been synthesized here, or for any error that synthesis may have introduced.
