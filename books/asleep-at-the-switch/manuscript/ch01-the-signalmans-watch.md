# The Signalman's Watch

At 7:20 on the morning of December 1, 2013, a Metro-North commuter train left Poughkeepsie bound for Grand Central Terminal, carrying just over a hundred passengers on the kind of ordinary Sunday run that does not usually make the news. Just north of the point in the Bronx where the Hudson and Harlem Rivers meet, the tracks bend sharply around a curve near a stretch of shoreline called Spuyten Duyvil, an old Dutch name meaning, roughly, "spitting devil," after the currents that churn where the two rivers converge. The curve is rated for 30 miles an hour, a limit set by the geometry of the bend and the physics of a loaded train trying to hold a rail at speed — enough banking and enough radius to keep a train's weight from carrying it off the outer rail, but only within a narrow band of speed the track was engineered to tolerate. Above that band, the same curve that carries a train safely at 30 becomes a place where the train's own momentum works against it. The train entered it at 82.

At the controls was engineer William Rockefeller Jr., a Metro-North veteran with more than a decade on the job. The train derailed. Four passengers were killed. Sixty-three more were injured. The wreck made front pages for a few days, the way wrecks do, and then it did what most wrecks do, which is recede into a paragraph in a larger story about railroad safety, until the investigators finished their work and the paragraph turned out to matter more than the headline had suggested.

The National Transportation Safety Board is the federal agency the United States built specifically for this kind of aftermath: a body with no regulatory power of its own, charged only with reconstructing, as precisely as the physical evidence allows, exactly what happened and why, and issuing findings that other agencies are then free to act on or ignore. Its investigators work backward from wreckage the way a coroner works backward from a body — event recorders, track geometry, maintenance logs, medical records, the testimony of anyone still able to give it — building a timeline sturdy enough to survive a courtroom, a congressional hearing, and a great deal of second-guessing from a public that generally wants a simpler answer than the one the evidence supports. In this case, the evidence did not point to a simple answer. It pointed to a body that had, for reasons no one at the railroad had thought to ask about, quietly stopped being able to do at 7:20 in the morning what its job required.

The investigation took just under a year to complete. What it found was not a story about recklessness in the way that word usually gets used — no evidence of drinking, no phone in hand, no deliberate disregard for the track ahead. What it found was that Rockefeller had undiagnosed, severe obstructive sleep apnea, a condition in which the soft tissue at the back of the throat collapses during sleep and blocks the airway, over and over, through the night. The body's response to each blockage is a brief, reflexive arousal just deep enough to reopen the airway and resume breathing, and just shallow enough that the sleeper almost never remembers it happening. In a severe case this can repeat dozens of times an hour, all night, every night, for years, so that a person can spend a full eight hours in bed and wake having gotten almost none of the restorative sleep those eight hours are supposed to provide — present at the scene of sleep, in a sense, without ever being allowed to complete it. The condition is common, frequently silent to the person experiencing it, and often invisible to the people around them, because its symptoms during the day look like ordinary tiredness rather than a diagnosable illness. The NTSB found that Rockefeller's case had gone undiagnosed for years, and that its effects on him that particular morning had been compounded by a recent, drastic change to his work-shift schedule — the kind of abrupt reassignment railroads make routinely, without much thought for the body clock of the person being reassigned, or for how a body already running a chronic sleep deficit absorbs one more disruption on top of it. NTSB chairman Christopher Hart summarized the board's finding in a sentence that reads, on the page, almost too plain for what it describes: the cause was "the engineer's non-compliance with the 30 mph speed restriction because he had fallen asleep," a lapse produced by a medical condition nobody had caught.

Nobody had caught it because nobody had been looking. The NTSB's investigation turned up a second finding that mattered as much as the first: Metro-North had no policy in place to screen engineers for sleep disorders. There was no test Rockefeller could have failed, no evaluation he could have been flagged by, no protocol that would have caught a man who was, by any ordinary standard, doing his job as trained, arriving on time, submitting to the schedule the railroad handed him. The system had no mechanism for asking the one question that turned out to be the operative one. The board added a third finding, almost clinical in its understatement: had the line been equipped with positive train control, an automatic system that can sense an overspeed condition and apply the brakes without waiting for a human hand to do it, the derailment would not have happened at all. The technology existed. It had not yet been installed on that curve. A machine that did not need to stay awake would have caught what a tired, sick, unscreened human being could not.

Read together, the three findings describe a system with a single point of failure sitting exactly where the least scrutiny had ever been applied. The railroad screened for plenty of things — drugs, alcohol, certification, seniority, hours worked against federal limits — and screened for none of the thing that turned out to matter most that morning, because sleep disorders had never been treated as a safety-critical category in the first place. The absence was not unique to Metro-North. It was, and largely still is, the industry norm across a great deal of transportation and heavy industry: an elaborate apparatus for detecting impairment from substances, and comparatively little apparatus for detecting impairment from an exhausted, malfunctioning sleep system, even though the resulting failure in attention and reaction time can look, on an event recorder, indistinguishable from the kind produced by a drink or a pill.

There is an old railroad phrase for exactly this kind of failure, and it predates diesel engines, air brakes, and electric signals by the better part of a century. In the early decades of American railroading, before automatic interlocking systems existed, trains were routed from one track to another by hand. A switchman stood at a trackside lever — the switch — and threw it at the right moment to send an approaching train onto the correct rail, out of the path of whatever else might be coming the other way. Two tracks converging at a junction, a siding where one train could wait for another to pass, a yard full of cars that needed sorting onto the right line: all of it depended, in the system's earliest form, on one person's hand being on the right lever at the right second. The job required no great technical skill, but it required constant, unglamorous attention at odd hours, often alone, often at night, often for long stretches with nothing happening at all followed by a narrow window in which everything happened at once. A switchman who dozed at his post, even briefly, could send two trains onto the same track, or leave a switch thrown the wrong way for the one train that needed it right. When that happened and the worst came of it, the newspapers and the company's own accident reports had a name for the cause, and it stuck: he had been asleep at the switch.

What made the phrase durable was not just its usefulness for railroad accident reports but the shape of the failure it described — a single, exhausted, underappreciated worker holding sole responsibility for a decision with catastrophic consequences on one side and total invisibility on the other, since a switch thrown correctly draws no attention at all. Nobody praises the switchman for the ten thousand ordinary nights nothing goes wrong. The job is judged entirely by its failures, which meant the switchman absorbed all of the blame for a system that had, in effect, decided it was cheaper to depend on one person's vigilance than to build in a mechanical backup. That imbalance — invisible when it works, catastrophic and personal when it does not — is what later industries inherited along with the phrase, whether or not they inherited the actual lever.

The phrase did what useful idioms do. It outlived the job that produced it. Manual switches gave way to remote-controlled ones, and remote-controlled ones gave way to automated interlocking, and the literal switchman all but disappeared from the American railroad by the middle of the twentieth century. But "asleep at the switch" kept working long after there was no switch left to sleep at, because it had stopped describing a specific job and started describing a specific kind of failure — a person whose attention was the last line of defense against a collision, and whose attention gave out at the wrong moment. It became a synonym for negligence at any post where someone was supposed to be watching and was not: a lifeguard, a night watchman, a regulator, a parent. By the time it reached the twentieth-century idiom dictionaries, it had traveled a long way from the specific lever and the specific hand, into a kind of general shorthand for institutional failure with a human being at its center.

What the Spuyten Duyvil derailment did, whether anyone intended it or not, was drag the idiom back to something close to its literal meaning. William Rockefeller Jr. was not a switchman standing at a trackside lever in 1890. He was an engineer at the controls of a modern commuter train in 2013, backed by union protections, federal oversight, medical benefits, a railroad with a century and a half of accumulated safety regulation behind it. And he was, in the specific and almost forgotten sense the old phrase describes, asleep — or near enough to it, in the fog of a body running on fragmented, apnea-shattered sleep and a schedule that had just been rearranged out from under him — at the one moment when being awake mattered more than at any other point in the run. The old danger had not been engineered away. It had been carried forward, mostly unexamined, into a system that had automated almost everything else about the job except the assumption that the person at the controls would simply be rested enough to do it.

This is the assumption this book is about, and the Spuyten Duyvil derailment is where it starts because the wreck makes the assumption unusually hard to look away from. Somewhere between the invention of the switch and the invention of positive train control, industrial societies built an enormous amount of infrastructure — legal, medical, mechanical, cultural — on top of a single, largely unexamined premise: that a healthy adult, given a night, would get the sleep the job required. Shift schedules were built on it. Screening requirements, where they existed at all, were built on it. The entire architecture of who gets tested for what, and when, and how often, assumed a baseline of consolidated, adequate, more or less standardized nightly sleep that the job could then be scheduled against. When that baseline turned out to be wrong for a given person, on a given night, the system had almost nothing in reserve. It had a rule about speed on a curve, a rule that assumed a rested human being would obey it, and no rule at all for the case where the human being had quietly, invisibly, stopped being able to.

The argument of this book is that this was never a solved problem so much as a redistributed one. The nineteenth-century switchman's job was eliminated by automation, and it is tempting to read that as a story of progress closing a gap — a dangerous, fatigue-prone human task engineered out of existence. Some of that is true. But the underlying vulnerability the switchman's job exposed, the fact that human vigilance is not a constant, that it degrades under fatigue in ways that are often invisible to the person experiencing the degradation and to everyone supervising them, did not disappear along with the trackside lever. It migrated. It moved into control rooms, cockpits, tanker helms, hospital wards, long-haul cabs, and, as this book will show, an enormous number of ordinary workplaces that never thought of themselves as safety-critical at all, because the design of industrial time — the single consolidated night, the fixed shift, the schedule built around a clock rather than a body — carried the same underlying assumption forward into every one of them.

It is worth pausing on how strange this is, once it is stated plainly. Modern industrial societies regulate an enormous range of human variability with considerable precision. They set standards for how much weight a bridge must bear, how much alcohol a driver's blood may carry, how many hours a truck driver may spend behind the wheel before a mandatory rest period, how loud a factory floor may be before hearing protection becomes compulsory. Almost all of that regulatory machinery assumes, without stating it, that the person subject to the rule arrives at the start of the shift adequately rested — that sleep itself, unlike blood alcohol or decibel level, is a private matter, handled the night before, off the clock, none of the employer's business until it fails badly enough to produce a body count. Rockefeller's case sits exactly at the seam in that assumption. His employer regulated his hours, his certifications, his substance use, and his schedule down to the minute. It never asked whether his sleep, within all of that, was actually working.

That design did not arrive by accident, and it did not arrive as a single event. It arrived in pieces, over roughly a century and a half, through decisions made by people who were mostly trying to solve other problems: how to light a factory floor after dark without the fire risk of gas lamps, how to keep expensive machinery running around the clock instead of standing idle at night, how to get more hours of production out of a workforce that used to stop when the sun went down. Those decisions were made by railroads setting shift schedules, by electric utilities selling kilowatt-hours, by factory owners who discovered that artificial light let them run a night shift as productively as a day one, and by a culture of industrial leadership that came, by the turn of the twentieth century, to treat a full night's sleep less as a biological requirement than as a mark of insufficient ambition. Thomas Edison, whose lighting system did more than any other single invention to make the modern night workable, said so more or less directly and more than once, and later chapters spend real time with exactly what he said and how it shaped the century that followed him.

None of this was engineered with malice, and very little of it was engineered with any real awareness of what it was displacing. The people who built the electrified, standardized, single-block night of industrial time were, for the most part, solving immediate problems of light, output, and cost, not running an experiment on human circadian biology. But an experiment is more or less what it turned out to be, run at a scale no laboratory could have arranged deliberately, on a population that never consented to it because it was never framed as a choice in the first place. This book follows that experiment from its beginning: how people slept before the electric light changed the terms, in a pattern historians only rediscovered because someone went looking through diaries, court records, and medical texts old enough to remember it; how Edison's own contempt for sleep became bound up with the technology he sold the world; how the same electrification that lit the factory floor also normalized, across an entire industrial workforce, a schedule that ran against the grain of the body's own clock. It follows the scientists who spent the middle of the twentieth century discovering, almost by accident, that sleep itself was a far stranger and more active process than anyone had assumed, and the disasters — a reactor at Three Mile Island, a night shift at Chernobyl, a supertanker's grounding off Alaska, a shuttle launch decided in the small hours of the morning — in which the gap between what the body needed and what the schedule provided turned out to have a body count. It follows the slower-moving verdicts: a World Health Organization panel classifying night work itself as a probable carcinogen, states rewriting the hour a teenager's school day begins, a scattering of statutes trying to define exactly how many hours awake makes a driver as dangerous as one who has been drinking, a clock that gets moved forward and back twice a year over the mild but real cardiac cost of the spring transition. And it follows the commerce that grew up on the far side of all of it — a century of pills built to manufacture sleep chemically when the schedule would not yield it any other way, a popular science book whose most famous claims did not all survive scrutiny, and a newer market of rings, mattresses, and corporate nap pods now selling back, hour by hour and often at a considerable price, some fraction of what industrial time took.

None of that is a bullet list of chapters so much as a single argument followed through a long series of rooms, each one built by different people solving different problems, none of whom were thinking of themselves as architects of a shared experiment in how little sleep a modern economy could get away with asking of its workers. The premise that put William Rockefeller Jr. in the seat of a train doing 82 miles an hour into a 30-mile-an-hour curve was not a defect unique to Metro-North's scheduling office. It was the same premise that has, in one form or another, sat underneath most of industrial society's arrangement of work and rest for the better part of a hundred and fifty years: that a rested worker is the default condition, that fatigue is a personal failing rather than a structural one, and that the systems built on top of that assumption do not need a backstop, because the assumption itself was never expected to fail. Positive train control exists today, in part, because the NTSB's investigation into a Sunday-morning derailment in the Bronx made it impossible to keep pretending otherwise on that one curve. The rest of this book is about how many other curves, in how many other industries, are still waiting for the same kind of proof — and about the parallel, more recent story of an entire industry that has grown up selling devices, pills, and services premised on the very deficit that the scheduling systems refuse to formally acknowledge exists.

There is a temptation, in a story like this one, to look for a villain — a railroad executive who should have known better, a manager who skipped a warning, a single decision that would have prevented everything if only someone braver or more careful had made it differently. The Metro-North case does not really support that reading, and neither does most of what follows in this book. Rockefeller had passed every check the system had ever asked him to pass. Metro-North was not unusual among American railroads in its lack of a sleep-screening policy; it was, if anything, typical. The failure was not a rogue actor cutting corners inside a sound system. It was a sound system with a hole in it precisely where nobody had thought to look, because the whole apparatus of industrial scheduling had been built for a century and a half on the working fiction that the hole did not need to exist — that sleep, being personal, private, and nightly, would simply take care of itself. The derailment did not expose a bad employer so much as a bad assumption, one so old and so deeply built into the infrastructure of work that almost nobody working inside it had occasion to question it until four people were dead and sixty-three more were hurt on a curve in the Bronx that the track itself had been rated to handle safely the entire time.

The old switchman's job disappeared because someone eventually built a machine that did not need to sleep. What this book asks, chapter by chapter, is how much of the rest of industrial life was ever built with the same honesty about what human beings actually require, and how much of it was simply built on the hope that no one would be watching the curve closely enough to notice when the hope ran out.
