# The Eye That Would Not Stay Still

On a December night in 1951, in a physiology laboratory at the University of Chicago, a graduate student named Eugene Aserinsky glued electrodes near the eyes of an eight-year-old boy and sent him to bed. The boy was his own son, Armond, and the arrangement had less to do with scientific design than with the ordinary economics of graduate school: Aserinsky needed a subject who would hold still for a night, would not object to wires taped to his face, and was available for free. He wired the boy to a polygraph, an Offner Dynograph, the kind of machine built to trace the electrical noise of a heart or a brain onto a moving strip of paper, and he let the pens run. For most of the night they behaved the way sleep researchers expected pens to behave over a sleeping subject — a slow, undifferentiated scribble, the physiological equivalent of static. Then, periodically, the pens tracking eye movement began to swing in wide, rapid arcs, as if the boy were watching something move quickly in front of his closed eyes. Aserinsky's first assumption was that the machine had failed. Old equipment did that. He checked the wiring, checked the pens, and found nothing wrong with either. Whatever was making the ink swing that way was happening inside his sleeping son.

To understand why that swinging ink was worth noticing at all, it helps to remember what sleep was assumed to be at the time, and not only in the factories and railroad yards this book has already spent several chapters inside. In the physiology departments of the early 1950s, sleep was generally treated as a trough — a period defined by the absence of waking activity rather than by any activity of its own. The going model, associated closely with Aserinsky's supervisor, Nathaniel Kleitman, described sleep in terms of depth: a person descended into it, reached some low point of unresponsiveness, and rose back out of it toward morning, the way a diver descends and ascends through featureless water. Kleitman had spent decades studying sleep and circadian rhythms with real rigor, including memorably isolating himself and a colleague in Mammoth Cave in Kentucky in 1938 to see what would happen to the human sleep-wake cycle without daylight to anchor it. But even in his own lab, sleep was still fundamentally a quantity — how much, how deep, how continuous — rather than a structure with moving parts. That assumption was not so different from the one running through the rest of industrial culture during those same decades: sleep as a single undifferentiated block, to be minimized, standardized, and scheduled around a whistle or a clock, exactly the picture this book traced through Edison's electrified factories and the shift systems that followed him. Nobody in a physiology lab or a personnel office had much reason to imagine that the inside of that block had a shape.

The tool that let Aserinsky look inside it was not new in principle. Electrooculography — recording the small electrical potential that exists between the cornea and the retina, which shifts detectably as the eye moves — had been used for years to study waking eye movement, in reading research and aviation studies among other places. What was comparatively rare was pointing that technique at a person for an entire night and simply watching what happened, rather than taking brief readings at set intervals. Continuous overnight recording was expensive in time, in paper, in a graduate student's patience, and in a era before anyone had reason to expect an all-night recording would show anything more interesting than an all-night silence. Aserinsky's own account, later in his career, made clear that the discovery grew as much out of persistence as out of insight: watching an eight-year-old's eyes twitch under closed lids for one strange night was the kind of anomaly a less stubborn observer might have filed away as noise from a machine that was, after all, secondhand and unreliable. He did not file it away. He went back and did it again, and then began doing it with adults.

Over the following year, working in Kleitman's lab, Aserinsky expanded the observation into something closer to a formal study. He and Kleitman monitored adult subjects overnight with the same electrooculographic technique, watching for the same periodic bursts of rapid movement, and cross-checking them against a simultaneous electroencephalogram, a recording of the brain's electrical activity from scalp electrodes. What they found was that the eye movements were not random twitches scattered across the night. They arrived in a small number of discrete episodes, three or four to a night, each one accompanied by a shift in the brain's electrical signature toward a pattern that looked, on the page, disconcertingly similar to waking activity — low-voltage, fast, nothing like the slow, high-amplitude waves associated with the deep, quiet stretches of sleep researchers already recognized. Respiration sped up during these episodes. Subjects sometimes moved their bodies. And when Aserinsky and Kleitman woke people specifically during these bursts, rather than during the quieter stretches between them, the sleepers frequently reported that they had been in the middle of a dream — often a vivid one, recalled with a level of narrative detail that woke-up-at-random controls, roused during the quiet periods, could rarely produce. Sleep, it turned out, was not one thing happening at one intensity. It was a cycle, repeating several times over the course of a night, alternating between a quiet phase and an active one, and the active phase looked, by several physiological measures, less like unconsciousness than like a different kind of consciousness altogether.

They published the finding in Science on September 4, 1953, in a paper that ran to roughly a page and a half — a length that says something about how the norms of scientific publishing have changed, and something about how modest the claim looked on the page relative to what it would come to mean. Twenty adult subjects, three to four nightly periods of rapid eye movement, the associated changes in breathing and body movement, the correlation with dream recall on waking. There is no rhetorical flourish in the paper, no claim to have discovered anything as large as a new state of consciousness. It reads like what it was: two researchers reporting a clean, repeatable, slightly startling observation and letting the observation speak for itself. It would take the following decade, and further work by Kleitman's other students, most notably William Dement, who helped establish and name what would become known as REM sleep and to chart its recurring cycle alongside the deeper, quieter stages of what is now called non-REM sleep, before the finding's full implications settled into place. But the foundational fact was already sitting in that short 1953 paper: sleep has an architecture. It moves through stages. Some of those stages are, by several real physiological measures, closer to wakefulness than to the flat, silent trough the field had been describing for decades.

It is hard to overstate how much rearranging that fact required, and not only within physiology departments. A discipline organized around the idea that sleep was a single, gradually deepening and then lightening descent had to accommodate the discovery that one of its most active-looking phases arrived and receded several times a night, on a schedule the brain appeared to generate on its own regardless of how deeply "asleep" the rest of the body seemed to be. Clinicians who had previously had almost no framework for thinking about disordered sleep beyond insomnia — too little of it — now had a physiological basis for describing sleep that was present in quantity but wrong in structure: sleep interrupted at the wrong stage, sleep that never reached certain stages at all, sleep whose staging looked normal on paper but whose timing, relative to the rest of a person's schedule, was chronically misaligned. Within a few decades, that basic insight had helped seed an entire clinical field of sleep medicine, with its own diagnostic categories, its own laboratories built around overnight polysomnography descended directly from Aserinsky's borrowed Dynograph, and its own vocabulary for describing disorders that would previously have gone unnamed or misdiagnosed as something else entirely. The condition that a Metro-North engineer was found, decades later, to have been living with undiagnosed — a fragmenting of sleep architecture severe enough to leave him profoundly impaired despite having, by the clock, spent enough hours in bed — belongs to a diagnostic category that the field could not have described with any precision before 1953. There is a reasonably direct line from an eight-year-old boy's twitching eyelids in a Chicago lab to the kind of screening question a railroad's medical department can now, in principle, ask an engineer before putting him behind the controls of a train.

The credit for that line of descent has never settled as cleanly as the textbook version of the story suggests, and it is worth sitting with the messiness rather than smoothing it into a tidy single-hero account. Aserinsky was the graduate student who noticed the anomaly, chased it down, and did the hands-on work of building the study; Kleitman was the senior physiologist whose lab, reputation, and co-authorship gave the 1953 paper the institutional standing to be taken seriously by a field that did not yet have any particular reason to trust a graduate student's borrowed equipment. Decades later, in his own retrospective account of how the discovery unfolded, Aserinsky described Kleitman's involvement in strikingly diminished terms, characterizing his supervisor's role in the specific study that produced the eye-movement finding as tangential, and going so far as to say he had once had misgivings about listing Kleitman as a co-author at all, despite Kleitman having supervised the thesis the work grew out of and having, by any conventional accounting, been essential to the paper's credibility and publication. Kleitman was still alive when that account appeared, well past a hundred years old by then, and there is no clear public record of how he might have responded to being described that way. Historians of the field have continued to debate how the credit should properly be divided — between a graduate student's original observation and a senior scientist's institutional backing, between the person who saw the pens swing and the person whose laboratory, funding, and standing made it possible for anyone to believe him. There is no single correct answer sitting in the archive waiting to be found. What is not in dispute is that the finding itself, whoever deserves the larger share of it, opened a door that a great deal of twentieth-century sleep science subsequently walked through.

There is a particular irony worth naming plainly, because it sits at the center of this book's argument rather than at its margins. The discovery that sleep possesses an intricate, cyclical, biologically active internal structure arrived in the same stretch of the twentieth century in which industrial and corporate culture was doing everything in its power to flatten sleep into a single manageable block — a fixed nightly allotment, ideally as short as ambition allowed, defended by almost no labor law, scheduled around shifts and alarm clocks rather than around anything the body itself might be doing on its own timetable. Electric light had already made it possible, as earlier chapters showed, to treat the night as an extension of the working day. What the Chicago lab showed, at almost exactly the moment that treatment was becoming standard, was that the hours being compressed and rescheduled were not empty. They were doing something. The brain during a REM episode was not idling; by several real measures, it was closer to working than resting. The century's most celebrated executives were, without knowing it, boasting about how efficiently they could avoid a process that turned out to be far more structured, and far less optional, than anyone standing on a factory floor or in a boardroom had any reason to suspect.

None of this reached the factory floor quickly, or the boardroom at all. The 1953 paper circulated first among physiologists and psychiatrists, and it would take years for its implications to filter into anything resembling public consciousness, let alone into labor policy or workplace scheduling. Aserinsky himself did not stay in sleep research for the rest of his career; he moved into other areas of physiology, and it fell largely to Kleitman's later students, and to independent researchers elsewhere — Michel Jouvet in France conducted parallel work on the same phenomenon in animals around the same period, adding a comparative dimension the original human study could not provide on its own — to build the field that eventually formalized REM and non-REM sleep into the multi-stage architecture recognized today. But the essential fact had already been established, in a page and a half of Science, by a graduate student with a secondhand polygraph and a son who agreed, or was simply too young to object, to spend a night wired up in his father's laboratory. Sleep was not a single thing. It moved. And for most of the century that followed, industrial civilization would go on treating it as if it did not.
