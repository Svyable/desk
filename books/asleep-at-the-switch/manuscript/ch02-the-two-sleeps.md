# The Two Sleeps

A. Roger Ekirch did not set out to study sleep. He was a historian at Virginia Tech researching something narrower and, on the surface, less promising: what ordinary people in preindustrial Europe and colonial America actually did at night, before the electric light made night a time when a person could reasonably expect to keep doing the same things they did during the day. Night, in the period Ekirch was studying, was dangerous, dark, and largely unlit — a time governed by curfews, watchmen, superstition, and the simple physical fact that beyond firelight and moonlight there was very little a person could see. He was reading diaries, court depositions, medical texts, and literary references from the period looking for evidence of how people organized their evenings, what they feared, what they did by candlelight, how households functioned once the sun went down. What he found, showing up again and again in sources that had no reason to coordinate with one another, was a detail that did not fit the modern assumption he had presumably carried into the research without examining it: people were routinely describing two sleeps a night, not one.

The pattern showed up in language before Ekirch understood it as a pattern. A diary entry would mention waking after a "first sleep" to do something — pray, tend a fire, talk with a spouse, visit a neighbor — and then returning to a "second sleep" until morning, phrased so casually, so much like a reference to breakfast or supper, that it was clear the writer assumed any reader would already know exactly what a first sleep and a second sleep were. Court records occasionally referenced the interval between the two sleeps as an ordinary landmark of time, the way a modern record might reference "shortly before lunch." Medical texts and household manuals occasionally offered advice about it, treating the waking period between the two sleeps as a normal, even useful, part of the night — a good time for reflection, for prayer, for the kind of conversation that daytime obligations crowded out, and, some sources noted with the frankness period medical literature was often willing to bring to the subject, a good time for sex, on the theory that a couple rested from their first sleep and unhurried by the day's demands ahead of them had a particular advantage over one exhausted at bedtime. None of these sources set out to describe a phenomenon named "segmented sleep." They simply assumed it, the way people assume the furniture of their own daily lives, which is exactly why the pattern had gone unnoticed by modern historians for so long: it was hiding in plain sight, in the connective tissue of sentences about other things.

Ekirch's method was, in essence, patient accumulation. A single reference to a "first sleep" in an isolated seventeenth-century diary could be a translation quirk, an idiosyncratic writer's private phrase, or an editorial error. A reference that recurred across French, English, Italian, and Latin sources, across genres as different as devotional literature, legal depositions, and popular fiction, across a span of centuries, was a different kind of evidence entirely — not a curiosity but a pattern with the kind of independent, cross-cultural corroboration historians take seriously. By the time he published *At Day's Close: Night in Times Past* in 2005, Ekirch had assembled more than five hundred such references, drawn from a wide span of preindustrial European and American sources, enough to argue that segmented sleep had not been a regional eccentricity or a literary convention but something close to the ordinary, unremarked default for how a substantial part of the pre-electric world slept. His later research expanded the count considerably further, eventually surpassing two thousand documented references spanning roughly a dozen languages, some reaching back to antiquity — a body of evidence broad enough that segmented sleep, once he had assembled it, looked less like an oddity to be explained and more like the norm against which the modern single-block night is the actual historical outlier.

It is worth sitting for a moment with how the pattern is described in the sources Ekirch collected, because the texture of it is easy to flatten into something too tidy. The first sleep began not long after dark, in households where "after dark" often meant an hour or two after sundown rather than a fixed clock time, since preindustrial life ran considerably closer to the solar day than modern life does. After some hours — commonly framed as a few hours of sleep, though the sources are naturally imprecise about exact durations — a person woke, and this waking was not treated as insomnia, disruption, or a problem calling for a remedy. It was treated as simply the middle of the night's business. The waking interval that followed, which sources suggest often ran an hour or more, was used for an unusually wide range of purposes: private prayer, particularly in devout households where the quiet, undistracted middle of the night was considered a fitting time to address God without the day's obligations competing for attention; small household tasks that did not require daylight, such as tending a fire that had burned low, checking on animals, or feeding an infant; visiting, in denser or rural communities where it was apparently not unusual for a neighbor to be awake at the same hour and available for company; reading, for the literate minority who owned books and could manage by candlelight or firelight; and simple reflection, the kind of unstructured, undemanded mental time that daytime rarely offered a working person in an agrarian or artisanal economy. Then, after this interval, came the second sleep, carrying the sleeper through to whatever hour dawn or the day's obligations required waking.

None of this evidence supports a claim more dramatic than the one it actually establishes, and it is worth being precise about the difference. What Ekirch's research demonstrates, at the level the documentary record can support, is that segmented sleep was a widely attested, ordinary pattern in preindustrial Europe and colonial America, referenced casually enough and across a broad enough range of sources to suggest it was common knowledge rather than a fringe habit. What it does not demonstrate, and what Ekirch himself has been careful not to claim, is that this pattern was healthier, more natural in some normative sense, or better suited to human biology than the consolidated single-block sleep that eventually replaced it. The written record documents a widespread historical practice and its disappearance. It does not, by itself, adjudicate whether the earlier practice served the sleeping body better than what came after — a question this book will return to more directly later, once the science of sleep itself, rather than only its history, enters the argument. This book treats the strong version of the claim — that segmented sleep was simply the healthy ancestral state from which modern life has fallen — with the same caution the historical evidence itself asks for: a documented pattern and a documented decline are not, on their own, a verdict about which arrangement served the sleeping body better.

What makes the disappearance of the pattern as interesting as its existence is how narrow the window was in which it vanished. By Ekirch's account, the transformation was neither sudden nor evenly distributed — wealthier, urban households, with greater access to candles, oil lamps, and eventually gas lighting, appear in the record adopting later bedtimes and a more consolidated night earlier than poorer and more rural households did, which fits a pattern this book will keep encountering in later chapters: that changes in the technology and economics of light tend to reach the sleep of the privileged before they reach everyone else's. Beginning in the early nineteenth century, sources increasingly describe the first sleep expanding, absorbing more of the night, while the waking interval and the second sleep correspondingly contracted. By the second half of the century, references to a distinct "second sleep" grow rarer in the documentary record, and where the interval survives at all, it survives as something closer to a few extra minutes of dozing rather than an hour or more of purposeful wakefulness. By the century's end, in the sources Ekirch surveyed, the concept had essentially disappeared from common reference altogether — not explicitly debated or rejected, so far as the record shows, but simply no longer mentioned, the way a household habit stops being described once a household has stopped practicing it and the next generation never learns the vocabulary for it in the first place.

The timing of that disappearance lines up, closely enough to be more than coincidence, with the spread of artificial lighting — first through improved oil lamps and coal gas street lighting in the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, and later, as later chapters of this book will trace in detail, through the domestic electric lighting that began reaching ordinary households by the end of the nineteenth century. It is a tidy correlation, and tidy correlations invite skepticism, particularly in a period that was also seeing rapid urbanization, the spread of mechanical clocks and standardized time, changes in work discipline associated with industrialization, and shifts in medical and religious attitudes toward the body and its habits, any of which could plausibly have contributed to a shift in how people organized their nights independent of lighting technology specifically. Ekirch's own account treats artificial light as the central, though not sole, force behind the change: cheaper, more reliable, and eventually ever-present light extended the useful evening, pushed bedtimes later, and compressed the total sleep window available before a working day's start time, with the segmented middle giving way first because it was the part of the night's architecture that had the least competing claim on a person's time once evenings themselves became productive, sociable, or simply more comfortable to remain awake through.

There is a way of reading this history that turns it into a simple story of loss — humanity possessed a gentler, more natural rhythm of rest, and industrial civilization, in its rush toward productivity, extinguished it. That reading is emotionally satisfying and only partly supported by what the documentary evidence actually shows. The record does not establish that people in the sixteenth or seventeenth century were, on the whole, better rested, healthier, or happier than people today; infant mortality, disease, chronic pain, and physical labor extracted costs on the sleeping body that no amount of segmented rest offset, and quiet wakefulness in the middle of a cold, unheated house was not universally experienced as restorative leisure. What the record does establish, more modestly and more durably, is that the specific architecture of sleep now treated as biologically obvious — one continuous block, roughly eight hours, beginning and ending at fixed clock times — is a comparatively recent arrangement, adopted within a few generations, coinciding with a specific technological change, and displacing an older pattern that had apparently persisted, with local variation, for a very long stretch of documented history before that. The consolidated night was not discovered to be correct. It became the default because the conditions that had made a different pattern normal — darkness cheap enough to fill with nothing, evenings with no competing use for a person's waking hours — stopped applying.

This distinction matters for the argument this book is making, and it is worth stating plainly rather than letting it hover as an implication. The point of recovering Ekirch's research is not to prescribe a return to segmented sleep, and this book takes no position on whether an individual reader would sleep better broken into two intervals than consolidated into one; that is an empirical question about a particular body, not a historical verdict this evidence is equipped to settle. The point is narrower and, in its way, more useful: it is to establish, on the strength of a historical record extensive enough to take seriously, that the single-block night now treated across the medical, corporate, and popular imagination as the biological baseline against which all other sleep patterns are judged deficient is not, in fact, a biological baseline at all. It is one historically contingent arrangement among others, adopted for reasons that had far more to do with the economics and technology of light than with anything discovered about what a human body actually requires. The next chapter follows that technology directly to its source, and to the man who did as much as anyone to make the modern night mean something entirely different from what it had meant to every generation of readers whose diaries, depositions, and devotional literature Ekirch spent a career reading — and who, as it happens, held some remarkably strong opinions of his own about what a properly modern person ought to be doing with the hours the old second sleep used to occupy.
