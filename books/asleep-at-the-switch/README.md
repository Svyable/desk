How Industrial Time Took a Third of Our Lives, and Who Is Selling It Back.

The working thesis is that the eight-hour, single-block night is not a biological baseline but an engineering decision. For most of human history sleep was commonly taken in two shorter installments separated by an hour or more of quiet wakefulness, a pattern the historian A. Roger Ekirch traced through thousands of pre-industrial documents before it disappeared within a few generations of the electric light. What replaced it was a single, standardized industrial schedule — defended by almost no law, timed to a factory whistle or an alarm clock, and treated by many of the last century's most admired executives as a weakness to be trained out of the body entirely. The book argues that society has spent the years since paying the bill for that compression in reactor control rooms, cockpits, tanker helms, classrooms, courtrooms, and a fast-growing market of rings, mattresses, pills, and nap pods now built to sell some of the lost hours back.

The book traces that argument from a 2013 commuter-train derailment that gave a nineteenth-century railroad idiom new, literal weight, back through the deep history of segmented sleep, Thomas Edison's electrified assault on the night and the shift work it created, the 1953 discovery of REM sleep, the twentieth century's cult of the sleepless executive, the control-room and cockpit disasters where inattention had a body count, the World Health Organization's cancer classification for night work itself, the laws written for drowsy teenagers and drowsy drivers, the clock change fought over by sleep scientists twice a year, a century of pills built to manufacture sleep chemically, the science-communication scandal that showed even sleep's most successful modern popularizer could overstate the evidence, the rings and wristbands now selling sleep back as data, the athletes and soldiers whose institutions finally started taking it seriously, the rare people whose genes let them opt out of most of this argument, the couples now sleeping apart to save what sleeping together was costing them, the unequal distribution of who gets to rest at all, and the same executive culture that once treated exhaustion as a status symbol now selling naps as a competitive advantage. Real counter-evidence and scientific overreach are preserved throughout rather than argued away, and no invented statistic or quotation appears anywhere in the text.
