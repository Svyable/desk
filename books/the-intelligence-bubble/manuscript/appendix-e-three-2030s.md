# Appendix E — Three 2030s

A capital-cycle thesis is useful only if it can be wrong.

The purpose of these scenarios is not to pick a favorite future and decorate it with confidence. It is to make the book's assumptions observable.

By 2030, artificial intelligence can be far more capable and far more widely used under all three scenarios below. What changes is the relationship among demand, supply, efficiency, price, utilization, bargaining power, and the claims that financed the buildout.

The scenarios are therefore financial states, not technological verdicts.

## 1. Scarcity Persists

In this world, demand for useful computation outruns both the construction cycle and the efficiency curve.

Agents become persistent consumers of inference rather than occasional chat sessions. Video, robotics, simulation, scientific workloads, and autonomous software create large new categories of demand. Frontier training remains strategically important and continues to grow. Better hardware and better software reduce the cost of individual tasks, but each reduction unlocks enough new usage that aggregate demand expands faster still.

Power remains the binding constraint in several important regions. Interconnection queues stay valuable because customers actually take the load they reserved. New generation and transmission arrive, but not quickly enough to eliminate local scarcity.

Model competition exists, yet the best systems maintain enough differentiated capability to charge meaningful premiums for the hardest work. Open and smaller models handle routine tasks without collapsing the economics of the frontier.

The investors who paid for scarce assets are rewarded because scarcity lasts longer than the financing needs it to last.

The strongest confirming evidence would include:

- high realized utilization across several hardware generations rather than only during a launch shortage;
- customer renewals that preserve attractive economics after initial contracts expire;
- strong secondary demand for older accelerators rather than abrupt residual-value collapse;
- power reservations becoming actual sustained load rather than shrinking as efficiency improves;
- frontier model pricing remaining materially above adequate substitutes because capability differences continue to matter economically;
- expansion capex beginning to produce free cash flow before replacement capex consumes the expected harvest period;
- infrastructure debt refinancing without repeated equity rescues or major covenant resets.

This is the world in which today's buildout can look conservative in hindsight.

The book's bear mechanism would not disappear completely. Individual projects can still be badly located, overleveraged, or poorly contracted. But the system-level scarcity would give owners more room to survive mistakes.

## 2. Abundance Arrives First

In this world, AI demand grows enormously and the unit economics deflate even faster.

Models become more efficient. Smaller systems become adequate for a widening share of commercial work. Routing software sends each task toward the cheapest system that clears a quality threshold. Quantization, caching, batching, better inference engines, improved chips, and software optimization compound.

The amount of machine cognition consumed by the economy explodes.

The price attached to a standardized unit of useful cognition collapses.

New data-center capacity authorized during scarcity arrives into a market where customers have more choices and need less premium compute per task than planners expected. Some customers still consume far more total inference, but the revenue per unit falls faster than workload volume rises for parts of the stack.

Utilization becomes a yield problem. Machines stay busy because providers discount spare capacity. Older hardware remains technically useful but moves down-market. Long contracts protect some owners for several years, then renewals reveal the new clearing price.

The financial pain is uneven. Hyperscalers with strong balance sheets can absorb disappointing projects and keep investing. Leveraged infrastructure owners face a harder transition. Model providers discover that scientific leadership does not automatically produce durable gross margins. Applications and enterprises enjoy a falling input cost.

The strongest confirming evidence would include:

- rapid declines in price per fixed capability or completed business task;
- narrower quality gaps among credible models for mainstream enterprise workloads;
- rising total inference volume alongside declining revenue yield per unit of compute;
- lower renewal prices for capacity even when physical utilization remains high;
- large discounts in secondary accelerator markets when new generations arrive;
- infrastructure restructurings that leave campuses operating under lower-cost ownership;
- enterprise buyers increasingly treating model choice as routing rather than commitment;
- application-company margins or product capabilities improving because intelligence has become a cheaper input.

This is the world closest to the fiber analogy.

The infrastructure succeeds socially because abundance is the product of the buildout.

Some of the investors who created that abundance discover that abundance was bad for the scarcity rent they underwrote.

## 3. The Split Market

The third world is less elegant and may therefore be the most useful to keep in mind.

Scarcity and abundance coexist.

Frontier training remains scarce, expensive, and strategically concentrated. Certain power-constrained locations retain enormous value. The newest accelerators command premiums when a workload genuinely requires them.

At the same time, ordinary inference becomes brutally competitive. Smaller and open systems are good enough for a large share of business tasks. Enterprise routing is common. Older accelerators remain useful at much lower prices. Model providers make money through bundles, tools, distribution, data, reliability, and workflow integration rather than through a universal intelligence premium.

Geography matters more than aggregate statistics. One region can be short power while another has underused data-center shells. One country's sovereign program can build capacity for strategic reasons while a private provider elsewhere restructures. A hyperscaler can earn attractive returns from a tightly integrated stack while an independent capacity owner struggles with the same hardware generation.

The phrase “the AI market” becomes misleading because different layers clear at different prices.

The strongest confirming evidence would include:

- persistent premium pricing for a small frontier tier combined with commodity pricing for routine work;
- high utilization at leading clusters and weak economics at older or poorly located sites;
- local power scarcity surviving even as global compute supply expands;
- sovereign and public-capital programs representing a meaningful share of incremental frontier capacity;
- model providers shifting monetization toward enterprise integration, agents, tools, support, proprietary data, distribution, or bundled cloud services;
- large differences in return on capital among projects using superficially similar equipment;
- customers using several models and several compute locations inside one product.

This world would vindicate neither a simple bubble call nor a simple supercycle call.

It would vindicate the book's narrower claim: value creation and value capture can separate sharply, and the unit of analysis must be the specific claim on the specific layer.

## What to Measure Every Six Months

The future will not announce which scenario has arrived.

Investors can still watch a small set of variables that discriminate among them.

**Price per fixed capability.** Do not track one provider's list price in isolation. Ask what it costs to achieve a roughly stable level of useful performance across credible alternatives. Continued rapid decline is evidence for abundance even if total spending rises.

**Utilization and yield together.** A full machine at a falling price can earn less than a less-full machine did during scarcity. Watch both occupancy and realized revenue per economically usable unit.

**Backlog conversion.** Compare announced capacity, signed commitments, delivered capacity, recognized revenue, and actual cash generation. A growing gap among these stages is a capital-cycle warning.

**Renewal economics.** The first contract can be signed during panic. The renewal is negotiated after both parties know what the workload really needs. Renewal price and committed volume contain more information than the launch announcement.

**Replacement versus expansion capex.** The harvest period never arrives if a large share of apparently discretionary capex is really the price of remaining competitive.

**Residual value.** Secondary hardware pricing, lease renegotiations, data-center transactions, and retrofit costs reveal which part of the asset stack actually survives a hardware cycle.

**Large-load contract protections.** Minimum bills, collateral, take-or-pay terms, termination payments, and utility rate classes show where infrastructure forecast risk is being pushed.

**Strategic share of demand.** Track how much new capacity is funded or guaranteed by governments, sovereign funds, national champions, or corporations explicitly buying resilience and option value. Strategic demand can sustain a buildout longer than commercial productivity alone—and can create future supply that private owners later have to compete with.

## How the Thesis Fails

The central investment thesis of this book weakens materially if several things happen together.

If inference prices stop falling for equivalent capability, frontier differentiation remains large, utilization stays high through multiple refresh cycles, contracts renew without meaningful price compression, old hardware retains strong residual value, and the largest infrastructure owners produce attractive free cash flow after replacement investment, then the capital cycle was better disciplined than the thesis feared.

The technology would have won.

The capital would have won too.

That outcome should be recorded as a miss rather than explained away.

The thesis strengthens if the opposite combination appears: extraordinary AI adoption, falling unit prices, increasingly substitutable models, high but lower-yield infrastructure usage, recurring replacement capex, refinancing pressure, and productive assets moving to new owners at lower bases.

The important evidence is not a stock-market drawdown.

Prices can fall for reasons unrelated to the operating mechanism described here.

The important evidence is whether abundance transfers bargaining power away from the layers whose valuations depended on scarcity.

That is the experiment.

2030 is only a convenient observation point.

The mechanism can reveal itself sooner.
