# The IPO Supply Shock

For several years, public-market investors who wanted concentrated exposure to artificial intelligence faced an oddly narrow menu.

They could buy the dominant accelerator supplier. They could buy hyperscale cloud companies spending heavily on AI. They could buy semiconductor equipment firms, networking suppliers, data-center operators, power-equipment manufacturers, and a handful of software companies that convinced the market AI would expand their franchises.

What they could not easily buy was the thing at the center of the cultural story: many of the frontier model labs and the fastest-growing private AI companies.

Scarcity has a price.

Not only in chips.

In securities.

**When the Proxy Becomes a Stock**

CoreWeave's March 2025 IPO provided an early glimpse of what happens when a private AI infrastructure story meets public-market price discovery.

The final prospectus offered 37.5 million Class A shares at $40. CoreWeave itself offered 36.59 million of them; selling stockholders offered another 910,000. The company later reported about $1.4 billion of net IPO proceeds after underwriting discounts and commissions, before certain offering costs.

Those details matter because the phrase “an IPO” compresses several different transactions into one event.

New shares sold by the company bring fresh capital onto the balance sheet. Existing shares sold by insiders transfer ownership without funding the business. Restricted shares that become saleable later create potential supply without appearing in the opening-day offering. Stock compensation can turn employees into future sellers even when no one behaved unusually or opportunistically. A company with large capital needs may return to the market again after the IPO through follow-on offerings, convertibles, or other financing.

The first day's float is therefore only the visible edge of the capitalization table.

CoreWeave's prospectus made that unusually legible. Directors, executive officers, selling stockholders, and certain other holders representing most of the company's outstanding Class A stock and related securities were subject to underwriter lockups that generally ran until an earnings-based release point or 180 days after the prospectus, subject to exceptions and other restrictions. The prospectus warned, in ordinary securities-law language, that substantial sales after those restrictions expired—or even the expectation of them—could pressure the share price.

This is not a special defect in CoreWeave. It is the normal geometry of a newly public company.

The important point is that public supply arrives in layers.

The offering creates a float. Earnings create new information. Lockups expire. Employees exercise and sell. Venture funds distribute shares to their own investors. Registration rights become usable. Follow-on offerings can add primary or secondary stock. Options markets and index membership change who can hold or hedge the security.

A private company becomes public gradually, then all at once, then gradually again.

The offering did not settle the argument over AI infrastructure. It created a tradable instrument through which the argument could move every day.

That change is easy to underestimate.

Private markets negotiate price episodically. A financing round may occur after months of preparation among a relatively small set of investors. Public markets reprice continuously against every earnings report, bond yield, competitor announcement, customer disclosure, lockup expiration, capital raise, and change in sentiment.

A public AI company therefore becomes more than a funding vehicle.

It becomes a measuring device.

The stock can force uncomfortable distinctions into the open:

Revenue growth versus free cash flow.

Backlog versus customer concentration.

Demand versus capital intensity.

Technological importance versus the price already paid for it.

As more AI companies enter public markets, investors will gain something the private boom often lacked: comparables.

Comparables are dangerous to narratives.

Once five companies sell related forms of compute, software, or model access, a valuation cannot rely as easily on uniqueness. Public investors can compare gross margins, depreciation, sales efficiency, debt, stock compensation, capital intensity, and customer concentration line by line.

The arrival of supply is therefore also the arrival of skepticism as a standardized product.

**The Proxy Premium**

When investors cannot own the asset they want, they buy the nearest liquid proxy.

This happens constantly.

Before a country opens its capital markets, investors may pay premiums for listed companies with indirect exposure. Before an industry produces many public firms, the few available names can attract thematic flows far beyond what their own fundamentals would justify. A scarce exchange-traded fund can briefly trade above the value of its assets. A company with a small relationship to a hot technology can re-rate because it provides a ticker symbol for a story.

AI created a sophisticated version of the same phenomenon.

A public cloud platform was not merely valued on cloud cash flows. It became a claim on future AI demand.

A semiconductor supplier was not merely valued on current chip sales. It became infrastructure for the next economic era.

A power-equipment company could be reinterpreted as an AI beneficiary because data centers need switchgear and transformers.

Many of these links were real.

The premium came from the concentration of attention.

The unusual thing about a proxy premium is that it can disappear without the proxy business deteriorating.

Suppose an investor wants direct exposure to frontier models but cannot buy any frontier-model company. The investor may accept an imperfect substitute: a cloud vendor, a chip supplier, a data-center landlord. If the desired company later lists, some of the capital that had been forced into the substitute can move toward the thing it originally wanted.

Nothing terrible has to happen to the proxy.

It can keep growing. Its products can remain essential. Its earnings can rise.

Its marginal buyer can still leave.

This is why thematic concentration is not the same thing as conviction in a particular business. A portfolio can become concentrated because the investable set is small. When the set expands, the same investors can become more selective without becoming less bullish on the technology.

The distinction is especially important for market leaders. Scarcity can make one listed company serve simultaneously as a semiconductor investment, an AI investment, a growth investment, a momentum investment, and a liquid institutional proxy for an otherwise private ecosystem. The company may deserve much of that enthusiasm. But one security doing five conceptual jobs is exposed when new securities arrive to take some of those jobs away.

**The Pipeline Stops Being Hypothetical**

By late August 2026, the supply-shock argument was beginning to move from thought experiment to calendar.

Reuters reported on August 27 that Anthropic was preparing to make an IPO prospectus public after Labor Day, citing The Information, with a possible listing in late September or early October. The date was not a promise from the company and could move. Earlier reports had already described Anthropic as having confidentially filed to go public.

That distinction belongs in the text because IPO calendars are contingent even when preparations are real.

A confidential filing is not a listed stock. A public prospectus is not a completed offering. A roadshow can be delayed. Price ranges can change. A company can decide that the market is offering terms it would rather refuse.

The pipeline is evidence of potential supply, not certainty about timing.

This is exactly why the episode matters.

Private-market scarcity is temporary by design.

A venture-backed company cannot remain a rare collectible forever. Eventually it becomes a public stock, is acquired, returns capital through secondary sales, or fails to provide liquidity at the valuation its investors expected.

As the most important AI companies approach public markets, the category changes from a story about a few liquid proxies into a capital-allocation contest among direct claims.

The portfolio manager who once asked, “How do I get more AI exposure?” gets a harder question:

“Which AI exposure deserves my next dollar?”

That is a healthier market question.

It is also a much harsher one for scarcity premiums.

**What Happens When the Shelves Fill Up**

Now imagine that the menu widens.

Frontier labs list.

AI infrastructure companies list.

Agent platforms list.

Robotics firms list.

Vertical AI software firms list.

Semiconductor startups list.

Private companies acquired during the boom are spun out or brought public.

Employees gain liquidity. Venture funds distribute shares. Founders sell modest stakes. Index providers add new companies. Analysts create new categories. The investment story that once had five obvious public expressions now has fifty.

This can be bullish for the industry and bearish for scarcity premiums.

A portfolio manager who once had to own the obvious proxy can now choose among direct exposures.

The existing winner has not become worse.

The capital allocation set has become larger.

This is the IPO supply shock.

But “supply shock” should not be mistaken for a claim that every new listing mechanically pushes the market down.

Public markets absorb new securities all the time. A large and growing pool of earnings can support a large and growing pool of equity. New issues can attract new investors. A successful IPO can increase confidence in an entire category. The appearance of a high-quality public company can draw capital into a sector rather than merely redistribute the capital already there.

The pressure appears when the growth of investable claims outruns the growth of money willing to own them at the prevailing prices.

That is a relative statement.

A market can absorb $10 billion of new AI equity easily if expected earnings and inflows rise faster. It can struggle with a smaller amount if investors are already fully positioned, financing conditions have tightened, or the new listings expose uncomfortable valuation comparisons.

Supply matters through price, timing, and alternatives—not through a simple tonnage equation.

**Great Company, Bad First Trade**

An IPO introduces a distinction that private markets can postpone: the difference between company quality and stock performance.

A great company can go public at a valuation that already discounts greatness.

The company can double revenue, gain customers, and establish a durable franchise while the stock underperforms because the initial price assumed even more.

This is one of the recurring cruelties of growth investing. Operational success and investment success can diverge for years.

A private investor may have bought the company when the outcome space was wide, the valuation was lower, and few competitors existed. A public investor may buy after much of the uncertainty has been resolved—but after the valuation has risen to pay for that resolution.

The second investor owns a safer business at a more dangerous price.

The first years after a thematic boom are especially dangerous because private valuations often carry memories of scarcity.

Investors who bought the final private round may have underwritten a world in which the company was one of very few opportunities to own the theme. Public investors may price a world in which substitutes are arriving every quarter.

Liquidity does not merely reveal value.

It changes the competitive environment for capital.

**The Capitalization Table Comes Public Too**

Investors often speak about an IPO as though the company itself were crossing a border.

What actually crosses is a capitalization table.

That table contains founders, employees, venture partnerships, strategic investors, preferred-stock holders, option holders, restricted stock units, debt instruments that may convert, registration rights, and contractual restrictions on sale. The operating company can look almost unchanged on the day before and the day after listing. The ownership machinery changes dramatically.

This is where dilution and supply get confused.

A secondary sale by an early investor increases tradable supply but does not dilute the percentage ownership of everyone else. A primary issuance raises capital but does dilute existing holders. Stock-based compensation can dilute over time even if employees retain every share. A convertible can behave like debt until the conditions that make equity issuance relevant suddenly matter. Share repurchases can offset some issuance while consuming cash that might otherwise have funded the business.

For an AI company, this is not accounting trivia.

Many companies in the sector are competing simultaneously for compute, engineers, energy, data-center capacity, and distribution. Equity compensation helps them recruit. Equity financing helps them build. Acquisitions can be paid for with stock. A richly valued share price is therefore a productive corporate asset.

That creates a feedback loop investors should not moralize but should understand.

A high valuation allows a company to finance growth cheaply. Cheap financing can help produce the growth that supports the valuation. If the valuation falls, the same company may find that acquisitions cost more shares, employee grants become less effective, and external capital becomes more expensive just as competitors are still spending aggressively.

The stock is not merely a scorecard.

For a capital-hungry company, it can become part of the production system.

**The Exit Machine**

Venture capital requires exits.

Employees eventually want liquidity.

Early investors have fund lives.

Founders diversify.

Acquirers need public comparables.

The private AI boom has created large pools of paper wealth whose eventual conversion into tradable securities is not a moral problem. It is a market-structure fact.

When that conversion occurs at scale, public markets must absorb the supply.

The mechanism is easy to misdescribe because not every early investor sells when a lockup ends. Some funds distribute shares rather than selling them. Some founders hold for years. Some employees sell only enough to pay taxes. Some institutional holders treat a newly liquid position as more attractive, not less.

A lockup expiration is therefore not a scheduled dump.

It is the removal of a restriction.

That can matter even without immediate selling because the market's possible future float has changed. An investor deciding what multiple to pay for a thinly traded stock may care whether 10 percent or 70 percent of the eventual ownership base can trade. The prospect of supply can affect price before the supply itself appears.

Absorption can be smooth if earnings growth is strong and capital flows remain abundant. It can be violent if the issuance arrives just as growth decelerates, interest rates rise, or investors decide they already own enough exposure to the theme.

Every new IPO asks the market a question:

Which existing position will you sell to buy this one?

Sometimes the answer is none. New money arrives.

Sometimes the answer is the old proxy.

In a concentrated thematic trade, that distinction matters.

**Employee Liquidity Is Not a Bear Signal**

One should be careful not to moralize insider selling.

An engineer who worked for a decade at a private company may reasonably want to buy a house, diversify, or turn illiquid compensation into savings. A venture fund returning capital is doing its job. A founder selling a fraction of a large position says little by itself about future prospects.

The issue is aggregate supply.

Even here, precision matters. There is no universal dollar threshold at which selling becomes dangerous. What matters is supply relative to trading liquidity, buyer demand, valuation, and the information investors infer from the sale.

A billion dollars of stock in one company can be trivial in a deep market and destabilizing in a thin one. A large secondary offering can clear cleanly if priced attractively. A small sale can alarm investors if it arrives after executives spent months emphasizing that the opportunity was barely beginning.

The individual transaction can be innocent while the system effect is material.

The reverse is also true: a large amount of new stock can be absorbed without drama when the business is earning its valuation.

**The Index Paradox**

Going public can create automatic demand.

Large companies enter benchmarks. Index funds buy. Active managers measured against those benchmarks may follow. Options markets develop. Liquidity increases. A new stock can become collateral, compensation currency, and a component of structured products.

This can support high valuations.

It can also intensify concentration if many AI companies become large benchmark weights simultaneously.

The paradox is not that passive investing is somehow irrational. Index funds are doing what they promise to do. The issue is that market-cap weighting can make a technological theme a growing share of a diversified portfolio precisely because the market has already capitalized that theme at enormous scale.

An investor can believe she owns “the market” while discovering that a meaningful fraction of her marginal risk comes from the same cluster of assumptions: continued AI demand, continued access to power and compute, continued capital spending, and continued willingness to assign high multiples to the companies supplying the buildout.

Diversification by ticker count is not necessarily diversification by economic driver.

The paradox is that passive demand can reduce the immediate effect of supply while increasing the market's shared exposure to a later repricing.

When everyone owns the category because the category owns the index, diversification is less comforting than it looks.

**The IPO Test**

When evaluating an AI IPO, ignore the question “Is this a great company?” for a moment.

Ask instead:

What scarcity premium existed when the last private round was priced?

How many comparable public companies now exist?

How much of the offered stock funds the company, and how much provides liquidity to existing holders?

How much stock can become saleable as contractual restrictions expire?

What additional issuance is likely if the company's growth plan requires more capital?

What capital intensity is hidden beneath adjusted earnings?

How much of growth comes from a small number of customers?

What portion of compensation is paid in stock, and what does the fully diluted share count look like rather than the basic count printed in the headline?

What would the company be worth at mature margins typical of its actual economic category rather than its marketing category?

And most importantly:

If I could not sell this stock for five years, what business would I own at the end?

That question strips away the IPO theater.

It also restores time to the analysis.

A prospectus is full of snapshots: current revenue, current customers, current debt, current capitalization. The investor's return depends on a movie. How much capital will the company consume? How many shares will exist? Which competitors will become public? Which scarce inputs will become commodities? Which apparently commoditized inputs will become scarce again?

The share price on the first day answers none of those questions.

It simply tells you what other people were willing to pay before the movie played.

**Abundance Reaches the Stock Market**

The intelligence bubble contains a pleasing symmetry.

Compute becomes abundant.

Models become abundant.

AI products become abundant.

Eventually AI securities become abundant too.

The symmetry is useful, but it should not be pushed too far. Securities are not interchangeable in the way standardized compute can become interchangeable. A company with better governance, stronger economics, lower capital needs, or a genuinely durable moat can deserve a premium even in a crowded category.

What abundance removes is the presumption that exposure itself deserves one.

At every layer, more alternatives shift some bargaining power toward the buyer.

The buyer of compute can negotiate harder.

The buyer of models can route workloads.

The enterprise buyer can demand outcomes.

The public investor can demand a better price.

Scarcity supported the first phase.

Abundance will judge the second.
