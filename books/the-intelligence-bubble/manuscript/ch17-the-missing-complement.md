# The Missing Complement

The easiest mistake in an AI forecast is to confuse access with adoption.

A company can buy a model in an afternoon. It can turn on a copilot for ten thousand employees in a quarter. It can announce that artificial intelligence is available across the organization before anyone has decided which decisions should change, which data can be trusted, who is responsible for errors, how work should be measured, or what the company will stop doing once the new system begins doing part of it.

The software can arrive before the organization knows what to do with it.

That gap matters because technology does not become productivity merely by being present. It becomes productivity when a firm changes the surrounding system of work.

The distinction sounds managerial. It is also financial.

The AI supply chain is spending money now on chips, power, land, networks, buildings, leases, and model training. The customer side of the economy is spending on a different kind of capital: experiments, integrations, data cleanup, new permissions, process redesign, training, measurement, and the slow discovery of which tasks are worth changing. Much of that second category does not look like a factory. It can be harder to see on a balance sheet and harder to finance against. Yet it may determine how much of the first category ultimately earns its keep.

This is the missing complement in the intelligence boom.

The hardware can be ready before the institution is.

A useful place to see the difference is a customer-support operation studied by Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li, and Lindsey Raymond. The researchers followed thousands of support agents as a generative-AI assistant was introduced. The system did not simply answer customers on its own. It listened to the conversation and suggested responses and internal documentation to the human agent, who could accept or ignore the advice.

The productivity effect was real. In the published study, access to the assistant increased issues resolved per hour by about 15 percent on average. The gains were much larger for less experienced and lower-performing workers. The most experienced agents gained far less and, on some quality measures, did slightly worse.

That result is more interesting than a headline saying AI raised productivity 15 percent.

The tool appears to have been valuable partly because it carried patterns from stronger workers toward weaker ones. In other words, the productivity gain was not just a property of the model. It was a property of the model placed inside a particular workflow, with a particular data environment, serving a particular kind of task, under a particular division of responsibility between machine and human.

Change any of those conditions and the result could change.

A law firm cannot assume a customer-support result applies to contract negotiation. A hospital cannot infer a clinical outcome from a call-center metric. A manufacturer cannot buy a language model and declare that its defect rate will fall 15 percent. Even within the support operation, the gains were not evenly distributed.

That is not a weakness in the evidence. It is the evidence.

AI value is conditional.

The condition is the organization around it.

By 2026, the U.S. Census Bureau had enough business use to begin observing this process at national scale. Its Business Trends and Outlook Survey showed that AI use was no longer confined to a small technical fringe. During the November 2025 to January 2026 reference period used in a Census working paper, about 18 percent of firms reported using AI in a business function, rising to 32 percent on an employment-weighted basis. Large firms and knowledge-intensive sectors reported much higher use.

But breadth was limited. Among firms that used AI, 57 percent deployed it in three or fewer business functions. Writing, document analysis, and information search were among the leading worker tasks. About two-thirds of users reported using AI only to augment tasks rather than to automate them. Employment decreases attributed to AI were rare in the survey, appearing in about 2 percent of firms.

Those numbers should make both the optimist and the pessimist uncomfortable.

The pessimist has to explain why adoption has moved this far into real firms if the technology is only a toy.

The optimist has to explain why broad use still so often means shallow use.

The answer may be that turning a general tool into a production system is harder than trying it.

It is easy to ask a model to draft an email. It is harder to redesign a claims process around model assistance when each claim has legal consequences, multiple data systems, escalation rules, fraud controls, audit requirements, and customers who expect someone to be accountable.

It is easy for an employee to paste a document into a chatbot. It is harder for an enterprise to decide which documents may leave which systems, how confidential information should be handled, which outputs must be reviewed, how errors are logged, and whether the resulting workflow is actually faster once review time is counted.

It is easy to produce code. It is harder to decide whether faster code production reduces the total cost of a software organization after testing, security, architecture, maintenance, deployment, and incident response are included.

The missing complement is not reluctance.

It is everything the organization must invent around the tool.

Economists have seen this pattern before. General-purpose technologies often require complementary investments that do not resemble the technology itself. Firms reorganize processes, train people, rewrite software, change reporting relationships, develop new products, and accumulate what researchers sometimes call intangible or organizational capital. Those investments can consume time and money before the measured payoff arrives.

Brynjolfsson, Daniel Rock, and Chad Syverson described one consequence as a productivity J-curve. When a new general-purpose technology arrives, companies may spend heavily on complementary assets before those assets produce measured output. Early productivity can therefore look disappointing even when useful capability is accumulating. Later, once the new processes begin paying off, measured productivity can improve more sharply.

The curve is not a law of nature. Some technologies disappoint because they are overhyped. Some firms implement them badly. Some complementary investments never earn a return. The idea is useful because it gives us a third possibility between instant transformation and total failure.

The technology can be good, adoption can be real, and the economic payoff can still arrive slowly.

That possibility is especially important for AI investors because the supply side is not waiting politely for the demand side to finish reorganizing.

Data centers are being built on construction schedules. Servers begin aging when they are installed. Debt carries interest every quarter. Leases begin on dates written into contracts. Power capacity has to be reserved before the customer knows exactly how much inference it will eventually need. Model companies must pay for training runs before the next generation of enterprise workflows has proven its willingness to pay.

The financial clock starts early.

The organizational clock starts when the customer begins learning.

Those clocks can diverge for years.

A survey of executives across the United States, United Kingdom, Germany, and Australia published in 2026 made the tension unusually visible. The researchers found widespread reported AI use, particularly among younger and more productive firms. Yet more than 80 percent of firms said AI had produced no impact on employment or productivity over the previous three years. The same executives expected considerably larger effects over the next three years, including an average productivity increase of about 1.4 percent.

There are several ways to read that gap.

One is that executives are simply overoptimistic about the future. Another is that measurement is lagging reality. A third is that firms are still in the expensive part of adoption: using tools, experimenting, integrating, and training without yet having redesigned enough work to produce a large measured effect.

The correct interpretation will differ by firm. What matters for an investor is that “we use AI” and “AI has changed our economics” are not the same statement.

This should change how demand is modeled.

If an enterprise buys model access because a chief executive wants every employee experimenting, that is real revenue for the supplier. But it may not be durable demand at the same price. The first contract can be paid from an innovation budget. The renewal may have to survive an operating-budget review. At that point the question changes from “Are we using AI?” to “What did it improve?”

A vendor can enjoy rapid growth while its customers are still discovering the answer.

That is normal during a technology transition. It is also dangerous if the vendor's valuation assumes that experimentation automatically becomes permanent, expanding, high-margin production usage.

Some experiments will.

Some will not.

Some will migrate to cheaper models once the task is understood. Some will become embedded in software the company already buys. Some will disappear because the workflow was not important enough. Some will produce enough value that the company increases spending tenfold. Some will reduce the amount of expensive inference required because the organization learns where the model matters and where ordinary software works better.

The first wave of adoption therefore contains very little information about the final distribution of profits.

It tells us that customers are searching.

The search itself can be valuable. A company that learns early which tasks respond to AI may build an advantage even before the financial statements show a large gain. It can clean data that competitors have ignored. It can identify which human experts should train or supervise the systems. It can discover where automation creates unacceptable risk and where augmentation works well. It can redesign products around capabilities that would have seemed too expensive two years earlier.

This is organizational capital.

It is also path dependent.

Two firms can buy access to the same model and get very different results because one has clean data, clear processes, experienced managers, and a culture that can change the way work is done, while the other has fragmented systems and no agreement about who owns the outcome.

The model is identical.

The complement is not.

This is one reason the eventual winners of the AI transition may be difficult to identify by looking only at AI vendors. A retailer with excellent inventory data and disciplined operating processes may extract more value from commodity models than a glamorous AI startup captures from selling them. A bank with deeply integrated risk systems may turn cheap inference into lower processing costs. A manufacturer with years of maintenance records may improve uptime without ever owning a frontier model. A software company may use AI to reduce customer-support cost and accelerate product development, then pass none of that gain through a separate “AI revenue” line.

The value appears in the user.

That creates an accounting problem for anyone trying to measure the size of the AI economy by adding up the revenues of companies that call themselves AI businesses.

If intelligence becomes an input, much of its value will disappear into other industries.

A cheaper forecast does not remain a forecasting-industry revenue stream if a logistics company embeds it in routing. A cheaper legal draft may appear as higher margins at an insurer. Better code completion may show up as faster product release at a retailer. Improved document extraction may appear as lower administrative cost at a hospital.

The supplier sees a token bill.

The customer sees a changed process.

The economy sees whatever the process produces next.

This is why the abundance thesis and the investment thesis can separate so sharply.

The technology can diffuse widely while profit migrates away from the companies that made the diffusion possible. Falling model prices can accelerate adoption while compressing model margins. Better open systems can increase total use while weakening proprietary scarcity. More efficient inference can make AI economically viable in more workflows while reducing the compute required for any one workflow.

Every improvement can enlarge the market and intensify competition at the same time.

Investors should therefore become suspicious of demand forecasts that move directly from task exposure to supplier revenue.

A study showing that millions of workers perform tasks that AI can assist does not tell you which company will be paid, how much it will charge, how much inference the task will require, or how much of the economic gain competition will force back to the customer.

A productivity estimate is not a revenue estimate.

A revenue estimate is not a margin estimate.

A margin estimate is not a return on invested capital.

Those are four different bridges.

The missing complement sits on the first one.

Before asking who captures the value, the firm has to create it.

The practical test is simple enough to state and difficult to answer: what changed besides the model?

If a company says AI improved customer service, ask whether it changed staffing, routing, training, escalation, knowledge management, or measurement. If a manufacturer says AI reduced downtime, ask what sensors, maintenance records, parts systems, and technician workflows made the prediction actionable. If a bank says AI improved underwriting, ask how data access, review authority, audit, and risk controls changed. If a software firm says developers became faster, ask whether release frequency, defect rates, or feature throughput actually moved.

If nothing around the model changed, the productivity story may still be early.

Or it may be imaginary.

The distinction cannot be settled by the benchmark score.

This is the part of the boom that cannot be solved by building another data center.

The supply side can manufacture intelligence. It cannot manufacture a customer's willingness or ability to reorganize around it.

That work happens firm by firm, process by process, budget by budget.

It is slower than a model release and faster than institutional history. It is where most of the economic uncertainty now lives.

And it is why a world flooded with machine intelligence can still spend years learning how to use it.
