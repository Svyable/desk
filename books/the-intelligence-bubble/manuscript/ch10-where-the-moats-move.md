# Where the Moats Move

When a layer of technology becomes cheap, value does not disappear.

It relocates.

The history of computing is a history of relocation.

Mainframe hardware once captured enormous value. Then standardized components and personal computers changed the stack. Operating systems became strategic. Then browsers and the internet weakened some forms of desktop control while creating new distribution monopolies. Cloud computing abstracted servers and created giant platforms. Smartphones created new gatekeepers around app distribution. Open-source software commoditized products while managed services rebuilt margins elsewhere.

AI will not be different in principle.

The difficult question is where value moves as raw intelligence becomes cheaper.

Four moats deserve special attention.

**The Data Moat**

“Data moat” is one of the most abused phrases in technology.

Having data is not a moat.

Every neglected corporate drive is full of data.

A durable data moat requires something stronger:

The data must be proprietary or rights-cleared.

It must be relevant to a valuable task.

It must be difficult for competitors to reproduce.

It must improve the product or decision system.

And ideally, product usage should generate more of it, creating a feedback loop.

Consider an industrial maintenance platform that receives permissioned sensor histories from thousands of machines, links failures to service outcomes, and improves recommendations as the installed base grows. A general model may supply reasoning, but the proprietary operational history can make the system better in a way a new entrant cannot instantly copy.

The model is replaceable.

The learning loop is not.

**The Workflow Moat**

The strongest AI companies may look boring from the outside because they own a painful process.

Claims adjudication.

Clinical documentation.

Freight brokerage.

Tax compliance.

Drug discovery workflows.

Construction estimating.

Procurement.

Security operations.

Customer support.

Software deployment.

In these markets, intelligence is one component of a larger sequence involving data access, approvals, integrations, exception handling, audit trails, payments, and human responsibility.

A model can be swapped.

The workflow cannot be rebuilt over a weekend.

Workflow ownership creates switching cost because the customer has reorganized operations around the product. The software knows the edge cases. Employees are trained. Integrations exist. Historical records live there. Policies have been encoded. Managers trust the dashboard.

This is a much stronger place to capture value than raw model access.

**The Distribution Moat**

The best product does not always win.

The product with trusted access to the buyer often does.

Distribution matters in AI because customers face uncertainty. They do not merely ask whether a model can perform a task. They ask whether they should permit the model to touch sensitive data, interact with customers, move money, alter code, make recommendations, or act autonomously.

Trust lowers friction.

A company that already owns the customer relationship can introduce AI into an existing workflow without asking the buyer to adopt an entirely new vendor. A bank can add an assistant to its banking interface. A software suite can add agents to the tools employees already use. A marketplace can add automation to a transaction network. A medical software vendor can insert intelligence into a clinician's existing system.

This advantage is easy for technologists to underestimate because it is not elegant.

Distribution is rarely elegant.

It is often decisive.

**The Cost Moat**

If intelligence becomes abundant, the low-cost producer can still win.

But a cost moat must be structural, not merely temporary access to discounted chips.

It can come from custom hardware, superior utilization, energy advantage, workload-specific models, software optimization, scale purchasing, better scheduling, or a vertically integrated system that removes middlemen.

Cost moats become more valuable as customers learn to compare equivalent outcomes.

In a market where “smartest model” dominates purchasing, technical prestige matters.

In a market where three systems are good enough, pennies matter.

**The Fifth Moat: Responsibility**

There is another advantage that does not fit neatly into the four-part framework.

Responsibility.

As AI moves from suggestion to action, customers care about who stands behind the outcome.

Who warrants the system?

Who carries insurance?

Who provides audit trails?

Who answers the regulator?

Who compensates the customer if the system causes a loss?

Who has the balance sheet to survive a catastrophic error?

This can create a trust-and-liability moat in high-consequence industries.

A raw model says, “Here is an answer.”

A durable business says, “Here is the outcome, the evidence, the audit record, the escalation path, and the contractual responsibility.”

Those are different products.

**The Four AI Moats**

The reusable framework is simple:

**Data moat:** proprietary, rights-cleared, high-quality data connected to a compounding feedback loop.

**Workflow moat:** deep embedding in a difficult-to-replace business process.

**Distribution moat:** trusted access to customers, users, or regulated markets.

**Cost moat:** a structural ability to deliver equivalent outcomes more cheaply.

The strongest businesses will combine two or more.

A company with workflow but no cost discipline may be disrupted by a cheaper integrated rival.

A company with low cost but no distribution may become a supplier with thin margins.

A company with proprietary data but no rights clarity may own a legal problem rather than a moat.

A company with distribution but poor product quality can squander trust.

Moats are systems too.

**Moats Move When Inputs Get Cheap**

A moat can be real and temporary.

This is uncomfortable because the word suggests geology: something dug around a castle that remains where it is.

Technology moats behave more like weather systems.

A company can possess a genuine advantage because a crucial input is scarce. It can hire the few people who know how to build a frontier model. It can obtain accelerators competitors cannot. It can secure early access to a distribution channel. It can own a dataset that substitutes cannot yet reproduce.

Then the environment changes.

Talent diffuses.

Hardware supply expands.

Open tools improve.

Customers learn how to switch.

Standards emerge.

A capability that once required a specialist becomes a feature in a platform.

The business did not imagine its moat. The moat migrated.

This is why investors should ask a second-order question about every competitive advantage:

**What happens to this moat if the underlying input becomes ten times cheaper and twice as common?**

If the advantage becomes stronger, the business may be positioned above the deflation curve.

If the advantage disappears, the business was renting scarcity.

Consider two companies.

Company A sells access to a capability that is difficult to reproduce today. Its customers integrate the API lightly and can move to another provider with modest engineering work.

Company B uses the same capability inside a regulated workflow with proprietary data, audit history, distribution, and contractual responsibility for the outcome.

As model quality diffuses, Company A's differentiation shrinks.

Company B's input costs fall.

The same technical progress harms one moat and strengthens the other.

This is moat migration: the source of defensibility moves away from the ingredient that is becoming abundant and toward the complementary asset that remains scarce.

The best AI businesses may therefore become less obsessed with owning intelligence over time, not more.

**Where Value Accrues**

Return now to the AI Value Stack:

1. Energy and land
2. Data-center infrastructure
3. Compute hardware
4. Cloud and capacity markets
5. Models
6. Tools and agent infrastructure
7. Enterprise workflows
8. Distribution and customer trust
9. End-user economic outcomes

The naive assumption is that value rises with technical sophistication.

The historical reality is messier.

Sometimes the most technically difficult layer becomes fiercely competitive because everyone recognizes its importance. Meanwhile, a less glamorous layer quietly owns the customer.

The investor should ask not, “Where is the smartest technology?”

Ask:

Where is switching hardest?

Where is supply most disciplined?

Where does proprietary learning compound?

Where can price fall without destroying economics?

Where does the product own an outcome rather than an ingredient?

Where is the customer's willingness to pay linked to value created rather than compute consumed?

These questions point upward in the stack more often than the market narrative does.

The deepest opportunity in AI may be created by companies that are almost indifferent to which model wins.

They will happily use whichever intelligence is cheapest and adequate.

That is what customers do when a technology becomes infrastructure.
