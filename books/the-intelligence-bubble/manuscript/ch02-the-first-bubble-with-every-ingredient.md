# The First Bubble With Every Ingredient

Bubbles are rarely made of lies.

They are made of truths arranged in the wrong proportions.

A real technological breakthrough becomes a story about limitless markets. A genuine shortage becomes a story about permanent pricing power. A handful of exceptional companies becomes evidence that an entire category deserves exceptional valuations. A sensible strategic investment becomes a mandate to invest regardless of price. A geopolitical concern becomes an excuse to suspend return thresholds. A temporary lead becomes a permanent moat.

Each step is plausible on its own.

Together they form a machine for converting narrative momentum into capital commitments.

Artificial intelligence has nearly every ingredient this machine requires.

**Ingredient One: A Technology Worth Believing In**

The most durable bubbles attach themselves to things that matter.

Tulips are remembered because the story is colorful, but the economically significant booms are usually tied to productive systems: canals, railroads, electricity, radio, automobiles, telecom networks, the internet, housing, renewable energy, shale. Investors can tell themselves they are financing the future because, often, they are.

AI is an unusually powerful substrate for belief because the product can demonstrate itself in seconds. A user can watch a model write code, translate a contract, generate an image, explain a medical paper, manipulate a spreadsheet, or navigate a software interface. The evidence is intimate. The person is not being asked to imagine a factory that may work in five years. The machine performs in the browser.

That matters because investment narratives usually have to cross a gap between technical possibility and ordinary experience. Railroads had to be surveyed, financed, graded, and laid. Electrification arrived unevenly, block by block and factory by factory. The commercial internet was visible before many people could explain what they would actually do with it. Generative AI compressed that distance. The demonstration could precede the spreadsheet by minutes.

This does not make the enthusiasm irrational. It makes extrapolation unusually easy.

A great demo can become a total-addressable-market estimate before lunch.

The more consequential question is what happens after the demo becomes ordinary. A capability that seems miraculous when only a few firms can supply it may become expected when it appears in every office suite, phone, browser, cloud platform, and internal tool. The social importance of the capability can keep rising even as the willingness to pay for any one implementation falls.

That is the first recurring tension of the AI capital cycle: technological conviction and investor returns can move together for a while without being the same thing.

**Ingredient Two: A Scarcity Story**

Early in the boom, advanced accelerators were scarce. So was the expertise required to train frontier models. So was large-scale data-center capacity. So were power interconnections in the right places. Scarcity supported high margins and strategic behavior.

Customers reserved capacity before they fully knew how they would use it. Companies signed long-term commitments because being short of compute seemed more dangerous than being long. Governments treated semiconductor access as a national-security concern. Executives described shortages not in months but in generations of hardware.

The scarcity was not imaginary. Nvidia reported $115.2 billion of data-center revenue for its fiscal 2025, up 142 percent from the prior year, while Blackwell generated $11 billion in its first reported quarter of material sales. Large cloud providers accounted for roughly half of Nvidia's data-center revenue in that quarter. Those numbers are consistent with a market in which a small number of buyers were competing aggressively for high-end systems rather than casually testing a new product category.

But a shortage has two clocks.

The first is the clock of the buyer, who experiences scarcity now. The second is the clock of the supplier, financier, utility, developer, and government, all of whom respond to the shortage by adding capacity that arrives later.

In the short run those clocks reinforce the boom. Buyers sign commitments because supply is tight. Suppliers interpret the commitments as evidence that the tightness will persist. Lenders see contracted demand and become more comfortable financing expansion. Utilities receive interconnection requests. Data-center developers order switchgear and cooling systems. Chipmakers expand packaging and memory capacity. Governments subsidize domestic production.

Then the second clock catches the first.

Scarcity is economically intoxicating because it makes today's returns look structural.

But scarcity attracts its own destruction.

Every high-margin bottleneck tells capital where to go.

The mistake is not believing a bottleneck exists. The mistake is valuing the bottleneck as though engineers, competitors, customers, and governments will leave it alone.

**Ingredient Three: Concentrated Winners**

Markets love a simple map.

During the early AI boom, the map looked simple: a few chip companies, a few hyperscalers, a few frontier labs. Public-market investors seeking AI exposure crowded into a limited number of liquid securities. The concentration created reflexivity. Rising share prices increased index weights, reinforced institutional ownership, lowered perceived risk, and made the companies even more obvious destinations for the next dollar of thematic capital.

Scarcity existed not only in compute. It existed in investable proxies.

This matters because a stock can receive a scarcity premium simply because investors have too few ways to express a popular view.

Proxy scarcity can be mistaken for business quality because the two often arrive together. The company receiving the premium may genuinely be excellent. It may also be the only liquid way for a pension fund, mutual fund, retail investor, or benchmarked manager to obtain exposure to a theme that is still mostly private. The valuation then reflects both the economics of the company and the shortage of substitutes for the security.

Those are different things.

A useful historical analogy is not that every proxy premium collapses. It is that the menu eventually gets larger. Railroad investors eventually had many railroads. Internet investors eventually had many public internet companies. Telecom investors eventually had many carriers, equipment vendors, fiber owners, and service providers. Once a category becomes financeable, capital markets become creative about manufacturing exposure to it.

AI is following the same path. GPU clouds, model companies, robotics firms, data-center operators, networking vendors, power developers, and application companies are all potential public-market claims on roughly the same secular story.

That expansion can be healthy. It can also redistribute the premium that originally sat on a few obvious names.

That premium is vulnerable when the menu expands.

**Ingredient Four: Private Valuations Waiting for Liquidity**

Private markets can sustain a narrative in ways public markets cannot.

A private company raises infrequently. Its valuation is marked at negotiated moments. The investor base is small. Financial disclosure is limited. Employees may be locked up. Comparable companies are scarce. Secondary markets are imperfect.

Public markets are more merciless. Prices update continuously. Capital rotates. Analysts compare margins line by line. Employees sell. Index rules matter. A company can be excellent and still suffer because there are suddenly five other excellent companies competing for the same growth allocation.

The transition changes not only valuation transparency but supply. A private funding round places a relatively small amount of stock with a selected group. An IPO starts a process in which primary shares, secondary sales, employee liquidity, lock-up expirations, follow-on offerings, and eventually index inclusion can move much larger quantities of stock into portfolios.

That is why the IPO pipeline matters even before any one offering happens.

Investors accustomed to paying a scarcity premium for a small number of AI proxies have to ask what happens when the capital market begins producing more of them. A market can remain enthusiastic about AI while becoming less enthusiastic about paying any price for the first liquid exposure it found.

As AI labs, infrastructure providers, robotics companies, agent platforms, and specialized software businesses approach public markets, the supply of AI securities expands.

Investors accustomed to paying a premium for the few available proxies may discover that abundance is not bullish for the proxy premium.

**Ingredient Five: Strategic Panic**

Fear of missing out is powerful when it affects a portfolio manager.

It is stronger when it affects a chief executive.

No board wants to hear that a competitor has become an “AI-native” company while its own organization is still running pilots. No government wants to discover that strategic compute is controlled abroad. No cloud provider wants to admit it lacks capacity during a demand surge. No venture fund wants to explain why it skipped the defining platform shift of the decade.

The result is not merely speculation. It is defensive capital expenditure.

Defensive capex behaves differently from ordinary investment because its hurdle rate is partly political and reputational. The question changes from “Will this project earn an adequate return?” to “Can we afford not to do it?”

That distinction becomes visible when the buyers are already enormously profitable.

Microsoft reported $34.9 billion of capital expenditures in the first quarter of fiscal 2026 alone. Roughly half went to short-lived assets, primarily GPUs and CPUs; much of the remainder went to long-lived data-center assets. By the fourth quarter, quarterly capex had risen to $41 billion, with roughly two-thirds directed to short-lived assets. Those figures do not prove overinvestment. Microsoft was also reporting strong cloud demand and cash generation. They show something subtler: one of the world's largest and most profitable companies was willing to deploy tens of billions of dollars per quarter to avoid being capacity-constrained in a market it considered strategically important.

That is a difficult competitor for a spreadsheet to model.

A project-level return threshold can weaken when the relevant comparison is not the return on the next data center but the possibility of losing a platform position. The hyperscaler can accept a mediocre return on one layer if the investment protects a more valuable customer relationship somewhere else. A sovereign government can accept an even lower financial return if the capacity is treated as strategic infrastructure.

This is why a capital cycle can remain rational at the level of each participant while becoming excessive in aggregate.

Everyone may have a reason to build.

The system can still build too much.

That is how capital cycles become crowded.

**Ingredient Six: Debt Arrives After Equity Proves the Story**

Booms often begin with equity and end with leverage.

Equity absorbs uncertainty. Debt prefers evidence. Once utilization looks high, contracts lengthen, asset values rise, and early investors report gains, lenders become comfortable. The financing stack deepens. Projects that would have required expensive equity can now be funded with loans, notes, leases, asset-backed structures, vendor financing, and special-purpose vehicles.

By 2025 and 2026, that migration was visible in AI infrastructure.

CoreWeave, one of the emblematic GPU-cloud companies of the cycle, disclosed billions of dollars of senior notes, equipment financing, term loans, and rapidly expanding data-center lease obligations. By March 2026 it reported roughly $10.1 billion of operating lease liabilities, alongside substantial debt. Earlier filings described tens of billions of additional lease payments for agreements not yet commenced.

The point is not that CoreWeave must fail. The point is that AI infrastructure has become financeable in the language of industrial assets.

That changes the shape of the downside.

A pure equity boom can collapse brutally and still leave the losses concentrated among shareholders who knowingly owned a residual claim. A leveraged infrastructure boom can transmit stress through landlords, lenders, equipment lessors, private-credit vehicles, suppliers, and customers that depend on the capacity. The asset may continue operating while ownership changes and creditors absorb the loss.

This is another reason technological success can coexist with financial damage. The machine can remain useful after the original capital structure stops working.

**Ingredient Seven: The Product Gets Better While the Price Falls**

Most bubbles depend on an optimistic assumption about future price.

AI has a stranger feature: the product may become dramatically better even as the unit price falls.

This is a dream for demand and a nightmare for weak moats.

A model provider can improve quality, cut price, and still lose relative advantage if rivals improve faster. A cloud provider can lower the cost per unit of inference while spending more in aggregate because usage explodes. A hardware owner can see token demand multiply and still face poor returns if newer chips reduce the amount of hardware required for a given workload.

The mechanism resembles other deflationary technologies but with an unusually fast replacement cycle. The buyer is not only deciding whether to use more intelligence. The buyer is repeatedly deciding which model, chip, service, or architecture should provide it. Each improvement can stimulate demand while simultaneously weakening yesterday's scarcity rent.

That means investors have to distinguish elasticity from capture.

If the cost of inference falls by half and usage triples, the world consumes more machine intelligence. But whether the supplier earns more depends on where the price falls, who finances the new capacity, what happens to utilization, and how rapidly competitors match the improvement. Volume growth can be spectacular while returns on a particular generation of capital deteriorate.

This is the deflationary paradox that runs through the book.

More usage does not guarantee more profit at the layer supplying the usage.

**Ingredient Eight: Geopolitics Justifies Redundancy**

In ordinary business, redundant capacity is inefficient.

In geopolitics, redundancy can be the point.

Governments increasingly view compute, chips, power systems, and model capability as strategic assets. Sovereign AI programs, domestic semiconductor subsidies, national data rules, defense demand, export controls, and energy-security concerns can all support investment that would look irrational under a narrow corporate return model.

The United States' CHIPS program makes the logic explicit. Commerce described semiconductor incentives as a way to rebuild domestic manufacturing, strengthen supply-chain resilience, and support national security. Awards included up to $7.865 billion for Intel, up to $458 million for SK hynix, and smaller grants to suppliers of lithography materials, vacuum pumps, photonics, and advanced packaging. The policy objective was not simply to maximize the return on each subsidized fab. It was to create domestic capacity that might matter during disruption or geopolitical conflict.

That is rational from the perspective of a state.

It can still lower the private return available to the industry as a whole.

If two countries both decide that dependence is unacceptable, both may subsidize capacity. If several cloud providers all decide that shortage risk is intolerable, all may build spare capacity. If enterprises reserve more compute than they immediately need because underprovisioning looks strategically dangerous, suppliers see demand that partly reflects insurance rather than current use.

The result can be overcapacity without irrational actors.

This can extend a boom far longer than short sellers expect.

It can also create more eventual overcapacity.

A bubble supported by strategy does not disappear. It changes who is willing to accept the low return.

**The Dangerous Combination**

None of these ingredients proves that AI assets are in a bubble.

That word should not be used as a substitute for valuation work.

There is a strong countercase. The extraordinary capital spending may be the rational response to a platform shift whose eventual demand is still being underestimated. Capacity that looks excessive against today's usage could be scarce against tomorrow's. A hyperscaler with a broad installed base may earn attractive returns even if a particular generation of servers is underutilized for a time. Governments may reasonably pay for resilience that private markets would underprovide. And falling unit costs can create demand large enough to absorb astonishing amounts of new supply.

The history of technology contains many skeptics who were correct about excess and wrong about scale.

That is why the useful question is not “Is this a bubble?”

It is “Which assumptions have to remain true for today's capital to earn tomorrow's return?”

But together these ingredients create a market structure unusually prone to excess:

A technology of obvious importance.

A product that inspires direct emotional conviction.

Shortages that create spectacular early profits.

A concentrated set of public winners.

A private-market pipeline waiting to go public.

Strategic fear among companies and governments.

An infrastructure buildout increasingly supported by leverage and long-duration commitments.

And a product whose unit price falls as engineers make it better.

Earlier bubbles often contained several of these elements.

AI may contain all of them at once.

The mistake is to imagine that this guarantees an imminent crash. Bubbles can persist. Capital can be rational for longer than valuation skeptics expect. Demand can outrun supply for years. Winners can grow into valuations that once looked absurd.

The correct conclusion is more useful: the range of outcomes is wider than the story implies.

When a market narrative requires technological success, perfect capital allocation, durable scarcity, disciplined competition, high utilization, favorable financing, and stable margins all at once, the investor is no longer making one bet.

The investor is making a parlay.

And parlays are expensive because every leg has to hit.
