# The First Bubble With Every Ingredient

Bubbles are rarely made of lies.

They are made of truths arranged in the wrong proportions.

A real technological breakthrough becomes a story about limitless markets. A genuine shortage becomes a story about permanent pricing power. A handful of exceptional companies becomes evidence that an entire category deserves exceptional valuations. A sensible strategic investment becomes a mandate to invest regardless of price. A geopolitical concern becomes an excuse to suspend return thresholds. A temporary lead becomes a permanent moat.

Each step is plausible on its own.

Together they form a machine for converting narrative momentum into capital commitments.

Artificial intelligence has nearly every ingredient this machine requires.

**Ingredient One: A Technology Worth Believing In**

The most durable bubbles attach themselves to things that matter.

Tulips are remembered because the story is colorful, but the economically significant booms are usually tied to productive systems: canals, railroads, electricity, radio, automobiles, telecom networks, the internet, housing, renewable energy, shale. Investors can tell themselves they are financing the future because, often, they are.

AI is an unusually powerful substrate for belief because the product can demonstrate itself in seconds. A user can watch a model write code, translate a contract, generate an image, explain a medical paper, manipulate a spreadsheet, or navigate a software interface. The evidence is intimate. The person is not being asked to imagine a factory that may work in five years. The machine performs in the browser.

This shortens the distance between technological wonder and financial extrapolation.

A great demo can become a total-addressable-market estimate before lunch.

**Ingredient Two: A Scarcity Story**

Early in the boom, advanced accelerators were scarce. So was the expertise required to train frontier models. So was large-scale data-center capacity. So were power interconnections in the right places. Scarcity supported high margins and strategic behavior.

Customers reserved capacity before they fully knew how they would use it. Companies signed long-term commitments because being short of compute seemed more dangerous than being long. Governments treated semiconductor access as a national-security concern. Executives described shortages not in months but in generations of hardware.

Scarcity is economically intoxicating because it makes today's returns look structural.

But scarcity attracts its own destruction.

Every high-margin bottleneck tells capital where to go.

**Ingredient Three: Concentrated Winners**

Markets love a simple map.

During the early AI boom, the map looked simple: a few chip companies, a few hyperscalers, a few frontier labs. Public-market investors seeking AI exposure crowded into a limited number of liquid securities. The concentration created reflexivity. Rising share prices increased index weights, reinforced institutional ownership, lowered perceived risk, and made the companies even more obvious destinations for the next dollar of thematic capital.

Scarcity existed not only in compute. It existed in investable proxies.

This matters because a stock can receive a scarcity premium simply because investors have too few ways to express a popular view.

That premium is vulnerable when the menu expands.

**Ingredient Four: Private Valuations Waiting for Liquidity**

Private markets can sustain a narrative in ways public markets cannot.

A private company raises infrequently. Its valuation is marked at negotiated moments. The investor base is small. Financial disclosure is limited. Employees may be locked up. Comparable companies are scarce. Secondary markets are imperfect.

Public markets are more merciless. Prices update continuously. Capital rotates. Analysts compare margins line by line. Employees sell. Index rules matter. A company can be excellent and still suffer because there are suddenly five other excellent companies competing for the same growth allocation.

As AI labs, infrastructure providers, robotics companies, agent platforms, and specialized software businesses approach public markets, the supply of AI securities expands.

Investors accustomed to paying a premium for the few available proxies may discover that abundance is not bullish for the proxy premium.

**Ingredient Five: Strategic Panic**

Fear of missing out is powerful when it affects a portfolio manager.

It is stronger when it affects a chief executive.

No board wants to hear that a competitor has become an “AI-native” company while its own organization is still running pilots. No government wants to discover that strategic compute is controlled abroad. No cloud provider wants to admit it lacks capacity during a demand surge. No venture fund wants to explain why it skipped the defining platform shift of the decade.

The result is not merely speculation. It is defensive capital expenditure.

Defensive capex behaves differently from ordinary investment because its hurdle rate is partly political and reputational. The question changes from “Will this project earn an adequate return?” to “Can we afford not to do it?”

That is how capital cycles become crowded.

**Ingredient Six: Debt Arrives After Equity Proves the Story**

Booms often begin with equity and end with leverage.

Equity absorbs uncertainty. Debt prefers evidence. Once utilization looks high, contracts lengthen, asset values rise, and early investors report gains, lenders become comfortable. The financing stack deepens. Projects that would have required expensive equity can now be funded with loans, notes, leases, asset-backed structures, vendor financing, and special-purpose vehicles.

By 2025 and 2026, that migration was visible in AI infrastructure.

CoreWeave, one of the emblematic GPU-cloud companies of the cycle, disclosed billions of dollars of senior notes, equipment financing, term loans, and rapidly expanding data-center lease obligations. By March 2026 it reported roughly $10.1 billion of operating lease liabilities, alongside substantial debt. Earlier filings described tens of billions of additional lease payments for agreements not yet commenced.

The point is not that CoreWeave must fail. The point is that AI infrastructure has become financeable in the language of industrial assets.

That changes the shape of the downside.

**Ingredient Seven: The Product Gets Better While the Price Falls**

Most bubbles depend on an optimistic assumption about future price.

AI has a stranger feature: the product may become dramatically better even as the unit price falls.

This is a dream for demand and a nightmare for weak moats.

A model provider can improve quality, cut price, and still lose relative advantage if rivals improve faster. A cloud provider can lower the cost per unit of inference while spending more in aggregate because usage explodes. A hardware owner can see token demand multiply and still face poor returns if newer chips reduce the amount of hardware required for a given workload.

This is the deflationary paradox that runs through the book.

More usage does not guarantee more profit at the layer supplying the usage.

**Ingredient Eight: Geopolitics Justifies Redundancy**

In ordinary business, redundant capacity is inefficient.

In geopolitics, redundancy can be the point.

Governments increasingly view compute, chips, power systems, and model capability as strategic assets. Sovereign AI programs, domestic semiconductor subsidies, national data rules, defense demand, export controls, and energy-security concerns can all support investment that would look irrational under a narrow corporate return model.

This can extend a boom far longer than short sellers expect.

It can also create more eventual overcapacity.

A bubble supported by strategy does not disappear. It changes who is willing to accept the low return.

**The Dangerous Combination**

None of these ingredients proves that AI assets are in a bubble.

That word should not be used as a substitute for valuation work.

But together they create a market structure unusually prone to excess:

A technology of obvious importance.

A product that inspires direct emotional conviction.

Shortages that create spectacular early profits.

A concentrated set of public winners.

A private-market pipeline waiting to go public.

Strategic fear among companies and governments.

An infrastructure buildout increasingly supported by leverage and long-duration commitments.

And a product whose unit price falls as engineers make it better.

Earlier bubbles often contained several of these elements.

AI may contain all of them at once.

The mistake is to imagine that this guarantees an imminent crash. Bubbles can persist. Capital can be rational for longer than valuation skeptics expect. Demand can outrun supply for years. Winners can grow into valuations that once looked absurd.

The correct conclusion is more useful: the range of outcomes is wider than the story implies.

When a market narrative requires technological success, perfect capital allocation, durable scarcity, disciplined competition, high utilization, favorable financing, and stable margins all at once, the investor is no longer making one bet.

The investor is making a parlay.

And parlays are expensive because every leg has to hit.
