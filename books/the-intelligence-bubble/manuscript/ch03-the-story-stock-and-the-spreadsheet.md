# The Story Stock and the Spreadsheet

The most dangerous cell in a valuation model is often the one nobody argues about.

It might contain a long-run operating margin. It might contain a discount rate. It might be the year in which capital expenditure supposedly “normalizes.” It might be a terminal growth assumption placed fifteen years into the future, where the number looks harmless because the spreadsheet has become too wide to see without scrolling.

Markets do not usually break because investors cannot multiply.

They break because a story has been translated into numbers so smoothly that nobody remembers where the story ends and the evidence begins.

Consider a fictional company called Prometheus Intelligence.

Prometheus sells access to a powerful general-purpose model. Its revenue is $2 billion this year and doubles next year. Gross margins are improving because new accelerators are more efficient. The company has a famous brand, elite researchers, millions of users, and glowing enterprise case studies. Every quarter produces a new capability that would have looked like science fiction two years earlier.

A growth investor builds a model.

Revenue doubles, then grows 70 percent, then 50 percent, then 35 percent, then 25 percent. Gross margin rises from 55 percent to 70 percent. Research spending grows more slowly than revenue. Capital intensity falls after the current buildout. Prometheus becomes the operating system for machine intelligence. By year ten, cash flow is immense.

The valuation looks demanding but defensible.

Then change three assumptions.

First, inference prices fall faster than expected. Prometheus still handles vastly more tokens, but revenue per token declines.

Second, enterprise customers adopt routing software that automatically sends each task to the cheapest model that meets a quality threshold. Prometheus remains the premium choice for difficult work but loses commodity requests.

Third, depreciation and replacement capex remain high because the hardware fleet turns over rapidly and frontier training keeps expanding.

Nothing about the technology has failed.

Revenue may still grow 30 percent.

Users may still love the product.

Prometheus may still be one of the most important companies in the world.

But the stock can be worth half as much.

That is the story-stock problem.

**Growth Has a Denominator**

Financial headlines favor numerators.

Revenue up 80 percent.

Users up 200 percent.

Tokens served up 500 percent.

Compute installed up 300 percent.

But businesses live inside fractions.

Return on invested capital is profit relative to the capital required to generate it.

Free cash flow is operating cash flow after investment.

Gross margin is gross profit relative to revenue.

Utilization is productive usage relative to capacity.

Customer acquisition payback is lifetime economics relative to the cost of winning the account.

The numerator can look heroic while the denominator quietly destroys the investment case.

An AI company that grows revenue from $1 billion to $5 billion while expanding its capital base from $2 billion to $20 billion has created something valuable. It has not necessarily created value for shareholders.

The distinction becomes especially important when a formerly asset-light industry becomes asset-heavy.

Software investors were trained for decades to prize businesses in which the marginal cost of serving another customer approached zero. The ideal software company wrote code once, distributed copies cheaply, collected subscriptions, and converted a rising share of revenue into cash. Capital expenditure was modest. Inventory was abstract. Physical depreciation barely entered the conversation.

Large-scale AI complicates this ideal.

The code may be software, but the service consumes scarce hardware and electricity every time it runs. The model itself may be an intangible asset; delivering its output is not.

An investor valuing an AI business as if it were traditional software while the economics increasingly resemble a blend of software, cloud infrastructure, and industrial capacity can make a category error before entering a single number.

**The Company That Wins and Still Disappoints**

Return to Prometheus Intelligence and make the numbers concrete.

In year one, it produces $2 billion of revenue and negligible free cash flow because it is building capacity.

By year five, revenue has reached $12 billion.

That is an extraordinary company.

Now suppose the market originally valued Prometheus as if year-five revenue would be $15 billion, gross margin would reach 72 percent, and annual capital expenditure would fall to 8 percent of revenue once the initial infrastructure wave ended.

Reality arrives slightly less perfect.

Revenue reaches $12 billion instead of $15 billion.

Gross margin reaches 62 percent instead of 72 because model competition forces price cuts.

Capital expenditure remains at 18 percent of revenue because training keeps scaling and the company refreshes accelerators more often than expected.

Nothing in those numbers describes failure.

The company has increased revenue sixfold.

Its product may be dramatically better.

Its customers may be saving billions of dollars.

Its brand may be stronger.

But the difference between the original story and the realized business is enormous.

At $15 billion of revenue and a 72 percent gross margin, Prometheus produces $10.8 billion of gross profit.

At $12 billion and a 62 percent gross margin, it produces $7.44 billion.

The gap is $3.36 billion of annual gross profit before we even discuss research expense, sales expense, depreciation, taxes, or capital expenditure.

Now look at the capital side.

Eight percent of $15 billion is $1.2 billion of annual capex.

Eighteen percent of $12 billion is $2.16 billion.

The realized company therefore produces billions less gross profit while requiring nearly a billion dollars more annual investment than the model expected.

A heroic revenue chart can conceal a radically different free-cash-flow machine.

This is why investors should learn to think in paired scenarios.

Do not ask only:

How big can this become?

Ask:

What happens if it becomes very big but less profitable than expected?

That is the dangerous middle state in technology investing. Failure is easy to model. Success is easy to celebrate. The hard case is *successful disappointment*—a company that changes the world and still fails to earn the return embedded in its stock price.

**Depreciation Is Not a Vibe**

Depreciation is often treated by excited markets as a bookkeeping nuisance.

Sometimes it is conservative. Sometimes useful lives underestimate actual asset value. Sometimes a heavily depreciated data center remains productive for years.

But an accelerator is not an office building.

When a new generation of hardware offers materially better performance per watt, better memory bandwidth, improved interconnect, and better economics for the dominant workloads, the older fleet may still function while becoming economically obsolete.

This matters because accounting asks one question—over what period should we expense the cost?—while competition asks another—how long before a rival can deliver the same outcome more cheaply?

The second clock can move faster.

If a provider buys $10 billion of accelerators and depreciates them over five years, the accounting charge is orderly. If two years later a new system cuts the cost of a key workload in half, the competitive value of the old fleet may deteriorate long before the depreciation schedule reaches zero.

The asset works.

The economics moved.

**Two Companies, Two Clocks**

The useful-life problem became unusually visible in 2025 because two of the largest technology companies looked at server fleets exposed to the same broad AI transition and moved their accounting assumptions in opposite directions.

Meta extended the estimated useful lives of most of its servers and network assets to 5.5 years effective January 1, 2025. Its 2025 annual report later quantified the effect: the change reduced depreciation expense for the year by $2.92 billion and increased net income by $2.59 billion, or $1.00 per diluted share.

The company did not hide what it was doing. Its filings describe useful lives as management estimates that are reviewed routinely and can differ from actual lives because of changes in operations, planned use, and technological advancement. That is ordinary accounting. If equipment remains productive longer than previously expected, extending its useful life can produce a more faithful allocation of cost.

Amazon, looking at part of its own fleet, reached the opposite conclusion.

It had increased the estimated useful life of servers from five years to six years at the beginning of 2024. That change reduced 2024 depreciation and amortization expense by $3.2 billion and increased net income by $2.5 billion. Then, effective January 1, 2025, Amazon shortened the estimated useful life of a subset of servers and networking equipment from six years back to five.

The reason in its filing is strikingly direct: an increased pace of technology development, particularly in artificial intelligence and machine learning.

Amazon also recorded about $920 million of accelerated depreciation and related charges in the fourth quarter of 2024 after deciding to retire certain servers and networking equipment early. It expected the shorter useful-life estimate and those retirement decisions together to reduce 2025 operating income further.

Neither accounting decision proves that one company understood AI hardware correctly and the other did not.

Their fleets are not identical. Their workloads are not identical. Their procurement contracts, software stacks, network designs, internal reuse opportunities, and refresh strategies differ. A server that is uneconomic for one workload may remain useful for another. A hyperscaler can move older hardware down a hierarchy of workloads rather than throw it away. Custom silicon can change the comparison again.

That is precisely why the disagreement matters.

There is no universal “AI server life” waiting to be looked up in a table.

The accounting life of a fleet is partly an engineering estimate and partly an operating assumption. It depends on what the owner thinks it will do with the machine, how fast better machines arrive, whether software extracts more from old hardware, what electricity costs, which customers will accept the performance, and whether the hardware can be redeployed.

A valuation model that treats depreciation as a mechanically given percentage of revenue is therefore importing somebody else's judgment without admitting it.

The investor should separate at least three clocks.

The first is **physical life**: how long the equipment can continue to function.

The second is **accounting life**: how long management expects to allocate its cost through depreciation.

The third is **competitive economic life**: how long the asset can deliver a workload at an attractive cost compared with the alternatives available to customers and rivals.

Those clocks can disagree for years.

A machine may be physically healthy after it has lost its most profitable workload. An asset may remain economically useful after it is fully depreciated. Management may lengthen an accounting estimate because software, networking, and workload placement extend productive service. Management may shorten one because a new hardware generation changes the replacement calculus faster than expected.

This is not an argument for automatically adding back depreciation when valuing AI businesses, and it is not an argument for treating every accounting estimate as aggressive.

It is an argument for refusing to let the income statement answer an engineering question by itself.

If a company reports rising operating income partly because the same installed asset base is being depreciated more slowly, the next question is not whether the accounting is legal. The next question is whether replacement behavior supports the assumption.

How old is the fleet when it is retired?

Where do older accelerators go?

Does the company move them to lower-value inference, internal workloads, or customers with looser latency requirements?

Are power and cooling costs making older systems uneconomic even when the chips still work?

Does software improvement extend their usefulness, or does a new architecture require so much surrounding change that the old system becomes stranded?

What proportion of current capital expenditure is genuinely expansion, and what proportion is replacement wearing the same capex label?

These are not footnote questions anymore.

At AI-scale capital intensity, they can move billions of dollars of annual expense and alter the cash-flow story an investor believes will arrive after the buildout.

The awkward conclusion is that a cleaner earnings trajectory can coexist with a harder replacement problem.

That is why the depreciation line deserves the same skepticism as the revenue-growth line. Both are summaries of a much messier physical system.

**The Terminal-Value Trap**

High-growth valuations are unusually sensitive to what happens far away.

This feels backwards. Surely the near future should matter more because it is easier to forecast.

In discounted cash-flow models, however, a large share of value can sit in the terminal period, especially for companies whose profits are assumed to mature years from now. The calculation quietly says: most of what you are paying for today depends on the business eventually reaching durable margins and then defending them for a very long time.

This is why a debate about AI moats is not philosophical.

It is a debate about terminal value.

If models become substitutes, terminal margins fall.

If switching gets easier, terminal margins fall.

If distribution owners bargain away model economics, terminal margins fall.

If regulation raises compliance cost, terminal margins may fall.

If capital intensity remains high, terminal free cash flow falls.

If cost declines are passed through to customers, terminal gross profit may grow more slowly than usage.

The spreadsheet can withstand bad news this quarter and still collapse under a two-percentage-point change in an assumption fifteen years away.

**The Narrative Premium**

Narratives are not irrational. They are necessary.

No investor can model the future without a view of how the world works. The problem begins when narrative strength becomes a substitute for probability.

AI narratives are unusually strong because they operate simultaneously at personal, corporate, national, and civilizational levels.

Personal: this tool can make me smarter.

Corporate: this technology can automate work.

National: this capability may determine geopolitical power.

Civilizational: this could transform what humans can discover and produce.

An asset associated with all four levels can acquire a moral aura. Owning it feels like participating in the future. Selling it can feel like confessing that one “doesn't get it.”

That emotional structure is hostile to valuation discipline.

The investor's job is to restore boredom.

How much cash?

How much capital?

How long is the advantage?

Who can switch?

What does the customer save?

Who owns the customer relationship?

What happens if price falls 80 percent?

What does refinancing cost?

What proportion of demand is contractual, and what proportion is merely reserved?

What happens when the growth rate slows?

These questions are not anti-technology.

They are the technology translated into economics.

**A Better Spreadsheet**

A useful AI valuation model should contain explicit bear cases that do not require technological failure.

That last condition is essential.

The lazy bear case says: people stop using AI.

The serious bear case says: people use much more AI, but competition, deflation, capital intensity, and bargaining power distribute the value differently from what today's price assumes.

Model a world where tokens grow tenfold and price falls twentyfold.

Model a world where enterprise adoption is strong but routing software commoditizes models.

Model a world where utilization reaches only 65 percent rather than 85 percent.

Model a world where depreciation stays elevated.

Model a world where financing costs rise by 300 basis points.

Model a world where the company's product remains excellent but loses its benchmark lead.

If the investment thesis survives those worlds, you may own a durable business.

If it survives only a world in which every current advantage persists, you own a story with a ticker symbol.
