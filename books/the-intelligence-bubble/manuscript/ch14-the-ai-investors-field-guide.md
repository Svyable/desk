# The AI Investor's Field Guide

A good framework should make you harder to impress.

Not cynical.

Harder to impress.

AI investing is currently rich in numbers designed to produce awe: parameter counts, benchmark scores, GPU counts, megawatts, tokens, users, bookings, total addressable markets, model releases, capex plans. Many are useful. None substitutes for understanding the economic claim you are buying.

The field guide that follows is designed to force every AI investment back into ordinary questions about value.

**Question One: What Exactly Is Being Sold?**

Begin by refusing the category label.

“AI company” is not an economic description.

Does the company sell:

- electricity;
- powered land;
- data-center capacity;
- chips;
- networking;
- cloud compute;
- model access;
- developer infrastructure;
- workflow software;
- autonomous labor;
- advertising improved by AI;
- financial claims on infrastructure;
- or outcomes for a customer?

Each category has different capital intensity, competition, margins, and risks.

A model lab and a data-center landlord can both benefit from AI demand while having opposite exposure to falling inference costs.

Know what you own before you decide what you believe.

**Question Two: Who Pays—and Why?**

Revenue is an accounting fact.

Willingness to pay is an economic fact.

Who is the final customer?

What measurable benefit does the customer receive?

Does the product save labor, reduce errors, increase revenue, shorten cycle time, lower risk, improve conversion, or create something the customer could not previously do?

Is the buyer paying from a proven operating budget or from an experimental innovation budget?

Would the customer renew if the chief executive stopped mentioning AI in speeches?

A durable AI business should become less dependent on the word AI as the product matures.

**Question Three: What Happens If Intelligence Gets 90 Percent Cheaper?**

Apply the Deflation Test.

Do not ask whether costs *will* fall 90 percent on a convenient schedule. Ask what the business would look like if they did.

For a workflow owner, cheaper intelligence may widen margins and enable more usage.

For a model reseller, cheaper intelligence may destroy pricing.

For a capacity owner, lower cost per task may be good if volume overwhelms the efficiency gain and bad if customers simply need fewer resources.

For a hardware company, the answer may depend on whether falling cost expands the total market faster than architecture competition erodes share.

A company that benefits from deflation has a structurally attractive position.

A company that needs permanent scarcity deserves a lower multiple than a scarcity narrative implies.

**Question Four: Where Does the Cash Come From?**

Growth funded by customers is different from growth funded by investors.

Map the capital sources.

Operating cash flow.

Equity issuance.

Debt.

Finance leases.

Operating leases.

Vendor financing.

Prepayments from customers.

Infrastructure partners.

Government subsidies.

Strategic investors.

These are not equivalent.

A project funded by customer prepayments has transferred part of the risk.

A project funded by high-coupon debt has concentrated the risk.

A project funded by equity has more flexibility but can dilute shareholders.

A project funded by a strategic supplier may have hidden commercial dependencies.

Follow the money before following the GPUs.

**Question Five: What Is Actual Utilization?**

Capacity claims invite optimism.

Ask for economic utilization.

What percentage of installed capacity is billable?

What is contracted versus consumed?

How much is internal?

How much is reserved for peak demand?

How much sits on old hardware generations?

How does realized revenue per unit compare with theoretical list price?

How does utilization change when a large training run ends?

How concentrated is demand by customer and workload?

If management cannot discuss these issues with precision, the investor should model a larger uncertainty band.

**Question Six: Does the Moat Survive Model Substitution?**

Run the Weekend Test.

Could customers replace the underlying model without replacing the product?

If yes, that can be good. It means the company may own the workflow rather than the model.

Could customers replace the company by switching models?

If yes, that is bad. It means the company may be a wrapper around temporary access.

Then score the four moats:

**Data.** Is it proprietary, rights-cleared, and compounding?

**Workflow.** Is the product embedded in operations?

**Distribution.** Does the company own trusted customer access?

**Cost.** Can it deliver equivalent outcomes structurally cheaper?

No moat is permanent. The point is to identify what competitors must overcome besides technical competence.

**Question Seven: What Assumptions Are Inside the Price?**

Reverse the valuation.

Do not begin with your forecast and calculate a value.

Begin with the market value and ask what future cash flows would justify it.

What revenue scale is implied?

What mature margin?

What capital intensity?

What market share?

What duration of competitive advantage?

What dilution?

What return on incremental invested capital?

This is one of the best defenses against story stocks because it changes the burden of proof.

Instead of asking “Can this company become huge?” you ask “How huge must it become before today's price works?”

The difference is enormous.

**Question Eight: Which Risk Is Being Called Strategic?**

AI companies increasingly justify spending as strategic necessity.

Sometimes that is correct.

A hyperscaler that underbuilds can lose an important platform transition. A country without advanced compute may become dependent on foreign suppliers. An enterprise that refuses to experiment may fall behind.

But “strategic” can become a word used to exempt investment from financial discipline.

Ask what happens if the strategic project earns below the cost of capital.

Is the low return justified by benefits elsewhere in the company?

Can those benefits be measured?

Is management spending to create advantage or merely to avoid embarrassment?

Strategic fear is real.

So is strategic waste.

**Question Nine: What Is the Refinancing Scenario?**

If the business uses debt, run a maturity test.

When does the debt come due?

At what interest rate was it issued?

What would the same capital cost today?

Which covenants matter?

What assets secure the borrowing?

How much residual value do those assets have if hardware economics change?

What happens if the equity price is down 60 percent at the refinancing date?

A company can be operationally healthy and financially trapped by the timing of its obligations.

That is why debt puts a date on the argument.

**Question Ten: Who Keeps the Customer's ROI?**

Suppose an AI system saves a customer one million dollars a year.

How much of that million can the vendor capture?

If competition is intense and switching is easy, perhaps only fifty thousand.

If the vendor owns the workflow and guarantees the outcome, perhaps several hundred thousand.

If the tool is bundled into a platform the customer already buys, the incremental price may be tiny even though the social value is large.

The existence of customer value does not answer the supplier-value question.

This is the central confusion in miniature.

**The AI Investment Scorecard**

For a fast first pass, score each investment from one to five on the following dimensions:

**End-demand quality.** Is usage producing proven customer value?

**Deflation resilience.** Does cheaper intelligence help or hurt?

**Capital efficiency.** How much incremental capital is required for growth?

**Utilization visibility.** Can productive use be measured clearly?

**Moat strength.** Do data, workflow, distribution, or cost advantages compound?

**Customer diversity.** Can one buyer impair the business?

**Financing resilience.** Can the company survive a bad capital market?

**Valuation tolerance.** How much can go wrong before the stock becomes a poor investment?

The score is not an answer.

It is a machine for generating better questions.

**The Most Important Discipline**

Never let the technology thesis substitute for the security thesis.

Write them separately.

**Technology thesis:** AI inference demand will grow dramatically over the next decade.

**Security thesis:** This particular data-center operator, at this enterprise value, with this debt load, these contracts, this power cost, this hardware fleet, and this utilization profile will earn returns above its cost of capital.

The first can be obvious.

The second is where fortunes are made and lost.
