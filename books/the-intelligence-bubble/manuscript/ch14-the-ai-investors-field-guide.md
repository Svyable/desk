# The AI Investor's Field Guide

A good framework should make you harder to impress.

Not cynical.

Harder to impress.

AI investing is currently rich in numbers designed to produce awe: parameter counts, benchmark scores, GPU counts, megawatts, tokens, users, bookings, total addressable markets, model releases, capex plans. Many are useful. None substitutes for understanding the economic claim you are buying.

The difficulty is that the numbers arrive from different layers of the stack and are routinely treated as though they describe the same thing. A utility announces a large-load request. A developer announces a campus. A chip company reports an order. A cloud provider signs a capacity contract. A model company reports token growth. An application company reports users. The same expected unit of future demand can appear in several of those numbers before an end customer has paid for an economically useful result.

That does not make the numbers false. It makes them related.

An investor who adds them together can count the same optimism several times.

The field guide that follows is designed to force every AI investment back into ordinary questions about value.

**Question One: What Exactly Is Being Sold?**

Begin by refusing the category label.

“AI company” is not an economic description.

Does the company sell:

- electricity;
- powered land;
- data-center capacity;
- chips;
- networking;
- cloud compute;
- model access;
- developer infrastructure;
- workflow software;
- autonomous labor;
- advertising improved by AI;
- financial claims on infrastructure;
- or outcomes for a customer?

Each category has different capital intensity, competition, margins, and risks.

A model lab and a data-center landlord can both benefit from AI demand while having opposite exposure to falling inference costs.

The distinction matters most when a company spans several layers. A cloud provider may own buildings, lease buildings, buy accelerators, resell model access, operate proprietary models, and use those models to defend a software franchise. The consolidated income statement can make these activities look like one AI business. Economically they are several businesses transferring costs and benefits among themselves.

That is not a reason to distrust the accounting. It is a reason to identify where the return is supposed to appear.

If a company spends heavily on compute so that a larger software franchise becomes harder to leave, the compute layer may not need to earn a standalone venture-style return. If an independent infrastructure company owns the same machines, it usually does. The physical asset can be identical while the investment proposition is not.

Know what you own before you decide what you believe.

**Question Two: Who Pays—and Why?**

Revenue is an accounting fact.

Willingness to pay is an economic fact.

Who is the final customer?

What measurable benefit does the customer receive?

Does the product save labor, reduce errors, increase revenue, shorten cycle time, lower risk, improve conversion, or create something the customer could not previously do?

Is the buyer paying from a proven operating budget or from an experimental innovation budget?

Would the customer renew if the chief executive stopped mentioning AI in speeches?

A durable AI business should become less dependent on the word AI as the product matures.

The strongest evidence is usually boring. A customer renews after the pilot. Usage survives a budget review. The product moves from an innovation team into an operating department. A buyer expands because the first deployment produced a result somebody inside the company is willing to defend in dollars, hours, defects, claims, sales, or throughput.

The weaker evidence is one step removed: letters of intent, reservations, announced partnerships, framework agreements, demonstrations, waitlists, capacity requests. These can be rational leading indicators. They can also be options purchased cheaply against a future that has not arrived.

The investor's job is not to dismiss soft evidence. It is to avoid pricing soft evidence as though it were collected cash.

**Question Three: What Happens If Intelligence Gets 90 Percent Cheaper?**

Apply the Deflation Test.

Do not ask whether costs *will* fall 90 percent on a convenient schedule. Ask what the business would look like if they did.

For a workflow owner, cheaper intelligence may widen margins and enable more usage.

For a model reseller, cheaper intelligence may destroy pricing.

For a capacity owner, lower cost per task may be good if volume overwhelms the efficiency gain and bad if customers simply need fewer resources.

For a hardware company, the answer may depend on whether falling cost expands the total market faster than architecture competition erodes share.

A company that benefits from deflation has a structurally attractive position.

A company that needs permanent scarcity deserves a lower multiple than a scarcity narrative implies.

There is a second version of the test that is less comfortable: what happens if intelligence gets cheaper for everyone except this company?

Industry deflation is not automatically company deflation. A business can be locked into expensive leases, an older accelerator fleet, unfavorable power contracts, or a software architecture built around a costly model. The market price of an AI task can fall faster than the company's cost of producing it.

That is how technological progress becomes margin pressure.

**Question Four: Where Does the Cash Come From?**

Growth funded by customers is different from growth funded by investors.

Map the capital sources.

Operating cash flow.

Equity issuance.

Debt.

Finance leases.

Operating leases.

Vendor financing.

Prepayments from customers.

Infrastructure partners.

Government subsidies.

Strategic investors.

These are not equivalent.

A project funded by customer prepayments has transferred part of the risk.

A project funded by high-coupon debt has concentrated the risk.

A project funded by equity has more flexibility but can dilute shareholders.

A project funded by a strategic supplier may have hidden commercial dependencies.

Then ask the question one level deeper: what funded the funder?

A capacity contract may look like customer-financed growth until the customer itself is spending venture capital. A supplier's strategic investment may look like outside validation until part of the proceeds returns to that supplier as equipment purchases. A cloud commitment may look like end demand when it is really one technology company financing another technology company's access to its platform.

None of these arrangements is inherently circular or unsound. Strategic ecosystems often finance their own development. But related financing can make several balance sheets appear to confirm the same demand thesis.

Independent end demand deserves more weight than mutually reinforcing capital flows.

Follow the money before following the GPUs.

**Question Five: What Is Actual Utilization?**

Capacity claims invite optimism.

Ask for economic utilization.

What percentage of installed capacity is billable?

What is contracted versus consumed?

How much is internal?

How much is reserved for peak demand?

How much sits on old hardware generations?

How does realized revenue per unit compare with theoretical list price?

How does utilization change when a large training run ends?

How concentrated is demand by customer and workload?

If management cannot discuss these issues with precision, the investor should model a larger uncertainty band.

Utilization also has a price. A machine can be technically busy while producing disappointing economics. Discounts, committed-use credits, free migration periods, bundled services, internal workloads, and low-value batch jobs can all raise occupancy without producing the return implied by a headline capacity number.

The useful question is therefore not merely whether the asset is occupied. It is whether the asset is occupied at a price that covers its full economic cost, including the capital required to replace what wears out or becomes obsolete.

**Question Six: Does the Moat Survive Model Substitution?**

Run the Weekend Test.

Could customers replace the underlying model without replacing the product?

If yes, that can be good. It means the company may own the workflow rather than the model.

Could customers replace the company by switching models?

If yes, that is bad. It means the company may be a wrapper around temporary access.

Then score the four moats:

**Data.** Is it proprietary, rights-cleared, and compounding?

**Workflow.** Is the product embedded in operations?

**Distribution.** Does the company own trusted customer access?

**Cost.** Can it deliver equivalent outcomes structurally cheaper?

No moat is permanent. The point is to identify what competitors must overcome besides technical competence.

The test should be applied to the customer's engineering team as well as to outside competitors. Sometimes the most dangerous substitute is not another startup. It is a competent customer discovering that falling model prices have made the formerly difficult layer cheap enough to build internally.

**Question Seven: What Assumptions Are Inside the Price?**

Reverse the valuation.

Do not begin with your forecast and calculate a value.

Begin with the market value and ask what future cash flows would justify it.

What revenue scale is implied?

What mature margin?

What capital intensity?

What market share?

What duration of competitive advantage?

What dilution?

What return on incremental invested capital?

This is one of the best defenses against story stocks because it changes the burden of proof.

Instead of asking “Can this company become huge?” you ask “How huge must it become before today's price works?”

The difference is enormous.

A useful reverse valuation should also contain a physically plausible world. If the implied revenue requires a certain amount of electricity, accelerators, customers, inference, or enterprise spending, compare that requirement with the size of the system that would have to support it. A spreadsheet can compound revenue at forty percent for a decade without ever asking where the substations come from or how many customers must sign checks.

Physical plausibility does not prove valuation. It can reveal when valuation has quietly become science fiction.

The reverse exercise also exposes a subtler problem. Several companies in the same portfolio may each be priced as though they will capture the same industry profit pool. The chip supplier assumes durable scarcity economics. The cloud assumes attractive infrastructure margins. The model provider assumes premium pricing. The application company assumes most of the customer's productivity surplus accrues to software.

All four can grow. They cannot all capture the same dollar.

Portfolio-level consistency matters when an investment theme stretches across a value chain.

**Question Eight: Which Risk Is Being Called Strategic?**

AI companies increasingly justify spending as strategic necessity.

Sometimes that is correct.

A hyperscaler that underbuilds can lose an important platform transition. A country without advanced compute may become dependent on foreign suppliers. An enterprise that refuses to experiment may fall behind.

But “strategic” can become a word used to exempt investment from financial discipline.

Ask what happens if the strategic project earns below the cost of capital.

Is the low return justified by benefits elsewhere in the company?

Can those benefits be measured?

Is management spending to create advantage or merely to avoid embarrassment?

Strategic fear is real.

So is strategic waste.

There is no contradiction in deciding that an investment is strategically necessary and financially mediocre. Companies make defensive expenditures all the time. The analytical mistake is to grant a defensive project the valuation multiple of a high-return growth business merely because management cannot afford to ignore it.

**Question Nine: What Is the Refinancing Scenario?**

If the business uses debt, run a maturity test.

When does the debt come due?

At what interest rate was it issued?

What would the same capital cost today?

Which covenants matter?

What assets secure the borrowing?

How much residual value do those assets have if hardware economics change?

What happens if the equity price is down 60 percent at the refinancing date?

A company can be operationally healthy and financially trapped by the timing of its obligations.

That is why debt puts a date on the argument.

Do not stop at funded debt. Long leases, purchase commitments, minimum-volume agreements, take-or-pay power arrangements, and contracted construction can behave like debt when revenue disappoints. The legal labels differ. The economic question is the same: which cash outflows remain mandatory after management changes its mind?

Flexibility has value in a technology cycle because the useful life of the forecast may be shorter than the useful life of the financing.

**Question Ten: Who Keeps the Customer's ROI?**

Suppose an AI system saves a customer one million dollars a year.

How much of that million can the vendor capture?

If competition is intense and switching is easy, perhaps only fifty thousand.

If the vendor owns the workflow and guarantees the outcome, perhaps several hundred thousand.

If the tool is bundled into a platform the customer already buys, the incremental price may be tiny even though the social value is large.

The existence of customer value does not answer the supplier-value question.

This is the central confusion in miniature.

The answer can also change over time. Early in a market, a scarce supplier may capture much of the surplus because the buyer has few alternatives and little internal knowledge. Later, competitors arrive, procurement learns how to benchmark them, open alternatives improve, and customers redesign workflows around substitutable components. The customer may keep more of the productivity gain even as the technology becomes more useful.

That is wonderful for the economy and uncomfortable for a valuation built on permanent supplier rents.

**The AI Investment Scorecard**

For a fast first pass, score each investment from one to five on the following dimensions:

**End-demand quality.** Is usage producing proven customer value?

**Deflation resilience.** Does cheaper intelligence help or hurt?

**Capital efficiency.** How much incremental capital is required for growth?

**Utilization visibility.** Can productive use be measured clearly?

**Moat strength.** Do data, workflow, distribution, or cost advantages compound?

**Customer diversity.** Can one buyer impair the business?

**Financing resilience.** Can the company survive a bad capital market?

**Valuation tolerance.** How much can go wrong before the stock becomes a poor investment?

The score is not an answer.

It is a machine for generating better questions.

Two companies with the same total can still be radically different investments. One may have superb demand and dangerous financing. Another may have mediocre growth and a fortress balance sheet. Adding the numbers destroys precisely the information the exercise is supposed to expose.

Keep the ugly dimension visible.

A field guide is useful partly because markets reward forgetting. During a boom, financing risk looks pedantic because capital is available. During a shortage, substitution risk looks theoretical because customers take whatever they can get. During rapid growth, valuation discipline looks like an inability to understand the future. The neglected question changes with the cycle.

That is why the questions should survive even when their answers do not.

**The Most Important Discipline**

Never let the technology thesis substitute for the security thesis.

Write them separately.

**Technology thesis:** AI inference demand will grow dramatically over the next decade.

**Security thesis:** This particular data-center operator, at this enterprise value, with this debt load, these contracts, this power cost, this hardware fleet, and this utilization profile will earn returns above its cost of capital.

The first can be obvious.

The second is where fortunes are made and lost.

And sometimes the most useful conclusion is that the first thesis is stronger than the second. You can believe the factories will be built, the models will improve, the applications will spread, and the cost of useful intelligence will fall without knowing which security deserves your money today.

Waiting is also an investment decision.
