# Debt in the Machine

Equity lets you be wrong slowly.

Debt puts a date on the argument.

This is why leverage changes the character of a capital cycle. An all-equity project can disappoint for years while investors debate strategy. A leveraged project must still pay interest while everyone is debating.

The machine may be magnificent.

The coupon is unmoved.

**How a GPU Becomes a Bond Story**

The first generation of AI infrastructure was funded largely by the balance sheets of rich technology companies and venture investors willing to absorb uncertainty.

Then the assets began to look financeable.

A lender could see expensive hardware with resale value.

A data-center operator could present contracted revenue.

A cloud provider could show utilization.

A developer could point to a long lease with a recognizable counterparty.

A financing market emerged around the physical substrate of AI.

This was inevitable. Capitalism specializes in turning cash-flow expectations into securities.

The forms vary:

Term loans.

Senior notes.

Convertible debt.

Equipment financing.

Finance leases.

Operating leases.

Vendor financing.

Special-purpose vehicles.

Joint ventures with infrastructure investors.

Long-term take-or-pay contracts.

The accounting treatment differs. The economic question is the same: who has promised to pay whom, for how long, under what assumptions about utilization and residual value?

**CoreWeave and the New Financing Grammar**

By 2026, CoreWeave had become a useful case study not because it proved the bear case but because its filings revealed the financing grammar of the new industry.

Start with the balance sheet.

At June 30, 2026, CoreWeave reported about $16.3 billion of operating lease liabilities, up from roughly $8.2 billion at the end of 2025. The undiscounted payments behind those recorded operating leases totaled about $29.1 billion. The company also disclosed another $35.5 billion of estimated future payments on leases that had been signed but had not yet commenced.

Those are not typo-sized numbers.

They are the financial shadow cast by an industrial buildout.

At one site, CoreWeave disclosed that 393 megawatts of electrical power remained undelivered and was expected to arrive in phases. Elsewhere in the same filing, the company said it expected to commit substantial financial and operational resources—including long-term power contracts—before securing customer contracts for some future sites.

That sentence contains the capital cycle in miniature.

Commit first.

Monetize later.

The demand side was equally large. CoreWeave reported $103.7 billion of remaining performance obligations at June 30, 2026, with most expected to be recognized over the following several years. Contracted demand is precisely what makes infrastructure financeable.

But contracts do not abolish concentration.

In the second quarter of 2026, the company's top three customers accounted for roughly 36 percent, 26 percent, and 10 percent of revenue. The filing also described multi-billion-dollar commitments from large AI customers. Long contracts can therefore reduce one risk while increasing another: the capacity owner becomes more dependent on a small number of counterparties whose own AI spending plans are changing rapidly.

A reader trained on asset-light software might find this accounting alien.

An infrastructure investor would recognize the species immediately.

Debt.

Leases.

Long-duration customer commitments.

Construction schedules.

Power delivery.

Counterparty concentration.

Residual value.

The important thing is not to declare this structure reckless or brilliant from the outside. CoreWeave may execute extremely well. Customers may consume every unit they have reserved. Power may arrive on schedule. The company may earn excellent returns.

The point is that the business cannot be evaluated as if it were merely software with servers attached.

It is a duration transformation machine.

Capital is committed today against cash flows expected years from now in an industry where the underlying hardware, model architectures, customer economics, and price per unit of intelligence can all change dramatically before the contracts mature.

This creates a strange mixture of certainty and uncertainty.

The lease payment is certain.

The bond coupon is certain.

The exact economic usefulness of a 2026 accelerator fleet in 2029 is not.

The contract may be firm.

The customer's willingness to sign the same contract at renewal is not.

The megawatts may be reserved.

The date the utility actually delivers them may not be.

The backlog can therefore be enormous and the business can still remain exposed to forecast error.

That is not a contradiction.

It is infrastructure.

**A Balance Sheet Measured in Megawatts**

Traditional software companies teach investors to think in users, seats, retention, gross margin, and recurring revenue.

AI infrastructure requires a second vocabulary.

Megawatts.

Rack density.

Depreciation schedules.

Lease terms.

Power-delivery milestones.

Network topology.

Hardware generations.

Customer prepayments.

A useful exercise is to read an AI infrastructure filing and translate every major liability back into its physical counterpart.

An operating lease is not a line item. It is a building, equipment, and a calendar of future payments.

A capacity commitment is not merely backlog. It is a promise that somebody will be able to deliver usable compute when the customer expects it.

A power agreement is not an abstract procurement contract. It is a dependency on generators, wires, substations, transformers, permits, and the reliability of a local grid.

A depreciation charge is not accounting pessimism. It is recognition that a machine bought at the frontier is marching toward obsolescence whether or not it has earned its expected return.

This translation matters because financial statements can make industrial risk look cleaner than it feels on the ground.

Imagine a 2026 AI campus financed around a seven-year customer agreement. The building may last thirty years. The electrical infrastructure may last longer. The accelerators inside may be economically frontier-grade for only a fraction of that period.

The project therefore contains assets with radically different half-lives.

Concrete is slow.

Chips are fast.

Debt sits in the middle.

That mismatch is one of the defining financial facts of the AI buildout.

If the hardware is refreshed frequently, capital expenditure remains high even after the campus is complete. If it is not refreshed, the provider risks losing price-performance competitiveness. If the old hardware can be redeployed to lower-value workloads, residual value may be significant—but that market price will be determined by whatever newer machines can do by then.

The financier is not merely underwriting demand for AI.

The financier is underwriting the slope of technological progress.

**Utilization Is the Debt Covenant You Cannot Waive**

A lender may waive a financial covenant.

Physics will not waive utilization.

Suppose a capacity owner has fixed annual obligations of $1 billion across rent, interest, staffing, maintenance, and committed power. At 90 percent utilization, those costs are spread across a huge volume of billable work. At 60 percent utilization, each billable unit must carry a much larger share of the fixed burden.

Now introduce falling market prices.

Revenue per unit declines just as fixed cost per used unit rises.

This is the utilization trap in leveraged form.

It is why infrastructure booms can unravel quickly after long periods of apparent stability. The P&L is not linear. A modest decline in utilization can produce a disproportionate decline in cash flow.

**Off Balance Sheet, On Planet Earth**

Financial engineering can change presentation without changing reality.

If a company signs a long-term lease rather than owning a data center, the accounting may look different from direct capex. If a special-purpose vehicle owns the building, the legal entity may be separate. If an infrastructure fund provides capital, the technology company may preserve more cash today.

But someone still financed the steel.

Someone still owns the power contract.

Someone still expects a return.

Someone still bears residual-value risk.

The habit of describing obligations as “off balance sheet” can create a false sense that they are also off economy.

They are not.

The most useful analysis consolidates the system conceptually, even when accounting rules do not.

What capital has been committed to serve this expected demand?

What fixed payments must be made if demand disappoints?

Who has the right to walk away?

Who provided guarantees?

Which assets can be redeployed?

How quickly do those assets become obsolete?

What happens when the customer and the financier are exposed to each other elsewhere in the ecosystem?

These questions matter more than the label on the liability.

**Circular Confidence**

Booms become fragile when financing and demand begin to validate each other.

A supplier invests in a customer.

The customer uses the capital to buy the supplier's product.

The resulting revenue supports the supplier's valuation.

The supplier's rising valuation supports more financing.

None of this is necessarily improper. Strategic investments can be rational. Vendor financing can help customers adopt useful technology. Ecosystems often grow through cross-investment.

The risk is epistemic.

Revenue generated by a customer whose financing depends on the same ecosystem may be less independent evidence of demand than it appears.

The analyst must distinguish *end demand* from *financed demand*.

Who ultimately pays from cash generated outside the loop?

That question eventually arrives in every credit cycle.

**Debt Does Not Cause the Bubble**

It is tempting to blame leverage because leverage appears at the scene of so many disasters.

But debt is an amplifier, not the original forecast.

The forecast comes first.

We will need this much capacity.

Customers will pay this price.

The hardware will remain economically useful for this long.

The residual value will be at least this much.

Refinancing will be available.

Contracts will hold.

Power will arrive.

Leverage simply makes those assumptions binding.

The central lesson is therefore narrower and more powerful:

> Debt does not cause a bubble, but it converts a forecast error into a solvency event.

In an industry changing as quickly as AI, forecast error is not an edge case.

It is the base condition.

The only question is who has enough balance-sheet flexibility to survive being wrong.
