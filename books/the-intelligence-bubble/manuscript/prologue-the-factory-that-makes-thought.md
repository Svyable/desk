# The Factory That Makes Thought

At three in the morning, a data center under construction looks less like the future than a port before a storm.

Floodlights bleach the gravel. Trucks idle beside concrete walls. Steel racks arrive wrapped in plastic. Cable spools sit in rows like industrial millstones. Somewhere beyond the fence, a substation hums with the calm menace of a machine that cannot be persuaded by a slide deck. There are no chatbots here. No glowing orb. No synthetic voice promising to organize your life. There is mud, copper, coolant, transformers, switchgear, generators, fiber, concrete, and men in reflective jackets checking whether the electrical work is ready for equipment that will cost more than some skyscrapers.

The building is often called a cloud facility. This is a triumph of branding. Clouds are light. Clouds drift. Clouds do not require transmission upgrades, debt covenants, replacement parts, water negotiations, turbine queues, semiconductor fabs, or billions of dollars of depreciating hardware.

Artificial intelligence has a body.

Its body is industrial.

In 2024, data centers consumed roughly 415 terawatt-hours of electricity worldwide, according to the International Energy Agency, about 1.5 percent of global electricity consumption. The IEA's 2025 base case projected that demand could more than double to about 945 terawatt-hours by 2030. By 2026, the agency reported that data-center electricity use had surged 17 percent in a single year, while AI-focused facilities grew faster still. The important detail is not the exact forecast. Forecasts will be wrong. The important detail is the collision of two clocks: computing can be ordered on a technology schedule; power infrastructure arrives on an industrial schedule.

A model can improve in months. A transformer supply chain does not care.

This mismatch explains much of the mania now visible across the AI economy. Compute is not merely being purchased. Capacity is being secured in advance. Power is being reserved. Data-center campuses are being planned years before all the workloads that might occupy them are known. Companies are issuing debt, signing leases, raising equity, partnering with infrastructure funds, and building financial structures around the proposition that demand for machine intelligence will arrive quickly enough, at high enough prices, to justify the capital already committed.

It may.

But the wager is stranger than it first appears, because the commodity produced by these factories is getting cheaper.

The word *token* sounds like software accounting, and in one sense it is. Models receive and produce units of text, images, audio, code, and other representations. Providers price usage by the million tokens, by time, by compute, or by some bundled measure of work. But economically, the token is a useful symbol for something larger: a metered unit of machine cognition.

And its price has been collapsing.

Stanford's 2025 AI Index noted that the cost of querying a model at roughly GPT-3.5-level performance on one common benchmark fell from about twenty dollars per million tokens in late 2022 to seven cents by late 2024, a decline of more than 280-fold in roughly eighteen months. Different tasks show different curves. Frontier systems are not identical. Price is not the same as cost. Benchmarks can mislead. Yet the direction is unmistakable: hardware improves, software becomes more efficient, models get distilled, inference gets batched, quantization reduces resource needs, and competitors cut prices.

The AI industry is building some of the most capital-intensive infrastructure on Earth to manufacture a product whose unit economics are under relentless deflationary pressure.

This is not necessarily bad. It may be wonderful.

If intelligence becomes cheap, society gets more of it. A small manufacturer can afford engineering assistance that once required a staff. A student can have a tutor at midnight. A rural clinic can augment scarce expertise. A scientist can search wider hypothesis spaces. A one-person firm can perform work that once required a department. Falling prices can spread a technology more quickly than any marketing campaign.

But what is good for civilization is not automatically good for the seller.

Economics is full of gifts delivered by industries that disappointed their investors. Air travel became cheap and ubiquitous while airlines spent decades destroying capital. Fiber-optic networks became essential after a telecommunications bust. Railroads remade continents while waves of their securities failed. Solar power became dramatically cheaper while manufacturers endured brutal competition. Personal computers transformed work while many hardware makers vanished.

The public confuses adoption with profit because adoption is visible. The invisible part is the capital cycle.

Imagine two curves.

The first is demand for AI: rising steeply.

The second is capacity to supply AI: also rising steeply, pushed by extraordinary capital expenditure.

Now add a third curve: the cost of producing a given unit of useful intelligence. That curve is falling.

The resulting business outcomes depend on which curve moves fastest, how concentrated the suppliers remain, whether customers can switch, whether new applications create enough incremental demand, and whether the owners of fixed infrastructure can keep utilization high while technology generations turn over beneath them.

This is why the central question of the AI era is not whether machines become more intelligent.

They will.

It is not whether people use more AI.

They almost certainly will.

The defining question is who earns the returns after intelligence becomes abundant.

By the middle of 2026, the scale of the wager was difficult to overstate. Alphabet told investors it expected 2026 capital expenditures of roughly $180 billion to $190 billion, about six times its 2022 level, and raised nearly $50 billion of equity in June while also issuing debt. Meta guided to $130 billion to $145 billion of 2026 capital expenditures. Microsoft reported quarterly capital expenditures of $41 billion in its fiscal fourth quarter, with roughly two-thirds devoted to shorter-lived assets such as CPUs and GPUs. Amazon's trailing free cash flow had turned negative as property and equipment purchases surged, even as AWS growth accelerated.

These are not weak companies desperately borrowing against vapor. They are among the strongest cash-generating corporations ever created. That fact is a bull argument—and a warning.

When rich incumbents all race to build the same new capacity, the relevant historical analogy is not a fraud. It is a capital cycle.

A capital cycle begins with genuine scarcity. Scarcity produces high returns. High returns attract investment. Investment expands capacity. Expanded capacity reduces scarcity. Prices fall. Returns normalize. If capital arrives slowly, the transition can be healthy. If capital arrives all at once, financed by optimistic assumptions and long-lived commitments, the cycle can overshoot.

This book is about the possibility that artificial intelligence is entering the overshoot phase before most people have learned to see the buildout as a capital cycle at all.

The irony is that the better the technology becomes, the more severe the economic test may be.

Inside the data center, a row of racks waits for accelerators. Outside, the power contract runs for years. The lease runs for years. The debt has a maturity date. The chips will be obsolete sooner. The customers are negotiating lower prices. The models are improving. The financiers have built spreadsheets in which utilization rises smoothly toward the top right corner.

Every bubble has a sentence people repeat because it is true.

Railroads will connect the country.

The internet will change commerce.

Housing is essential.

Smartphones will put a computer in every pocket.

AI will transform the economy.

The danger begins when a true sentence is mistaken for a complete investment thesis.
