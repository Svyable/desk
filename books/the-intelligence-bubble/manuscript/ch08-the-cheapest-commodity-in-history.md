# The Cheapest Commodity in History

Civilization has spent most of its history trying to make physical things cheaper.

Food.

Energy.

Transport.

Steel.

Light.

Communication.

Computation.

Artificial intelligence adds a stranger target: cognition.

Not consciousness. Not wisdom. Not judgment in the deepest human sense. But many useful cognitive operations—classification, translation, summarization, drafting, pattern recognition, coding, search, planning, simulation, explanation—can increasingly be purchased from machines.

The unit cost is falling with astonishing speed.

We are witnessing the industrialization of portions of thought.

**What Is a Token Worth?**

Technically, a token is a unit into which model inputs and outputs are segmented.

Economically, it is better to think of tokens as metered machine work.

The comparison is imperfect. One token can be trivial while another sits inside a valuable decision. A million tokens of low-quality chatter are not equivalent to a concise answer that prevents a million-dollar error. Different modalities consume different resources. Reasoning systems may use hidden computation beyond visible output.

Still, token prices reveal a powerful fact: the market price of generic model usage tends to fall as capability diffuses.

Stanford's AI Index captured one dramatic version of the curve. By late 2024, the cost of accessing performance comparable to GPT-3.5 on a commonly used benchmark had fallen more than 280-fold from late 2022. Hardware price-performance improved. Smaller models became more capable. Inference stacks improved. Competition widened.

A cost curve like that changes behavior.

Tasks that were absurd to automate become cheap enough to try.

Features that required a premium subscription become default.

Developers stop asking, “Can we afford to call the model?” and start asking, “Why are we not calling the model here too?”

This is how abundance arrives.

**Six Engines of Deflation**

AI inference costs fall through several mechanisms at once.

**Hardware improvement.** New accelerators deliver more useful computation per dollar and per watt.

**Model efficiency.** Better architectures can reach similar quality with fewer parameters, fewer operations, or more efficient attention and routing.

**Quantization.** Lower-precision representations reduce memory and compute requirements while preserving adequate quality for many workloads.

**Distillation.** Smaller models learn from larger ones, bringing useful capability into cheaper forms.

**Batching, caching, and systems optimization.** Providers improve how workloads share hardware, reuse common context, and avoid redundant computation.

**Competition.** Even when underlying costs stay flat, providers may pass savings to customers or cut margins to gain market share.

These forces compound.

The output of one generation becomes the training data, benchmark, target, or architectural inspiration for the next. Hardware improves while software learns to use hardware more efficiently. Models improve while developers learn which tasks do not need the best model.

The customer does not experience one deflation curve.

The customer experiences a stack of them.

**Better and Cheaper Are Different Revolutions**

Technology commentary tends to focus on better.

The new model writes better code.

The new model reasons more reliably.

The new model handles video.

The new model uses tools.

But cheaper can matter more.

A model that is 20 percent better may delight enthusiasts.

A model that is 90 percent cheaper can reorganize an industry.

Cost determines whether intelligence is a feature or a substrate.

At high cost, an AI system is reserved for valuable requests.

At low cost, it runs continuously.

It reads every document.

It watches every process.

It drafts alternatives automatically.

It monitors equipment.

It translates every interaction.

It generates thousands of simulations before a human sees the best five.

The world changes not when machine intelligence becomes perfect, but when it becomes cheap enough to waste.

Electric light became transformative when illumination could be left on.

Computation became transformative when programmers stopped counting every instruction.

Bandwidth became transformative when people streamed video without thinking about the bytes.

AI may cross the same threshold when cognition is no longer rationed.

**Deflation Does Not Mean Less Compute**

There is an apparent contradiction at the heart of the AI economy.

The price of useful intelligence can fall while total spending on computation rises.

Stanford's 2026 AI Index estimated that global AI compute capacity had grown at roughly 3.3 times per year since 2022, reaching the equivalent of about 17.1 million H100-class accelerators. At the same time, the prior year's Index documented extraordinary declines in the price required to reach a fixed level of benchmark performance.

Both curves can continue.

That is not paradoxical once the unit of analysis is separated.

A factory can produce vastly more steel while the price per ton falls.

A telecom network can carry exponentially more bits while the price per bit collapses.

A semiconductor industry can ship more computation every year while the cost per operation falls.

AI can consume more electricity, deploy more chips, and generate more tokens while the price of a standardized cognitive task declines.

This distinction matters because different investors own different curves.

The hardware supplier may benefit from the aggregate volume curve.

The model provider may suffer from the price-per-task curve.

The application company may benefit from both: more capability available at lower unit cost.

The infrastructure owner can sit awkwardly between them, enjoying rising workload volumes while facing continual pressure to refresh equipment and lower price.

When people say, “AI demand is exploding,” ask which demand.

Demand for chips?

Demand for megawatt-hours?

Demand for tokens?

Demand for completed business outcomes?

Those quantities can all rise while the profit pool migrates from one layer to another.

**The Producer-Margin Problem**

Abundance is wonderful for customers.

It is ambiguous for producers.

Suppose the cost of serving a request falls from one dollar to ten cents. If the provider keeps the price at one dollar, margins explode.

But competitors notice.

One charges fifty cents.

Another charges twenty.

An open-weight model makes the service available at near-infrastructure cost.

A cloud platform bundles the capability into a broader contract.

A software company gives it away to protect its application franchise.

The customer learns to route requests across suppliers.

Eventually the ten-cent cost may support a twelve-cent market price.

The customer captures most of the efficiency gain.

This is what competition is supposed to do.

The economic surplus from innovation does not necessarily remain with the inventor.

**Intelligence as a Commodity—and Not a Commodity**

Calling intelligence a commodity is provocative because models are plainly differentiated.

Some are better at coding.

Some reason better.

Some are faster.

Some are safer for regulated environments.

Some handle long contexts.

Some are cheaper.

Some can run locally.

Some integrate tightly with particular tools.

A useful commodity argument does not claim that every model is identical. It claims that for a growing set of tasks, multiple suppliers may become good enough.

Commoditization happens at the margin.

If five models can summarize a meeting adequately, the buyer gains power even if one model is still the global frontier.

If three models can draft a routine contract clause, the premium for the fourth collapses on that workload.

If a local model can classify support tickets cheaply, the enterprise no longer cares that a remote frontier model performs five points better on an unrelated benchmark.

The market fragments into capability tiers.

Only the difficult tail remains scarce.

**The Deflation Test**

This gives us one of the book's central frameworks.

Ask of any AI business:

> If the cost of intelligence falls by 90 percent, does the company earn more, earn less, or become irrelevant?

**Earns more.** These businesses use cheap intelligence as an input and capture value elsewhere. Workflow owners, distributors, marketplaces, and companies selling high-value outcomes can benefit enormously.

**Earns less.** These businesses sell undifferentiated intelligence or capacity while carrying fixed costs. Falling unit prices threaten margins unless volume grows faster.

**Becomes irrelevant.** These businesses exist mainly because they had temporary privileged access to a model, a prompt trick, or an integration that becomes native elsewhere.

The test is crude.

That is why it is useful.

It forces the analyst to imagine success in the technology and then ask whether the business survives success.

**The Greatest Gift, the Hardest Business**

Cheap intelligence may be one of the great economic gifts of the century.

It can democratize access to expertise, compress the cost of experimentation, and increase the productive range of individuals and small firms. It may help scientists, teachers, doctors, lawyers, engineers, artists, and operators perform more work at higher quality.

But gifts to society are often produced by brutal industries.

The more abundant intelligence becomes, the harder it may be to charge monopoly-like prices for raw access to it.

The winners will therefore be found not only among those who make intelligence.

They will be found among those who know what to do when intelligence is nearly free.
