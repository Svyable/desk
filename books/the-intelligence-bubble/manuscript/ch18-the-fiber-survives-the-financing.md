# The Fiber Survives the Financing

In early 2001, the telecommunications boom had already begun to turn, but the physical buildout had not received the message.

New fiber was still going into the ground. Networks planned during the years of easy money were still being completed. Equipment ordered under one set of assumptions was arriving into another. A contemporary industry estimate, later quoted by economists at the Federal Reserve Bank of Richmond, put the amount of new inter-city fiber then on the way at nearly 600,000 miles.

The problem was not that people had stopped communicating.

They were communicating more.

The problem was that the industry had built capacity faster than the financial claims on that capacity could be justified.

That distinction is the reason the telecom bust belongs in a book about artificial intelligence.

The late-1990s telecommunications boom is often remembered as part of the dot-com mania: a supporting cast of fiber companies, competitive carriers, equipment makers, and debt-financed network builders surrounding the more photogenic internet startups. But the telecom story is useful precisely because so much of the boom was physical. Companies raised capital and turned it into trenches, conduit, switches, routers, rights of way, optical equipment, long-haul routes, metropolitan networks, and glass fiber.

The money did not disappear into a website.

It became infrastructure.

Then much of the money disappeared anyway.

That is the unsettling possibility at the center of an AI capital cycle. A society can end up with useful infrastructure and disappointing investment returns at the same time. The physical success of the build does not rescue the price paid for it. In some cases the success makes the old price harder to defend because abundance destroys the scarcity on which the original return depended.

The telecom boom shows how that happens.

A strand of fiber is not a fixed quantity of bandwidth. The glass is only part of the system. Improvements in the equipment attached to it can increase how much information the same physical strand carries. The Richmond Fed's retrospective on the boom noted that a strand transmitting about 2.5 gigabits per second in 1996 could, by 2000, carry roughly 100 gigabits per second through advances in optical multiplexing. More wavelengths could be sent through the same fiber, and each wavelength could carry more information.

The capacity of the network therefore increased in two ways at once.

Companies laid more fiber.

Technology made each fiber more productive.

This is a wonderful outcome if you are a user of bandwidth.

It is dangerous if your business plan assumes bandwidth will remain scarce.

Imagine underwriting a warehouse while the usable square footage of every competing warehouse is multiplying without new construction. Demand can grow rapidly and the market can still be oversupplied because the productive capacity of the existing asset base is improving faster.

That was one of the surprises in long-haul telecommunications.

It is also one of the risks in AI.

The AI equivalent is not hard to see. The industry can build more data centers while software makes each accelerator more useful. Better quantization can reduce memory requirements. Better inference engines can improve throughput. Batching and caching can reduce the resources required per request. Model architectures can become more efficient. Smaller systems can become good enough for tasks that once required a frontier model. New chips can deliver more useful work per watt and per dollar.

Demand for intelligence can rise spectacularly while the amount of expensive capacity required for a particular unit of useful work falls.

That is not a contradiction.

It is the mechanism by which abundance arrives.

The telecom builders faced a version of the same arithmetic. They were not foolish to believe internet traffic would grow. Internet traffic did grow. Telecommunications consumption grew. New services appeared. The mistake was often in the conversion from a true technology thesis into a financial thesis about how much capacity should be built, how quickly, with what leverage, and at what future price.

By late 2000 the signs had become difficult to ignore. The Richmond Fed researchers describe a long-haul market in significant overcapacity as the broader economy weakened. Capacity prices were falling. Customers that had been expected to absorb new supply were themselves failing. Competitive carriers that had promised to break open local markets had gained less share than the most exuberant forecasts implied.

A boom built on projected scarcity encountered technological abundance and slower-than-expected commercial absorption at the same time.

There is a temptation, with twenty-five years of hindsight, to describe the outcome as obvious.

It was not obvious while the capital was being committed.

That matters because investment decisions are made without the final chart.

Early in a boom, each new project can look individually rational. Traffic is growing. Customers want more capacity. Financing is available. Competitors are building. Equipment is improving. Regulators are encouraging competition. Equity markets reward expansion. If a company refuses to build, it may lose the route, the customer, the market window, or the valuation premium that makes future financing possible.

The industry can therefore create an outcome that few participants would have chosen if they could coordinate: too much capacity arriving at roughly the same time.

Nobody needs to believe an absurd demand forecast.

They need only believe that their project will be one of the winners.

This is how a capital cycle becomes crowded.

The visible demand signal attracts supply. The supply response has a lag. During that lag, the original demand story can strengthen, making the buildout look even safer. Then the projects arrive together. Prices adjust before the physical assets disappear.

In telecom, the adjustment was brutal.

The communications industry lost nearly $20 billion in 2001 according to the Richmond Fed retrospective. Companies failed. Equity values collapsed. Debt was impaired. Equipment makers cut jobs. Networks changed hands. Global Crossing filed for bankruptcy in 2002. WorldCom filed for bankruptcy protection in July of that year after an accounting scandal that required massive restatements and led to criminal cases against executives.

WorldCom deserves care in any historical analogy because fraud was central to its collapse. It would be wrong to use the company as proof that ordinary overcapacity alone destroys a network builder. Accounting misconduct can turn a difficult industry into a criminal enterprise, and the causal stories should not be blended for rhetorical convenience.

The reason WorldCom still belongs in the landscape is narrower. Its bankruptcy shows how little the usefulness of a communications network guarantees the survival of the financial structure placed on top of it. The network kept carrying traffic while the corporation beneath the ticker symbol went through bankruptcy. Customers still needed communication. The claims on the assets had to be reorganized.

The service survived the security.

That phrase is worth remembering.

Investors often talk about a company and its technology as if they are the same object. They are not.

A technology can persist through bankruptcy.

A factory can keep producing after its equity is wiped out.

A railroad can run after bondholders take losses.

A fiber route can carry traffic under a new owner at a lower cost basis.

A data center can host servers after the financing vehicle that built it has been restructured.

A model can remain useful after the company that trained it is acquired, recapitalized, or forced to change its economics.

The physical or technical asset and the financial claim are different layers.

Bubbles become confusing when those layers are treated as one.

The telecom bust did not reverse the demand for communication. The Richmond Fed's numbers make the point almost painfully clear. Real consumption of telecommunications services rose from roughly $88 billion in 1995 to about $151 billion in 2001, measured in constant 1996 dollars. Telecom consumption grew faster than total personal consumption before and during the boom.

The customers were there.

So were the losses.

That combination is the entire thesis of this book in miniature.

Strong demand does not guarantee strong supplier returns when capacity, competition, financing, and technology are moving at the same time.

The price data tell the second half of the story. Long-distance telephone prices fell materially from the late 1990s into the early 2000s. Wireless prices also declined sharply. Users received more communications capability at lower prices while parts of the industry that had financed the expansion suffered.

The social value of the network and the private return on the original capital diverged.

That divergence is not an accident at the edge of capitalism. It is one of the ways technological progress becomes cheap enough to spread.

A new owner buying an asset out of distress does not inherit the old owner's required return.

Suppose a network cost ten billion dollars to build and was financed on the assumption that scarcity prices would persist. If the original capital structure fails and the productive assets are sold for four billion, the new owner can charge less and still earn an acceptable return on the lower basis. Nothing about the fiber has to improve during the bankruptcy for the economics of using it to improve.

Finance has reduced the economy's cost basis.

The original investors supplied part of the subsidy.

That sentence sounds cruel because it is.

Losses are not abstract. Shareholders can lose savings. Pension funds can take write-downs. Workers can lose jobs. Suppliers can go unpaid. Communities that expected tax revenue can be left with unfinished projects. A restructuring can transfer value through pain.

There is no need to celebrate the process.

But refusing to describe it does not make it disappear.

Overbuilt infrastructure can become more socially useful after the financial claims on it are marked down.

This is the part of a bubble that a technology optimist should understand most clearly.

If AI infrastructure is overbuilt, that does not imply the infrastructure was useless.

It may imply that the first owners paid too much for it.

A rack of accelerators bought under peak scarcity pricing can still perform useful inference after the revenue assumptions attached to it fail. A powered data-center shell can still host future hardware after its first tenant renegotiates. Transmission upgrades built for a wave of large loads can serve other customers. Fiber installed to connect AI campuses can carry traffic long after the specific model economics that justified the original project have changed.

The asset can be durable even when the thesis that financed it is not.

There are limits to the analogy.

Fiber has unusually long physical life. Accelerators age quickly. A strand of glass installed decades ago can remain useful as the electronics on either end improve. A GPU generation can lose relative economic value in a few years if newer hardware offers much better performance per watt or per dollar. Data centers also depend on cooling, power density, networking, and physical designs that can become less suitable as hardware changes.

AI infrastructure therefore contains a mixture of durations.

Land can be long lived.

Power connections can be long lived.

Buildings can be long lived if adaptable.

Network fiber can be long lived.

Transformers and substations can be long lived.

Accelerators can be short lived.

The software stack can change monthly.

The model itself may lose pricing power faster than any of them.

That mixture makes residual value harder to underwrite than a simple “data centers are the new railroads” story suggests.

An investor should ask which layer survives the financing.

The answer may differ inside the same project.

A lender secured by land, utility infrastructure, and a flexible building has a different claim from a lender economically dependent on the resale value of a particular accelerator fleet. A landlord with a twenty-year lease to a strong tenant has different exposure from a compute provider selling short-term capacity against long-term hardware financing. A utility that earns a regulated return on infrastructure has different economics from a developer that needs a single customer to take the full load.

The word infrastructure conceals these differences.

Telecom teaches us to open the box.

The other lesson is that demand forecasts are dangerous when they ignore supply productivity.

In the 1990s, network builders could point to genuine growth in internet and telecommunications use. What mattered was not only how much traffic would grow, but how much traffic each dollar of network could carry by the time new capacity arrived.

AI investors face the same denominator problem.

It is not enough to forecast the number of tokens, agents, users, or AI-assisted tasks.

You also need a view on the cost per useful task.

If model capability improves while inference efficiency rises, the same economic output may require fewer premium tokens than a straight-line forecast suggests. If open models narrow the gap with proprietary systems, price can fall even while usage rises. If enterprises route routine work to smaller models and reserve frontier systems for difficult cases, total AI activity can expand without frontier-model revenue scaling proportionally.

The numerator can be right and the investment can still be wrong because the denominator moved faster.

Telecom's capacity expansion was partly the result of competitive overbuilding and partly the result of technology making the installed base more productive. AI can experience both at once too: more campuses, more accelerators, more model providers, and more useful work per unit of compute.

That combination is wonderful for users.

It is exactly what a scarcity multiple should fear.

There is one more reason the fiber story matters.

After the bust, the internet did not become less important.

It became less exotic.

Cheap bandwidth enabled companies whose business models did not need to own the long-haul network. Video became practical. Cloud services expanded. Software could be delivered continuously. Businesses could move data among offices, customers, and data centers at scales that would have been expensive under the old scarcity assumptions.

The application layer benefited from infrastructure whose first financial chapter had ended badly.

This does not mean every later internet success can be credited to distressed fiber. Causality is distributed across better computers, storage, wireless networks, software, standards, entrepreneurship, and many other changes. The narrower point is enough: lower communication costs widened the set of businesses that could be built on top of the network.

AI abundance could follow the same pattern.

A model company may hate a 90 percent decline in inference prices.

A customer building an AI-intensive product may love it.

A compute provider may hate surplus capacity.

A startup with no balance sheet may rent it cheaply.

A creditor may hate restructuring.

The next owner may discover that the asset finally works at the new purchase price.

This is why the question “Is AI a bubble?” is too crude to be useful.

A bubble is not a verdict on the thing being built.

It is a statement about the relationship between capital supplied, expectations embedded in prices, and cash flows ultimately available to the claims that financed the build.

Telecommunications at the turn of the century was real technology, real infrastructure, real demand, real competition, real overcapacity, and real financial destruction.

All of those statements can be true together.

Artificial intelligence does not need to repeat the sequence exactly. The industry structures differ. The assets have different lives. The largest AI investors today include highly profitable companies capable of self-financing enormous capital programs, not only fragile venture-backed entrants or debt-heavy carriers. Some demand may arrive faster than telecom demand did. Some bottlenecks, especially power and advanced chips, may keep parts of the system scarce longer than bandwidth remained scarce.

History is not a script.

It is a way to notice mechanisms before they become obvious in your own cycle.

The mechanism here is simple.

Capital sees scarcity and builds.

Technology increases capacity.

Competition adds more capacity.

Demand grows, but not always fast enough to preserve the old price.

The financial claims are repriced.

The useful assets remain.

New users inherit a cheaper platform.

The original investors can be both essential to the technological transformation and disappointed by their returns.

That is not failure in the ordinary sense.

It is a transfer from the people who financed scarcity to the people who arrive after abundance.

The fiber survived the financing.

Some of the machines that make intelligence may do the same.
