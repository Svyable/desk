# The Technology Can Win While Investors Lose

In September 1873, one of the most important banking houses in the United States closed its doors.

Jay Cooke & Company had helped finance the Union during the Civil War. Its name carried the kind of authority that a century and a half later might belong to a great investment bank, a sovereign fund, or a technology platform whose logo had become shorthand for inevitability. Yet Cooke had become deeply entangled with the Northern Pacific Railway, a vast project intended to connect the Great Lakes with Puget Sound.

**The Bond House That Sold the Future**

Before the panic, Jay Cooke had been in the business of making distant promises feel tangible.

During the Civil War, his firm had become famous for selling government bonds to ordinary Americans. Cooke understood something that every great promoter understands: finance is partly the art of converting an abstraction about the future into an object a person can buy today.

The Northern Pacific required the same trick on a continental scale.

The proposed railway would run from Lake Superior toward Puget Sound across country that, from the perspective of an eastern bond buyer, was almost mythic in its distance. Much of the route still lacked the traffic that would someday be needed to support it. The economic proposition therefore ran forward in time. Build the line, the promoters argued, and settlement would follow. Settlement would create farms, towns, freight, tourism, land sales, and therefore traffic. Traffic would validate the bonds that had financed the line that had attracted the traffic.

It was an infrastructure flywheel before anyone used the term.

The bond marketing was not merely selling rails and ties. It was selling the future population of the American Northwest.

There is a recognizable structure here. The asset was costly and largely fixed. The demand case depended on a transformation the asset itself was supposed to accelerate. The financing arrived before the mature cash flows. Promoters pointed to a vast addressable market. The underlying technology had already proved itself elsewhere. And the social value of completing the network was much easier to imagine than the returns on a particular security issued at a particular moment.

This is the point at which technological history becomes investment history.

According to the Library of Congress, construction pushed the Northern Pacific to Bismarck by 1873, while costs mounted and the company's bonds became harder to place. Federal Reserve histories of the panic emphasize the broader pattern: railway investment had expanded rapidly, returns were declining, and refinancing became increasingly difficult. When European investors pulled back from American railway securities, the system lost one of the sources of capital required to keep the expansion moving.

On September 18, 1873, Jay Cooke & Company suspended payments.

The failure was not a verdict on rail transportation. It was a verdict on the capital structure supporting one phase of rail transportation.

That distinction is the seed of this entire book.

A railway can be useful and overfinanced.

A fiber network can be necessary and overbuilt.

A data center can be full of astonishing machines and still have been financed at a price that the eventual cash flows cannot justify.

When a new technology changes what is possible, financiers must estimate a future that has no stable historical base rate. That invites two opposite errors. Skeptics can underestimate the technology because they anchor to the old world. Enthusiasts can overestimate the returns because they confuse the size of the new world with the share of it that any one capital provider will own.

The first error misses revolutions.

The second funds them for everyone else.

The railroad was not a stupid idea.

That is the first thing to understand.

The United States needed railroads. Railroads lowered transport costs, linked farms to cities, increased land values, accelerated migration, created national markets, and helped turn a continental republic into an industrial power. The thesis that railroads would transform America was spectacularly correct.

The securities issued to finance every railroad were another matter.

The Federal Reserve's history of the Panic of 1873 describes the mechanism with unusual clarity: investment expanded rapidly; projects began to outpace demand for new capacity; returns declined; European investors sold American railroad securities; financing tightened; leveraged companies struggled to refinance; failures spread from railroads to their bankers.

The technology won.

Many investors lost.

This pattern is so common that it deserves to be treated as a basic law of technological capitalism. The faster a new system changes the world, the more capital it attracts. The more capital it attracts, the greater the probability that investment will outrun the ability of the system to earn adequate returns on every dollar deployed.

The problem is not optimism. The problem is competition acting on optimism.

Suppose a new technology opens a market with unusually high returns. The first firms enjoy scarcity pricing. Investors observe strong demand and extrapolate it. Competitors raise money. Suppliers expand. Infrastructure is ordered. Talent floods in. Governments add incentives because the technology looks strategic. Lenders become comfortable because collateral values are rising and recent growth looks exceptional. Analysts build valuation models that incorporate long periods of elevated margins.

Then capitalism performs its most reliable trick: it attacks the high returns.

High returns are a beacon. They tell every ambitious rival where to point capital.

The most important mistake in investing is therefore not failing to identify a great technology. It is failing to distinguish a great technology from a great security at a given price.

Those are different disciplines.

A technologist asks: what can this do?

An entrepreneur asks: what can we build with it?

A customer asks: what is it worth to me?

An investor must ask one more question: what portion of that value will remain with the owners of this asset after competitors, suppliers, employees, lenders, customers, governments, and substitutes take their shares?

That final question is less exciting. It is also where the money is.

**The Internet Did Not Need Pets.com to Survive**

The late 1990s produced another version of the confusion. The internet was obviously important before the stock market peak. Email worked. Web traffic was exploding. Corporate networks were spreading. Software distribution was changing. Search, e-commerce, online advertising, and digital media were emerging in recognizable form.

Investors did not need to be fools to be excited.

They needed only to make the subtle mistake of assuming that because the internet would become larger, the companies most associated with the internet in 1999 would necessarily become more valuable from their 1999 prices.

Some did. Many did not.

The survivors were not merely the companies with the most fashionable stories. They were firms that eventually constructed durable economic positions: marketplaces with liquidity, software with switching costs, platforms with distribution, networks with scale, businesses capable of generating cash after competition arrived.

The wreckage mattered because the wreckage did not invalidate the technology. After the Nasdaq collapse, consumers did not stop using the web. Companies did not uninstall TCP/IP. Fiber did not climb back out of the ground. The underlying infrastructure became cheaper, more available, and more useful.

The crash was, in part, a transfer.

Capital owners absorbed losses. Future users inherited lower-cost infrastructure.

This is one of the most important ways a bubble can subsidize progress.

Investors pay for excess capacity. Society later buys services from the wreckage at cents on the dollar.

**Demand Is Not the Same as Economic Rent**

The AI investment case is often presented as a demand case.

More people will use AI.

More enterprises will deploy AI.

Agents will consume more tokens than chatbots.

Scientific workloads will expand.

Software will be rewritten around models.

Robots will require inference.

All of these claims may be correct. None, by itself, establishes who earns excess returns.

A product can have enormous demand and poor supplier economics. Commodity markets make this obvious. Oil is essential, but a high-cost oil producer can still fail. Steel is indispensable, but a steel mill built at the top of a cycle can destroy capital. Shipping carries world trade, yet shipowners repeatedly endure brutal cycles because a surge in freight rates triggers orders for more vessels, which arrive after the shortage has passed.

What matters is not demand in isolation but the relation among demand, supply, price, cost, capital intensity, and competitive structure.

AI makes the problem harder because supply is layered.

There is supply of electricity.

Supply of powered data-center shells.

Supply of accelerators.

Supply of cloud capacity.

Supply of model capability.

Supply of developer tools.

Supply of workflow software.

Supply of expert outcomes.

A shortage in one layer can create extraordinary profits there while the next layer suffers. When the shortage moves, the profits move with it.

In the first phase of a technological boom, investors often confuse a bottleneck with a moat.

A bottleneck is a temporary constraint.

A moat is a structural reason competitors cannot easily take your economics.

The distinction is worth billions.

**The Price You Pay Is Part of the Business**

A brilliant company can be a bad investment because valuation is not decoration added after the business analysis. Valuation is part of the business analysis.

Imagine a company capable of growing revenue 40 percent a year for five years. That sounds extraordinary. Now imagine the stock is priced as if revenue will grow 50 percent, margins will expand, capital intensity will decline, competition will remain limited, and the company will possess a durable terminal franchise fifteen years from now.

If the company merely performs extraordinarily, the stock can fall.

This is the psychological trap of elite growth investing: when expectations are high enough, reality can be excellent and still disappoint.

The higher the valuation, the less the investor is buying the current business and the more the investor is buying a specific future.

In AI, that future often contains several hidden assumptions:

- that current scarcity will persist;
- that model leaders will remain leaders;
- that customers will not gain bargaining power;
- that falling compute costs will expand margins rather than be passed through in price;
- that capex will produce durable assets rather than rapidly depreciating equipment;
- that utilization will remain high;
- that regulation will not change economics materially;
- that the cost of capital will stay cooperative;
- and that competitive entry will not redistribute value to customers.

Any one of those assumptions can be reasonable. It is the combination that becomes dangerous.

**The Rule of Revolutionary Technologies**

The first principle of this book is simple:

> A revolutionary technology can be a terrible investment at the wrong price.

There is a companion principle:

> The collapse of an investment boom does not prove that the technology was a fad.

Together, these principles let us escape the most tedious argument in modern markets: whether one is “bullish” or “bearish” on AI.

The labels are too crude.

One can be bullish on AI adoption, bearish on undifferentiated model margins, bullish on enterprise workflow software, cautious on leveraged infrastructure, bullish on power equipment, skeptical of scarcity premiums, enthusiastic about robotics, and unwilling to pay a heroic multiple for any of it.

That is not inconsistency.

It is analysis.

At the end of the nineteenth century, America did not conclude that railroads had been a mistake. It reorganized ownership, wrote down claims, consolidated networks, and kept the trains running.

The tracks survived the securities.

The same possibility should sit at the center of our thinking about artificial intelligence.

The models may improve long after the multiples do not.
