# Chapter 18 — residual-value research note

## Federal Reserve Bank of Richmond — *Boom and Bust in Telecommunications* (Fall 2003)

Source: https://www.richmondfed.org/publications/research/economic_quarterly/2003/fall/couperhejkalwolman

Use: historical backbone for the telecom capital-cycle comparison. The Richmond Fed describes the sector's boom beginning in 1997 and dramatic bust beginning in 2000, emphasizing technological and regulatory change, uncertain forecasts, long-haul overcapacity, falling capacity prices, and the simultaneous improvement in fiber productivity.

Falsification / limitation: telecom is an analogy, not a forecast. AI assets have heterogeneous lives, and today's largest buyers/builders include firms with much stronger balance sheets than many competitive telecom carriers had.

## Federal Reserve Bank of Richmond — *Beyond the Turmoil* (Winter 2003)

Source: https://www.richmondfed.org/publications/research/econ_focus/2003/winter/cover

Use: concrete evidence for post-boom repricing and reuse. The Richmond Fed reported a long-haul connection priced at about $590,000 per year in 2001 and $194,000 in 2002, and described opportunistic carriers using abundant long-haul capacity to expand at lower cost.

Falsification / limitation: one quoted route is illustrative rather than a complete market price index. It should support the mechanism of repricing, not a claim that all telecom capacity fell by the same percentage.

## Federal Communications Commission — Digital Teleport bankruptcy asset transfer, DA 03-1289 (2003)

Source: https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DA-03-1289A1.pdf

Use: primary-source example of functioning telecom infrastructure changing hands through a bankruptcy-court process. The FCC described the proposed acquisition of Digital Teleport's core domestic telecommunications assets, including a network spanning thousands of route miles, with the buyer continuing operations.

Falsification / limitation: the filing establishes the transaction and intended continued operation; it does not by itself establish the buyer's eventual investment return or prove that distressed acquisitions generally succeed.
