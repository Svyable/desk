# Chapter 17 research — The Missing Complement

Research checked for the chapter-scoped drafting pass on September 1, 2026.

## Generative AI at Work

- Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li, and Lindsey R. Raymond, “Generative AI at Work,” *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 140(2), 2025; working-paper record and DOI: https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161
- Use in chapter: field evidence from a staggered rollout to more than 5,000 customer-support agents. The published version reports roughly 15% average productivity improvement, with materially larger gains for less experienced/lower-skilled agents and much smaller gains for the most experienced workers.
- Discipline: this is evidence for one workflow and one organizational implementation, not a universal productivity coefficient for generative AI.

## U.S. Census Bureau — 2026 AI supplement

- Kathryn Bonney, Cory Breaux, Emin Dinlersoz, Lucia Foster, John Haltiwanger, and Aditya Pande, “The Microstructure of AI Diffusion: Evidence from Firms, Business Functions, and Worker Tasks,” CES Working Paper 26-25, April 2026: https://www.census.gov/library/working-papers/2026/adrm/CES-WP-26-25.html
- Current BTOS data page: https://www.census.gov/hfp/btos/data
- Use in chapter: during the November 2025–January 2026 reference period, 18% of firms used AI in a business function and 32% did so on an employment-weighted basis; 57% of adopting firms used AI in three or fewer business functions; 66% used AI solely to augment tasks; AI-related employment decreases were reported by about 2% of firms.
- Discipline: Census revised the core AI-use question in November 2025. Do not compare the revised series mechanically with earlier BTOS readings without accounting for the wording change.

## Firm Data on AI

- Ivan Yotzov, Jose Maria Barrero, Nicholas Bloom, Philip Bunn, Steven J. Davis, Kevin M. Foster, Aaron Jalca, Brent H. Meyer, Paul Mizen, Michael A. Navarrete, Pawel Smietanka, Gregory Thwaites, and Ben Zhe Wang, “Firm Data on AI,” Stanford Institute for Economic Policy Research working paper, February 2026: https://siepr.stanford.edu/publications/working-paper/firm-data-ai
- Use in chapter: representative executive surveys across the United States, United Kingdom, Germany, and Australia found widespread reported AI use but little reported realized impact so far; more than 80% of firms reported no employment or productivity impact over the preceding three years, while respondents forecast an average 1.4% productivity increase over the next three years.
- Discipline: definitions and samples differ from the Census BTOS, so the adoption percentages should not be treated as competing estimates of one identical quantity. The chapter uses the study for the gap between current reported impact and expected future impact, not for a cross-survey adoption ranking.

## Productivity J-curve

- Erik Brynjolfsson, Daniel Rock, and Chad Syverson, “The Productivity J-Curve: How Intangibles Complement General Purpose Technologies,” *American Economic Journal: Macroeconomics* 13(1), 2021; working-paper record: https://www.nber.org/papers/w25148
- Use in chapter: general-purpose technologies can require substantial complementary investments in process redesign, products, business models, software, and human capital. Those intangible investments can precede measured output gains.
- Discipline: the J-curve is a framework and historical empirical result, not proof that current generative-AI spending will eventually pay off. The chapter explicitly preserves the possibilities of hype, failed implementation, and investment that never earns a return.

## Falsification note

The chapter’s mechanism would be weakened if broad firm-level evidence showed that merely enabling AI access reliably produced large, durable productivity gains without complementary workflow, data, training, or governance changes. Current evidence instead shows heterogeneous task-level gains, shallow breadth at many adopting firms, and a substantial gap between reported use and reported realized firm-level impact. That evidence is consistent with the chapter’s claim but does not prove that every organization requires the same complements or adoption lag.
