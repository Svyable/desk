# Chapter 18 research — The Fiber Survives the Financing

Research checked for the chapter-scoped drafting pass on September 1, 2026.

## Federal Reserve Bank of Richmond — telecom boom and bust

- E. A. Couper, John P. Hejkal, and Alexander L. Wolman, “Boom and Bust in Telecommunications,” *Federal Reserve Bank of Richmond Economic Quarterly* 89(4), Fall 2003: https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/title/economic-quarterly-federal-reserve-bank-richmond-960/fall-2003-477419/fulltext
- Use in chapter:
  - a fiber strand carrying roughly 2.5 Gbps in 1996 could reach roughly 100 Gbps by 2000 through multiplexing advances;
  - by late 2000 the long-haul market showed significant overcapacity;
  - a contemporary early-2001 industry report cited by the Richmond Fed described nearly 600,000 miles of new inter-city fiber on the way while capacity prices were falling and customers were failing;
  - communications-industry after-tax corporate profits were nearly negative $20 billion in 2001;
  - real telecommunications-services consumption rose from roughly $88 billion in 1995 to roughly $151 billion in 2001 in constant 1996 dollars;
  - the CPI for long-distance telephone service fell 18.5% from December 1997 to March 2003, while wireless-service prices also fell substantially over the late 1990s and early 2000s.
- Discipline: the chapter uses these figures to show simultaneous demand growth, capacity growth, falling prices, and investor losses. It does not claim that fiber overcapacity was the sole cause of the telecom bust; the Richmond Fed paper emphasizes interacting technological, regulatory, competitive, and macroeconomic forces.

## U.S. Government Accountability Office — WorldCom

- U.S. GAO, “GSA Actions Leading to Proposed Debarment of WorldCom,” GAO-04-741R, May 26, 2004: https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-04-741r
- Use in chapter: WorldCom announced in June 2002 that it would restate 2001 and first-quarter 2002 financial statements by nearly $4 billion and filed for bankruptcy protection in July 2002; subsequent investigations and criminal cases followed.
- Discipline: WorldCom is not presented as a clean overcapacity case. The manuscript explicitly separates accounting fraud from ordinary capital-cycle failure and uses the bankruptcy only for the narrower point that a useful communications network can continue to matter while the financial claims and corporate structure around it fail.

## U.S. Government Accountability Office — Global Crossing

- U.S. GAO, “Global Crossing Telecommunications, Inc.,” B-288413.6; B-288413.10, June 17, 2002: https://www.gao.gov/products/b-288413.6%2Cb-288413.10
- Use in chapter: contemporaneous federal procurement record documenting Global Crossing’s bankruptcy and resulting concern about financial risk and contract performance.
- Discipline: this is supporting evidence that major network operators entered distress; it is not used to assign a single cause to Global Crossing’s bankruptcy.

## Falsification note

The chapter’s analogy would be weak if the telecom episode were merely a case of demand collapse. The Richmond Fed record shows the opposite: telecommunications consumption continued to rise while capacity expanded, prices fell, and industry profits deteriorated. The analogy would also be misleading if AI infrastructure had uniform asset lives. It does not; the chapter therefore distinguishes long-lived land, power, buildings, and fiber from rapidly depreciating accelerators and even faster-changing software/model economics.
