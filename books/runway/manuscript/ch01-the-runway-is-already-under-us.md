# The Runway Is Already Under Us

The easiest way to imagine an artificial-intelligence takeoff is to picture a line on a chart. Capability rises slowly, then quickly, then almost vertically. Somewhere on that line sits the moment everyone has been waiting for: the breakthrough, the threshold, the point at which the machine becomes smart enough to improve itself and the future stops behaving like the recent past.

It is a clean picture. It may also be the wrong one.

An airplane does not leave the ground because its engine crosses a philosophical threshold. It leaves because a collection of systems, built by different people for different purposes, happen to be ready at the same time. The engines must produce enough thrust. The wings must produce enough lift. The tires must survive the acceleration. The runway must be long enough for the aircraft at that weight and in those conditions. The brakes matter even when the intention is to fly, because takeoff planning includes the possibility that the attempt will be rejected. Fuel, weather, traffic, maintenance, pilot judgment, airspace and airport design all enter a decision that looks simple only from a distance.

Then the aircraft moves.

For the first seconds, nothing appears discontinuous. It is rolling on the same pavement it occupied at the gate. The speed changes, the control surfaces begin to matter differently, and eventually the geometry changes. The ground that had been carrying the machine is no longer carrying it.

We have spent years arguing about the intelligence of the engine.

The runway has received less attention.

That matters because artificial intelligence is not a self-contained object. A model can be extraordinary and commercially irrelevant. It can be commercially valuable and economically narrow. It can write excellent code but lack permission to deploy it. It can propose a better chip and still wait for fabrication. It can design a better data center and still wait for transformers, permits, interconnection studies, turbines, cooling equipment and construction crews. It can help a scientist generate a hypothesis in seconds and then encounter a biological experiment that takes weeks. It can operate a robot in simulation and still fail on a warehouse floor where light changes, pallets arrive damaged and people walk where the planner did not expect them.

Capability is one component in a larger machine.

The most important question may therefore be less dramatic than “When will we get AGI?” and more useful than “How many parameters will the next model have?”

What, at any given moment, is actually holding the system back?

That question leads to a different map of the future.

In 2026, the frontier of AI already contains several clocks running at very different speeds. One clock is software. Models can be trained, fine-tuned, evaluated and deployed on cycles measured in months or less. Another clock is semiconductor manufacturing, where new process technologies and advanced packaging capacity require specialized equipment, factories, materials and years of preparation. Another is electrical infrastructure. A new data-center campus may be conceived during one model generation and receive all the power it wants several model generations later. Another is institutional adoption. A capability can exist long before a hospital, bank, insurer, government agency or manufacturer is willing to place it inside a consequential workflow. Another is the physical world, where atoms remain stubbornly unimpressed by a benchmark score.

The clocks do not have to agree.

They only have to interact.

This is where the usual takeoff story becomes more interesting. Suppose model capability continues improving rapidly. At first, compute may be the constraint. More chips are ordered. Then advanced packaging becomes scarce. Capacity expands. Then power becomes scarce. Utilities and developers respond. Then the scarce thing is not power but permission: interconnection queues, environmental reviews, land, transmission or local opposition. Those processes begin to change. Then a new model arrives that can automate parts of engineering, planning, code, procurement, legal review and scientific research. The same intelligence that was waiting on the runway begins helping to lengthen it.

The bottleneck moves.

That movement is the subject of this book.

The phrase “AI takeoff” carries a long intellectual history, and different communities use it differently. Some mean the interval between human-level and vastly superhuman intelligence. Some mean recursive self-improvement. Some mean a period of extremely rapid economic growth. Some use it loosely for a market boom or a sudden acceleration in adoption. Those distinctions matter, and no book should erase them for a catchy metaphor.

But all of them share an implicit assumption: there is some transition in which the rate of change itself changes.

The argument here is that we should look for that transition in the coupled system rather than in the model alone.

A model can improve without the economy taking off. A data center can grow without science taking off. A robot can become more dexterous without manufacturing taking off. A company can adopt agents without productivity taking off. Yet these improvements do not stay separate forever. The possibility worth examining is that enough of them begin reinforcing one another that the slow systems start accelerating too.

That would look less like a single intelligence explosion than a sequence of bottlenecks being removed faster than new ones can form.

This is not guaranteed.

It is also not science fiction in the ordinary sense. The ingredients are visible now.

Epoch AI’s estimates of frontier compute capacity suggest that the stock of leading AI compute has recently been growing on a timescale measured in months rather than decades. At the same time, the organization estimates that a gigawatt-scale data center takes years to build. The exact numbers will change. The asymmetry is the important part. Digital capability and physical capacity live on different calendars.

METR’s work on autonomous task horizons reveals another asymmetry. Frontier systems have been able to complete progressively longer software tasks under controlled evaluation, but a task lasting hours is not the same thing as a job, a project or an institution. Every additional step creates opportunities for mistakes, bad assumptions, permission failures, tool errors and misunderstood goals. A model can become better at remaining useful over time without becoming reliably autonomous in an open world.

The grid has its own asymmetry. In the United States, enormous volumes of proposed generation and storage sit in interconnection queues. Large new electrical loads, including data centers, are forcing regulators and grid operators to rethink rules built for a slower era. A developer can order servers faster than a region can build a power plant and transmission line. The queue does not care how impressive the model is.

Semiconductor manufacturing has another. A leading accelerator is the product of a supply chain that includes lithography systems, deposition and etch tools, wafers, chemicals, masks, advanced packaging, high-bandwidth memory and networking. A shortage in any one can leave expensive capacity underused. The most advanced AI system in the world cannot infer through a memory stack that does not exist.

And organizations have perhaps the strangest asymmetry of all.

A tool can become dramatically better while the people using it become only marginally more productive. One field study of customer-support agents found meaningful gains from generative AI, with the largest benefits going to less experienced workers. A randomized study of experienced open-source developers using early-2025 AI tools found the opposite of what both the developers and researchers expected: the participants were slower. These findings are not contradictions. They are a warning against treating capability as productivity by definition.

Technology has to fit the work.

The work often has to change to fit the technology.

That process is the runway.

The metaphor should not be pushed too far. Economies are not aircraft. They have no pilot, no single destination and no checklist that can declare them ready for departure. They contain competing interests, political vetoes, mistakes, fraud, standards battles, uneven geography and human preferences that do not yield to optimization. Some constraints are desirable. A safety review that prevents an unsafe system from controlling critical infrastructure is not merely friction to be removed. A worker who refuses to delegate a consequential decision may be protecting knowledge the organization has failed to encode. A regulator who slows a deployment can be wrong, prudent or both in different cases.

A runway is not a demand for speed at any price.

It is a way to see dependencies.

Once you look at AI through dependencies, several familiar arguments change shape.

The first is the argument over whether scaling is over. If larger training runs encounter diminishing returns, that matters enormously. But it does not tell us whether the overall system will stop improving. Better algorithms, inference-time computation, memory, tools, specialized chips, better data, improved training methods and application-level design can all move capability without simply making one model larger. Conversely, even dramatic model progress can run into infrastructure constraints that make a laboratory achievement expensive to spread.

The second is the argument over whether AI investment is a bubble. Capital can be badly allocated while the technology is transformative. Railroads changed countries and destroyed fortunes. Fiber-optic networks were overbuilt during one financial cycle and became valuable infrastructure for a later one. A data-center boom can contain both real demand and irrational pricing. The runway can be real even when some of its builders lose money.

The third is the argument over jobs. If you ask only whether a model can perform a task, you miss the surrounding system. Jobs contain permissions, relationships, physical actions, tacit knowledge, liability, exception handling and institutional memory. Some of that can be codified. Some can be delegated. Some becomes more valuable when routine cognition becomes cheap. The sequence matters.

The fourth is the argument over timelines.

Timelines invite false precision because they compress a network of dependencies into a date. The date becomes a magnet for attention. People debate 2028 versus 2032 as though the disagreement could be settled by adding four years to a spreadsheet.

A roadmap asks different questions.

What must become abundant?

What must become reliable?

What must become cheap?

What must receive power?

What must be permitted?

What must be connected to what?

What still takes human time?

Which constraints can AI itself help remove?

And which constraints become harder precisely because the system is more capable?

Security belongs in that final category. More capable systems are not only productive assets. They are attractive targets and, potentially, powerful instruments for attack. A world of autonomous agents needs stronger identity, authorization, audit and containment, not merely more autonomy. Frontier labs now publish safety frameworks that explicitly treat certain capabilities as triggers for stronger safeguards. NIST has been working on identity and authorization for software and AI agents because permission becomes a first-order infrastructure problem when software can act rather than merely advise.

Trust is part of the runway.

So is the ability to stop.

The analogy returns here to aviation in a useful way. A runway does not exist only to make acceleration possible. Its design is inseparable from the possibility of an aborted takeoff. Systems that can move quickly but cannot be interrupted are not mature infrastructure. They are a demonstration.

This is one reason the most interesting AI progress over the next several years may occur in places that do not resemble a chatbot becoming cleverer. It may appear in an interconnection tariff that lets a data center connect without shifting unreasonable costs to other customers. It may appear in a new generation of high-bandwidth memory that keeps expensive accelerators fed with data. It may appear in software that verifies another model’s work cheaply enough to make delegation economical. It may appear in a laboratory where AI proposes an experiment and automated equipment closes part of the loop. It may appear in a semiconductor plant where machine assistance shortens diagnosis time on a manufacturing problem. It may appear in an organization that redesigns a workflow around agents instead of adding a text box to the old workflow.

None of these moments is “the” takeoff.

Together they may determine whether one can happen.

There is another reason to watch the runway instead of the horizon. Horizons invite waiting. Runways invite observation.

If you believe a decisive intelligence threshold is ten years away, most events before the threshold can look like prelude. If you believe it is one year away, every benchmark becomes a countdown. Both habits distort what is happening in front of us.

The world is already committing capital, power, land, engineering talent and political attention on the assumption that machine intelligence will become more useful. Semiconductor companies are expanding capacity. Grid operators are rewriting rules. Utilities are reconsidering load growth after years in which electricity demand in many advanced economies was comparatively flat. Technology companies are signing long-term power agreements and exploring generation options that would have sounded eccentric for software businesses a decade ago. Enterprises are experimenting with agents whose main limitation is often not the quality of the prose they generate but access to systems and confidence in the actions they take.

The future is being financed before it is settled.

That does not prove the financiers are right.

It does mean the runway has consequences before takeoff.

A city that approves a large data center changes its grid planning. A utility that forecasts load growth changes generation procurement. A chipmaker that expands packaging changes the availability of future compute. A university that reorganizes courses around AI changes what new graduates learn. A regulator that defines acceptable agent identity can shape which architectures become easy to deploy. A company that exposes its internal systems through machine-readable interfaces makes future automation easier even if today’s models remain imperfect.

These are path-dependent decisions.

They are also bets on coordination.

The deeper thesis of this book is not that every bottleneck will disappear. There will always be bottlenecks. The thesis is that the identity of the bottleneck can move, and the speed of that movement can itself accelerate.

At first, humans build better models.

Then better models help humans write more software.

That software improves research, operations and design.

Better research helps build better models and hardware.

Better design can improve data centers, chips, energy systems and robots.

Better agents can help organizations absorb those capabilities.

More adoption justifies more capital.

More capital expands infrastructure.

Expanded infrastructure makes cheap intelligence more abundant.

Cheap intelligence creates uses that were uneconomic before.

Those uses create demand for still more infrastructure.

A loop does not have to be infinite to matter. It only has to close faster than the surrounding world expects.

This is where a roadmap becomes more useful than a forecast. A forecast asks where the system will be at a particular time. A roadmap identifies the junctions where the system could speed up, stall or change direction.

The chapters ahead follow those junctions.

They begin with the economics of intelligence because falling costs change what people attempt. They move into chips, power, memory, networking and capital because intelligence remains physical even when its output is digital. They turn toward delegation, verification, identity and organizational redesign because capability without access is only a suggestion. They follow AI into science, software and robotics because feedback loops differ depending on whether the thing being changed is code or matter. They examine permitting, security and governance because a civilization does not scale powerful systems by pretending institutions do not exist. Finally, they return to the possibility of AI improving the machinery that produces AI.

No chapter will name a takeoff date.

That omission is deliberate.

A date is the least interesting part of a transition we do not yet know how to measure well.

The more useful task is to recognize the conditions under which the transition becomes self-reinforcing, and the conditions under which it does not.

If the next generation of models becomes more capable but data-center power stalls, the bottleneck moved.

If power arrives but organizations cannot trust agents with permissions, it moved again.

If agents become reliable but robotics remains expensive and brittle, the digital economy may accelerate while the physical economy lags.

If AI materially shortens semiconductor design cycles, scientific discovery, software development, construction planning and regulatory processing, several slow clocks may begin to synchronize.

That is the possibility to watch.

The runway is not something we build after intelligence arrives.

We are building it now.
