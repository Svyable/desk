# The Power Line

For most of the digital age, electricity was deliberately boring.

Software companies worried about users, servers, storage, bandwidth and engineers. Electricity arrived in the background. A successful consumer application could add millions of users without its founder learning the difference between a substation and a switchyard. The grid was part of civilization’s operating system: essential, complicated and largely invisible to the companies building on top of it.

Artificial intelligence is making electricity visible again.

A large AI campus is not simply another office building with a bigger utility bill. At hundreds of megawatts, and increasingly in plans measured around a gigawatt, it begins to resemble industrial load. It can rival the demand of a city. It can alter regional generation forecasts. It can force new transmission, substations and tariff rules. It can turn a software company into a party to twenty-year power agreements and discussions about nuclear plants.

The token has found the grid.

That collision is one of the clearest examples of the moving bottleneck because the two systems were built on different assumptions.

AI infrastructure is being designed on technology cycles measured in months. Electric infrastructure is planned through processes that often take years. A model provider can change architecture between one generation and the next. A utility cannot move a transmission line because the next model uses a different mixture of training and inference.

The grid expects persistence.

AI expects iteration.

Somewhere between them sits the runway.

The scale of the collision is already large enough that institutions built for electricity are changing their procedures. The U.S. Department of Energy reported that data centers consumed roughly 4.4 percent of U.S. electricity in 2023 and cited estimates that their share could rise substantially by 2028. The International Energy Agency projects global data-center electricity use to climb sharply through 2030, with AI one of the main drivers. These are forecasts, not measurements of the future. They depend on efficiency gains, utilization, deployment and demand that nobody knows with confidence.

The infrastructure response matters even if the high estimates prove wrong.

Utilities have begun planning for load growth they did not expect a decade ago. Grid operators are dealing with proposals for enormous new customers. In 2026, the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission pushed several regional transmission organizations to address how very large loads such as data centers connect to the grid. PJM, which coordinates electricity across a large part of the eastern United States, has warned of significant demand growth in its planning horizon, much of it associated with data centers.

This is what a constraint looks like before it becomes a blackout.

It appears as a planning dispute.

Who gets connected first?

Who pays for upgrades?

What happens if a data center requests power that it never uses?

Can a large load bring its own generation?

Can it locate next to a power plant?

What reliability obligations come with that arrangement?

Should ordinary customers absorb network costs created by one unusually large user?

How much generation must exist before the load is allowed to connect?

These questions sound administrative because the grid converts engineering scarcity into rules.

The queue is where physics becomes paperwork.

Berkeley Lab’s interconnection data show enormous quantities of proposed generation and storage waiting in U.S. queues. Many projects will never be built. Those that do can spend years navigating studies, upgrades and changing economics. On the other side of the meter, large new loads are creating a parallel urgency. It is possible to have a country with abundant proposed generation and a data-center developer unable to get firm power at the site and date it wants.

Aggregate abundance does not eliminate local scarcity.

Electricity is unusually sensitive to place and time. A megawatt available in Texas does not automatically solve a constraint in Virginia. A solar plant producing at noon does not automatically serve a cluster that needs reliable power at midnight. A generator can exist physically while transmission remains insufficient. A transmission line can exist while a local substation lacks capacity. A utility can have long-run generation plans while a transformer manufacturer has a lead-time problem.

“Power” is a stack of constraints.

AI companies are learning it quickly.

This helps explain a set of deals that would have sounded improbable when internet companies were first building server farms. Technology companies have signed agreements connected to nuclear generation, advanced reactors and large renewable portfolios. Microsoft entered a long-term agreement with Constellation tied to restarting the reactor at the former Three Mile Island Unit 1 site under a new name. Google signed an agreement with Kairos Power around advanced nuclear reactors with a first deployment targeted for the end of the decade. Other companies are pursuing combinations of gas, renewables, storage and grid power.

The important fact is not that any one generation technology will win.

It is that computing companies are reaching backward into energy supply.

A company does this when the old boundary becomes strategically dangerous.

Vertical integration is often a response to uncertainty. If chips are scarce, secure chip supply. If power is scarce, secure power. If land with interconnection rights is scarce, secure land. The company moves toward the constraint.

This can make the system faster.

It can also make it stranger.

The technology industry is good at financing large projects, but electricity is not software. Power plants face fuel, equipment, construction, regulatory, environmental and operating risks that cannot be solved with a product launch. Nuclear projects can take years. Gas generation depends on pipelines and emissions constraints. Renewable projects depend on location, transmission and weather. Batteries can shift energy across hours, not manufacture energy from nothing.

There is no energy source called urgency.

That sentence is worth remembering when AI demand forecasts become extravagant.

The physical grid places a ceiling on how quickly installed compute can grow in a region. A company can buy equipment faster than the region can energize it. That mismatch can strand capital, move projects to other geographies or push developers toward on-site generation.

It can also create political backlash.

Electricity is a socialized network in the practical sense that many users share infrastructure. A new customer large enough to require generation and transmission upgrades raises distributional questions. If residential prices rise while a data center receives favorable terms, the technical expansion becomes a political problem. If a project consumes water in a constrained region, land-use politics join the grid politics. If backup generators affect local air quality, permits become harder.

A power constraint can turn into a legitimacy constraint.

This matters for takeoff because social permission is part of physical scaling.

The industry cannot simply assume that every economically valuable megawatt will be politically acceptable.

That does not mean public resistance will stop expansion. It means expansion has to become better at bargaining with the places where it occurs.

Data centers can offer tax revenue, jobs, grid investment and demand for new generation. They can also be designed with flexibility. Some workloads may be shifted in time or place. Backup capacity can potentially provide grid services. A site that can reduce load during tight periods may impose different costs from a site that demands maximum power continuously.

The shape of the load matters.

AI workloads may eventually become more flexible than many traditional industrial loads because computation can sometimes be scheduled. A training job that can move between regions or pause may respond to grid conditions. Inference serving interactive users is less flexible because latency matters. Scientific workloads can be batch-oriented. Model evaluation can often wait. Data preparation can move.

If software learns to treat electricity price and availability as input variables, compute can follow power more intelligently.

This is another place where AI can help build its own runway without creating energy from nowhere.

Better scheduling can raise utilization of existing infrastructure.

Better forecasting can improve operations.

Better data-center design can reduce cooling losses.

More efficient models can produce more useful work per watt.

Hardware can improve performance per unit of energy.

Workloads can be routed toward regions with available capacity.

A megawatt made more productive is economically similar to a partial megawatt of new supply.

Efficiency matters enormously.

It simply does not guarantee lower total demand.

If the cost per unit of useful intelligence falls, consumption can rise faster than efficiency improves. The same dynamic has appeared throughout computing. The total electricity used by data centers did not disappear because servers became more efficient. Efficiency made more computing economical.

The runaway version of the AI story therefore contains an odd possibility: the more efficiently we produce intelligence, the more electricity the intelligence economy may rationally consume.

This does not violate physics.

It follows demand.

A civilization with abundant machine cognition may choose to spend enormous amounts of energy on it because the returns are high. That makes energy abundance part of the long-run AI question.

There are only a few broad ways to respond.

Build more generation.

Build more transmission and distribution.

Use existing infrastructure more efficiently.

Move compute toward available power.

Reduce energy consumed per unit of useful work.

Shift work through time.

Accept slower growth.

Every energy strategy is some combination of those.

The balance varies by region.

A country with abundant gas and fast permitting may lean on gas turbines. A country with strong nuclear capability may extend existing reactors or build new ones. Regions with excellent solar or wind can combine renewables with transmission, storage and firm generation. Areas with hydroelectric capacity may attract data centers directly. Some developers may build behind the meter or co-locate near generation.

The AI map can begin following the energy map.

That would partially reverse a long trend in digital geography.

Software talent clustered in cities because people mattered more than power. Cloud computing allowed applications to be served from remote data centers. Frontier AI campuses may be located where land and megawatts are available even if the researchers who design the models live somewhere else.

Intelligence production separates from intelligence work.

That has geopolitical consequences.

Countries with reliable, expandable and relatively cheap electricity gain an industrial asset. So do countries that can permit new capacity quickly without destroying public trust. Energy policy becomes compute policy. Grid competence becomes a factor in technological competitiveness.

This is one reason the United States’ 2025 AI Action Plan devoted explicit attention to infrastructure and permitting. The federal government also explored using federal sites for data-center and energy development. Whatever one thinks of the politics, the policy diagnosis is revealing: national AI ambition has moved beyond research funding into power, land and construction.

The model is now large enough to encounter the state.

China encounters the same problem differently because its electricity system, industrial policy and construction capacity are organized differently. Other countries will build their own combinations. Some may decide that serving as compute hosts is economically attractive. Others may protect grids from large loads or insist that developers bring new generation.

There will not be one global runway.

There will be regional runways connected by networks.

This raises an underappreciated possibility: AI capability may diffuse unevenly not because models are unavailable but because electricity and infrastructure are uneven.

A small country can download an open model. It cannot download a gigawatt.

Cloud access can bridge some of that gap. International data centers can serve users remotely. But latency, sovereignty, regulation and cost can make local compute strategically valuable. Countries may seek domestic capacity for critical applications even when foreign cloud services are cheaper.

Energy becomes part of digital sovereignty.

The relationship runs both ways.

AI can also alter the energy system itself. Grid operators manage increasingly complex systems with variable renewable generation, storage, flexible demand and distributed resources. Better forecasting, optimization and control software can help. Google DeepMind’s AlphaEvolve has already been applied to algorithmic problems beyond language, and other machine-learning systems have long been used in weather and energy forecasting.

The important point is not that AI will magically solve the grid.

The grid is precisely the kind of system where better information has value but does not erase physical constraints.

A model can recommend the best transmission expansion. Someone still has to permit and build it.

A model can improve turbine maintenance. The turbine still needs parts.

A model can forecast load. Generation still has to exist when the load arrives.

AI can reduce the cognitive cost of operating the energy system while exposing the material cost more clearly.

The bottleneck moves.

This is where the runaway thesis becomes testable.

If AI progress continues but the average time required to add large blocks of power remains unchanged or worsens, electricity can become a durable brake. The industry will route around it through efficiency, geography and pricing, but the physical expansion rate will remain bounded.

If AI materially shortens grid studies, engineering, design, equipment procurement, construction planning and operations while policy reforms shorten interconnection and permitting, the energy clock can speed up.

The question is not whether an AI can design a power plant in an afternoon.

The question is whether the end-to-end time from recognized need to reliable megawatt begins shrinking.

That is the measurement that matters.

Today, many parts of that path remain stubborn.

Large transformers can have long lead times. Transmission projects can face years of review and litigation. Generation equipment comes from finite factories. Skilled electrical labor is not unlimited. Local communities can reject projects. Financial markets can change the cost of capital. Fuel infrastructure can constrain supposedly available generation.

The runway is full of inherited lead times.

AI takeoff becomes a different kind of event if intelligence can compress inherited lead times instead of merely waiting for them.

The same is true in every physical sector, but power is special because all the other sectors need it.

A fab needs electricity.

A data center needs electricity.

A robot needs electricity.

A laboratory needs electricity.

The network needs electricity.

The factory making the transformer needs electricity.

Energy is not one complementary input among many. It is the universal input beneath the computational economy.

That does not mean AI growth maps directly to electricity growth. Algorithms improve. Hardware improves. Workloads change. A future model may accomplish far more with less energy than a present one. Local devices may handle tasks that currently require a data center. Specialized chips may reduce waste.

But at civilization scale, intelligence has never been free of energy.

Human brains consume it metabolically.

Machines consume it electrically.

The sources and efficiencies differ. The dependency remains.

This places a useful boundary around fantasies of frictionless takeoff.

If digital intelligence begins improving itself rapidly, it still has to purchase time on physical machinery. If the loop demands more computation, energy appears inside the loop. If it discovers algorithms that reduce computational demand, the constraint loosens. If it helps build better generation, the loop expands. If neither happens quickly enough, the grid becomes the throttle.

The takeoff runway has a power line running beneath it.

That line can be extended.

It can be reinforced.

It can be used more intelligently.

It cannot be wished away.
