# Back Matter

The runway is deliberately unfinished.

Not the manuscript. The system.

Any edition of a book about artificial intelligence risks becoming a photograph of a moving object. New models will appear. Data-center projects will open. Some announced projects will disappear. Power forecasts will be revised. Agent reliability will improve unevenly. Robotics companies will discover that a difficult engineering problem was easier or harder than expected. Regulators will change rules. Capital markets will become enthusiastic and frightened in cycles.

That is why I have tried to build the argument around quantities that can be updated rather than around a prophecy that can only be defended.

The research trail for this edition records the sources behind concrete claims and, more importantly, the observations that would weaken the thesis.

The thesis is not that every constraint disappears.

It is not that physical industry reaches software speed.

It is not that artificial intelligence replaces every human role.

It is not that economic growth becomes infinite.

It is not that one company, model or country controls the entire feedback loop.

The thesis is that the production of useful artificial intelligence can enter a regime in which intelligence is increasingly used to expand, optimize and coordinate the capacity required for the next increment of intelligence, causing important cycle times to fall and bottlenecks to be removed more quickly.

That is a strong claim.

It should be tested strongly.

The easiest way to follow the thesis after this edition is to keep watching the instrument panel.

Watch the cost of a fixed level of useful capability.

Watch how long agents can stay on real tasks without rescue.

Watch the cost of accepted work after checking and correction.

Watch how many machine processes one qualified person can supervise.

Watch compute supply and useful work per unit of compute.

Watch the time from capital commitment to powered capacity.

Watch grid queues, transformers, turbines, memory, packaging and cooling.

Watch AI research cycle time.

Watch whether machine-generated engineering improvements become routine in production systems.

Watch laboratory throughput rather than hypothesis count.

Watch robot intervention rates rather than stage demonstrations.

Watch machine permissions and incidents per unit of autonomous work.

Watch bottleneck half-life.

If those metrics stop improving together, the takeoff thesis should be marked down.

If the constraints become harder to remove as the system scales, it should be marked down further.

If artificial intelligence remains mostly downstream—good at producing digital output but weak at improving the upstream systems that create compute, energy, hardware, experiments and physical work—then the runway will remain long and conventional.

If capital stops converting into productive capacity because returns fail to justify the buildout, the runway slows.

If model capability grows while accepted-work productivity stagnates, the runway slows.

If institutions discover that verification and governance require nearly one unit of expert human attention for every unit of machine work, parallelism remains limited.

If robotics requires so much intervention and custom integration that physical labor stays largely linear in human supervision, physical feedback stays weak.

Those are not footnotes.

They are the conditions under which this book is wrong.

There is another reason to leave the argument falsifiable.

AI has become a subject on which identity easily replaces observation.

Optimism and pessimism become affiliations. Every new result is recruited as evidence for a conclusion chosen earlier. A benchmark improvement proves inevitability. A failed deployment proves fraud. A data-center project proves demand. A canceled project proves collapse.

Reality is less cooperative.

A system can be advancing and overvalued.

It can be transformative and badly governed.

It can create enormous consumer value and disappointing returns for particular investors.

It can eliminate some kinds of work and increase demand for others.

It can accelerate software while remaining physically constrained.

Several apparently contradictory statements can be true at once because the runway has many layers.

The right response is not moderation for its own sake.

It is decomposition.

Which layer changed?

Which constraint moved?

What is now on the critical path?

How long is the wait?

Is the wait getting shorter?

Who has the ability to shorten it?

Is artificial intelligence part of that shortening?

Those questions keep the argument anchored.

The publication date of this edition is September 2026. Where later evidence contradicts a concrete number, prefer the later evidence. Where a source has been revised, inspect the revision. Where a company describes its own achievement, distinguish the reported claim from independent validation. Where a benchmark saturates or becomes contaminated, retire it rather than pretending the number retained meaning.

The research trail favors primary and institutional sources where possible: government and intergovernmental energy data, research organizations that publish methods, company technical reports for company-specific engineering claims, and established academic or standards sources for scientific and risk-management claims.

Company sources still deserve skepticism.

A company knows its internal deployment better than an outsider and has stronger incentives to describe that deployment favorably. Both facts matter.

The same is true of forecasts.

Forecasts are useful because infrastructure must be built before demand is certain. They are dangerous because uncertainty grows with the horizon. The IEA’s energy outlooks, Epoch AI’s compute estimates, Stanford’s AI Index and similar sources should be read as measurement efforts with methods and assumptions, not as oracles.

The book’s most important evidence will arrive after publication.

If AI-assisted software systems continue moving from suggestion toward delegated objectives, we will learn how quickly organizations can lower verification cost.

If systems like AlphaEvolve become routine parts of hardware, software and scientific engineering, we will learn how much machine search can compress development cycles.

If data-center electricity demand continues rising, we will learn whether generation, transmission, equipment supply and local permitting respond at industrial speed.

If robot fleets expand, we will learn whether data and software improvements can make physical capital more capable after deployment.

If automated laboratories spread, we will learn which sciences have evaluators fast enough for machine-driven iteration and which remain bound by long observation periods.

If all of these advances occur but aggregate productivity remains weak, the runway framework will need to explain why the gains are not propagating.

Perhaps reorganization costs dominate for longer than expected.

Perhaps demand is less elastic than assumed.

Perhaps the most capable AI work substitutes for existing spending rather than creating additional output.

Perhaps measurement fails to capture large consumer surplus.

Perhaps control and legitimacy remain the binding constraints.

The answer should be earned from evidence rather than protected by definition.

There is one claim I expect to age better than the numbers.

Abundance creates scarcity next door.

That is a general property of complementary systems.

When one input becomes easier to obtain, the value of the remaining scarce complements rises. The same logic appeared when railroads increased the value of land near stations, when electrification increased the value of motors and electrical equipment, when cheap computing increased the value of software, and when the internet made distribution and attention newly important.

Artificial intelligence is unlikely to be an exception.

If cognition becomes cheaper, the things cognition cannot cheaply reproduce become relatively more important.

Physical capacity.

Permission.

Trust.

Verified evidence.

Legitimate authority.

Human attention.

Then AI and capital attack those constraints too.

The moving bottleneck is not merely a problem to be solved.

It is a map of where value and power are moving.

That makes the runway useful even under a slower future.

Suppose extreme takeoff never arrives.

The framework still asks good questions about infrastructure investment, labor markets, corporate strategy and public policy.

A stronger grid is useful under moderate AI growth.

More resilient semiconductor supply is useful.

Machine-readable institutions are useful.

Better scientific automation is useful.

Safer agent controls are useful.

Faster permitting that preserves substantive protections is useful.

Robotics that raises physical productivity is useful.

The roadmap does not require the most dramatic scenario to justify every step.

That is intentional.

Civilizations should be cautious about infrastructure strategies that make sense only if one speculative forecast is exactly right.

Runway should be useful ground before it becomes a launch surface.

The other claim I expect to endure is that the calendar matters more than people think.

Technological history is often narrated through inventions.

The steam engine.

The transistor.

The internet.

The smartphone.

The invention matters, but diffusion and complement-building determine the economic transformation. Factories have to reorganize. Networks have to spread. Standards have to settle. Capital stock has to turn over. People have to learn new workflows.

Artificial intelligence may compress some of those diffusion lags because the technology itself can assist the adaptation process.

That possibility is what makes this transition unusual.

A general-purpose technology that helps write the software, design the hardware, coordinate the projects, analyze the failures, train the workers and optimize the infrastructure needed for its own deployment has an unusually direct route into the machinery of diffusion.

It can help build its complements.

The strength of that effect is the unresolved empirical question at the center of this book.

If the effect is weak, AI remains a powerful technology moving through a familiar industrial cycle.

If the effect is strong, the industrial cycle itself changes speed.

That is the fork worth watching.

The book’s title is therefore meant literally.

A roadmap names the route.

A runway measures whether enough route has become usable for acceleration to turn into flight.

The route is not hidden.

It runs through chips, power, capital, software, permissions, organizations, science and machines.

The future will add details I missed and remove assumptions that looked sensible in 2026.

That is expected.

What matters is whether the route keeps shortening as intelligence travels through it.

If it does, update the map.

If it does not, update the thesis.

Either way, keep watching the waits.

They are where the future spends its time.
