# Takeoff Is a Supply Chain

The popular image of artificial intelligence is almost aggressively immaterial.

A blank box waits on a screen. A person types. Words appear.

Everything difficult has been hidden.

The electricity disappears behind an outlet. The network disappears behind a wireless icon. The data center disappears behind the word cloud. The fabrication plant disappears behind the chip. The chip disappears behind an API. The engineers disappear behind a model name. Even time disappears. A response that arrives in seconds encourages the feeling that the thing producing it exists in the same frictionless world as the text itself.

It does not.

Every generated sentence sits at the end of one of the most complicated industrial systems humans have built.

Consider only the accelerator.

Before a leading AI chip reaches a data center, its design has passed through electronic-design software, intellectual-property blocks, verification, mask preparation and a fabrication process whose most advanced steps depend on machines that themselves contain some of the most precise systems ever manufactured. Extreme-ultraviolet lithography uses light with a wavelength of 13.5 nanometers. Producing and controlling that light at industrial throughput required decades of engineering across optics, lasers, vacuum systems, materials, metrology and control. ASML’s annual reports read less like the story of a single machine than a reminder that the machine is an ecosystem.

Then the wafer leaves lithography and the problem is still not finished.

The accelerator has to be packaged. It has to communicate with high-bandwidth memory. That memory has to be manufactured, stacked and bonded. The package has to survive power densities that would have looked extreme in an earlier generation of computing. Racks have to connect accelerators with switches, optical links and network interface hardware fast enough that expensive processors do not sit idle waiting for data. Servers need power conversion. Heat has to leave the silicon and then leave the building. Backup systems have to exist for equipment whose useful work may be worth far more than the electricity it is consuming at any instant.

The model is software running on a temporary truce among supply chains.

This is not a metaphorical point. It changes how we should think about the rate of progress.

When demand for intelligence rises, the first instinct is to ask how many more accelerators can be purchased. But accelerators are not interchangeable units that appear when money is offered. They sit inside a chain with different capacities and different expansion times. A shortage of leading-edge wafers can bind. So can advanced packaging. So can high-bandwidth memory. So can substrate capacity. So can networking. So can electrical switchgear. So can transformers. So can skilled labor at a construction site.

The chain does not need every link to be scarce.

One is enough.

Industrial history is full of products whose output was determined by an unglamorous component. During a shortage, the headline item is often not the binding constraint. A carmaker can have engines, body panels and customers while unfinished vehicles wait for semiconductors costing a tiny fraction of the final product. A factory can have orders and machinery while a missing bearing stops a line. A power plant can be largely complete while interconnection equipment delays operation.

AI infrastructure has the same character, with one complication: the product itself can help improve parts of the supply chain that produce it.

That is where the runway begins to curve back on itself.

The semiconductor chain already uses enormous amounts of software and statistical optimization. Chip designers rely on automated tools because no human can reason manually about every transistor and routing decision in a modern design. Fabrication plants use sophisticated process control and diagnostics because tiny deviations can destroy yield. Equipment makers use simulation, predictive maintenance and increasingly machine-learning systems because the machines operate near physical limits.

Artificial intelligence enters an industrial system that was already computational.

The interesting question is how much it can shorten the slow stages.

That distinction is easy to miss if all progress is translated into a single measure of compute. Imagine two futures with the same number of installed accelerators at the end of five years. In the first, every new generation still requires roughly the same human engineering effort, factory construction time, equipment lead time and troubleshooting cycle. In the second, AI systems materially improve chip placement, materials discovery, manufacturing diagnostics, facility design, procurement, code generation and construction planning. The final count may match, but the second system has acquired a different property: its infrastructure has become more responsive to the intelligence it supports.

The supply chain has started learning.

There are early examples, although they should not be mistaken for proof of a runaway loop.

ASML says it already uses AI in predictive maintenance, diagnostics and internal work, and has described AI as a tool for reducing time to market in research and development. Google DeepMind’s AlphaEvolve has shown that a coding agent paired with automated evaluators can discover and optimize algorithms, including some used in computing infrastructure. Semiconductor design companies have been applying machine learning to electronic design automation for years. None of this means an AI can decide to build itself a new fab and have the building appear.

It means the boundary between “AI progress” and “industrial progress” is becoming porous.

A supply-chain view also changes the meaning of concentration.

The leading edge of semiconductor manufacturing depends on a remarkably small number of companies with capabilities that are difficult to reproduce quickly. ASML occupies an extraordinary position in advanced lithography. TSMC fabricates a large share of the world’s most advanced logic chips. High-bandwidth memory is supplied by a small group of manufacturers. Advanced accelerators depend on specialized design expertise and packaging capacity. The cloud layer is concentrated among hyperscale operators capable of financing multi-billion-dollar campuses.

From one perspective, this concentration accelerates progress. Capital and expertise can be focused. Suppliers can co-design around predictable customers. A small number of operators can standardize enormous deployments and learn quickly from them.

From another perspective, concentration creates brittle junctions.

A natural disaster, geopolitical conflict, export restriction, equipment failure, manufacturing defect or financing shock at one point can propagate through the chain. Redundancy that looks economically wasteful during normal operation becomes valuable when the normal operation stops.

Takeoff therefore has a resilience problem before it has a speed problem.

A system that doubles capacity rapidly but cannot absorb interruption may not be accelerating in the durable sense. It may be running closer to failure.

This is one reason governments have entered semiconductor industrial policy so aggressively. Chips became strategic before generative AI, but AI increased the stakes. The United States, Europe, Japan and other jurisdictions have spent public money and political capital trying to expand domestic or allied manufacturing. TSMC has built and planned fabrication capacity outside Taiwan. Equipment makers are expanding support footprints. Memory suppliers are making large bets on HBM. The aim is partly economic and partly defensive: the intelligence economy depends on physical nodes that cannot be copied with software.

There is a temptation here to say that “atoms are slow” and leave it at that.

Atoms are not uniformly slow.

Factories can scale quickly when designs are standardized, suppliers are ready, permits are predictable and money is abundant. China has demonstrated extraordinary physical build speeds in infrastructure and manufacturing. Hyperscalers have learned to replicate data-center designs across regions. Modular construction can compress schedules. Equipment capacity can be expanded ahead of demand when suppliers trust the demand signal.

The useful contrast is not digital versus physical.

It is iteration cost versus coordination cost.

Software can often be copied at nearly zero marginal cost. A working improvement can spread across a fleet of machines quickly. Physical infrastructure requires materials to be ordered, sites prepared, crews scheduled, equipment installed and systems commissioned. The cost of coordination remains even when the design is known.

AI has a natural advantage in places where coordination is informational.

It can read documents, search catalogs, write schedules, compare specifications, inspect code, generate simulations, check drawings, classify defects and maintain a view across large bodies of technical information. These are not glamorous tasks. They are exactly the kind of tasks that fill the gap between an engineering idea and a functioning industrial system.

If AI removes hours from a design review, that is useful. If it removes months from the critical path of a factory, that changes the runway.

The difference lies in where the saved time sits.

A project can become ten percent more efficient without finishing one day sooner if the improvement occurs off the critical path. Construction managers understand this. A schedule is a network of dependencies. Some tasks can slip with little consequence. Others determine the completion date. Improving the wrong task creates local productivity but no system acceleration.

This gives us a better way to evaluate claims that AI is speeding up its own infrastructure.

Ask whether the improvement touches the critical path.

A faster legal memo may not matter if the transformer still arrives in thirty months. A better chip layout may not matter if packaging is fully allocated. A more efficient inference kernel may matter enormously because it effectively creates additional capacity from the installed hardware. A cooling improvement may matter because it raises the power density a site can support. A diagnostic system may matter because it improves uptime across thousands of machines. A better interconnection study may matter if it shortens the process that gates the energization of a campus.

The economics of the chain encourage this search for critical paths.

The leading AI infrastructure is expensive enough that idle capacity is painful. A processor that costs tens of thousands of dollars is only useful when it is doing work. A rack that cannot be energized is inventory. A cluster with weak networking converts scarce chips into expensive waiting. Memory bandwidth, network congestion and power availability become financial variables because they determine how much useful intelligence the capital produces.

This is why seemingly secondary components become strategic.

High-bandwidth memory is a good example. AI workloads move enormous volumes of data between memory and processors. Compute capability is wasted when data cannot arrive fast enough. The industry response has been to stack memory dies and widen interfaces so bandwidth can grow alongside processing power. Micron describes its HBM4 products as delivering more than double the bandwidth of its previous generation. SK hynix announced HBM4 production readiness with similar emphasis on bandwidth and power efficiency. The specifications will age. The architectural point will not: the processor is only one member of the system producing a token.

Networking tells the same story at a larger scale.

As training and inference spread across thousands or tens of thousands of accelerators, the fabric between them becomes part of the computer. Broadcom’s Tomahawk 6 and Nvidia’s Spectrum-X families are marketed around AI clusters precisely because ordinary assumptions about data-center traffic break when huge numbers of processors have to exchange synchronized data. The network is no longer a pipe connecting computers. At cluster scale, it is part of the computer.

Then the computer becomes a campus.

The campus becomes a grid problem.

The grid becomes a permitting problem.

The permitting problem becomes a political problem.

Takeoff is a supply chain all the way down.

This does not mean every layer has equal importance at all times. The whole point of a bottleneck is that most capacity is temporarily nonbinding. If a region has abundant electricity but no chips, more generation does little for near-term AI output. If chips arrive but the grid is full, more chip orders do not solve the problem. If the infrastructure is ready but model improvements stall, warehouses of servers cannot manufacture capability from desire.

The binding constraint migrates.

Companies already behave as if they know this. Hyperscalers sign long-term power contracts. Chip designers secure foundry capacity. Foundries reserve equipment. Memory suppliers expand HBM lines. Networking companies redesign switches around AI traffic. Data-center developers search for sites near large power sources. Technology companies invest in generation technologies outside their traditional competence. Each move is an attempt to keep the next constraint from arriving after the current one has been solved.

This is industrial choreography, not simply demand growth.

It also creates the possibility of overshoot.

When everyone sees the same bottleneck, capital rushes toward it. A shortage can become a glut by the time new capacity arrives. Semiconductor cycles have repeated this pattern for decades. Memory is especially famous for violent swings. Data centers can be built into demand that fails to materialize. Power plants have long lives that outlast model fashions. An interconnection queue can contain speculative projects that never get financed.

Runway construction is therefore full of bad bets.

That is not an argument against the runway thesis. It is part of it.

A system does not accelerate because planners forecast perfectly. It accelerates when enough capacity is built, even messily, that the next constraint becomes visible. The internet was overbuilt in places and underbuilt in others. Rail networks went through speculative booms. Electrical systems accumulated redundant capacity, standards and interconnections through decades of uneven investment. Infrastructure usually arrives through a mixture of foresight, rivalry, panic, policy and luck.

AI will be no tidier.

The relevant question is whether the aggregate result is increasing slack in the places that used to bind.

Slack sounds inefficient. It can be a source of speed.

A company with spare compute can try an experiment without waiting for procurement. A grid with spare capacity can connect a new customer without years of upgrades. A fab with available packaging can absorb a successful new accelerator. A network with headroom can support a more communication-intensive workload. A laboratory with automated equipment can run a machine-generated experiment immediately instead of entering a scheduling queue.

Slack converts surprise into action.

Takeoff becomes more plausible when the system contains enough slack to respond to capability gains instead of rationing them.

This is why the massive capital expenditures surrounding AI can be read in two ways at once. They are a bet on future demand. They are also an attempt to create optionality for capabilities that do not yet exist.

A data center ordered for one model generation may serve another. A power agreement may outlive several architecture cycles. A fiber route can carry traffic nobody imagined when it was permitted. A fab expansion creates capacity that can be redirected among products within technical limits. Infrastructure gives future software somewhere to land.

The phrase “somewhere to land” is useful because software abundance can otherwise become trapped in demonstrations.

A model that can design a better molecule needs experimental infrastructure. A model that can optimize a warehouse needs robots, sensors and operational permission. A model that can write industrial control code needs secure systems capable of accepting it. A model that can improve a chip needs a path through verification and fabrication. A model that can recommend grid upgrades needs utilities, regulators, manufacturers and construction firms capable of executing them.

Intelligence without a landing zone remains latent capacity.

The supply chain creates landing zones.

It also reveals where an apparent intelligence problem is actually an infrastructure problem. When a model response is too expensive, the answer may be a smaller model, a better algorithm, a more efficient accelerator, cheaper memory, higher utilization or lower-cost power. When an agent is too slow, the cause may be reasoning, tool latency, network calls or a human approval step. When a robot fails economically, the issue may be model capability, hardware cost, maintenance, safety procedures or the layout of the facility.

Once the system is viewed as a chain, “AI progress” stops being a single category.

This is uncomfortable for forecasting because chains multiply uncertainty. It is useful for building because chains expose leverage.

The industry is currently pouring effort into almost every link at once. That simultaneity is unusual. Advanced logic, memory, packaging, optics, networking, cooling, power generation, grid connection, model architecture, inference software, agent systems and robotics are all receiving extraordinary investment and attention. Some of that spending will be wasted. Some bottlenecks will persist longer than expected. New ones will appear.

But a runway does not require perfection.

It requires enough connected capacity that acceleration can continue.

The central danger in thinking only about models is that we notice the moment an engine becomes powerful and miss the years in which the pavement was being poured.

The central danger in thinking only about infrastructure is the reverse: we count megawatts and chips while missing the possibility that software changes what every unit of infrastructure can do.

Takeoff lives in the interaction.

The chip makes the model possible.

The model helps design the next system.

The next system produces more useful intelligence per dollar and per watt.

Lower cost creates more demand.

Demand finances more physical capacity.

Capacity makes larger or more numerous experiments possible.

Some of those experiments improve the system again.

No individual link has to become magical.

The supply chain only has to start closing the loop.
