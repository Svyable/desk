# Liftoff

The wheels leave the ground after the important work has already happened.

That is what makes takeoff a deceptive metaphor.

The visible moment is brief.

The causes are distributed across the aircraft, the engines, the weather, the runway, the fuel, the controls and the speed accumulated before anyone can point to a single instant and say: now.

AI takeoff is likely to be similar.

If it happens, the world may spend years arguing about whether it has begun while the underlying production system is already changing its generation interval.

The first mistake will be waiting for a declaration.

No authority can certify that civilization has entered takeoff.

A company may announce artificial general intelligence.

A benchmark may show extraordinary performance.

An agent may complete a week-long task.

A robot may perform a difficult sequence.

None of those events alone is liftoff in the sense used by this book.

Liftoff is a property of the system.

It occurs when intelligence becomes good enough, cheap enough, persistent enough and connected enough to the machinery of its own expansion that the rate of bottleneck removal begins increasing materially because of the intelligence already deployed.

That definition is deliberately circular.

Takeoff is a feedback phenomenon.

The output has to reach back into the input.

If better AI produces better software but software improvement has negligible effect on future AI, there is no strong loop.

If better AI accelerates chip design but fabrication dominates the calendar completely, the loop is weak.

If better AI produces scientific ideas but laboratories cannot test them faster, the loop is capped.

If better agents can operate infrastructure but institutions refuse them meaningful permissions, the loop is interrupted.

If data-center investment cannot earn enough to finance another wave, the capital loop weakens.

Liftoff requires enough loops to close with enough gain.

This is why it may arrive gradually and then feel sudden.

At first, each feedback is small.

Coding agents save some engineering time.

Scheduling software recovers some compute.

AI assists chip layout.

Models improve experimental planning.

Automated laboratories run overnight.

Robots handle a few more physical tasks.

Permitting systems become more digital.

Power operators use AI to improve forecasting.

Each gain looks incremental inside its own sector.

The system effect appears when those gains begin stacking inside the same production cycle.

A better model helps improve the compiler.

The compiler improves utilization.

Utilization frees compute for experiments.

Agents help run more experiments.

The experiments produce a better model.

The better model improves hardware design.

The hardware increases performance per watt.

The same power allocation serves more intelligence.

Cheaper intelligence makes more automation economical.

More automation improves the processes building the next data center.

The next data center arrives sooner.

The loop passes through several industries.

No single company controls it.

The aggregate can still accelerate.

Liftoff therefore resembles economic growth more than a software event.

It is emergent.

It appears in the interaction of technologies, institutions and capital.

This makes prediction hard because every link has its own delay and uncertainty.

A model forecast can be reasonably accurate and the takeoff forecast still wrong because electricity or regulation moves differently.

A power forecast can be accurate and the takeoff forecast still wrong because agent reliability stalls.

A robotics forecast can be accurate and the takeoff forecast still wrong because the dominant feedback occurs through software and chip design instead.

The roadmap is multi-causal.

That is a feature.

Single-cause predictions are fragile in systems with moving bottlenecks.

The more useful question is what combination of observations would justify saying liftoff is underway.

The first would be sustained declines in the cost of accepted cognitive work, not just inference.

Machine output would need to survive evaluation cheaply enough that organizations can expand its use without expanding human review proportionally.

The second would be sustained extension of autonomous task horizons in economically meaningful environments.

Agents would need to remain productive long enough that one person can supervise many concurrent objectives.

The third would be evidence that AI is materially increasing the rate of AI research and engineering.

Not occasional anecdotes.

Routine contributions to experiments, infrastructure, algorithms, model design or tooling that shorten the next development cycle.

The fourth would be growing machine participation in hardware and infrastructure optimization.

Software-created virtual capacity would rise while AI-assisted design improves the next hardware generation.

The fifth would be physical supply responding rather than freezing.

Power, chips, packaging, networking, cooling and construction would have to expand or be substituted around quickly enough that demand does not simply pile into queues.

The sixth would be falling bottleneck half-life.

When a new constraint appears, the system would mobilize capital, engineering and substitution faster than before.

The seventh would be expanding permission.

Institutions would allow machines to take more consequential actions under structured controls because failure rates and auditability justify it.

The eighth would be broader calendar compression.

Product development, scientific experiments, software releases and infrastructure planning would complete more validated cycles per year.

No item is sufficient by itself.

Several moving together would be difficult to dismiss.

This offers a way to distinguish liftoff from a bubble.

A bubble can produce enormous spending.

It can produce construction cranes, stock-market enthusiasm and extravagant forecasts.

It does not necessarily produce shrinking cycle times.

Capital expenditure is input.

Takeoff is output feeding back into input.

If hundreds of billions are spent and the next unit of useful AI capacity does not arrive faster or cheaper, the runway may be overbuilt rather than self-extending.

Conversely, takeoff can advance even during financial disappointment if efficiency and capability continue improving through the installed base.

The financial cycle and technical cycle are connected but not identical.

History is full of technologies whose investors lost money while society gained infrastructure.

Railroads transformed economies through bankruptcies.

Fiber networks built during a bubble became useful later.

Industrial overcapacity can become a platform for the next wave.

A capital bust would slow the runway, but it would not automatically erase the machinery already built.

This is one reason physical investment creates persistence.

A data center remains after a stock price falls.

A transmission line remains.

A fab remains.

A trained workforce remains.

The installed base gives future intelligence something to operate.

Liftoff can therefore be discontinuous financially while continuous industrially.

The more consequential discontinuity is in expectations.

Organizations plan differently once they believe cycle times will continue falling.

A company that expects a capability to improve dramatically within eighteen months may delay a fixed investment.

A company that expects machine labor to become cheaper may redesign processes before the capability fully arrives.

A utility that expects persistent data-center demand may build generation earlier.

A government that expects strategic competition may subsidize fabs or grid capacity.

Expectations pull future infrastructure into the present.

This is another feedback loop.

Belief in takeoff can help build runway.

It can also create waste.

Expectations can overshoot.

This is why the instrument panel matters. The system should be judged against operational evidence, not narrative momentum.

One of the strongest signs of liftoff would be a shift in corporate planning from adoption to anticipation.

Early in a technology cycle, firms ask whether they should use the tool.

Later they assume the tool will be present and redesign around its expected future state.

The distinction is visible in infrastructure decisions.

A company does not build a new data center because it wants to experiment with a chatbot.

It builds because it expects enormous future computation to be economically useful.

A manufacturer does not redesign a factory around robots because a robot video was impressive.

It does so because it expects reliable machine labor to improve over the asset’s life.

Long-lived capital reveals serious expectations.

It is a bet on the future capability curve.

The runway already contains many such bets.

The question is whether they become self-validating through real productivity.

Another sign would be changes in the unit of economic planning.

Companies today budget headcount, server instances, advertising dollars, square footage and capital equipment.

In a takeoff economy they may increasingly budget autonomous work capacity.

How many concurrent agent-hours does a process require?

What level of model is necessary?

How much verification budget?

What permission tier?

What human exception capacity?

Machine cognition becomes a managed resource.

This is similar to the way computing moved from a specialized department toward a general operating expense embedded everywhere.

Once a resource becomes infrastructural, organizations stop asking whether they use it and start asking how much and where.

Intelligence can cross that threshold.

The consequence for labor is not simply replacement.

The human role moves toward the points where scarcity survives.

Objective setting.

Judgment under ambiguity.

Relationship.

Accountability.

Physical intervention.

Legitimate authority.

Taste.

Care.

Some of these may also receive machine assistance.

The point is not that a permanent list can be named.

The point is that value migrates toward the remaining constraints.

People will follow the value.

This can produce enormous transition stress.

A skill that was scarce can become abundant quickly.

Wages and career ladders do not adjust smoothly.

Institutions train people for jobs that may be reorganizing faster than education systems update.

Assets can lose value.

Regions can gain or lose industrial importance.

The runway is an economic transformation before it is a philosophical one.

That transformation creates political feedback.

Benefits that feel diffuse and disruptions that feel concentrated are difficult to govern. A consumer may enjoy cheaper services while a profession experiences a visible loss of bargaining power. A country may gain productivity while a town loses a major employer.

Political resistance can become a bottleneck.

This is not irrational.

People live through transitions locally.

A takeoff that ignores distribution can build its own brakes.

Policy therefore joins the runway again.

Retraining, income support, mobility, competition policy, infrastructure investment and new forms of social insurance can influence whether societies accept acceleration or fight it.

The exact policies will vary. The structural point is that social adaptation has a cycle time too.

If technology changes faster than institutions can renegotiate expectations, legitimacy strain grows.

Liftoff can be technically possible and politically unstable.

A durable takeoff would require adaptation mechanisms that also become faster.

This may be the hardest bottleneck because human institutions deliberately move slower than software in order to aggregate interests and preserve continuity.

Some slowness is protective.

The goal cannot be to make democracy run at inference speed.

The goal is to make institutions capable of responding before obsolete assumptions become catastrophic.

This is another version of high-throughput safety.

The system accelerates while retaining control.

There is a geopolitical version too.

Intelligence is a strategic input.

Countries will care about chips, power, models, data centers, talent, networks and supply chains because these affect economic and military capability.

Competition can accelerate investment.

It can also fragment the system.

Export controls, security rules and rival standards can reduce global efficiency while increasing national resilience.

Takeoff may therefore occur through partially separated industrial systems rather than one global stack.

This can produce duplication.

Duplication is inefficient in static economics.

It can increase resilience and experimentation in dynamic systems.

Several competing stacks may discover different solutions to the same bottleneck.

The global runway becomes multiple runways.

That makes the trajectory less predictable, not less consequential.

What would it feel like from inside liftoff?

Probably frustrating.

That is not the answer people expect.

They imagine omnipotence.

A moving-bottleneck world feels like permanent shortage.

Yesterday there were not enough chips.

Today there is not enough power.

Tomorrow there are not enough electricians.

Then not enough transformers.

Then not enough reviewers.

Then not enough laboratories.

Then not enough robot bodies.

Every expansion creates demand that outruns another complement.

People living through the industrial revolution did not experience it as a clean upward curve. They experienced crowded cities, financial panics, labor conflict, pollution, dangerous machinery, fortunes, bankruptcies and constant rebuilding.

Acceleration is messy from the inside.

A civilization in takeoff can remain dissatisfied because expectations move faster than supply.

This is why abundance and scarcity coexist.

Intelligence can become radically abundant while scarce complements become painfully valuable.

The economic signal of liftoff may be extreme price pressure in things that intelligence cannot yet copy.

Land near power.

Grid equipment.

Specialized manufacturing.

Trusted human judgment.

Physical construction.

Certain minerals.

Certain regulatory permissions.

The scarce edges reveal where the runway is touching reality.

Then intelligence attacks those edges.

The process repeats.

At some point, if the half-life of those constraints keeps falling, a qualitative change occurs.

The system no longer waits for ordinary human institutional cycles to identify every problem, coordinate every response and execute every solution.

Machine intelligence participates throughout the response.

It detects the constraint.

Models alternatives.

Designs components.

Writes software.

Schedules resources.

Prepares documents.

Controls equipment.

Monitors outcomes.

Updates the plan.

Humans still set boundaries, own capital, authorize consequential actions and inhabit the world being changed.

But the number of coordination steps requiring scarce human cognition falls.

The system can traverse a bottleneck faster.

That is liftoff.

Not infinite speed.

Not omniscience.

Not the end of scarcity.

A change in the derivative.

Capacity is no longer merely increasing.

The rate at which capacity can be increased is itself increasing because intelligence has entered the process that increases it.

This definition is less dramatic than a machine waking up.

Its consequences are larger.

An awakening is a moment.

A production function compounds.

Once the machinery of expansion becomes more intelligent each cycle, the future stops being governed by the old calendar.

The map becomes unreliable because the time required to reach each point is changing while we travel.

That is the point at which a roadmap becomes a runway.

And the runway becomes flight.
