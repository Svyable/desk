# The Price of Acceleration

The first time a technology becomes cheap, people usually use it to do old things for less money.

The second time, they begin doing things that were previously too trivial, too speculative or too personal to justify the cost.

The second change is larger.

Artificial intelligence is moving through that transition now. The price of useful machine cognition has been falling quickly enough that an old unit of economic thought is becoming unstable: the professional hour.

For most of modern business, a large class of activities has been rationed by the cost of competent human attention. You did not ask a lawyer to review every ordinary email. You did not hire a programmer to automate every repetitive spreadsheet. You did not pay an analyst to investigate every weak hypothesis. You did not commission a designer to make fifty versions of a minor internal slide. You did not employ a tutor for every question a child might ask after dinner.

The work was not necessarily unhelpful.

It was uneconomic.

When the price falls by orders of magnitude, the old demand curve stops describing behavior.

Stanford’s AI Index has documented a sharp decline in the cost of querying systems at a given benchmark level. One comparison estimates that inference producing roughly GPT-3.5-level performance on a common benchmark moved from about twenty dollars per million tokens in late 2022 to only cents per million tokens by late 2024. The exact benchmark, the exact price and the exact model are not the point. They will all become dated.

The order of magnitude is the point.

At twenty dollars, a million tokens are a purchase.

At cents, they are a habit.

A resource does not need to be free to become abundant. It only needs to become cheap enough that the user stops conserving it in the old way.

Photography changed when the marginal cost of another picture approached zero. People did not merely take the same family portraits more cheaply. They photographed receipts, parking spots, whiteboards, pets, meals, bruises, serial numbers, store shelves, lecture slides and moments nobody would have spent film on. Digital storage changed when the question shifted from “What should we delete?” to “Why bother deleting it?” Bandwidth changed when households stopped thinking in pages and started thinking in streams.

Cheap cognition will produce its own strange categories.

Disposable analysis.

Temporary software.

Personalized explanations made for one person and never read again.

A translation that exists for the length of a conversation.

A simulation built only to expose one assumption.

A contract summarized from three opposing points of view and then discarded.

A hundred design variations generated so that ninety-nine can be rejected.

An agent sent down an avenue of research precisely because the avenue is probably wrong.

These uses are not substitutions for existing invoices.

They are demand that did not exist because the old price prevented the question from being asked.

This is why cost matters to takeoff independently of capability.

A model that can perform a task but costs more than the task is worth remains a demonstration. A model that performs the task imperfectly but cheaply may become useful when it can be run many times, checked, compared, routed or combined with other systems. Reliability and price interact.

Suppose a machine-generated plan is only moderately useful on the first attempt. If a second attempt costs almost nothing, the user can ask for alternatives. If verification is cheap, the system can check its own work through independent methods. If running a small model against thousands of documents is affordable, a task that would be absurd to assign to a human team becomes routine.

Economics can make capability compositional.

That effect is easy to underestimate because benchmark charts usually measure the quality of one attempt.

Real systems can spend.

They can sample several candidates. They can call tools. They can retrieve information. They can run tests. They can use a stronger model only when a weaker model is uncertain. They can debate among agents, execute code, search databases and retry failures. Inference-time computation turns the price of intelligence into a design variable.

The result is that declining token cost does more than make the same answer cheaper. It expands the number of architectures that are economically sensible.

A company can wrap a model in verification. A consumer product can personalize output for each user. A scientific system can generate thousands of candidates and filter them. A security service can inspect activity that no human team would read line by line. A programmer can launch several agents on different implementation strategies and keep the one that passes tests.

The cheap model becomes raw material for systems.

This creates an apparent paradox at the infrastructure layer.

If inference becomes vastly more efficient, electricity use can still rise.

There is nothing mysterious about this. Lower prices expand demand. The history of computing is full of efficiency gains that increased total consumption because they pushed computation into more places. A more efficient transistor did not cause society to want fewer transistors. It made it rational to place them inside objects that had never contained computers.

AI infrastructure is already being planned around that possibility.

The International Energy Agency projects substantial growth in data-center electricity demand through the end of the decade. The numbers are uncertain because the underlying variables are uncertain: model architectures, hardware efficiency, utilization, demand, data-center design, regional policy and the share of AI workloads all move at once. Forecasts should be treated as scenarios rather than prophecies.

Yet the direction of pressure is visible.

The industry is not behaving as if efficiency will end demand growth.

It is behaving as if efficiency will unlock more intelligence.

This is the first feedback loop on the runway.

Cheaper inference creates more uses.

More uses create more demand.

Demand justifies more infrastructure.

More infrastructure gives researchers more capacity to train, evaluate and deploy new systems.

Better systems can make each unit of compute more valuable.

The loop is economic before it is recursive.

That distinction matters because a takeoff does not require a model to rewrite its own source code in isolation. It can begin when falling costs and expanding demand finance the resources that make further capability progress possible.

Capital markets can accelerate a technology long before the technology becomes self-improving.

They can also overshoot spectacularly.

The history of infrastructure booms is full of people who correctly identified a transformative technology and still lost money. Railroads expanded economic possibility while bankrupting investors. Fiber-optic capacity built during the dot-com boom contributed to a later internet economy after many of the firms that financed it had collapsed. A useful resource can be overvalued. A bad investment can leave behind good infrastructure.

AI may repeat that pattern in its own form.

A data center can be strategically useful and financially disappointing. An accelerator can transform a workflow while its manufacturer’s valuation assumes impossible margins. A model can create enormous consumer surplus while model providers struggle to capture enough of that surplus to justify their fixed costs.

These are different questions.

Is the intelligence useful?

Can the provider charge for it?

Can the infrastructure owner earn an acceptable return on the capital?

Will the social benefits exceed the social costs?

A single word like “value” hides all four.

The fixed-cost structure of frontier AI makes the distinction sharper.

The data center is expensive.

The next token is cheap.

Training a frontier system can require immense capital. Serving one additional response on an installed system may cost very little relative to the value of the work it helps perform. This gap encourages enormous scale because fixed costs are easier to support when spread across more usage.

It also encourages price competition.

If several providers offer models that are good enough for the same task, the marginal price of intelligence can fall toward the cost of serving it. Open models can add pressure. Specialized smaller models can undercut general systems. Hardware and software efficiency can lower costs further. The provider with the smartest model may not capture the full value created by intelligence if competitors make “good enough” capability abundant.

Value moves to the scarce complement.

This is the economic form of the moving bottleneck.

When writing becomes cheap, editing and trust can become more valuable.

When code generation becomes cheap, product judgment, distribution, maintenance and proprietary integration can become more valuable.

When legal analysis becomes cheap, authority to make a binding legal decision remains scarce.

When scientific hypotheses become cheap, experimental throughput becomes more valuable.

When machine intelligence becomes cheap, energy, physical access, verification, reputation and permission can capture more of the surplus.

The winners of an intelligence boom may therefore include companies whose competitive advantage is not intelligence at all.

They own the doors intelligence wants to walk through.

This helps explain the strategic interest in data.

A general model can know an enormous amount about the world and still lack the particular information required to do work inside an organization. It does not know the latest state of an internal project unless it can read the project system. It cannot answer a customer’s account question without access to the account. It cannot schedule a shipment without seeing inventory and logistics data. It cannot make an authorized payment merely because it understands accounting.

Cheap cognition increases the value of connected context.

The model becomes less scarce.

The authenticated, current, proprietary state becomes more scarce.

This is why the agent era is likely to create battles over interfaces rather than only models. The company that controls a system of record can decide what an agent can see and do. The platform that becomes the trusted gateway for permissions can sit between abundant intelligence and valuable action. Standards that make tools interoperable can weaken some gatekeepers while strengthening others.

Price changes architecture.

It changes labor markets too, but not in the simple way of replacing expensive humans with cheap machines.

A low-cost cognitive system can complement workers before it substitutes for them. Research on customer-support agents found that generative AI improved productivity on average and disproportionately helped less experienced workers. That makes sense if the system supplies patterns, language and knowledge that experienced workers have already accumulated.

The same technology can have a different effect on experts.

METR’s randomized study of experienced open-source developers using early-2025 AI coding tools found that participants were slower on the evaluated tasks even though they expected to be faster. The developers spent time prompting, waiting, reviewing and correcting. They worked in repositories they knew deeply, which reduced the value of machine assistance and increased the cost of checking it.

A cheap tool can still be expensive in attention.

That observation will matter repeatedly in this book.

The true price of AI is not the API price.

It includes verification time, integration, latency, error recovery, security, training, organizational disruption and the opportunity cost of human supervision. A five-cent answer that consumes twenty minutes of expert review can cost more than a fifty-dollar answer that can be trusted.

Takeoff requires the total price of useful output to fall.

That is a harder standard.

It is also a more encouraging one, because there are many ways to reduce total cost.

Models can become more accurate. Verification can become automated. Better interfaces can reduce prompting. Agents can learn the context of a repository or organization. Permissions can become standardized. Tests can catch errors cheaply. Smaller models can handle routine work and escalate difficult cases. Hardware can improve. Networks can reduce latency. Organizations can redesign processes so machine output enters at the right point instead of creating another queue.

The economic unit shifts from token to completed objective.

That shift is already visible in how AI products are marketed. The early generative systems sold access to a model. Agentic systems increasingly sell work: a coding task completed, a support ticket resolved, a research job run, a workflow maintained. As the unit of value moves upward, customers care less about how many tokens were consumed and more about whether the objective was achieved.

This is familiar from earlier computing transitions. Most people do not buy CPU instructions. They buy software. They do not buy database queries. They buy an application that keeps track of customers or payroll. Infrastructure becomes strategically important while disappearing from the user’s mental model.

AI may follow the same path.

The intelligence becomes infrastructure.

The product becomes the outcome.

If that happens, usage can grow far beyond the visible market for chatbots because the intelligence is embedded inside other products and processes. A person may consume millions of machine-generated decisions without thinking of themselves as an AI user, just as they consume enormous quantities of database activity without thinking of themselves as database users.

This hidden demand is one reason the runway may lengthen even if public excitement cools.

Hype is not a prerequisite for infrastructure adoption.

A company can stop talking about AI and quietly place models inside fraud detection, logistics, software testing, customer service, forecasting, maintenance and internal search. In fact, mature adoption often becomes less visible because the technology disappears into normal operations.

The strongest signal of economic takeoff may be boredom.

Nobody announces that a spreadsheet calculated a formula correctly. Nobody marvels that a server returned a web page. Technologies become economically powerful when they stop demanding special attention.

For AI, that transition requires prices low enough that intelligence can be used indiscriminately in places where humans would never have been assigned.

Imagine software that reviews every change, not only risky ones.

A tutor that responds to every confusion, not only scheduled questions.

A compliance system that reads every transaction, not a sample.

A maintenance agent that monitors every machine continuously.

A design system that explores thousands of alternatives before a human sees ten.

A scientific agent that maintains hundreds of weak hypotheses because the carrying cost is negligible.

The amount of cognitive work explodes even if the amount of human work does not fall proportionally.

This is why arguments about whether AI “saves labor” can miss the larger change.

The economy may consume much more cognition than humans ever supplied.

If a small business gains access to analysis it never would have purchased, nobody loses an analyst hour because no analyst hour existed. If a child receives personalized explanation at midnight, no tutoring appointment was necessarily displaced. If an open-source maintainer uses agents to inspect an obscure dependency nobody had time to audit, the work is additive.

New demand expands the category.

Then the category begins altering expectations.

Once instant translation is normal, people become less tolerant of untranslated material. Once software can be personalized cheaply, users expect customization. Once every proposal can be stress-tested from several perspectives, a proposal that was not tested may look negligent. Once machine review becomes common, the standard of care can rise.

Abundance becomes obligation.

That creates another demand loop.

The more intelligence is available, the more intelligence organizations may be expected to use.

This dynamic can push adoption through institutions that would otherwise move slowly. It can also create waste. Generating analysis because analysis is cheap does not mean the analysis deserves attention. A world of abundant intelligence can drown itself in plausible output.

This is the central economic tension of the runway.

Production gets cheaper faster than consumption.

A model can generate a hundred pages in minutes. A person cannot read them in minutes. An agent can create thousands of software changes. An organization cannot safely merge thousands of changes without tests, ownership and maintenance. A scientific system can propose molecules faster than a laboratory can synthesize them.

Cheap cognition pushes scarcity toward selection.

The price of acceleration therefore has two sides.

The monetary price falls.

The filtering price rises.

Takeoff becomes plausible when machines begin helping with the second side as effectively as they help with the first. If AI can generate options but humans must evaluate every option, human attention remains the throttle. If AI can also rank, test, falsify, monitor and escalate, the throttle opens.

This is why verification will eventually matter as much as generation.

It is why infrastructure matters as much as model capability.

And it is why the cost curve beneath intelligence is not merely a story about cheaper tokens.

The runway gets longer when useful cognition becomes cheap enough to be wasted.

That sounds perverse until you remember that abundance is full of waste. We leave lights on. We take bad photographs. We store files we will never open. We stream video nobody finishes. We run software experiments that fail.

Civilizations discover new possibilities by spending cheap resources carelessly.

The important question is what becomes cheap enough to waste next.

When the answer is intelligence, the rate of experimentation can change.

And experimentation is one of the few forms of waste that reliably finds the future.
