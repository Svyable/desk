# Research — The Great American Upgrade

This directory is the working research trail for *The Great American Upgrade: Twenty-Six Inventions, Twenty-Six Decades, and the People Who Remade America*.

The manuscript makes one intentionally contestable selection for every named decade touched by the United States from 1776 through 2026. The research trail therefore has two jobs: support the concrete historical claims in the prose and make the ranking logic visible enough that a later editor can disagree with it intelligently.

## What “most important” means here

There is no composite score and no attempt to convert deaths prevented, productivity, cultural influence, military power, environmental cost, or descendant technologies into one number.

The manuscript instead uses four recurring questions as judgment tests:

1. **Reach:** how many people, sectors, places, or institutions eventually reorganized around the capability?
2. **Depth:** how strongly did it change what those people or institutions could do?
3. **Persistence:** did the capability survive beyond the original product form or company?
4. **Dependency:** how much of what followed quietly assumed the invention existed?

Dependency receives extra weight. That is why the list often favors enabling technologies and infrastructure over more spectacular objects.

Importance is not treated as moral approval. The cotton gin is the clearest case: a technically effective labor-saving machine helped strengthen and expand an economy built on enslaved labor. Similar dual accounting appears for synthetic polymers and waste, air conditioning and energy demand, automobiles and mass production, network technologies and concentrated gatekeeping, smartphones and surveillance, and generative AI and authenticity.

## What “American” means

The book is about inventions that remade America during America’s 250-year history, not a nationalist claim that every underlying idea was first conceived by a U.S.-born inventor on U.S. soil.

Where the ancestry is international, the manuscript says so. Examples include:

- electromagnetism: Ørsted, Ampère, Sturgeon, Henry, Faraday and others across Europe and the United States;
- steamboats: multiple American and European experimental lineages before Fulton’s commercial threshold;
- alternating current: Tesla, Westinghouse, Ferraris, Stanley and wider electrical-engineering work;
- the microprocessor: Intel and the Japanese calculator company Busicom, including Masatoshi Shima’s engineering role;
- the Web: invented by Tim Berners-Lee at CERN, with the American 1990s chapter awarded to the graphical browser interface that accelerated mass adoption;
- mRNA and CRISPR: multinational scientific lineages and commercialization ecosystems.

The manuscript distinguishes **first conception**, **first practical demonstration**, **commercial threshold**, **mass adoption**, and **historical importance** wherever those are meaningfully different.

## Source hierarchy

For claims they can answer directly, prefer:

1. original scientific papers, patents, technical reports, contemporary documents, and archival records;
2. U.S. government agencies and national archives;
3. Smithsonian Institution, Library of Congress, National Park Service, Computer History Museum, Nobel Prize and comparable institutional histories;
4. universities and the institutions where the work occurred;
5. reputable modern historical scholarship and high-quality journalism for contested interpretation or narrative context;
6. company histories only for company-specific dates, products, or documented internal lineage, and with caution around self-promotional claims.

A company saying it “invented” a category is never treated as sufficient evidence of sole priority when credible competing claims exist.

## Credit rules

The manuscript does not manufacture a cleaner inventor story than the evidence supports.

- A patent is evidence of a legal claim and date, not proof that no related work preceded it.
- Near-simultaneous invention is preserved rather than resolved by choosing a favorite.
- Employees, collaborators, customers, manufacturing engineers, institutions, government purchasers, and earlier scientific contributors are included when their role changes the causal story.
- Where the historical archive is structurally incomplete—especially around enslaved labor, tacit craft knowledge, or contributors excluded from formal institutions—the prose should state the limitation rather than invent a compensating anecdote.
- Quotations are not used unless traceable to a source. Paraphrase is preferred when the exact wording is unnecessary.

## Runner-ups and falsification

Each chapter brief records at least one serious rival and a falsification note.

A falsification note is not a claim that historical ranking can be scientifically disproved. It is a discipline: state which assumption is carrying the choice, then identify evidence that would weaken or reverse it.

For example:

- air conditioning beats the airplane in the 1900s because the ranking weights ordinary cross-sector dependency and controlled indoor climate more heavily than transport impact; a different weighting of global mobility could reasonably reverse it;
- the transistor beats the atomic bomb in the 1940s because distributed downstream dependency is weighted more heavily than concentrated geopolitical consequence;
- the graphical browser beats lithium-ion batteries in the 1990s because the browser reorganized publishing, commerce, software distribution, and knowledge through one mass interface; a ranking weighted toward physical mobility and electrification could reverse it;
- generative AI is the provisional 2020s winner only if it becomes a durable general-purpose layer across cognitive work. The chapter itself states how mRNA or another technology could take the title by decade end.

## Current-information cutoff

The working manuscript’s current-information cutoff is **September 6, 2026**.

Claims about the unfinished 2020s should be checked again before publication. In particular, the 2020s winner should be revisited after the decade closes. Product adoption, regulatory approvals, model capability, infrastructure investment, therapeutic results, and market structure are snapshots rather than permanent facts.

## Files

- `chapter-briefs.md` records the winner, causal thesis, runner-up, and falsification question for every decade.
- `source-ledger.csv` records checkable sources tied to chapter claims and caveats.

The source ledger is the canonical working record for later endnote production. It is not intended to make the published prose read like a bibliography. The manuscript should sound researched because the history is metabolized into the argument, not because citations interrupt every paragraph.
