# The Computer Becomes a Part

The computer became smaller for years before it became small enough to disappear.

Mainframes filled rooms. Minicomputers filled cabinets. Integrated circuits reduced size and cost, but a computer still looked like a system assembled from many specialized chips and boards.

Then the processor became a component.

In 1971, Intel announced the 4004, a general-purpose programmable microprocessor developed for a Japanese calculator project. It contained about 2,300 transistors and could perform the central processing functions of a small computer on a single chip.

The object was modest.

The category was enormous.

The microprocessor wins the 1970s because it changed computing from a machine you bought into a capability you could put inside another machine.

That sentence is the bridge to the present.

Before the microprocessor, a product designer who wanted electronic computation had to assemble processing logic from multiple components or purchase a larger computing system. After the microprocessor matured, computation could become one line item in a bill of materials.

Add processor.

Add memory.

Add software.

The product can now behave.

This is why the microprocessor matters more than the first personal computers that followed it. The personal computer was an application of cheap programmable processing. The microprocessor was the general capability that allowed not only personal computers but thousands of other products to become computational.

Cars acquired engine controls.

Industrial machines acquired digital controllers.

Calculators became tiny.

Toys acquired behavior.

Medical devices acquired logic.

Telecommunications equipment became programmable.

Cameras, appliances, instruments, weapons, and eventually nearly every category of engineered object could contain a computer without looking like one.

The computer stopped being the product and became an ingredient.

The Intel 4004 story is unusually good at punishing national and corporate mythology because the chip emerged from a transpacific collaboration.

Busicom, a Japanese calculator company, approached Intel with plans for a family of calculator chips. The initial proposal involved multiple specialized integrated circuits. Intel engineer Marcian “Ted” Hoff argued for a more general programmable architecture that could reduce the number of custom chips required. Stanley Mazor contributed to architectural work. Federico Faggin led the crucial chip design and silicon implementation that turned the concept into manufacturable hardware. Masatoshi Shima, working from Busicom, played an important role in logic design and coordination.

The first commercial microprocessor was not an American idea delivered to a passive foreign customer.

It was a negotiated design problem between companies, engineers, and markets on opposite sides of the Pacific.

That matters because the mythology of Silicon Valley often treats customers as merely buyers.

Busicom’s requirements created the problem.

The customer helped invent the product category by being demanding enough to require a new architecture.

This pattern is common in industrial technology. Sophisticated customers push suppliers into capabilities the suppliers later sell elsewhere. Airlines influence aircraft design. The military influences electronics. Automobile manufacturers pressure materials and semiconductor suppliers. Large cloud companies push chip designers and data-center equipment makers.

Demand can conduct research.

Intel itself was young. Founded in 1968 by Robert Noyce and Gordon Moore, the company initially focused strongly on semiconductor memory. The calculator project was not obviously the foundation of a future processor empire.

That uncertainty is important.

Technologies later remembered as strategic breakthroughs often begin as side problems inside companies pursuing something else.

The 4004 was built for calculators.

The Web was built to solve information-sharing problems among physicists.

The smartphone emerged by combining communication, computing, and media technologies already serving different markets.

Generative language models grew partly from research on predicting sequences of text.

The killer application rarely arrives carrying a label that says HISTORY STARTS HERE.

The microprocessor’s technical significance was programmability.

Custom electronic logic does what its hardware is built to do. A programmable processor can execute different instructions depending on software. The same physical chip can therefore perform different functions without redesigning the silicon each time.

Software separates behavior from hardware.

That is one of the most powerful abstractions ever invented.

A typewriter’s mechanism determines what it does.

A microprocessor waits to be told.

The hardware becomes general enough that new products can be created by changing code.

This reduces the cost of experimentation because behavior can be revised after fabrication. It allows one processor design to serve many markets. It creates an ecosystem in which software developers become inventors without owning semiconductor factories.

Programmability democratizes one layer by concentrating another.

You no longer need a fab to create new behavior.

You do need access to a computing platform somebody else fabricated.

The result is a division of innovation between hardware and software.

This division would generate entire industries.

Microsoft could become enormously valuable without manufacturing processors.

Software companies could ship products by copying code rather than building physical machines.

Developers could create applications that run on standardized hardware.

The marginal cost of reproducing software approached zero compared with manufactured goods.

The economics of invention changed.

A mechanical product must be fabricated again for every customer.

Software can be duplicated almost perfectly at negligible distribution cost once written.

This created extraordinary returns to successful programs and new forms of market concentration. The company that owns a dominant operating system can reach millions of machines without building each application separately. Network effects, standards, and compatibility can make software markets tip strongly toward leaders.

The microprocessor did not create monopoly.

It created a substrate on which software scale became extreme.

The 1970s contain competitors that could plausibly win the entire book, not merely the decade.

The modern GPS program began in 1973 under the U.S. Department of Defense, building on earlier satellite-navigation systems. The first GPS satellites launched later in the decade and the system matured over the following years. GPS eventually changed navigation, logistics, agriculture, finance, telecommunications timing, warfare, mapping, and smartphones.

It is an extraordinary invention.

The personal computer also emerged in recognizable form. The Altair 8800 appeared in 1975. Apple Computer was founded in 1976, and the Apple II became a major early mass-market machine in 1977. Video games entered homes. Digital cameras were prototyped. Recombinant DNA technology opened biotechnology.

Why not choose recombinant DNA?

In 1973, Stanley Cohen and Herbert Boyer demonstrated techniques for combining DNA from different organisms using plasmids and restriction enzymes, helping create modern genetic engineering and the biotechnology industry. The consequences for medicine and biology are enormous.

The microprocessor wins because programmable computation became the common enabling layer beneath nearly all these fields.

Biotechnology laboratories use computers.

GPS receivers use processors.

Personal computers are made possible directly by processors.

Digital cameras depend on processors.

Telecommunications networks depend on processors.

The microprocessor is the tool that other inventions increasingly assume exists.

That status justifies the win.

The 4004 itself was not powerful by modern standards. Its transistor count was tiny compared with modern chips. It operated on four-bit data. It belonged to a family of supporting chips. Later Intel processors such as the 8008 and 8080 became more influential in general computing. Competing companies developed their own microprocessors. Motorola’s 6800, MOS Technology’s 6502, Zilog’s Z80, and others powered important systems.

The invention category outruns the first product quickly.

This is another warning against fetishizing “first.”

The first microprocessor establishes possibility.

The later processor that hits the right price, software ecosystem, architecture, or market may matter more commercially.

Technology history needs both dates.

Invention and takeoff are not the same event.

The microprocessor also changed product lifecycles. A mechanical product can remain stable for years because its behavior is embodied in hardware. A programmable product can improve through software updates, at least in principle. This sounds like an unqualified benefit until software complexity becomes a new source of failure.

Bugs replace some mechanical defects.

Security vulnerabilities become product defects.

Updates can improve a device and break it.

Manufacturers can change behavior after sale.

The customer may own the hardware and only license parts of the software.

Programmability creates post-purchase power.

This would become central to the modern economy. Cars receive over-the-air updates. Phones change interfaces overnight. Subscription software alters features continuously. Tractors contain software restrictions that affect repair. Medical devices require firmware maintenance. Connected appliances can lose functions when services shut down.

A mechanical object is finished when it leaves the factory.

A software-defined object may never be finished.

That changes ownership in subtle ways.

If the manufacturer can remotely alter the product, what exactly did the buyer purchase?

The 1970s microprocessor contains the ancestor of that question.

Programmability also creates security as a permanent problem. A purely mechanical thermostat cannot be remotely hacked. A programmable networked thermostat can. Put software into cars, factories, hospitals, power grids, and weapons and code defects become physical risks.

The computer as component expands capability and attack surface together.

This is the general-purpose technology bargain.

The more functions a platform can perform, the more harmful functions it can accidentally or deliberately perform.

A calculator chip cannot steal passwords.

A general processor running complex software can.

The microprocessor’s success therefore created entire industries in debugging, cybersecurity, patching, testing, access control, and software assurance.

The invention created the need to distrust instructions.

The labor consequences were equally deep. Software became a major occupation because programmable machines needed instructions. Earlier computers required programmers too, of course, and women played major roles in early programming even when their contributions were undervalued. The microprocessor vastly expanded the number of machines needing code and the number of organizations that could afford computing.

Demand for programmers grew because computation became cheap enough to waste on more problems.

Again efficiency increases total activity.

The cheaper the processor, the more software society writes.

The more software exists, the more maintenance it requires.

A technology sold as automation creates employment maintaining the automation.

This is not contradiction.

It is expansion.

The personal-computer revolution made the mechanism visible. Hobbyists could buy machines and program them. Small firms could own computers without mainframe budgets. Students could learn by direct interaction. The keyboard and screen became a personal interface to programmable logic.

Control moved closer to the user.

This was culturally important because computing had previously been mediated heavily by institutions and specialists. Time-sharing had already made interactive computing more accessible within organizations. The personal computer pushed further: the machine sat on your desk and waited for your instructions.

Personal possession changed imagination.

A person could write software without asking a central computing department for machine time.

That autonomy helped produce games, spreadsheets, word processors, desktop publishing, and countless niche applications.

The microprocessor lowered the permission cost of computing.

It did not eliminate gatekeepers. Hardware platforms, operating systems, retail distribution, and later app stores created new ones.

But the old gate changed.

This is how technological freedom often works: one dependency disappears and another becomes important.

The spreadsheet is a perfect example. VisiCalc, released for the Apple II in 1979, turned the personal computer into a business tool. Accounting and planning models that required manual recalculation could update automatically when inputs changed.

The microprocessor made the spreadsheet possible as a mass-market product.

The spreadsheet then changed finance, management, planning, and organizational decision-making.

A cheap processor therefore indirectly changed how companies imagined the future.

This chain shows why enabling technologies earn disproportionate influence. Their important uses are discovered after they are sold.

The same process drove embedded computing. Manufacturers discovered that a processor could replace custom control logic and add features through software. As chips became cheaper, engineers put them into products where the customer might never know a computer existed.

The washing machine became a computer with a drum.

The automobile became a network of computers with wheels.

The camera became a computer with a lens.

The telephone became a computer with a radio.

The distinction between “computer industry” and “everything else” began to collapse.

That collapse defines the remaining decades.

The microprocessor industry also intensified global manufacturing interdependence. Chip design and fabrication became strategically important. Japanese semiconductor companies rose strongly in memory markets in the 1980s. American firms shifted strategies. Taiwan built foundry capabilities. Design tools became specialized. Manufacturing nodes grew harder and more expensive.

The tiny processor produced giant geopolitical stakes.

The irony is familiar by now.

Miniaturization concentrates industrial complexity upstream.

The device gets smaller.

The factory gets harder.

An advanced semiconductor fab requires facilities and equipment at a scale unimaginable to the customer holding the chip. Precision, contamination control, lithography, materials, metrology, and capital intensify as features shrink.

The user experiences simplicity because the production system absorbs complexity.

This is the core bargain of industrial civilization.

Complexity moves away from the user until it reaches institutions capable of carrying it.

Air conditioning hides refrigeration engineering behind a thermostat.

Telephony hides switching behind a number.

The Web hides networking behind a link.

The microprocessor hides computer architecture behind software.

The more successful the invention, the less the user needs to know.

The counterfactual is crowded. Texas Instruments developed a single-chip processor around the same era. Garrett AiResearch produced a multi-chip central processor for the F-14’s flight computer earlier, though it was not a general commercial single-chip microprocessor in the same sense. Semiconductor firms were moving toward greater integration. The concept was technically near.

If Intel had not produced the 4004, the microprocessor would have arrived.

The question is how quickly and in what architecture.

Intel’s success turned the processor into a product line and then a platform. Compatibility across generations became strategically valuable. Instruction sets became long-lived standards. Software ecosystems reinforced hardware choices.

A chip architecture acquired network effects.

That outcome was not contained in the 4004’s 2,300 transistors.

It grew from millions of later decisions about compatibility.

The microprocessor wins the 1970s because it converted computation from capital equipment into a reusable component.

The computer had already learned to calculate.

Now it learned to hide inside things.

Once it disappeared, it spread everywhere.
