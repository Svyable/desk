# The Magnet That Learned to Work

The 1820s winner was not invented in America.

Good.

A book about American invention that cannot admit this fact would misunderstand how invention works.

In 1825, the English experimenter William Sturgeon demonstrated an electromagnet: a piece of iron made strongly magnetic by electric current flowing through wire. The underlying science came from a rapidly developing international conversation. In 1820, the Danish physicist Hans Christian Ørsted had shown that an electric current could deflect a compass needle, revealing a relationship between electricity and magnetism that had not previously been understood in practical terms. André-Marie Ampère in France developed the theoretical picture. Sturgeon turned the relationship into a useful device. In the United States, Joseph Henry soon made major improvements by changing the way wire was insulated and wound, producing electromagnets of striking strength and showing how different winding configurations could support different electrical purposes.

The invention crossed borders faster than the countries involved could claim it.

That is why it matters.

An electromagnet is conceptually simple enough to describe to a child. Pass current through a coil of wire around suitable iron and the iron becomes magnetic. Stop the current and much of the magnetism disappears. Electricity has been converted into controllable force.

The last word is the revolution.

Controllable.

A permanent magnet pulls whether you want it to or not. An electromagnet can be switched. Its strength can be changed. It can act at a distance through a circuit. It can become one component in a larger system of signaling, motion, switching, measurement, sound, and control.

The electromagnet wins the 1820s because it is a hinge between invisible electrical phenomena and mechanical action.

The telegraph will win the 1830s. Electric motors will become central to industry. Relays will help signals travel. Telephone receivers will convert electrical changes into sound. Generators and motors will make electricity and magnetism reciprocal parts of an energy system. Speakers, transformers, solenoids, doorbells, locks, hard drives, MRI systems, industrial controls, and countless other devices will depend on electromagnetic principles.

The 1820s object itself is small compared with the family it creates.

That is another pattern in this book: enabling inventions often look unimpressive before their descendants arrive.

Imagine encountering an early electromagnet before the telegraph. A current passes through wire and a piece of iron lifts more weight than expected. Interesting. Perhaps useful. But it does not yet look like continental communication, industrial automation, recorded music, electric transportation, data storage, or medical imaging.

The future is latent rather than visible.

This makes the electromagnet an especially strong answer to the decade rule. The Erie Canal opened in 1825 and transformed American commerce, but it is a monumental infrastructure project rather than an invention in the narrower sense used here. Railroads began to emerge. The mechanical reaper was near. Photography was developing abroad. Steam power was improving. Any of those stories could carry a history chapter.

The electromagnet wins because so many later technologies become harder without it.

It also corrects a bad habit in national histories: treating knowledge as cargo that belongs to the place where it was unloaded.

Ørsted was Danish. Ampère was French. Sturgeon was English. Henry was American. Michael Faraday, another Englishman, would soon make foundational discoveries in electromagnetic induction. Samuel Morse would develop his telegraph in the United States with crucial contributions from Alfred Vail and others. The first commercial electrical age was a relay race with runners scattered across the Atlantic world.

A national border can tell us where a laboratory sat. It tells us less about where an idea came from.

Scientific knowledge is especially resistant to patriotic ownership because experiments become inputs to other experiments. A published observation in Copenhagen can become a device in London, an improved coil in Albany, and a communication system between Washington and Baltimore.

The United States’ rise as an inventive nation depended partly on being connected to this circulation.

Joseph Henry’s story is useful because he is often overshadowed by the men who commercialized applications of the principles he explored. Born in Albany in 1797, Henry trained in science at a time when American scientific institutions were still modest. He worked at the Albany Academy and later at the College of New Jersey, now Princeton. His experiments with electromagnets and induction made him one of the most important American physicists of his generation. He eventually became the first secretary of the Smithsonian Institution.

Henry did not build the telegraph business that made Morse famous.

He helped make such a business technically plausible.

The distinction between foundational science and commercial application is one of invention history’s recurring sources of injustice and confusion. The market tends to remember the product. Science remembers the principle. Patent law remembers the claims. National mythology often remembers whoever can be placed in a single dramatic scene.

Real technologies require all of them.

Morse’s eventual telegraph needed a way to turn a weak electrical signal into visible or audible action. Electromagnets provided the conversion. Relays using electromagnets could allow one circuit to control another, helping signals travel farther. Henry’s experiments demonstrated important aspects of these possibilities before Morse’s commercial system matured.

This led to disputes over credit that would echo through later legal and historical arguments.

Priority conflicts are not signs that invention history has gone wrong. They are evidence that technologies emerge from overlapping work.

The patent system tries to draw boundaries around contributions because commerce requires boundaries. Nature does not.

Electricity and magnetism do not care which laboratory discovered which relationship first. Engineers care because careers, money, status, and ownership follow.

The electromagnet also introduces a new kind of machine into this book: a component with no obvious purpose by itself.

The Turtle was a whole vessel. Evans built a milling system. Whitney’s gin processed a crop. Fulton’s boat carried people. Blanchard’s lathe made shapes.

An electromagnet waits to be embedded.

This makes it closer to a transistor than a steamboat. Its power comes from combination. Put it with a switch and a bell. Put it with a moving armature and make a relay. Arrange changing fields and conductors and make a motor or generator. Put it in a receiver and reproduce sound. Put many electromagnetic actuators in an industrial system and control machinery at a distance.

Component inventions change the grammar of design.

Before them, an engineer asks how to build one machine.

After them, engineers ask what machines can be assembled from a new primitive.

That is why basic components can create innovation explosions disproportionate to their size.

The screw, bearing, gear, spring, transistor, integrated circuit, laser, lithium-ion cell, GPS receiver, and software API each becomes more powerful when designers no longer have to reinvent the underlying function. The component turns a hard technical problem into a purchasable assumption.

Electromagnetism would eventually become one of those assumptions.

We can see the transition in the telegraph. Before electrical signaling, long-distance information traveled at the speed of a physical messenger, optical line of sight, or sound over limited distance. Once electrical circuits could control visible or audible indicators far away, information could outrun transportation.

That conceptual break is so large that the telegraph deserves the next decade. But the telegraph’s drama can hide its dependence on a less theatrical device.

Without controlled electromagnetic action at the receiving end, a wire carrying current is just a wire carrying current.

The magnet gives the current a body.

That phrase should not be mistaken for mysticism. It is a mechanical fact. Electrical state becomes motion or force. The abstract becomes actionable.

Modern computing performs a similar conversion in microscopic form. A logical state becomes a voltage range. Transistors switch. Signals propagate. Software eventually causes a motor to turn, a pixel to light, a message to send, a valve to close, or money to move. The apparent immateriality of digital life rests on layers of physical conversion.

The 1820s are where this book first touches that chain directly.

There was an economic context too. Early electrical experiments occurred before electric utilities, before standardized components, before a commercial market for electrical equipment. Researchers built batteries, wound wire, improvised instruments, and exchanged letters and publications. The infrastructure for electrical invention barely existed.

This is easy to underestimate because later industries make earlier science look as if it occurred inside a pipeline designed for commercialization.

It did not.

A laboratory discovery could sit for years before manufacturing, capital, and demand created a market. There were no venture firms waiting to fund “electromagnet startups.” There was no supply chain of insulated copper wire optimized for communication networks. There was no electrical engineering profession in the modern sense.

The profession had to be invented around the phenomenon.

That process is common. New technological domains initially belong to tinkerers, scientists, instrument makers, and enthusiasts whose boundaries blur. As the field becomes economically important, specialized training, standards, journals, companies, suppliers, regulators, and job titles emerge.

Electricity created electricians.

Radio created broadcast engineers.

Computing created programmers and computer scientists.

The internet created network engineers and web developers.

Artificial intelligence is currently creating a vocabulary of occupations whose final form is not yet clear.

An invention can create the people required to maintain it.

The electromagnet also forces a distinction between invention and discovery.

Ørsted discovered an effect. Sturgeon built a device. Henry improved performance and explored configurations. Faraday discovered induction. Engineers later converted these facts into motors, generators, relays, instruments, and networks.

Where does discovery end and invention begin?

The line is useful legally and often blurry historically.

A scientific discovery expands the set of known relationships in nature. An invention arranges matter or process to accomplish a human purpose. But the two feed each other continuously. New instruments enable new discoveries. New discoveries enable new instruments. The telescope changes astronomy; astronomy changes optics. Better microscopes change biology; biological questions drive better imaging. Semiconductor physics creates transistors; demand for better electronics drives materials science.

The 1820s winner sits precisely on this border.

A controllable magnet is a device created from a newly understood relationship.

Its importance was not obvious to everyone because early electricity was still partly a spectacle. Public demonstrations of electrical effects had entertained audiences for decades. Sparks, shocks, static charges, and strange attractions made electricity seem wondrous long before it became infrastructure.

The difficult transition was from wonder to work.

Sturgeon’s electromagnet helped make electricity useful as controlled force. Henry made it stronger and more adaptable. Faraday’s induction work would soon show how mechanical and electrical energy could be converted in the other direction.

By the end of the nineteenth century, cities would depend on systems descended from principles that had looked like laboratory curiosities in the early part of the century.

This is a good warning for anyone trying to identify the important inventions of the present.

We naturally overweight things that already have obvious consumer uses. Foundational discoveries without mature applications look speculative. Infrastructure components look boring. The market value attached to a technology today can become a substitute for thinking about its long-term dependency value.

A decade contest judged in 1829 might not have picked the electromagnet.

A contest judged from 2026 can.

That advantage of hindsight must be used carefully. We should not pretend early experimenters foresaw everything that followed. Henry did not look at a coil of insulated wire and see a data center. Sturgeon did not imagine MRI. Faraday did not imagine electric vehicles in their modern form.

Their ignorance of the future does not reduce the importance of what they made possible.

Invention is often more consequential than intention.

The same will be true of the transistor. Bell Labs wanted a better electronic switching and amplifying device for communications. The resulting component eventually became the substrate of computation, entertainment, finance, medicine, weapons, vehicles, and almost every modern industry. The inventors did not need to predict social media for the transistor to deserve credit for enabling it.

An invention’s descendants vote retroactively.

That is what lets the electromagnet beat the decade’s more visible rivals.

The counterfactual is still important. If Sturgeon had never made his device, would electromagnets have appeared? Almost certainly. The scientific path after Ørsted and Ampère made the possibility accessible. Multiple experimenters were exploring the relationship. Henry’s work itself might have produced a comparable device.

This is not a story of one indispensable man.

It is a story of an idea whose time had arrived because scientific knowledge had reached a threshold.

That may seem to weaken the word “invention.” It actually strengthens our understanding of it.

Some inventions are improbable leaps. Others are convergent: several people reach similar solutions because the prerequisites have accumulated. The modern world contains many examples of multiple discovery for exactly this reason. Once enough pieces exist, the solution space narrows.

The important historical question becomes not “who alone created the future?” but “what conditions made this future buildable now?”

For electromagnets, those conditions included experimental physics, improved batteries, wire-making, insulation techniques, instrument craftsmanship, scientific communication, and a growing appetite for practical electrical systems.

The United States contributed to that ecology and benefited from an ecology larger than itself.

This is why the chapter begins by saying the winner was not invented in America and refusing to apologize.

A nation’s inventive strength includes its ability to absorb, improve, apply, scale, and circulate discoveries made elsewhere. The alternative is technological provincialism dressed up as pride.

American history is full of imported breakthroughs that became domestic industries and American breakthroughs that became global infrastructure. The direction of flow changes constantly.

The electromagnet crossed the Atlantic as knowledge and came back transformed as application.

Soon, current would move through wires stretched across the continent. A little controlled magnet at the far end would click.

The horse would lose its monopoly on information.
