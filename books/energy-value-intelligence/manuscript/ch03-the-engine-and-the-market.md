# The Engine and the Market

Coal is stored sunlight with a very long memory.

That is physically imprecise in ways a geologist would rightly qualify, but economically useful. Fossil fuels allowed industrial societies to draw on ancient stocks of chemical energy rather than depending almost entirely on current flows of sunlight, wind, water and biological growth. The consequence was not simply “more energy.” It was a change in what could be concentrated, transported, scheduled and controlled.

A forest grows on land. A river runs where geology puts it. Wind arrives according to weather. An animal needs feed even when it is not working. Coal could be dug, piled, shipped and burned when required. Its energy density and storability changed the geography of useful power.

The steam engine then converted that stored energy into mechanical work.

Early engines were inefficient. They were valuable anyway where the alternative was worse. In British mines, pumping water was a binding constraint. An engine could burn coal near the mine to keep the mine operating, even if it consumed fuel profligately. This is an important pattern in technological history: a new technology does not need to be universally superior. It needs to be useful enough in a niche where its weaknesses matter less than its particular advantage.

The Newcomen engine is almost a caricature of this principle.

The first practical atmospheric engines of the early eighteenth century consumed fuel at a rate that would have made them unattractive in many settings. But coal mines supplied exactly the environment in which the weakness could be tolerated. Fuel was nearby and the task—lifting water so miners could reach deeper seams—had high value. The machine did not need to beat every competing power source. It needed to solve the mine's specific constraint at a price the mine could bear.

Then James Watt changed the arithmetic.

Watt's separate condenser attacked a central source of waste in the Newcomen design. Instead of repeatedly heating and cooling the working cylinder during every cycle, steam could be condensed in a separate vessel kept cool while the cylinder remained hot. The Science Museum describes the resulting improvement as reducing coal consumption by roughly two-thirds relative to older engines. The exact performance of particular machines varied, but the economic direction is what matters here: the same basic service could be obtained with dramatically less fuel.

That improvement changed the addressable market for steam.

An engine that makes sense only beside cheap coal is one kind of technology. An engine efficient enough to justify its fuel cost in mills, workshops and factories is another. Watt did not merely save coal inside the old niche. He widened the set of places where controllable mechanical power could earn its keep.

Efficiency expanded the frontier.

Improvement changes the niche.

As steam engines became more efficient, reliable and adaptable, they escaped the mine. Power could be applied to manufacturing and transport. Railways reduced the cost of moving people, goods and fuel. Steamships became less dependent on wind. Factories could organize around controllable mechanical power. Coal regions became nodes in an expanding industrial system.

The relationship between energy and value became visible in new markets.

Coal had a price. Engines had a capital cost. Railways sold transport. Manufacturers compared steam with water power, animal power and labor. Investors financed infrastructure on expectations of future traffic. Engineers improved thermal efficiency. Workers moved. Cities grew. Mines deepened. New industrial firms appeared around an energy conversion system that had not existed at scale a few generations earlier.

It is tempting to tell this as a triumph of efficiency. William Stanley Jevons noticed a complication in the nineteenth century.

If improvements made coal-powered engines more efficient, one might expect the economy to use less coal for the same work. Jevons argued that cheaper effective power could instead expand the range of profitable uses enough that total coal consumption rose. The proposition later became known as Jevons paradox, although modern research treats rebound effects with more precision and caution than the slogan usually receives.

The broad mechanism is straightforward.

Suppose a machine produces twice as much useful work from a ton of fuel. If society wants exactly the same amount of work as before, fuel use can fall roughly in proportion to the efficiency gain. But the cost of useful work has also declined. Cheaper work may make previously marginal activities economical. Producers may expand output. Consumers may use more of the service. New industries may appear. Income saved in one place may be spent on other energy-using activities. At the level of an entire economy, technology can change structure enough that the original before-and-after comparison becomes misleading.

Efficiency is not a thermostat controlling total demand.

Modern research is useful here because it prevents Jevons from becoming a magic incantation.

There is no single rebound number. Direct rebound for a household heating service is not the same phenomenon as economy-wide rebound from a general productivity improvement. Definitions differ. Time horizons differ. Prices and income adjust. New products appear. Empirical estimates range widely depending on the service and the method.

That uncertainty does not make rebound irrelevant. It makes the boundary of the claim important.

A 2021 review of economy-wide evidence found that many studies imply large rebound effects and argued that models which ignore those mechanisms can overstate the energy savings delivered by efficiency alone. Other reviews have emphasized that evidence for full “backfire”—where total energy use rises by more than the technical savings—is far from universal. Some recent econometric work for industrialized economies has estimated very large economy-wide rebound over a period of years, while earlier micro-level studies often found much smaller effects for specific household services.

The responsible conclusion is therefore more interesting than the slogan.

Efficiency reliably changes the effective price of an energy service. What happens to total energy demand depends on how people, firms and the rest of the economy respond.

That is exactly the kind of response an intelligence-rich economy will accelerate.

This matters well beyond coal. More efficient aircraft help make air travel cheaper, which can increase flying. More efficient lighting lowers the cost of illumination, which encourages more illumination. More efficient computing lowers the cost of computation and creates applications that were impractical before. More efficient AI accelerators lower the cost of training and inference while making larger models, more queries and more autonomous agents economically possible.

The rebound can be small, large, or in some cases greater than the initial savings. It is an empirical question, not a metaphysical rule. The mistake is to assume that efficiency automatically translates into proportional reductions in total resource use.

The Industrial Revolution makes the mistake especially obvious because energy efficiency helped expand the industrial frontier.

An efficient steam engine did not merely allow the same mine to pump the same water with less coal. It made steam power attractive in more contexts. Those new contexts then altered the economy around them. Cheaper transport increased the size of markets. Larger markets supported more specialization. Specialization created new products and supply chains. Railways increased demand for coal, iron, steel, timber, land, engineering, finance and administration. The energy technology reorganized value.

This is the first major example of a theme that will become central later: lower cost at one layer can increase the value of complementary layers.

Railways needed rights of way. They needed stations, maintenance yards, signaling systems, schedules, financing, standards and regulation. They needed enough traffic to justify fixed costs. A locomotive alone was not a transportation revolution. The network was.

General-purpose technologies often work this way. The device gets attention because it is visible. The productivity gain arrives through complementary reorganization.

A factory receiving steam power had to decide where and how to apply it. Machinery changed. Work discipline changed. Supply requirements changed. The economics of scale changed. A railway connected two points, but the larger value could appear in farms, mines, factories and cities whose markets expanded because the connection existed.

Investors therefore faced a difficult problem. The technology could be genuinely transformative while individual investments in it were terrible.

This distinction is one of the most important in the entire history of technology.

Railway booms built useful infrastructure and destroyed capital. Telecom booms laid fiber that later carried enormous traffic and bankrupted firms. Internet companies demonstrated real new forms of commerce while investors paid absurd prices for many businesses. Solar manufacturing became strategically and environmentally important while repeated waves of producers suffered brutal competition. A technology can create social value while the suppliers of capital capture little or lose money.

The railway case adds another subtlety: overbuilding can be socially useful after it is financially disastrous.

Promoters can raise capital on forecasts that prove too optimistic. Multiple lines can chase the same traffic. Land speculation can inflate projected value. Competitive construction can create more capacity than current demand will support. When revenues disappoint, companies fail or restructure.

But the steel does not vanish with the equity.

Tracks, stations, rights of way and engineering knowledge can continue serving traffic under new ownership and lower capital costs. A financial bust can therefore leave behind a physical platform on which later growth occurs. The original investors may have been wrong about returns and right about the direction of civilization.

This is a dangerous thought if used carelessly because it can rationalize any bubble after the fact. Most bad investments do not become public gifts. The useful point is narrower: the social value of infrastructure and the private return to the first financing round are different ledgers.

That distinction matters whenever a technology race produces durable assets faster than its business model matures.

Energy, intelligence and value diverge again.

The steam engine created a new physical capability. Engineers improved the intelligence embodied in the machine's design. Markets assigned prices to fuel, equipment, transport and shares. But none of those prices guaranteed that the investor funding the latest railway would receive the social benefit created by the line.

Value creation and value capture are different phenomena.

The distinction matters because modern discussions of AI infrastructure often blur them. If AI becomes essential across the economy, that does not prove that every data center, chip company, model provider or AI application will earn high returns. Transformative technologies can induce competitive overbuilding precisely because the opportunity is real. If many firms see the same high expected demand, they can collectively create enough capacity to drive future returns down.

Coal and steam also changed labor, but not in the simple way that machines “replaced muscle.”

Machines substituted for some forms of human and animal work while creating demand for other forms. Mining expanded. Machine tending grew. Maintenance, engineering, accounting, management and logistics became more important. Factory work could be brutal, dangerous and deskilling in some tasks while also generating new technical specializations. Urbanization changed household structure, politics, public health and education.

The value of particular capabilities moved.

A hand skill valuable in a dispersed craft system could lose bargaining power when production became mechanized. A mechanic who understood the new machinery could gain it. Owners of scarce capital could capture rents. Workers could organize collectively. Governments could regulate hours, safety and pollution. Education systems could respond to a more technical economy.

The energy transition therefore became an institutional transition.

This point gets lost when historical change is represented by a curve of energy consumption per capita. The curve matters. A society capable of commanding more useful energy can generally do more physical work. But the lived consequences depend on ownership, labor institutions, public health, housing, law, education and political response.

Coal made cities more productive and more polluted. Steam expanded trade and imperial reach. Railways integrated markets and enabled military logistics. Industrial output raised incomes over time while early industrial workers often lived under conditions nobody should romanticize. The same energy system supported both flourishing and coercion because energy is capacity, not purpose.

The environment also kept a ledger.

Coal smoke damaged urban air. Mining altered landscapes and killed workers. Fossil carbon accumulated in the atmosphere. The climate consequences emerged at a scale and delay poorly represented by the local price of coal. Industrial economies became extraordinarily good at converting fossil energy into private and public value while externalizing part of the cost across distance and time.

This is a value-accounting problem with physical consequences.

If the market price of an activity omits a cost, the activity can appear more valuable than it is from a broader social perspective. Economists call the missing cost an externality. Policy then tries, imperfectly, to bring it back inside the decision through taxes, regulation, liability, standards or cap-and-trade systems. The argument over climate policy is therefore partly an argument over which ledger should govern investment: the immediate private ledger, the long-run social ledger, or some negotiated combination.

The difficulty is not just moral. It is temporal.

Infrastructure lasts. A railway, mine, pipeline, power plant, transmission line or factory can operate for decades. Capital invested today embeds assumptions about future demand, regulation and technology. The physical system moves slowly compared with financial markets and software. That difference in speed creates recurring mismatches.

A government can announce a new target this year. A transmission line may take many years. A company can change its AI model in weeks. A power plant cannot relocate because a benchmark changed. Capital markets can reverse enthusiasm in an afternoon while concrete remains poured.

Industrial civilization therefore contains several clocks.

The energy clock is often slow because physical assets are durable and construction is difficult. The intelligence clock can be fast because information copies quickly and software can change behavior without rebuilding every machine. The value clock can be extremely fast in markets but slow in institutions, norms and law.

Much of the future will be shaped by synchronization failures between these clocks.

The steam age offers an early version. Railway promoters could raise money faster than profitable traffic could develop. Track could be laid before complementary economic activity matured. Technical enthusiasm and financial enthusiasm could outrun demand. Yet once built, the infrastructure could later become useful at lower prices or under different ownership.

A bust does not unbuild the rails.

That is why technology bubbles are intellectually interesting. They reveal the difference between a technology being wrong and a valuation being wrong. A railway can be useful even if the company financing it goes bankrupt. Fiber can be useful after the equity is wiped out. A data center can be technically productive even if it was built on unrealistic pricing assumptions.

From society's perspective, bankruptcy can transfer useful assets to new owners at lower capital cost. From the original investor's perspective, this is cold comfort.

Value has a point of view.

The engine-and-market relationship also produced a new form of managerial intelligence. Industrial firms grew larger and more complex. They had to coordinate flows of materials, people, orders, machinery and money. Accounting systems evolved. Timetables became important. Standardization became valuable. Statistical control and operations research would later formalize some of these problems.

The factory was not merely a place where energy powered machines. It was an information-processing institution.

Managers decided what to produce, how much, in what sequence, with which inputs and under which quality constraints. Markets communicated prices from outside. Hierarchy coordinated inside. Ronald Coase would later ask why firms exist at all if markets can coordinate through prices. His answer centered on transaction costs: sometimes organizing activities within a firm is cheaper than repeatedly contracting for them through the market.

The industrial economy therefore expanded two coordination technologies at once: markets and organizations.

Both convert intelligence into action.

A price says something has become scarce or desired. A manager interprets the signal. A firm may invest. A railway carries the input. An engine supplies mechanical power. Workers operate the system. Customers decide whether the output is worth the asking price. The entire process loops through energy, intelligence and value.

No single participant sees the whole system.

That distributed blindness is not necessarily a defect. Complex economies work partly because people do not need total knowledge. A manufacturer can respond to a higher input price without knowing every cause. A railway dispatcher can route trains without knowing the ultimate purpose of every shipment. A worker can master a specialized task. A financier can compare returns. Specialization reduces the amount any one mind must hold.

But specialization creates interfaces, and interfaces create failure modes.

The engineer may optimize thermal efficiency without seeing the business case. The financier may model demand without understanding the physical bottleneck. The regulator may set a rule without understanding how firms will route around it. The manager may cut maintenance because the cost is visible and the avoided failure is hypothetical. The investor may see rising demand and ignore the capacity response from competitors.

Industrialization multiplied both productive power and the consequences of coordination error.

The same is true of modern AI.

A frontier model may be trained by one company on accelerators designed by another, fabricated by another, packaged using equipment from specialized suppliers, installed in a data center financed through a web of contracts, powered by a utility operating under public regulation, connected to generation developed by still other firms, then served through software to a user whose willingness to pay depends on a task none of the infrastructure providers understands in detail.

This is not a break from industrial history. It is industrial history with a more cognitive output.

The novelty is that the machine increasingly participates in the coordination itself.

AI systems can forecast demand, schedule logistics, write software, monitor equipment, negotiate simple workflows, detect anomalies and assist decision-makers. Intelligence is moving from being embodied mainly in human specialists and fixed procedures toward being available as a flexible service. That may improve the efficiency of industrial systems. It may also accelerate investment cycles, increase demand and create new forms of herd behavior if many actors rely on similar models and data.

The steam engine expanded the supply of mechanical power. Machine intelligence may expand the supply of cognitive power.

The comparison should not be pushed too far. Human cognition is not steam. Software does not obey the economics of coal. But the old transition teaches us where to look.

Look for complementary assets.

Look for rebound.

Look for value moving away from the newly abundant input and toward what remains scarce.

Look for infrastructure whose construction clock is slower than the demand story.

Look for externalities that the local price omits.

Look for the difference between social usefulness and investor returns.

Look for new managerial and institutional forms built to coordinate the larger capacity.

Above all, do not confuse a better engine with a better objective.

The Industrial Revolution gave humanity vastly more power to rearrange matter. It did not tell humanity which arrangements were worth making. That argument remained with people, markets and politics.

The next major energy transformation made the distinction even sharper because electricity separated the location of generation from the location of use and made power extraordinarily controllable.

The factory would no longer have to organize itself around a shaft.

Once power could arrive through a wire, intelligence could begin redesigning the whole floor.
