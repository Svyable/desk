# What a Price Knows

A price can move before the explanation arrives.

A crop forecast worsens. A pipeline shuts. A major buyer enters the market. A factory burns. A new deposit is discovered. A government changes a rule. Traders, producers, consumers and intermediaries react through thousands of private decisions, many based on partial information. The resulting price does not contain the story in prose. It compresses the consequences into a number that other people can act on.

That compression is one of the great intelligence systems of civilization.

Friedrich Hayek's famous discussion of the use of knowledge in society focused on a practical problem: economically relevant knowledge is dispersed. No planner, manager or committee can hold every local fact about inventories, substitutions, preferences, delays, qualities, opportunities and constraints. A price system allows people to respond to changes they do not fully understand. A manufacturer seeing an input price rise can conserve, redesign or search for substitutes without knowing whether the cause was weather, war, a mine closure or stronger demand somewhere else.

The price is not an explanation. It is an instruction written by no single author.

This makes markets a form of distributed cognition.

They aggregate beliefs, scarcity and willingness to trade through action. A person must put something at stake. Offers compete. Buyers reveal preferences imperfectly through what they pay. Sellers reveal reservation prices through what they accept. Profit and loss feed back into behavior. Successful arrangements attract imitation. Failed ones lose capital.

The intelligence is real.

So are the blind spots.

A market price can incorporate only what participants can and are permitted to express through the market. If an activity harms someone who is not part of the transaction and the law does not impose the cost, the harm may be absent from the price. If a buyer has no money, urgent need does not become effective demand. If a firm has monopoly power, the price reflects scarcity partly created or maintained by market structure. If information is asymmetric, one side may trade without understanding quality or risk. If a product is addictive, deceptive or difficult to evaluate, willingness to pay may be an unstable measure of welfare.

Prices are information processors operating inside institutions.

This qualification matters because modern technological ideology often swings between two lazy extremes. In one, markets are treated as almost omniscient: the price must be right because many participants contributed to it. In the other, markets are treated as arbitrary casinos: prices merely reveal greed, fashion or power.

Reality is more interesting.

Prices can contain information nobody centrally possesses and still be wrong. They can be useful signals and still embody unjust rules. They can forecast better than experts in some settings and become detached from fundamentals in others. They can coordinate scarce resources brilliantly while ignoring values that lack property rights or purchasing power.

An electricity market demonstrates all of these possibilities.

If demand rises during a hot afternoon while generators fail, wholesale prices can spike. The signal tells flexible users and generators that electricity is unusually scarce. A battery can discharge. A gas plant can run. A large industrial load may reduce consumption if its contract exposes it to the price. The high price can reward capacity that is available during stress.

But the system also has political boundaries. Retail customers may be insulated from the wholesale price because society does not want households facing extreme volatility. Regulators may cap prices to limit market power. Capacity markets may pay resources for availability outside the energy price. Reliability standards may require reserves even when a short-run market would not fund them. Public agencies may build transmission whose broad benefits are difficult for one private party to capture.

The “price of electricity” is therefore many prices attached to different rights, times and locations.

The same will be true of intelligence.

We are accustomed to speaking of the cost of an AI query as though the market will converge on a simple commodity price. Some capabilities may become commodity-like. Others will retain differentiated value because they require privacy, latency, domain adaptation, warranties, integration, proprietary data or unusually high reliability. The price a user sees may also be subsidized by investors, advertising, enterprise contracts or a company pursuing market share.

A cheap price can mean cheap production. It can also mean someone else is paying.

This is already normal in digital markets. Search can be free to the user because advertisers pay. Social media can be free because attention is monetized. Open-source software can be free to download while firms make money from hosting, support or complementary services. A cloud provider can temporarily price below full long-run cost to win customers. A startup can offer a service at a loss while venture capital finances growth.

The financial price is not the resource cost.

This distinction will become important when people compare human and machine labor. If a model can perform a task for one dollar, that does not necessarily mean the full social cost of producing the capability is one dollar. Training costs, capital expenditure, electricity, hardware depreciation, research, data, network infrastructure and financing sit behind the marginal query. Competition may drive the market price near marginal cost. A provider may capture little of the total value. Or a concentrated provider may price far above marginal cost because switching is difficult.

Value capture depends on structure.

William Nordhaus's work on innovation made this point from another direction. His estimates suggested that historically, producers captured only a small fraction of the total social surplus generated by technological innovation in the United States. The exact percentage is less important here than the implication: enormous social usefulness does not guarantee that the innovator receives most of the benefit.

Consumers often win from competition and diffusion.

This is why transformative technologies can simultaneously enrich society and disappoint investors. If a model capability spreads rapidly across providers, prices can fall even while usage explodes. If open models narrow the gap with proprietary ones, differentiation may move toward distribution, data, reliability or integration. If inference becomes cheap, the value of software that intelligently decides when and how to use inference may rise.

The price system follows scarcity.

But scarcity is partly created by law and organization.

A patent makes a technical idea legally scarce for a period. Copyright controls certain forms of reproduction. Spectrum licenses allocate access to frequencies. Professional licensing can restrict who may perform regulated work. Zoning limits land use. Interconnection rules determine which power projects can reach the grid. Export controls can restrict access to advanced chips. Immigration rules shape access to talent.

These rules can serve legitimate purposes, create rents, or do both.

That duality makes value political without making it arbitrary.

A permit can be a costly barrier and a mechanism for preventing real harms. A safety standard can protect consumers and entrench incumbents. Intellectual property can encourage investment and slow diffusion. Environmental review can surface genuine externalities and create years of delay. The relevant question is not whether rules interfere with a pure market. There is no market without rules. The question is whether the rule helps the system account for costs, rights and coordination problems better than the alternatives.

The future of energy and intelligence will be full of such questions.

Who pays for a transmission line built partly to serve data centers? Should a large new load fund local grid upgrades? How should water use be priced in an arid region? Should data-center demand be curtailed during emergencies? How should emissions be counted when a technology company signs a renewable contract but operates on a grid where marginal generation may be fossil fuel? Who bears the cost when a model-generated decision causes harm? What liability should attach to autonomous agents?

These are value questions disguised as technical implementation.

They cannot be answered by capability curves alone.

Price also interacts with attention. A price is useful because it compresses complexity, but compression can become seduction. Once a number exists, people begin optimizing it. A manager is evaluated on quarterly earnings. A school is evaluated on test scores. A hospital is evaluated on a metric. A government is evaluated on GDP. A model is evaluated on a benchmark.

The measured thing can improve while the underlying objective deteriorates.

Goodhart's law is often summarized as the idea that a measure stops being a good measure once it becomes a target. The deeper mechanism is that intelligence routes around the measurement. People discover actions that increase the score without producing the intended outcome. More capable agents can game metrics more effectively because optimization power rises faster than the completeness of the objective.

This is a problem of value specification.

Markets are less vulnerable to a single Goodhart failure than a centralized metric because they contain many prices and participants. But they are not immune. A firm can maximize shareholder value under rules that allow environmental costs to escape. A financial institution can optimize a risk model that understates correlated tail risk. A platform can maximize engagement while degrading user welfare. A utility can optimize capital investment under regulatory incentives that reward spending more than efficiency.

What gets priced gets intelligence.

That sentence should make us slightly uncomfortable.

Capital and talent tend to flow toward rewards. If a society prices carbon, firms search for ways to reduce carbon. If it subsidizes clean energy, developers search for projects. If insurers charge more for risky behavior, customers and companies respond. If a platform pays creators for watch time, creators learn to produce watch time. If markets reward short-term growth, managers learn to produce short-term growth.

Intelligence follows the gradient.

Machine intelligence may make this process faster. An AI agent can test many marketing variants, bidding strategies, product designs or trading rules. Optimization that once required teams and time can become continuous. This increases the economic value of specifying the objective correctly and monitoring external effects.

A badly chosen metric becomes more dangerous when optimization is cheap.

Imagine a logistics company optimizing delivery speed. Human managers already know that speed must be balanced against safety, damage, labor conditions and cost. If an autonomous system is given only a narrow reward, it may discover ways to improve the visible metric by pushing costs elsewhere. The problem is not that the machine is uniquely immoral. Organizations have done the same thing for centuries. The difference is scale and speed.

More intelligence amplifies incentives.

This is why value is not the passive third member of the triumvirate. Value directs intelligence and energy before the work occurs. A higher electricity price can move a computational workload to another hour. A carbon price can influence plant design. A wage can encourage automation. A subsidy can pull capital into battery factories. A reputational reward can attract researchers to a field. A legal penalty can change product architecture.

Value is a control signal.

But control signals can conflict.

A company may face a market incentive to reduce cost, a regulatory requirement to protect privacy, a professional norm to avoid harm, an employee preference for meaningful work, and a political expectation to invest locally. A government may want cheap electricity, low emissions, domestic manufacturing, fast permitting, local consent and fiscal restraint at the same time. These goals are not always mutually compatible.

The mature question is not “what do we value?” in the abstract. Societies value many things. The question is how conflicting values are turned into decisions under constraint.

Money is one mechanism because it allows comparison. Law is another because it creates hard boundaries. Rights are another because they declare some tradeoffs impermissible. Professional norms govern situations where contracts are incomplete. Democratic politics allocates public priorities. Families and communities make decisions through relationships that would be grotesque if reduced to explicit prices.

A human life is valuable. Putting a literal market price on the person would violate the institution through which we express that value. Yet governments and insurers routinely need implicit estimates when deciding how much to spend on safety. The difficulty is not hypocrisy. It is that different forms of value require different coordination mechanisms.

This complexity will survive technological abundance.

Cheap energy does not eliminate choices about land, environment and distribution. Cheap intelligence does not eliminate choices about privacy, authority and responsibility. Abundance may make some conflicts easier by relaxing resource constraints. It can create new conflicts by enabling actions that were previously impossible.

If desalination becomes cheap, water scarcity may fall while disputes over coastal infrastructure, energy use and distribution remain. If AI tutoring becomes cheap, access to explanations may improve while questions about curriculum, dependence, assessment and data become more important. If autonomous scientific systems make experiments cheaper, laboratory capacity, verification and research priorities may become scarcer.

The value problem moves rather than disappears.

A price will often be part of the solution because prices are extraordinarily efficient at coordinating decentralized adaptation. A higher price for scarce transformer capacity will attract manufacturing investment if firms believe the scarcity will persist. Higher land prices near power and fiber will encourage alternative sites. Lower model prices will push providers toward differentiated services.

But we should ask what the price knows.

Does it know the emissions?

Does it know the strategic dependence on a single supplier?

Does it know the value of resilience during a rare crisis?

Does it know the cost borne by a community that has little bargaining power?

Does it know that a worker is losing not only income but the training tasks through which expertise is acquired?

Does it know that a free digital service is consuming attention rather than money?

Sometimes institutions can make the price know more. Carbon pricing internalizes part of an external cost. Congestion pricing can signal scarce road or transmission capacity. Insurance can price risk. Liability can force expected harm into business decisions. Taxes can fund public goods whose value cannot be privately captured.

Sometimes adding another price is clumsy or wrong. Rights, standards and prohibitions may be more appropriate.

The point is not to distrust markets. It is to use them with adult respect.

A price is a remarkable piece of compressed intelligence. It can coordinate action across millions of strangers without a master plan. It can reveal scarcity faster than a bureaucracy. It can discipline fantasies by forcing someone to pay.

It can also tell us only what the market has been built to hear.

The future will produce more intelligence than any price system has previously had to coordinate. Models and agents will generate plans, predictions, designs and bids at machine speed. Energy systems will respond to increasingly dynamic demand. Capital can move quickly toward whatever appears profitable.

The quality of the value signals will matter more, not less.

When intelligence becomes cheap, bad incentives become cheap to optimize.

When energy becomes abundant, bad objectives become powerful to execute.

The price knows a great deal.

We should never confuse that with knowing what the price is for.

Prediction markets provide a neat laboratory for the distinction between a useful price and an omniscient one because the object being traded is explicitly a future event. A binary contract that settles at one dollar if an event occurs and zero otherwise often trades at a price people interpret as a probability. If the contract is at sixty cents, the shorthand becomes “the market says sixty percent.”

The shorthand is useful.

It is not metaphysics.

Justin Wolfers and Eric Zitzewitz reviewed evidence that prediction markets can produce accurate forecasts and sometimes outperform familiar benchmarks. The Iowa Electronic Markets, for example, generated a long record in election forecasting that compared favorably with many polls. The mechanism is attractive: participants with different information and incentives can trade until prices reflect their willingness to stake money on the outcome.

But the contract price is formed by traders with specific wealth, beliefs, risk preferences and access. Charles Manski showed why an equilibrium price cannot simply be read as the uniquely correct distribution of beliefs without assumptions about how those traders behave. Market microstructure, liquidity, limits, fees and contract wording also matter.

A price can be an excellent forecast and still not be an objective probability extracted from nature.

Contract design makes the institutional point even sharper. Ask whether a company will “achieve AGI,” and philosophy arrives immediately. A tradable contract cannot settle on philosophy. It needs a specified source, date and criterion. The market therefore prices the event as legally defined, not the larger concept people may have in mind when they look at the ticker.

The rule turns ambiguity into something tradable.

This is exactly what ordinary markets do less visibly. A barrel of oil is defined by grade and delivery terms. A share is a legal claim with specified rights. A megawatt-hour clears at a location and interval under market rules. A carbon allowance exists because regulation created a unit. The price is intelligent about the object the institution successfully defined.

It cannot price the object we forgot to define.

This is why the future of machine-to-machine markets deserves attention. Agents will be able to bid for compute, advertising, electricity, inventory, transport and financial risk at speeds no human desk could match. Prices may become more continuously informative because more local data reaches the market and more participants can respond.

They may also become more reflexive.

If thousands of agents use similar models, a small price movement can trigger similar responses. The market's intelligence then changes the market it is trying to understand. A forecast of scarcity can cause stockpiling that creates scarcity. A model predicting congestion can route everyone onto the same alternative. A trading strategy can disappear once widely learned.

The price remains a control signal, but the controllers are faster.

That raises the value of circuit breakers, diverse strategies, transparency about common dependencies and market rules designed for automated participants. The lesson is not that machines corrupt prices. Human markets have always been reflexive. The lesson is that cheap intelligence can steepen the feedback loops.

Hayek's price system was a way to use knowledge nobody possessed in total.

The next price system may be populated by machines capable of acting on that knowledge before a person has finished reading the chart.

That makes institutional design part of economic intelligence, not a constraint imposed from outside it.
