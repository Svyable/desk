# Energy, Value & Intelligence

*The Triumvirate Shaping the Future*

**Sven Hardy Benson**

---

A civilization can have power and still be stupid. It can be clever and still be poor. It can become rich while destroying the conditions that made the richness possible.

Those three failures are easy to treat as separate subjects. Energy belongs to engineers and physicists. Value belongs to economists, investors, merchants, accountants, and anyone who has ever argued about what something is worth. Intelligence belongs to psychologists, computer scientists, teachers, managers, biologists, and, increasingly, machines. The departments are different. The conferences are different. The units are different. A megawatt does not look much like a price and neither resembles an answer.

Yet most of the future will be built where the three meet.

Energy is the ability to cause physical change. It lifts, heats, cools, moves, separates, compresses, illuminates, calculates, and keeps the improbable arrangements of modern life from relaxing back toward disorder. Intelligence chooses among possible changes. It notices patterns, models consequences, solves problems, coordinates action, searches for better arrangements, and sometimes discovers that the obvious objective was the wrong one. Value decides which changes are wanted badly enough to sustain. It is expressed imperfectly through prices, wages, profits, budgets, votes, laws, customs, attention, legitimacy, status, love, fear, and the other mechanisms by which human beings decide what should continue.

None of the three can simply replace the others.

Abundant energy without intelligence can dig the wrong hole at extraordinary speed. Intelligence without energy can design a machine that cannot be built, a medicine that cannot be manufactured, or an electric grid that exists only in a slide deck. Energy and intelligence without value can produce technical wonders nobody wants, systems nobody trusts, and public works that lose consent before they deliver benefit. Value without either of the other two becomes aspiration without capacity.

The interesting unit, then, is not energy, intelligence, or value by itself. It is the conversion between them.

How much useful work can we obtain from a unit of energy? How much better can intelligence make that conversion? How much energy does intelligence itself require? When better thinking lowers the cost of producing something, who receives the benefit? What becomes scarce after a formerly scarce input becomes abundant? Which institutions can recognize value that markets underprice, and which institutions preserve activities whose price exceeds their social worth? What happens when a machine can generate plausible plans faster than a grid can connect the factories needed to execute them?

These are not questions for a remote century. They are visible already in the queue for a transmission connection, in the capital budget for an AI data center, in the price of an accelerator chip, in the difficulty of permitting a power line, in the falling cost of a machine-generated draft, in the rising value of accountable judgment, and in the political argument over who should pay for infrastructure that benefits one region, one industry, or one generation more than another.

The present moment makes the relationship unusually easy to see because intelligence has become conspicuously physical again. A person can open a laptop and ask a model to write code, inspect a contract, search a scientific literature, generate an image, translate a language, or plan a project. The response seems weightless. Behind it sit semiconductors, substations, cooling systems, transformers, financing agreements, transmission lines, data, engineers, fiber, water, land, and power plants. The abstraction arrives through a socket.

At the same time, energy is becoming more informational. Modern grids must coordinate variable generation, storage, millions of devices, weather forecasts, wholesale markets, and increasingly flexible demand. Oil fields, wind farms, batteries, reactors, heat pumps, factories, and buildings are wrapped in sensors and software. Better prediction can reduce waste. Better control can shift demand through time. Better materials discovery can alter the feasible engineering frontier. Intelligence is becoming part of the energy system rather than merely a consumer of it.

Value sits between these worlds and causes trouble.

The price of electricity can tell a data center operator that power is scarce at a particular hour. It cannot, by itself, settle who should bear the cost of a new transmission corridor. A company's market capitalization can signal expectations about future profits. It does not measure the full social usefulness of the technology it sells. A wage can reveal bargaining conditions and market demand for labor. It is not a moral measurement of the worker. A zero-dollar software tool can create enormous consumer value while capturing little revenue. A profitable activity can impose costs on people who never appear in the transaction.

Prices are brilliant compression devices. They are also incomplete.

The same is true of intelligence metrics. A benchmark score can reveal that a system improved on a particular test while saying much less about what happens when the system is embedded inside an organization with incentives, mistakes, legacy software, politics, fatigue, and customers. An intelligence that cannot get authority to act may have little economic effect. An intelligence that receives too much authority may create effects far beyond its competence.

Energy metrics have their own traps. A society can become more efficient and still use more energy because lower costs invite new uses. A country can reduce the energy intensity of domestic GDP while importing products whose production consumed energy elsewhere. A power plant can be cheap per unit of generated electricity and still be difficult to integrate into a grid at the time and place it is needed. A fuel can be energy-dense while carrying environmental or geopolitical costs that the meter does not show.

This book therefore avoids the temptation to reduce civilization to one master variable. Energy matters profoundly, but an energy theory of everything misses information, institutions, and desire. Intelligence matters profoundly, but a cognition theory of everything ignores the physical bill. Value matters profoundly, but an economic theory that treats matter and information as frictionless abstractions eventually meets a transformer, a mine, a human body, a port, a drought, or a law.

The three constrain one another. More importantly, they change one another's meaning.

When energy becomes cheaper, formerly extravagant processes become ordinary. When intelligence becomes cheaper, analysis that once required a specialist or a meeting can become a background process. When value moves toward a new output, capital can pull energy and intelligence toward it at startling speed. These shifts create second-order effects. Cheap light does not merely produce the same number of brighter rooms; it changes how long cities remain active. Cheap computation does not merely produce yesterday's calculations at lower cost; it creates video games, search engines, digital markets, cryptography, simulations, and forms of communication that were not practical at the old price. Cheap machine intelligence will not simply reproduce today's white-collar tasks at lower wages. It will create more attempts, more personalization, more monitoring, more experimentation, more synthetic media, more software, more decisions, and more demand for the physical and institutional complements that those activities require.

Abundance moves the bottleneck.

That sentence will recur in different forms throughout the book, though it should not be mistaken for a law. Some bottlenecks disappear. Some are manufactured by incumbents. Some are relieved by substitution. Some remain binding because physics is stubborn. Some become politically binding because people refuse the technically easiest solution. A useful account of the future needs to tell these cases apart.

The chapters begin with energy because every thought that changes the world must eventually recruit matter. They move through prices and intelligence because physical possibility is not enough. They then return to the places where all three collide: data centers, semiconductor fabrication, electric grids, firms, labor markets, cities, states, climate policy, and geopolitics. The final chapters ask what failure looks like when one member of the triumvirate outruns the others.

This is not a forecast of a single technological destiny. The phrase “the future” often tempts writers into drawing one clean line through a messy landscape. I am more interested in the terrain: the constraints that will make some futures cheap and others expensive, some politically legitimate and others impossible, some profitable but socially corrosive, some socially valuable but difficult to finance.

We are entering a period in which intelligence may become far more abundant while the world simultaneously tries to electrify transport, heat, industry, and computation. Capital is being asked to build both the nervous system and the metabolism of a more automated economy. The opportunity is enormous. So is the possibility of confusing a local abundance for universal abundance.

A model may be able to propose a thousand factory designs before dinner. Copper, transformers, skilled electricians, land, permits, financing, and community consent will not materialize at the same speed. Then intelligence will be applied to those constraints. Some will soften. New ones will appear.

That is the game.

Energy expands what can be done. Intelligence expands what can be imagined and coordinated. Value determines what people will support, pay for, protect, imitate, prohibit, or abandon.

The future will be shaped by all three, and by our skill at converting one into another without losing sight of what the conversion was for.
