# Cheap Intelligence, Expensive Consequences

The first effect of cheap intelligence is obvious: some answers become cheaper.

The second effect is less obvious: more decisions get made.

That difference matters.

If a company pays a lawyer less to produce the same number of contract drafts, we can describe the change as cost reduction. If the same company begins reviewing every low-value agreement it previously ignored, generating alternative clauses, simulating disputes, monitoring obligations and auditing counterparties continuously, the quantity of legal cognition has changed. The task has expanded because the cost structure changed.

Cheap intelligence does not merely substitute for expensive intelligence. It changes what is worth thinking about.

This is the cognitive version of cheap light.

When illumination was expensive, people economized on illumination. When computation was expensive, programmers optimized ruthlessly and organizations rationed access. When storage was expensive, people deleted files. Lower the cost and behavior changes. Streets remain lit. Logs are preserved. Cameras record continuously. Software checks conditions in the background that no person would have monitored manually.

The same will happen to analysis.

A retailer can ask for demand forecasts by store, product and hour rather than region and week. A teacher can request a different explanation for each student. A software team can generate tests for obscure edge cases. A factory can classify every maintenance note. A government can inspect every application for missing information. A customer can ask ten follow-up questions instead of accepting a generic FAQ.

Some of this is clearly useful.

Some will be noise.

The ability to generate cognition cheaply does not create a corresponding abundance of attention, authority, trust or physical capacity. It can instead flood those scarce layers.

The email inbox is an early warning.

Digital communication made sending a message extraordinarily cheap. The result was not merely lower communication costs. It was more communication. The sender can impose a small reading cost on many recipients at almost no marginal expense. Spam is the pathological end state: the cost of producing and sending messages falls below the cost imposed on everyone who must filter them.

Generative AI lowers the production cost further.

Text, images, pitches, proposals, code, support requests, legal threats, job applications, academic assignments and political messaging can be produced at greater volume. The recipient's verification capacity does not automatically scale.

Cheap generation can make attention more expensive.

This is a central value migration.

When producing a plausible artifact is costly, the artifact itself carries some information. A detailed report implies that someone spent time. A personalized letter implies effort. A working software prototype implies technical labor. A photograph implies that a camera encountered a scene, though editing has always complicated that assumption.

As generation becomes cheap, the artifact loses some of its evidentiary weight.

The question moves from “can this be produced?” to “why should I trust it?”

Verification becomes a complement.

This does not mean every AI-generated artifact is untrustworthy. Human beings have generated false, sloppy and manipulative material forever. The change is economic. The supply of plausible artifacts can rise faster than the supply of trusted review.

A company using AI to produce code may therefore need more automated testing, stronger type systems, better observability and careful review. A publisher using machine assistance may need better source verification. A bank automating document analysis may need audit trails. A medical system using model recommendations may need prospective validation and clear responsibility.

The verification layer captures value because failure remains expensive.

This pattern will be uneven across domains.

For low-consequence tasks, verification may be minimal. A user asking for dinner ideas does not need an audit trail. A marketing team brainstorming slogans can tolerate errors. A person translating a casual message can inspect the result informally.

As consequence rises, cheap generation stops being the whole economic story.

A bridge calculation, cancer diagnosis, trading instruction, legal filing or industrial control decision has a tail cost. Reliability requirements increase. Insurance and liability matter. Human oversight may remain valuable even if the model is usually right.

The relevant price is expected consequence, not average accuracy.

This can create a counterintuitive result: the better AI becomes, the more valuable some forms of human accountability become.

If the machine produces an answer nobody trusts, humans simply ignore it. If the machine produces answers good enough to become operationally central, organizations need to decide who is responsible when the system fails. The human may perform less generation and more authorization.

Responsibility becomes a scarce service.

This scarcity appears in professions already. Senior doctors, partners, architects, officers and executives are not paid only for producing information. They are paid partly for making consequential judgments under uncertainty and owning the outcome. Their signatures convert analysis into action.

Machine intelligence can produce more analysis than the institution has signatures.

That imbalance creates a new queue.

Organizations will respond by changing the architecture of authorization. Some decisions will be delegated entirely to machines within bounded conditions. Some will use automated verification. Some will require humans only for exceptions. Some high-stakes domains will retain explicit human approval.

The economic frontier will be drawn where the cost of human attention equals the expected cost of machine error plus the value of faster action.

This is not a fixed line.

As models improve, it moves. As liability rules change, it moves. As organizations collect better data, it moves. As users become more comfortable, it moves. A task that requires manual review today may become automated later, while new forms of oversight emerge above it.

The transition will be experienced as labor-market change.

The easiest stories are wrong in opposite directions.

One says AI replaces jobs because it performs tasks. The other says technology always creates new jobs, so concern is unnecessary. History supports neither certainty. Technologies reorganize tasks, demand and bargaining power. Some occupations disappear. Some expand. New ones emerge. The transition can create aggregate gains and concentrated losses.

The task is not the job.

An occupation contains a bundle of activities. A paralegal searches documents, communicates with clients, organizes files, drafts text, tracks deadlines and applies procedural knowledge. A programmer writes code, understands requirements, debugs systems, negotiates tradeoffs, reviews others' work and takes responsibility for production failures. A teacher explains material, motivates students, manages a room, evaluates progress, notices distress, sets standards and creates social structure.

AI may affect each task differently.

Where machine capability substitutes for a large share of the bundle and demand does not expand enough, employment can fall. Where AI complements workers and raises output, employment can rise. Where it lowers barriers to entry, wages can compress. Where it increases the productivity of a scarce expert, wages can rise.

The value effect depends on elasticity.

Suppose a radiologist can interpret twice as many images with AI assistance. If the number of scans is fixed, fewer radiologists may be required. If lower cost and faster turnaround expand screening, demand may offset the productivity gain. If specialists shift toward complex cases and consultation, the occupation may change rather than shrink proportionally.

The same arithmetic applies to software.

If AI makes each developer twice as productive and demand for software grows fourfold because software becomes cheaper to create, employment can rise even as code generation is automated. If organizations already have all the software they want, employment can fall.

We do not know the elasticity in advance.

What we can expect is more software.

The universe of economically viable custom software expands when development cost falls. Small firms can build tools that were previously too expensive. Internal departments can automate workflows that did not justify a dedicated engineering team. Individuals can create niche applications for themselves.

Software abundance then increases demand for maintenance, security, integration and governance.

Cheap creation creates an estate.

Someone has to know which systems exist, whether they still work, who owns them, what data they access and how they fail. A thousand disposable scripts can become operational dependency without anyone intending to build a platform.

This is another general rule of abundance: accumulation creates maintenance.

Energy abundance created sprawling infrastructure that must be repaired. Cheap goods created waste streams. Cheap storage created data-hoarding and governance problems. Cheap software will create software archaeology. Cheap agents may create agent supervision.

The initial unit cost understates the lifecycle.

This has direct energy consequences.

A generated application does not merely consume inference during creation. It may run continuously, trigger other services, store data and generate more model calls. Millions of tiny automations can create aggregate compute demand that no single user notices.

The intelligence economy can become energy-intensive through proliferation rather than through one giant model.

This is why the distinction between frontier training and diffuse inference matters so much. The spectacle is the supercomputer. The long-run demand may be ambient machine cognition embedded everywhere.

Ambient cognition changes organizations.

Today, many companies are held together by recurring information rituals: meetings, status reports, dashboards, approvals, forecasts, reviews and handoffs. These rituals exist partly because human attention is intermittent. A manager asks for a weekly report because continuous observation is impossible. A team meets because information has accumulated in separate heads. A compliance review samples cases because reviewing all of them is too costly.

Machine intelligence can make some monitoring continuous.

That could reduce meetings and bureaucracy.

It could also create more surveillance and bureaucracy.

A system capable of monitoring everything may encourage management to measure everything. Employees may face automated evaluation of communications, productivity, location and output. Governments may inspect transactions at greater scale. Platforms may personalize manipulation with finer resolution.

Cheap intelligence increases both administrative capacity and administrative temptation.

This is where value becomes constitutional.

A society needs boundaries around what may be optimized, not merely accurate optimizers. Privacy law, due process, labor rights, professional ethics and political norms determine which uses of intelligence remain legitimate.

The technology does not decide.

Cheap intelligence also changes competition.

If every firm can access capable models, the model itself may cease to differentiate them. Advantage moves to proprietary data, distribution, customer relationships, workflows, physical assets, brand, trust and the speed with which an organization learns.

This can democratize capability and reinforce incumbency at the same time.

A small firm gains access to tools once affordable only to a large firm. Good.

A large firm also applies those tools to a richer data set, larger customer base and deeper capital pool. It can amortize fixed costs, integrate across more workflows and use AI to strengthen existing network effects.

The net competitive effect is ambiguous.

Again, abundance moves the bottleneck but does not tell us who owns the new bottleneck.

A similar shift happens in education.

If explanations, practice questions and feedback become abundant, the scarce layer may move toward motivation, trusted assessment, community and proof of mastery. A student can obtain a beautiful essay in seconds, which makes the essay less reliable as evidence that the student learned.

Assessment must move closer to demonstrated capability.

Oral exams may return. In-class work may matter more. Projects may require provenance. Employers may test skills directly rather than trusting credentials that become easier to game.

Cheap intelligence changes the value of evidence.

This is a larger economic theme than it first appears.

Many markets depend on signals. A degree signals persistence and knowledge. A polished proposal signals effort. A portfolio signals ability. A personal message signals attention. A photograph signals presence. A signature signals authorization.

Generative systems weaken some signals by lowering the cost of producing what used to be difficult.

When a signal becomes cheap to fake, markets search for a more costly signal.

That cost can be money, time, identity, reputation, verification or physical presence. We should expect growth in provenance systems, trusted networks, certification, live interaction and other mechanisms that reconnect artifacts to accountable sources.

The human premium will not come from mystical uniqueness alone.

It will come from scarcity.

A live surgeon standing in the operating room is scarce. A manager with authority to commit the firm is scarce. A performer sharing a room with an audience is scarce. A trusted person willing to stake reputation on a claim is scarce. A craft object with documented provenance is scarce.

Machine abundance can increase the price of these scarce experiences.

But it can also reduce them to luxuries if access becomes stratified. Wealthy clients may pay for human doctors, teachers and artists while mass-market services become automated. This is not inevitable. Automated services may improve access dramatically and free humans for the cases where they matter most.

The distribution will be designed through institutions and business models.

Cheap intelligence is therefore not a single productivity shock. It is a cascade through price, volume, evidence, labor, trust, governance and energy.

The first-order question is how much the model costs.

The second-order question is what becomes worth doing once the model is cheap.

The third-order question is what scarce layer receives the resulting pressure.

That is where expensive consequences begin.

A society can generate unlimited recommendations and still have limited operating rooms. Unlimited code and limited cybersecurity expertise. Unlimited legal arguments and limited judges. Unlimited designs and limited construction crews. Unlimited scientific hypotheses and limited laboratory throughput. Unlimited personalized persuasion and limited human attention.

The future is not a world without scarcity.

It is a world in which scarcity moves faster.

The winning skill, for firms and states, will be noticing where it moved before everyone else bids up the price.

The strongest early evidence for this task-level view came not from a speculative forecast but from a call center. Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li and Lindsey Raymond studied 5,172 customer-support agents using an AI assistant. The system suggested responses and drew on patterns from prior conversations. On average, assisted workers resolved about fifteen percent more issues per hour. The gains were much larger for less experienced and lower-skill workers, while the best workers saw smaller benefits and, on some measures, little improvement.

The distribution matters more than the headline.

The system did not simply replace one agent with 1.15 agents' worth of output. It changed the productivity gradient inside the workplace. Less experienced workers gained access to patterns that more experienced colleagues had accumulated over time. Some tacit organizational knowledge became easier to transmit.

Intelligence compressed part of the learning curve.

That creates at least three possible labor-market futures, even in one narrow setting. A company can keep the same workforce and improve service. It can use the productivity gain to handle more customers without hiring as fast. Or it can lower prices and expand the market enough that employment rises. The technology result alone does not tell us which choice will dominate.

The business model and demand curve complete the story.

This is why estimates of occupational “exposure” should be read as maps of possible task change rather than layoffs already scheduled. A profession can be highly exposed because many tasks can be assisted, while total employment remains robust because output expands or workers move toward complementary activities. Another profession can have a smaller set of automatable tasks but suffer large wage pressure if those tasks were the source of bargaining power.

Automation research before generative AI already showed this distinction. Daron Acemoglu and Pascual Restrepo describe technological change as a contest between displacement and reinstatement. Automation assigns tasks previously performed by labor to capital, pushing down labor demand in those tasks. New tasks can emerge in which labor has comparative advantage, partly restoring labor demand. The balance is historical, institutional and contingent.

There is no law requiring reinstatement to arrive on schedule.

That is the uncomfortable part of technological optimism. A society can become more productive while particular workers lose income, status and the accumulated value of specialized training. Aggregate consumer gains do not automatically pay the mortgage of a person whose occupation compresses faster than she can retrain.

Transition costs are not errors in the spreadsheet. They are lived years.

Acemoglu and Restrepo's work on U.S. wage inequality also attributes a substantial share of changes in the wage structure to the relative wage declines of groups specializing in routine tasks in industries undergoing rapid automation. One need not accept every parameter of that accounting to see the mechanism: when a technology substitutes disproportionately for tasks concentrated among certain workers, distribution can shift even if total output rises.

Generative AI may cut across a different part of the labor market because it reaches language, analysis and software. That makes the politics less predictable. A technology that affects clerks, designers, lawyers, programmers, teachers and managers does not map neatly onto the old divide between “routine manual” and “high skill.” Some high-wage tasks are highly legible to models. Some lower-wage physical work remains awkward to automate.

Status is not a technical category.

The early empirical labor signal is also less dramatic than the loudest forecasts. Research on earlier waves of AI adoption has found effects on hiring within exposed establishments without clear aggregate effects on wages or employment over the period studied. The responsible conclusion is not that labor consequences are small. It is that diffusion, organizational change and market response take time, and the macroeconomy aggregates many opposing mechanisms.

That lag has a historical analogue in electrification. Factories did not receive an electric motor and instantly double productivity. As Paul David argued in comparing the dynamo with computers, the larger gains depended on complementary organizational changes: redesigning factories, decentralizing power, changing layouts and learning new operating methods. The general-purpose technology mattered because firms eventually reorganized around what it made possible.

AI may demand the same humility.

Installing a model is easy. Rewriting the firm is hard.

An enterprise can buy licenses in an afternoon and still have unclear data ownership, broken processes, incompatible incentives and managers who do not trust automated output. The first wave of adoption may therefore look like an old organization with a new assistant attached. The larger gains, if they arrive, may come when companies redesign workflows so that machines handle abundant generation while humans concentrate on exception handling, customer trust, physical execution and consequential judgment.

That redesign can remove jobs as well as create them.

A process that once required five handoffs may require two. Middle layers of coordination can shrink. Entry-level work can become scarce if the machine handles exactly the tasks through which novices used to learn. A firm can become more productive while accidentally destroying its apprenticeship system.

This is one of the expensive consequences worth watching.

If juniors no longer draft the first memo, debug the easy bug, review the routine case or speak to the ordinary customer, where do future seniors acquire judgment? The experienced worker using AI may be extraordinarily productive for a decade while the pipeline behind her weakens.

Organizations will have to manufacture experience more deliberately.

Simulations, supervised exceptions, rotations, synthetic cases and explicit teaching can replace some lost repetition. But this creates a cost that an accounting system focused only on current productivity will miss. Training was partly hidden inside low-productivity work.

Cheap intelligence can strip away the inefficiency that was quietly carrying education.

The energy analogy appears again. A reserve generator looks wasteful until the grid fails. An apprenticeship task can look wasteful until the firm needs another expert.

This does not justify preserving obsolete work as ritual. It means institutions should identify which capabilities the old work was producing before automating it away.

The same care belongs in public policy. Governments can help workers move through training, wage insurance, portable benefits, education and macroeconomic demand. They can also freeze labor markets by protecting every existing task. Neither extreme respects the underlying transition.

The objective is not job preservation at any cost.

It is preserving people's capacity to earn, adapt and share in the productivity gains.

That makes cheap intelligence a value problem in the deepest sense. The machine changes the feasible set. Markets discover profitable uses. Institutions determine how much of the surplus reaches consumers, workers, owners and the public.

The consequences become expensive when we pretend those distributions are automatic.
