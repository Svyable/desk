# The Grid Becomes a Computer Constraint

Software engineers are used to waiting on computers. The reversal now arriving in parts of the AI economy is stranger: computers are waiting on electricity.

Not everywhere. The global power system is vast, many regions can add data-center load without drama, and efficiency keeps changing how much useful computation a megawatt can buy. But where large facilities cluster, power has moved from operating expense to schedule risk. A company can raise capital, order accelerators, secure land and fiber, then discover that the delivery date is controlled by a substation, a transmission upgrade, a transformer order, or a study queue. A model roadmap can change in months; electric infrastructure moves through engineering studies, equipment orders, rights-of-way, permits, construction and reliability review. The mismatch does not mean the world is running out of energy. It means potential energy and deliverable power are different products.

Berkeley Lab's 2026 interconnection data make the distinction unusually visible. At the end of 2025, about 8,200 U.S. generation and storage projects were actively seeking transmission interconnection, representing 1,312 gigawatts of generation and roughly 749 gigawatts of storage. The total, about 2,060 gigawatts, was actually down 10 percent from the previous year as withdrawals remained high. That decline matters because the queue is not a one-directional meter of national electricity scarcity. Projects enter, withdraw, mature, duplicate one another and compete for the same network capability; most requests do not become operating plants.

What the queue reveals more reliably is the cost of conversion. A project can exist as finance, land, equipment plans and a requested connection long before it exists as power available to a customer. Berkeley Lab reports that projects reaching operation are taking longer on average to complete the required interconnection process. The queue is better read as a map of intentions colliding with a network than as a forecast of future capacity.

For AI, the generation queue and the large-load queue meet in the same physical system. A region can have developers proposing solar, batteries, wind or gas while data centers request large new loads, yet still lack the studies, transmission, substations, equipment and approvals needed to join supply and demand on the required date. The International Energy Agency describes the same timing problem globally. In *Electricity 2026*, it estimated that more than 2,500 gigawatts of renewable, storage and large-load projects were stalled in grid-connection queues. Data centers can often be built in roughly one to three years; new grid infrastructure can take five to fifteen. The consequential number is not 2,500 gigawatts by itself but the gap between those clocks.

AI can help with part of that gap. Better software can improve demand forecasts, power-flow studies, queue administration and network planning. Regulators can change readiness requirements so engineers spend less time repeatedly studying speculative projects. Grid-enhancing technologies can increase the useful capacity of existing lines, and the IEA estimates that combinations of technology upgrades and regulatory changes could unlock substantial hosting capacity for projects already at advanced stages. Sometimes the cheapest megawatt is hidden inside equipment that already exists: dynamic line ratings, topology optimization, reconductoring, batteries used as transmission assets and better operating rules can all increase the amount of generation or load a network can accommodate.

But software cannot repeal the remaining constraint. A faster study can still conclude that a new line is needed. A better forecast can still identify a transformer that has to be manufactured. A more intelligent queue can still end at a corridor requiring permits, land, steel and crews. Some bottlenecks are information friction; others are copper.

That distinction becomes economically valuable when time matters. A developer may prefer a region with somewhat higher electricity prices and a credible near-term connection over a nominally cheaper region whose upgrade schedule is uncertain. An old industrial site can become valuable because the interconnection survives the machinery that once used it. If earlier compute materially changes product capability or market position, a company may pay for dedicated generation, storage, upgraded substations or other routes around the ordinary schedule. A megawatt available in two years can be worth more than a cheaper one promised in six.

Data centers are not passive victims of a slow grid. Large loads can create the problem they are trying to escape. Clusters form because firms want the same fiber, suppliers, customers, workforce, tax treatment and infrastructure. Once a cluster becomes valuable, new projects arrive at the same constrained nodes. Network effects and network stress grow together, and the resulting costs extend beyond the customer's meter.

That revives a question utilities and regulators have faced for generations with factories, mines and housing growth: who pays for infrastructure whose benefits and risks are shared unevenly? If a data center triggers a substation and transmission reinforcement, charging the customer for every dollar may be wrong when the upgrades also serve future customers or improve reliability. Socializing every dollar may be equally wrong when the investment exists mainly because one customer wants a huge connection on an accelerated schedule and can later leave. The useful quantity is stranding exposure: how much of the asset remains useful if the initiating load disappears?

That is more informative than the slogan “make the data center pay.” A dedicated facility with little alternative use differs from a network upgrade that unlocks capacity for an entire region. The more specific the benefit, the stronger the case for specific cost responsibility; broader usefulness makes some sharing more plausible. Long-term commitments, minimum payments, collateral and curtailment terms can expose that distinction before steel goes into the ground. They force a customer to show whether a forecast is merely optimistic or valuable enough to underwrite, and give utilities a better signal about which announced loads deserve long-lived investment. A forecast is information. A contract makes somebody carry its consequences.

The same principle applies to flexibility. Data centers are not one kind of load. Some computation is latency-sensitive and difficult to interrupt, while training, batch processing and internal jobs may have more freedom to move through time or, within limits, geography. Backup generation and storage add optionality. Yet flexible compute is not free: accelerators are expensive, operators value utilization, customers expect service levels, and delaying a training run can have commercial cost. The IEA has made this counterpoint explicitly for highly capital-intensive AI-focused data centers. The grid obtains flexibility only when contracts and prices make the trade worthwhile.

Software scheduling can therefore become an infrastructure resource. A workload that can move but is trapped behind an inflexible service promise is not flexible in practice; a data center that can curtail but receives no economic value for doing so has little reason to reorganize around the grid. Electricity systems care about controllability as well as raw megawatts because changing the shape of demand can be valuable even when no energy is stored.

For computation, geography is unusually negotiable because the output is information. Aluminum smelters have long turned local electricity into a tradable product. A data center can consume local power, perform computation and export the result through fiber rather than ship a heavy material. Geography is negotiable, not irrelevant: latency, data sovereignty, networking, chips, water, political stability, technical labor and customer proximity still matter. Cheap electricity alone cannot conjure an AI industry any more than cheap hydropower creates an aluminum industry without ore, capital, equipment and markets.

This is why a rising electricity forecast should not be mistaken for failure. Growth in electricity use can accompany economic improvement, electrification and valuable digital services. The relevant question is whether the system can add valuable demand while assigning infrastructure costs, reliability consequences and externalities intelligently. AI is only one claimant. Electric vehicles, heat pumps, advanced manufacturing, cooling and industrial electrification arrive with different load shapes and geographies, while more demand can itself justify generation, transmission and equipment manufacturing.

The strongest counterargument to the “grid as bottleneck” story is that bottlenecks adapt. Berkeley Lab's 2025 queue volume fell rather than rose. The IEA identifies hosting capacity that could be unlocked without waiting for entirely new networks. Higher demand attracts investment; technology improves; developers move; regulators change rules. The narrower claim is enough: the grid matters when those adaptations remain slower than the demand trying to use them.

That timing difference changes where data centers are built, what customers will pay for connection certainty, how utilities evaluate load forecasts, which brownfield sites become attractive, and which equipment manufacturers gain bargaining power. An existing interconnection can become more valuable than the building attached to it. It also carries obligations. A hyperscaler can build dedicated generation and still depend on the public grid for backup. A behind-the-meter plant can reduce average purchases while creating a large contingency if it trips. A private project can alter public transmission flows. The accounting boundary becomes the policy fight because the electrons do not respect the financing diagram.

Standby reliability, shared upgrades, fuel-price risk and stranded thirty-year assets are less cinematic than artificial general intelligence, but they can have more influence on electricity bills. The mature position is neither that software will solve the grid nor that the grid will stop AI. Intelligence can improve utilization, forecasting, siting, queue management and flexibility. Capital can add generation and network capacity. Contracts can allocate forecast risk. Prices can expose scarcity by place and time. Communities and regulators still decide which costs and benefits count.

The grid belongs in this book because it exposes the conversion problem cleanly. Energy may exist in abundance at the system level while being unavailable at the socket where intelligence wants to become physical. Intelligence can identify a better path without manufacturing the transformer instantly. Value determines whether anyone will pay for that path, who carries the downside if the forecast is wrong, and whether the arrangement remains politically legitimate. Software scales by replication; infrastructure scales by projects. Machine intelligence will depend partly on how well those two processes are made to meet.
