# The Grid Becomes a Computer Constraint

Software engineers are used to waiting on computers.

The strange reversal is that computers are beginning to wait on electricity.

Not everywhere. Not for every workload. The global power system is enormous compared with the AI sector, and many regions can add data-center load without crisis. But in the places where hyperscale facilities cluster, power availability has become a gating factor in project schedules.

A company can raise capital, order servers and secure land, then discover that the real delivery date is controlled by a substation.

This is what it looks like when a fast industry hits a slow network.

Electric infrastructure moves at civil-engineering speed. Large transformers are specialized pieces of equipment. Substations require design and construction. Transmission lines cross land owned by many parties and often require regulatory approval. New generation has to connect through interconnection studies intended to protect reliability. Utilities plan years ahead because mistakes can leave customers without power or saddle them with unnecessary costs.

AI demand, by contrast, can change on software time.

A model becomes popular. A company revises its capacity forecast. A new accelerator generation makes a larger cluster attractive. Competitors announce investments. Investors fund a new data-center developer. The expected load can jump before the local grid has finished studying the previous queue.

The mismatch creates value in waiting lists.

By the end of 2025, Berkeley Lab counted more than two thousand gigawatts of proposed generation and storage actively seeking interconnection to U.S. grids. Most projects in such queues never get built, so the total is not a forecast of actual capacity. The queue is still a signal. There is vastly more interest in connecting new resources than the current process can turn quickly into operating assets.

The generation queue and the data-center load queue are different problems, but they meet in the same network.

Developers can want to build solar, batteries, wind, gas or other generation. Data centers can want to consume more power. A region can have both projects on paper and still lack the transmission, studies, equipment and approvals needed to connect them on the required schedule.

This is a coordination bottleneck, not an energy shortage in the geological sense.

The sun did not stop shining. Gas did not disappear. Engineers did not forget how to build generators. The constraint is conversion from potential supply into deliverable power at a specific place and time.

That distinction changes what intelligence can do.

AI can help forecast demand, automate portions of engineering review, optimize network planning and identify upgrades. Better software can improve the interconnection process. Regulators can reform queue rules. Standardized equipment and study procedures can accelerate projects.

But some delays are irreducibly physical or political.

A transmission line still crosses land. A transformer still has to be manufactured. A community may oppose a project for reasons that cannot be optimized away. Construction needs skilled crews. Reliability studies reflect real interactions on a synchronized network.

Intelligence can reduce friction. It cannot always delete the thing causing the friction.

This is a useful corrective to a broad belief in software culture that every bottleneck is an information problem.

Many are.

Some are concrete.

The grid makes the difference impossible to ignore because electricity cannot be routed like internet packets without regard to physics. Power flows according to network conditions. Congestion in one part of a system can prevent cheap generation from serving load elsewhere. A new large consumer can require upgrades beyond its immediate connection point.

The cost of serving that consumer therefore raises a political question: who pays?

If a data center requires a new substation and transmission reinforcement, should the company fund the full cost? If the upgrade also benefits other customers and future development, should costs be shared? If the project creates tax revenue and jobs but raises local electricity prices, how should the tradeoff be judged? If the customer can leave after receiving favorable infrastructure terms, who carries stranded costs?

These are not engineering questions, even though engineers calculate the numbers.

They are value allocation.

Utilities and regulators have long faced versions of this problem with factories, mines, housing growth and other large loads. Data centers change the scale and speed. A single campus can request a quantity of power comparable to a large industrial facility, and multiple developers may arrive in the same region because they want the same combination of cheap electricity, fiber, land, tax treatment and proximity to existing clusters.

Clustering creates network value and network stress at the same time.

Northern Virginia became a major data-center region partly because of connectivity, customers and an established ecosystem. Once the cluster exists, additional firms benefit from the surrounding expertise and infrastructure. That concentration then puts more pressure on transmission and generation.

Value attracts load toward the place where energy becomes scarce.

The resulting price signal can push back. Power contracts become more expensive. Connection delays lengthen. Developers explore other regions. Utilities propose new generation and transmission. States compete for projects. The system adapts.

But adaptation can be slow enough that strategic firms try to route around the grid.

One route is to contract directly for generation. Another is to build or finance dedicated power. Some developers consider behind-the-meter gas generation, fuel cells, batteries, nuclear projects or co-location with power plants. The motivation is partly price and partly schedule. A megawatt available sooner can be more valuable than a cheaper megawatt that arrives five years late.

Time has a price.

This is especially true in a technology race. If a firm believes model capability and market position depend on having compute now, the opportunity cost of waiting can exceed ordinary electricity-price differences. The company may rationally pay a premium for faster power.

That willingness to pay is a transfer of value from intelligence scarcity into energy infrastructure.

It can also create external effects.

If wealthy data-center customers can pay far more for transformers, construction labor or generation, they may bid resources away from utilities and other industrial users. If they finance new capacity that expands the system for everyone, the effect can be positive. If they cause fossil plants to run longer or delay retirements, emissions can rise. If flexible compute absorbs surplus renewable energy, it can improve project economics.

There is no universal “AI is good for the grid” or “AI is bad for the grid.”

Load shape matters.

A constant, inflexible load has different system effects from a flexible workload that can shift through time or location. Some computation can pause. Training runs may be schedulable. Batch processing can follow low-price hours. Latency-sensitive inference for interactive users is less flexible. Reliability requirements differ across workloads.

Computational demand could become a grid resource if software exposes that flexibility.

This is one of the most interesting conversion opportunities between intelligence and energy.

Data centers already manage internal workloads. In principle, a system could route nonurgent computation toward times and places with lower electricity cost or cleaner supply. It could reduce power during grid stress and catch up later. Models could schedule training around renewable availability.

The technical possibility is real.

The economic incentive is less automatic.

A model-training team values completion time. A cloud provider promises service levels. Customers may demand latency. Hardware is expensive, so operators want high utilization. Shifting work to accommodate the grid can leave capital idle or complicate operations.

Flexibility has an opportunity cost.

The grid will obtain it only if the price or contract rewards it enough.

This is another case where value determines whether intelligence improves energy conversion.

Dynamic pricing, demand-response programs and specialized tariffs can create the signal. Some utilities are developing structures for large loads that tie discounts or faster service to curtailment obligations. A data center willing to reduce load during rare emergencies can be cheaper to serve than one demanding absolute firmness.

Reliability can be decomposed.

Not every electron needs the same promise.

This idea sounds obvious and is organizationally difficult. Data-center architecture, customer contracts, software schedulers and utility tariffs all need to agree on what can be interrupted, by how much and for how long. The physical flexibility exists only if the contractual and software layers expose it.

A flexible load trapped behind an inflexible business model is not flexible.

The same principle applies to storage, electric vehicles, heat pumps and industrial processes. Electrification can stress grids or strengthen them depending on how intelligently new demand is coordinated.

The future electricity system will increasingly value controllability.

That is intelligence turning into capacity.

A battery has physical energy capacity. A smart thermostat has little energy capacity but can shift demand. Millions of controllable devices can change the shape of system load. Forecasts and automated controls can coordinate them.

This does not make software a substitute for wires.

Demand flexibility can defer some upgrades. It cannot eliminate every bottleneck. If a region's underlying demand grows persistently, new generation and networks will still be needed. Optimization can increase utilization of existing assets, but assets have limits.

The mature strategy is both: make the existing system smarter and build the capacity that intelligence cannot substitute for.

This balance is difficult politically because software savings are attractive and infrastructure costs are visible. A planner can promise “grid-enhancing technologies,” advanced conductors, dynamic line ratings and demand response, all of which can increase the usefulness of existing networks. Critics may then claim new transmission is unnecessary. Or infrastructure advocates may dismiss software as marginal and overbuild expensive assets.

The correct mix depends on the constraint.

Sometimes the bottleneck is a line that can carry more power with better monitoring. Sometimes it is a corridor that simply needs more capacity. Sometimes the problem is peak demand a few hours per year. Sometimes it is sustained industrial growth.

Intelligence should identify which case we are in.

The grid also exposes a deeper difference between computation and energy: computation is more movable.

A steel mill is tied to a physical process and supply chain. A data-center workload can, within limits, be routed to another facility. Latency, data sovereignty, networking cost and hardware availability constrain movement, but the underlying task may be more geographically flexible than many industrial loads.

This creates the possibility of energy arbitrage through intelligence location.

Regions with abundant low-cost power could attract computational work. Stranded or curtailed generation could support flexible data centers. Countries with renewable resources could export intelligence services rather than transmitting electricity over impossible distances.

In effect, bits can transport the economic value of energy.

Instead of sending electrons across an ocean, a country can use local electricity to perform computation and export the result through fiber.

This is not entirely new. Aluminum smelting has long allowed energy-rich regions to export embodied electricity in metal. Data centers may become another form of energy conversion export, with the advantage that the output is information rather than a heavy material.

The comparison clarifies what else matters.

Aluminum plants need bauxite, equipment, shipping and markets. Data centers need chips, fiber, software, political stability and customers. Cheap power alone does not create an industry.

Complementary assets determine whether stranded energy becomes valuable intelligence.

This could reshape development strategy. Countries with strong energy resources but limited industrial bases may see data centers as a way to monetize power. The promise should be treated carefully. Data centers can be capital-intensive and may create fewer permanent jobs than political announcements imply. Their tax arrangements, water use, imported equipment and power requirements matter. A facility can produce valuable exports without creating a broad local ecosystem.

Value capture is local only if the local system captures it.

The same caution applies within countries. A rural region may host generation and data centers while much of the profit flows elsewhere. Tax revenue and construction activity can still matter. So can land-use conflict and electricity prices.

The triumvirate forces all of these questions into one frame.

Energy asks whether the region can supply the load reliably.

Intelligence asks what useful computation the infrastructure will produce and how flexibly it can operate.

Value asks who pays for the grid, who receives the income, what externalities count and whether the arrangement remains politically legitimate.

A project that passes only one test is fragile.

The data-center developer who sees only energy can overpay for capacity nobody wants. The model company that sees only intelligence can discover its growth forecast is trapped behind interconnection. The utility that sees only rate-base value can build infrastructure customers later abandon. The state that sees only tax revenue can create local opposition strong enough to stop construction.

The grid is where abstractions become negotiations.

This will become more pronounced as electricity demand grows from several sources at once. AI is not alone. Electric vehicles, heat pumps, advanced manufacturing, industrial electrification and cooling all add load in different regions and seasons. The IEA's 2026 electricity outlook expects demand to grow faster in the second half of the decade than during the previous ten years.

Competition for infrastructure does not mean a zero-sum fight forever. Higher demand can justify more investment. New generation can lower prices. Transmission can unlock resources. Technology can improve efficiency.

But construction must happen before abundance arrives.

That sentence is obvious to an engineer and often missing from economic narratives about exponential technologies.

Software can scale by replication.

Infrastructure scales by projects.

The future will be determined partly by how well we make those two verbs work together.

Berkeley Lab's interconnection numbers are worth dwelling on because they reveal how much “capacity” can exist before it exists. At the end of 2024, the lab counted roughly 1,400 gigawatts of proposed generation and about 890 gigawatts of proposed storage in active U.S. interconnection queues. The projects covered nearly the whole installed power system in aggregate ambition. Yet historical completion rates were low, and projects often spent years navigating studies before construction.

A queue is therefore not a power plant.

It is a population of intentions competing for engineering attention, network capacity, land, financing, equipment and belief.

This distinction becomes important when people respond to data-center demand with a spreadsheet sum: there are hundreds of gigawatts of renewable projects in queue, therefore power is available. The queue may include multiple competing projects seeking the same transmission capability. Some have no offtaker. Some are speculative. Some will withdraw when upgrade costs arrive. Some will be delayed by equipment or permitting. The gross number measures search, not delivery.

Intelligence can improve the quality of that search.

One promising reform is simply to make projects reveal seriousness earlier. Cluster studies, stronger readiness requirements and better queue management can reduce the time engineers spend repeatedly studying projects that never intend to build. Better power-flow models and automated data handling can accelerate portions of analysis. Standardized interconnection designs can reduce bespoke engineering.

But a faster study can still conclude that a new line is needed.

The International Energy Agency's *Electricity 2026* report put the global timing mismatch in stark terms. More than 2,500 gigawatts of generation, storage and large-load projects were stalled in grid-connection queues worldwide. The agency noted that data centers can be delivered in roughly one to three years while many grid projects take five to fifteen. It also pointed to rising prices and longer procurement times for critical equipment such as transformers and cables.

This is a systems problem expressed as lead time.

A fast-growing industry can pay higher prices to jump some bottlenecks. It cannot bid a decade down to zero.

That changes the economics of site selection. A developer may prefer a region with somewhat higher wholesale electricity prices but a credible near-term connection over a nominally cheaper region whose upgrade schedule is uncertain. A utility with spare substation capacity acquires an option it may not have known was valuable five years earlier. An old industrial site with a large existing interconnection can become attractive even if the building itself is obsolete.

The scarce asset is sometimes the right to draw power.

This has already changed how companies look at retired power plants and brownfield sites. The grid connection can outlive the original machine. A coal unit may close while the transmission access remains. Reusing that access for a data center, battery, renewable project or new generator can be faster than creating a greenfield connection, though the engineering is never automatic.

Infrastructure stores history as option value.

There is a risk, however, that the scramble for speed produces a private solution that pushes public costs outward. A hyperscaler can sign a contract for dedicated generation and still rely on the public grid for backup. A behind-the-meter plant can reduce average grid purchases but create a large contingency if it trips. A co-located load can alter the economics of an existing generator and the transmission system around it.

The accounting boundary becomes the policy fight.

Who is paying for standby reliability? Who funds network upgrades? Who bears fuel-price risk? Who absorbs the cost if the data center leaves before long-lived infrastructure is depreciated?

These questions are mundane enough to disappear beneath headlines about artificial general intelligence. They will determine electricity bills.

That is why large-load tariff design matters. A well-designed tariff can require a customer to make credible long-term commitments, cover directly attributable infrastructure and offer curtailment where it reduces system cost. A badly designed subsidy can shift risk toward households and smaller businesses while privatizing the upside of a global digital business.

The point is not to punish new load.

New industrial demand can be exactly what justifies useful investment. A large creditworthy customer can finance generation and grid upgrades that would otherwise arrive slowly. Construction creates a market for equipment suppliers. Higher utilization can lower average network costs. A data center willing to sign a long-term power contract can make a new plant bankable.

Demand can build supply.

The value question is whether the contract makes the party creating the cost also carry enough of the risk to make good decisions.

This is the same principle used throughout the book. Good price signals are compressed intelligence about consequence.

If connection is free regardless of location, developers have weak incentives to avoid congested nodes. If electricity prices ignore scarcity by hour, flexible compute has little reason to move. If a utility can recover every speculative investment from captive customers, it has weaker incentives to distinguish durable growth from a temporary boom. If data-center customers bear every system cost even when upgrades benefit everyone, useful investment can be deterred.

The price has to be intelligent enough to preserve the option to build.

There is also a deeper reason not to panic at a rising electricity forecast. Growth in electricity use is not itself evidence of decline. Electrification often replaces direct combustion with more efficient electric machines. Data centers can create services worth far more than the energy they consume. Low-income societies need more electricity, not less. The relevant question is the value and external cost of the marginal load.

The grid's task is not to prevent demand.

It is to make enough reliable capacity that valuable demand can arrive without making everyone else regret it.

That is a harder ambition than “optimize the grid,” because it requires software and steel, foresight and construction, prices and politics.

It is also a better one.
