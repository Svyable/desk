# Intelligence Is a Productive Force

A factory can be idle because the machine is broken.

It can also be idle because nobody knows what to do next.

The first failure is easy to classify as physical. The second is informational, organizational or cognitive, which can make it seem less concrete. The workers, machines and materials may all be present. The energy supply may be available. Yet production waits on a drawing, a decision, an approval, a diagnosis, a missing measurement, a schedule, a piece of code, or an answer from someone who is in another meeting.

Intelligence is productive because decisions sit inside every conversion.

Where should the factory be built? Which process should it use? How much inventory should it hold? Which supplier is reliable? What caused the defect? When should the equipment be maintained? What price should be charged? Which product does the customer actually want? Which risk is real? Which experiment should run next?

These questions do not move matter directly. They determine how matter, energy, labor and capital move.

Human societies have always invested heavily in systems that reduce the cost of answering them.

Writing externalized memory. Accounting made obligations and flows more legible. Maps compressed geography. Calendars coordinated time. Standard units allowed quantities to travel between people. Double-entry bookkeeping improved the ability of merchants to track a complex business. The telegraph collapsed communication time over distance. Filing systems allowed organizations to preserve decisions beyond individual memory. The spreadsheet let managers recalculate consequences quickly. Databases made records queryable. Search engines lowered the cost of finding information in a vast public corpus.

These are intelligence technologies even when we do not call them artificial intelligence.

Each changes the cost of cognition somewhere in the system.

A map does not make a navigator smarter in the biological sense. It allows intelligence accumulated by others to be reused. A checklist does not increase a surgeon's IQ. It reduces the probability that working memory will omit a critical step. A spreadsheet does not understand finance, but it allows a person to test scenarios faster than manual arithmetic. A search engine does not know every fact it retrieves, but it makes distributed knowledge more accessible.

Civilization scales by storing intelligence outside the skull.

Institutions are part of this external memory. A building code records lessons from failures most builders did not personally witness. A professional standard embeds experience. A court precedent stores reasoning. A scientific literature accumulates observations and disputes. A company's procedure manual represents yesterday's decisions about how work should be done.

Stored intelligence has a maintenance problem.

A procedure can become obsolete. A database can contain bad data. A standard can freeze an old technology. A bureaucracy can preserve a rule after the reason for the rule has vanished. Externalized intelligence reduces the need to rediscover lessons, but it can also fossilize them.

This tension will become important as AI systems ingest institutional memory and make it easier to apply. Automation can execute a good rule more consistently. It can execute a bad rule more consistently too.

The productive value of intelligence therefore depends on feedback.

A system needs some way to notice when its model of the world has become wrong.

Markets provide one form of feedback through profit and loss. Science provides another through experiment, replication and criticism. Engineering uses tests, sensors, failure analysis and safety margins. Democratic politics provides elections, opposition and public scrutiny, imperfectly and noisily. Firms use customer behavior, metrics and internal review.

Machine learning is itself a feedback technology. Models adjust parameters from data to improve performance on an objective. The strength of the method comes from its ability to extract patterns too complex to specify manually. The weakness is that the objective and data remain part of the problem.

A system can learn the wrong lesson efficiently.

Before reaching machine learning, it helps to notice how expensive human cognition is inside ordinary organizations.

Experts take years to train. Attention cannot be copied. A senior engineer can answer only so many questions in a day. A doctor can examine only so many patients. A lawyer can read only so many documents. A manager can attend only so many meetings. A teacher can give detailed feedback to only so many students before time runs out.

Organizations respond by rationing intelligence.

Junior staff do first passes. Forms standardize input. Queues decide which problems get attention. Committees pool judgment. Escalation rules reserve senior time for exceptions. Software handles routine cases. Consultants are hired for bursts of specialized expertise. Some questions are simply never asked because the cost of asking exceeds the expected benefit.

That last category is easy to miss.

When a resource becomes cheaper, we see substitution from expensive to cheap. We also see latent demand.

A small company may not currently commission a weekly legal review of every marketing claim. If competent preliminary review costs pennies, it may. A teacher may not currently produce ten individualized practice sets for every student. If generation and checking become cheap enough, she might. An engineer may not currently run thousands of design variants because the analysis is too time-consuming. Lower the cost and the search space expands.

Machine intelligence makes cognition elastic.

This is the deeper economic significance of generative AI. The visible outputs—text, images, code, audio—are products. The larger change is that some forms of cognitive effort can be summoned without recruiting another human mind each time.

A human worker is not a query endpoint. Employment brings fixed costs, relationships, scheduling, training, rights, expectations and a whole person. A software model can be called for a narrow task, replicated across many users and scaled according to infrastructure. This makes it economically suitable for work that was too small, irregular or low-value to justify human specialization.

The comparison can become dehumanizing if pushed into the claim that people are merely expensive models. They are not. Humans have bodies, social identities, responsibility, desires, tacit knowledge and general capacities that current AI systems do not reproduce as a package.

The economic substitution still matters.

If a task can be decomposed enough for a model to perform it reliably, the price of that task may fall even though the value of the human role surrounding it rises. A lawyer who used to spend hours drafting may spend more time on strategy, client trust and responsibility. A programmer may spend less time producing syntax and more time specifying behavior, reviewing architecture and deciding what should exist. A researcher may spend less time searching papers and more time judging evidence.

Or the role may shrink.

There is no law requiring technology to preserve every occupation by upgrading it.

Economic history contains both complementarity and displacement. Mechanization eliminated some tasks, changed others and created new ones. Computers reduced demand for some clerical work while expanding software, analysis and digital services. Automation can improve worker productivity in one setting and reduce headcount in another.

The distribution depends on task structure, demand elasticity, labor institutions, capital costs and how quickly complementary uses develop.

Recent field experiments with generative AI already show this heterogeneity. Studies in customer support have found productivity gains, especially for less experienced workers. Experiments in knowledge work have found meaningful time savings on email and writing without immediate large changes in the overall composition of work. Other studies show AI-enabled individuals performing at levels previously associated with small teams in particular structured tasks.

These results are important and limited.

A field experiment is evidence about a workflow, population, tool and time period. It is not a universal productivity coefficient for the economy. Early studies often focus on tasks that can be measured. The largest organizational changes may occur later, once processes are redesigned and adoption diffuses.

Electrification should make us patient with the macroeconomic data and impatient with shallow implementation.

The central productive effect may come from organizational redesign.

Herbert Simon described organizations partly as systems for boundedly rational decision-making. People cannot optimize over every possibility. They satisfice, use routines and operate with incomplete information. Firms exist in part to coordinate decisions that would be costly to negotiate through markets every moment.

Cheap machine intelligence changes some of those costs.

Search can become wider. Monitoring can become continuous. Communication can be summarized. Documentation can be generated. Decisions can be simulated against more scenarios. Routine negotiations between software agents may occur without human attention. Small organizations may acquire capabilities that once required large staffs.

This could reduce the minimum efficient scale of some forms of organization.

It could also increase the maximum efficient scale.

If AI lowers coordination costs, a large firm may manage more complexity. A platform may supervise millions of automated transactions. A government may process far more applications. A military may coordinate more sensors and autonomous systems. A scientific organization may run more experiments in parallel.

Cheaper coordination cuts both ways.

The size and structure of institutions depend partly on the cost of intelligence.

This is where machine intelligence meets energy directly. Human cognition is powered by food and supported by an enormous social infrastructure, but the marginal electrical footprint of hiring one more analyst is not visible in the same way as adding a rack of accelerators. Machine cognition concentrates energy demand in data centers.

The cost structure changes from distributed biological labor to capital-intensive computational infrastructure for some tasks.

That does not mean the total energy comparison is obvious. A human office worker uses buildings, commuting, devices, food systems and services. A model uses data-center electricity, hardware manufacturing and networks. Proper lifecycle comparisons are task-specific and difficult. Simplistic claims that AI is automatically greener than human work or automatically an environmental disaster usually hide the boundary conditions.

What we can say is that machine intelligence turns electricity into a more direct input to cognitive production.

This creates a new economic conversion rate: useful cognitive work per unit of compute, energy and capital.

Model developers pursue it aggressively. Better algorithms can produce more capability from the same hardware. Better chips produce more computation per watt. Quantization, caching, distillation and specialized architectures can reduce inference cost. Software can route easy questions to smaller models and reserve expensive reasoning for harder cases.

These improvements lower the price of intelligence.

Then rebound begins.

If an organization can ask a model once, it may ask ten times. If an agent can retry, it may run until a verifier accepts the result. If several models can critique one another, a single task may consume many inference passes. If software generation becomes cheap, more software will be written, executed and maintained. If personalized services become economical, the number of interactions can multiply.

The relevant unit of progress is therefore not energy per token or cost per query. It is total value delivered per unit of resource across a changing workload.

That value is hard to measure because intelligence changes the workload itself.

Suppose a medical model reduces the time required to review an image. The hospital might process the same number of images with less labor. It might process more images. It might shift specialists toward difficult cases. It might expand screening. It might generate more incidental findings requiring follow-up. It might create new liability and verification costs.

The initial productivity gain propagates through the system.

This propagation is the real subject.

Intelligence is productive not because answers are valuable in isolation, but because answers alter actions. A forecast changes inventory. A diagnosis changes treatment. A code suggestion changes software. A design changes material use. A pricing model changes customer behavior. A recommendation changes attention.

The economic value of intelligence depends on the action channel.

This explains why some extraordinary models have modest value in a particular setting. If the user lacks authority, data, capital or physical capacity to act, the intelligence is stranded. A perfect recommendation to replace a transformer does not help if no transformer is available. A brilliant factory design does not build the factory. A climate model does not pass a law.

Intelligence needs actuators.

In robotics, that statement is literal. A control system needs motors and sensors. In organizations, actuators are people, budgets, permissions, contracts and infrastructure.

As AI improves, actuators may become the scarce complement.

This is one reason enthusiasm about “agents” should lead immediately to questions about credentials and permissions. An agent that can draft an email is useful. An agent that can spend money, modify production systems, sign contracts or operate equipment can be far more valuable and far more dangerous. Capability without authority is bounded. Capability with authority becomes governance.

The question shifts from can it think to who lets it act.

That is a value problem.

Professional systems have long linked expertise with responsibility. A licensed engineer may sign a design. A doctor prescribes. An officer authorizes. A manager approves capital. The point is not only that the person knows something. The person occupies an accountable role.

Machine intelligence can unbundle knowledge from accountability.

A model may produce a better answer than the authorized person and still be unable to bear legal or moral responsibility. This creates a new kind of complementarity. As generation becomes cheap, accountable judgment may become more valuable.

The phrase “human in the loop” is too weak for this. Sometimes the human is not there to improve average accuracy. The human is there because society requires someone to own the consequence.

Ownership of consequence is an economic function.

Insurers price it. Courts enforce it. Firms organize around it. Professions train for it. Customers pay for it when mistakes are costly.

Cheap intelligence may therefore increase the price of trusted responsibility even as it lowers the price of producing an answer.

This is the value migration that later chapters will examine in detail.

For now, the important change is conceptual.

We should treat intelligence as a productive force alongside energy and capital, but not as a magical input that floats above them. Intelligence is embodied in brains, institutions, tools and machines. It has costs. It has bottlenecks. It has quality differences. It can be copied in some forms and not others. It can improve conversion at every layer and optimize the wrong objective just as effectively.

The history of externalized intelligence suggests that the largest gains come when information becomes easier to store, transmit, search and act upon.

Machine learning adds something new: some transformations of information that previously required a human specialist can now be generated on demand.

That is a profound shift.

It also gives the shift a power cord.

The customer-support study is useful here because it makes “productive intelligence” measurable without pretending the result generalizes to the whole economy. Across 5,172 agents, Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond found that access to a generative-AI assistant increased issues resolved per hour by about fifteen percent on average. The largest gains accrued to less experienced workers. In effect, the system helped diffuse patterns associated with stronger performers through the organization.

That is not merely automation of typing.

It is partial transmission of know-how.

The finding suggests one route by which machine intelligence can raise productivity without replacing the worker who uses it. A novice encounters a difficult customer and receives a useful suggestion derived from the organization's prior experience. The machine makes some institutional memory available at the moment of need. What previously required tenure, a nearby mentor or a search through manuals becomes cheaper to retrieve.

The limit appears in the same study. The strongest workers gained much less. A tool that lifts the bottom of a performance distribution does not necessarily extend the frontier by the same amount. The model can compress existing know-how before it creates genuinely new know-how.

That distinction will matter for national productivity.

A country can obtain large gains by diffusing best practice even if frontier capability barely moves. Manufacturing history is full of this. The average plant often lags the best plant by enough that management, maintenance, process control and organizational learning matter enormously. An AI system that helps ordinary organizations behave more like competent ones could create value without inventing a new material or theorem.

Diffusion is innovation's quiet sibling.

The digital-capital literature makes the same point from the firm side. Researchers studying the productivity effects of information technology have found that purchased hardware and software explain only part of the difference between firms. Organizations build intangible complements: redesigned workflows, skilled workers, databases, managerial practices and accumulated process knowledge. Those complements can be large, concentrated and slow to reproduce.

Buying the machine is the easy step.

This is why two firms can license the same model and diverge sharply in outcome. One has clean records, clear decision rights, automated tests, managers willing to change processes and customers whose needs are understood. The other has fragmented data, incentives that reward the old workflow and no way to verify what the model produces.

Equal intelligence at the API boundary becomes unequal productivity after organizational conversion.

Paul David's work on the dynamo and the computer gives this pattern historical depth. Electrification did not transform factories simply because an electric motor replaced a steam drive. The larger gains appeared as plants were reorganized around distributed electric power. Machines could be placed according to workflow rather than around a central shaft. Buildings, layouts and management changed.

The physical technology changed the feasible organization.

Computers later showed a similar lag between investment and broad measured productivity. Learning how to reorganize around a general-purpose technology takes time because the complements are not delivered in the crate.

Artificial intelligence may compress some of that learning while also demanding a new round of it.

A model inserted into the old workflow can save minutes. A redesigned workflow can eliminate handoffs, change which decisions are centralized, increase the span of control, move review to exceptions and make information continuously available. Those changes can produce larger gains and larger disruption.

The distinction between assistance and redesign is economically consequential.

Assistance leaves the organization legible. The lawyer still drafts; the model helps. The support agent still answers; the model suggests. The programmer still writes; the model completes. Redesign asks whether the old sequence of tasks is needed at all.

That is where productivity becomes organizational politics.

A manager who controlled a reporting process may lose authority when reports become automatic. A junior role may shrink when first drafts become machine-generated. A centralized expert group may lose its monopoly on knowledge when every branch office can query the same system. A compliance department may expand if cheap review makes universal monitoring feasible.

Machine intelligence changes the internal price of asking for expertise.

Organizations will spend more of what becomes cheap.

This helps explain why measured productivity can lag capability. The first months of adoption often include experimentation, duplicate systems, training, security review and human checking. Firms run the old process and the new process together because they do not yet trust the new one. Costs rise before they fall.

Learning has a carrying cost.

A model can be excellent on a benchmark while the organization remains bad at deployment. The gap is not evidence that the capability is fake. It is evidence that productive intelligence is a system property.

The same logic applies to governments. Cheap language and analysis can improve drafting, translation, case triage and citizen service. Those gains will be limited if agencies cannot change procedures, share data lawfully or empower employees to act on the analysis. A state can own frontier models and remain administratively weak.

Intelligence has to reach a lever.

This is perhaps the simplest way to distinguish information from productive force. Information becomes productive when it changes a decision that changes the world.

The model can supply more possible decisions.

The institution still has to choose and act.
