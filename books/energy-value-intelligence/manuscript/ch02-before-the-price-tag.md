# Before the Price Tag

Long before there was a market for energy, there was hunger.

The first energy economy is metabolism. A living organism must obtain usable energy often enough to keep its internal order from collapsing. For most of human history, this fact sat much closer to the surface of daily life than it does for someone who buys food under fluorescent lights and pays an electricity bill by automatic transfer. The body needed calories. The fire needed fuel. Winter needed preparation. Distance needed muscle.

Civilization did not abolish these requirements. It built layers between us and them.

That layering can create an odd intellectual mistake. Because modern economies express so much through money, we can begin to imagine that money is the resource. A factory needs financing, so financing appears to be what makes the factory possible. A household receives a salary and buys heat, so income appears to produce warmth. A government passes a budget and announces an infrastructure program, so appropriated funds appear to be infrastructure.

Money is a claim. The world still has to answer it.

If there are no turbines, transformers, electricians, fuels, transmission lines, rights of way, ports, mines, roads, batteries or factories available, additional money may raise prices more quickly than it raises physical output. The ancient version was simpler. A ruler could possess gold and still face famine if grain did not arrive. A merchant could hold silver and still be stranded if no ship sailed. A household could own land and still go cold if labor, weather or violence prevented fuel from being gathered.

The price tag came late. Constraint came first.

This does not mean economics can be reduced to calories or joules. Attempts to explain history from energy alone usually become too neat. Human beings are not engines that convert fuel into GDP according to a fixed efficiency curve. Institutions decide who can use land. Customs determine inheritance. Military power redirects harvests. Disease removes workers. Religious rules shape consumption. Trade allows one region to borrow the productive ecology of another. Knowledge changes what can be extracted from the same acre. Politics decides whether a surplus becomes a granary, a palace, an army, a road, a feast, a school or private wealth.

Energy sets possibilities. Intelligence and value choose among them.

For hunter-gatherers, the energy landscape was dispersed. Plants converted sunlight into chemical energy. Animals concentrated some of that energy in bodies that could move. Humans used knowledge of seasons, terrain, migration, water and plant ecology to obtain enough food from landscapes that could not be forced to yield arbitrarily more on demand. Intelligence was intimate with energy because a bad model of the environment could be fatal. Where is the water? Which plants are edible? When do the herds move? What weather is approaching? Which route costs less effort than it saves?

Much of this knowledge was not written. It was embodied in people, stories, practices and social memory. That made human networks part of the energy system. Cooperation allowed hunting, food sharing, child care, defense and risk pooling. The value of belonging to a group could not be represented by a market price because exclusion could mean death.

Then agriculture changed the conversion.

Cultivation did not simply give humans “more food.” It changed the timing, density and controllability of energy flows. A field could concentrate edible biomass in one place. Domesticated plants and animals transformed ecosystems around human preferences. Grain could be stored. Draft animals could convert feed into traction. Irrigation could redirect water. A harvest could support people who were not producing food directly, at least if the system generated enough surplus and if institutions could move that surplus from field to storehouse to mouth.

That “if” contains much of political history.

Stored grain is not merely calories. It is time. It allows consumption to be separated from harvest. It creates a buffer against seasonality and, when reserves are sufficient, against bad years. It can support armies, officials, craftspeople, priests, builders and rulers. It can be taxed. It can be stolen. It can be lent. It can rot. Once food becomes storable at scale, the question of who controls the store becomes almost as important as the question of who grew the food.

Energy acquired an institutional layer.

A granary is therefore both a physical technology and a political object. Its value depends on the ability to keep grain dry, defend it, count it, allocate it and retain enough legitimacy or coercive power to make the allocation stick. A society may possess sufficient harvests in aggregate and still produce starvation through war, entitlement failure, hoarding, transport breakdown or exclusion. Amartya Sen's work on famine would later make this point with economic precision: food availability and the ability of particular people to command food are not the same thing.

Sen's argument is worth lingering over because it exposes the difference between a stock and an entitlement to the stock. He was shaped in part by the Bengal famine of 1943, which he witnessed as a child. The catastrophe did not strike every class equally. People with secure incomes and claims on food could continue eating while landless laborers and others whose purchasing power collapsed were devastated. In later work Sen challenged the idea that famine should be explained only by a fall in aggregate food availability. The harder question was which groups had lost the ability to obtain food through wages, production, exchange, public relief or other socially recognized claims.

That is a profound systems lesson.

The existence of a resource does not guarantee access to the service produced from it.

A warehouse can be full and a household hungry. A reservoir can be high and a neighborhood dry. A country can have generating capacity and a factory can still lack a connection. A company can have a subscription to the best model in the world and still be unable to use it because its data are inaccessible, its systems are incompatible or its employees lack authority to change the process.

The failure can occur after abundance.

This is why physical inventories and social capabilities have to be analyzed together. Energy history tempts us to count tons, acres, horsepower and kilowatt-hours because those quantities are tangible. Yet human welfare depends on the conversion path from resource to access to use. An acre of grain means one thing to its owner, another to a tenant, another to a laborer paid in cash, and still another to an urban household dependent on a functioning market. The same crop can be simultaneously abundant in aggregate and inaccessible to the people most exposed to a price shock.

This distinction between supply and access will recur throughout the book.

There can be enough electricity in a region and not enough transmission to a load. Enough compute in the world and no affordable access for a small firm. Enough intelligence in a population and institutions that waste it. Enough money in a budget and no administrative capacity to execute. Enough food in a country and households without entitlement to it.

Inventory is not conversion.

Agriculture also changed the relation between human and nonhuman power. A person can sustain only a limited amount of mechanical work over a day. Draft animals expanded that capacity. Wind could move ships and turn mills. Waterwheels converted flowing water into rotary motion. Wood and charcoal supplied heat. Each source had geography attached. Water power required suitable sites. Wind varied. Forests could be depleted. Animals consumed land through feed. Transporting bulky fuel imposed costs.

This is easy to underestimate from a world in which energy arrives through pipes and wires. Before fossil fuels, much useful energy had to be harvested from current biological or climatic flows. Land was therefore not merely an asset in the financial sense. It was an energy collector. Forests stored solar energy in wood. Fields stored it in crops. Pasture stored it in plants that animals could convert into work, milk, meat and manure.

The economy was closer to the photosynthetic budget.

That budget placed pressure on land in several roles at once. A hectare might grow food for people, fodder for animals, timber for construction, or fuel for heat. Expanding one use could tighten another. A city that consumed more firewood drew on a larger hinterland. A cavalry force did not merely require horses; it required the land and logistical system that fed them. A charcoal-intensive industry could put pressure on forests long before anyone had a modern concept of energy policy.

The constraint was not simply “low technology.” It was competition among conversions of current solar income.

Fossil fuels later mattered in part because they loosened this land-energy coupling. Coal could provide heat without requiring an equivalent annual harvest of wood. Steam could provide mechanical work without continuously feeding an animal. The transition did not abolish land or biology, but it allowed industrial output to grow beyond some of the tightest limits of an organic energy economy.

That is a physical change with enormous economic consequences.

The budget could also be extended through trade. A city could import grain, timber, oil, fodder and other energy-rich goods from a larger hinterland. Empires could compel flows across enormous distances. Merchant networks could do so through exchange. Ports became conversion points between regional ecologies and urban consumption. The larger the network, the more a population could draw on energy and materials outside its immediate land base.

Trade is often described in monetary terms because prices are visible. Physically, it also rearranges energy.

A ship carrying grain transfers stored sunlight from one place to another. A timber shipment transfers years of biological growth. A bale of cotton carries the result of land, sunlight, water and labor into a mill where other forms of energy transform it again. Modern trade performs the same trick at greater scale with fuels, ores, food, chemicals and manufactured goods. A country can appear to reduce the material or energy intensity of its domestic production while importing energy-intensive products made elsewhere. The border can move accounting without moving physics.

The ancient and preindustrial world therefore offers a useful warning against one-number explanations. A kingdom with fertile land could be poor because institutions were predatory, transport was weak or war was constant. A city with little local farmland could become rich by organizing trade. A region with excellent water power might fail to industrialize if capital, knowledge, demand or institutions were absent. Energy availability matters enormously, but what matters economically is usable energy inside a system capable of directing it.

Useful is the difficult word.

Energy economists distinguish primary energy, final energy and useful energy because conversion losses matter. Coal in the ground is not electricity at a motor. Oil in a reservoir is not movement at a wheel. Sunlight on a roof is not refrigerated food. Each conversion step loses some energy and requires infrastructure.

Societies have an analogous set of conversion losses that are harder to measure.

A child may have extraordinary cognitive potential and never receive enough nutrition or education to develop it. A farmer may know how to improve yields and lack credit to buy the equipment. A community may possess a valuable resource and lack secure property rights or infrastructure. A state may collect taxes and lose much of the budget to corruption. A scientific discovery may exist in a journal for decades before complementary technologies make it practical.

Intelligence can be stranded just as energy can be stranded.

Value can be stranded too. A person can derive enormous benefit from an activity that no seller can charge for. Public sanitation creates value across a city, including for people who never enter a transaction with the sewer operator. Knowledge spills. A beautiful public square can increase welfare, land values and social life in ways that are difficult to capture in one revenue stream. Conversely, an activity can generate revenue while destroying resources or trust that are not priced.

The oldest societies knew this without using modern terminology. They maintained irrigation works, walls, roads, temples, storage systems and legal institutions that could not be understood as isolated household purchases. They also frequently exploited labor, concentrated land, imposed taxes and fought wars whose costs fell unevenly. Collective capacity and predation were often built by the same institutions.

That ambiguity matters when modern technology enthusiasts speak about “civilizational capacity” as though more capacity were automatically benevolent.

A state capable of building a dam can irrigate farms, generate electricity and control floods. It can also displace communities, entrench patronage or use the project to consolidate power. An empire capable of organizing roads can expand commerce and armies along the same route. A sophisticated bureaucracy can distribute grain and collect tribute with equal competence.

Intelligence improves the ability to act. Value determines which actions are rewarded or authorized.

The deeper lesson of preindustrial energy is not that humanity was trapped until coal arrived, although fossil energy eventually changed the scale dramatically. The lesson is that every society lives inside a conversion problem.

Food must become health and labor. Fuel must become heat. Water must become crops. Labor must become structures, goods and services. Knowledge must become decisions. Surplus must become institutions or investment if it is to persist. Institutions must convert coercion, consent and information into coordination. Fail at enough interfaces and abundance at one stage does little good.

This explains why surplus has such an important place in theories of civilization. A society operating at the edge of subsistence has less room for specialized roles, long projects, experimentation and buffers. Surplus creates option value. It can support a person who spends years learning medicine, mathematics, metallurgy or administration rather than producing food directly. It can finance ships whose return is uncertain. It can maintain reserve capacity. It can allow failure without immediate catastrophe.

Surplus buys search.

That is one bridge from energy to intelligence.

A society with more reliable food and energy can support more people whose daily task is to manipulate information rather than directly secure calories. Scribes, clerks, navigators, engineers, scholars, merchants, lawyers and officials are forms of cognitive specialization. Their work may then improve the society's ability to obtain, distribute and use energy. Better navigation expands trade. Better accounting expands organizations. Better metallurgy improves tools. Better administration can maintain canals. Better military intelligence can redirect resources toward conquest, which is less comforting but equally real.

Energy can fund intelligence. Intelligence can improve energy conversion. Value decides where the loop points.

This feedback loop is older than industrial machinery.

It also reveals why some energy abundance produces less human flourishing than we might expect. Resource wealth can strengthen institutions, but it can also create rents large enough to fight over. A government financed by resource revenue may be less dependent on broad taxation and therefore less accountable to citizens. Easy export earnings can appreciate a currency and damage other tradable sectors. Powerful outsiders may compete for access. None of these outcomes is mechanically determined, which is precisely the point. The same physical resource can support different social trajectories depending on institutions and incentives.

The future of abundant electricity and machine intelligence will face a modern version of the same problem.

Suppose clean electricity becomes dramatically cheaper in a region. That creates possibilities: industrial heat, electric transport, desalination, synthetic fuels, cooling, data centers, fertilizer, materials processing, automated manufacturing. Which possibilities are actually built will depend on capital, regulation, land, transmission, technology, markets and political consent. If machine intelligence also lowers the cost of design and operation, more projects become conceivable. But cheap power and cheap cognition do not decide whether the region becomes a manufacturing hub, a speculative data-center cluster, a cleaner consumer economy, an extractive enclave, or a place where infrastructure fails because institutions cannot coordinate construction.

The same abundance can be converted into different futures.

This is why value deserves equal billing with energy and intelligence. Value is not just the reward at the end of production. It is the selection pressure operating throughout the system. Activities that receive revenue attract capital. Activities that receive status attract talent. Activities that receive legal protection survive. Activities that win elections receive public budgets. Activities that command fear receive military resources. Activities that satisfy deep human needs persist even when markets struggle to price them.

A civilization is always deciding what to feed.

Sometimes those decisions are decentralized through markets. Sometimes they are made through households, firms, states, religions, professional norms or informal communities. Often several systems overlap and conflict. A scientific project may be publicly funded, privately commercialized, professionally prestigious and personally meaningful all at once. A new power line may be economically valuable to a region, profitable to some firms, costly to nearby landowners, essential to climate goals and politically toxic.

There is no master price that resolves every dimension.

The pre-price-tag world makes that easier to see because fewer layers conceal the physical base. A harvest failed or it did not. A river flowed or did not. A forest was nearby or distant. The ship arrived or disappeared. Human and animal muscles tired. Winter imposed a deadline.

Modern systems buffer these facts with extraordinary success. Warehouses, global trade, refrigeration, insurance, futures markets, central banks, public relief, electric grids, fuels and digital coordination make daily life less dependent on one local season or one local resource. That buffering is one of civilization's great achievements.

It can also make us forget what the buffer is doing.

A supermarket is an energy system disguised as retail. A hospital is an energy system disguised as medicine. A cloud service is an energy system disguised as software. A university is partly an energy system that supports sustained cognitive specialization. A financial market is an information system that redirects claims on future physical and organizational capacity. A city is an enormous machine for importing energy, matter and information, transforming them into valued experiences and outputs, then exporting waste, products, culture, services and decisions.

The disguise is useful. We should not spend every breakfast contemplating supply chains. Abstraction saves attention.

Trouble begins when the abstraction is mistaken for independence.

The people of a preindustrial town did not have to be reminded that a mill depended on water or wind. A software executive may need to be reminded that a data center cannot open until power is available. A government may announce industrial subsidies before confirming that grid connections, skilled labor and equipment can arrive on the same schedule. An investor may treat projected demand as equivalent to deliverable supply. A model may generate a plan whose verbs conceal years of physical construction.

Before the price tag, the body knew better.

Hunger, cold and fatigue enforced a direct accounting. Modern civilization should be grateful that most people in wealthy societies can live at greater distance from those signals. The goal is not to romanticize scarcity. Scarcity was deadly, exhausting and limiting.

The goal is to retain the lesson after success has hidden it.

Every claim eventually settles against capacity.

And every capacity settles against access.

The first question is whether the resource exists. The second is whether the system can convert it. The third is whether the people who need the result are allowed, able and equipped to obtain it.

The history before the price tag teaches all three at once.

The question is what kind of capacity, who controls it, and how intelligently we convert it into a life worth valuing.
