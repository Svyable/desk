# Abundance Is Political

A town can have more electricity and still hate the power plant.

This is not irrational.

The benefits and costs may belong to different people. The electricity may be sold to customers elsewhere. The tax revenue may flow to a county budget while nearby residents bear noise, traffic, land-use change or environmental risk. Workers may arrive temporarily for construction and leave. Property owners may receive large payments while neighbors receive none.

An economist can calculate a positive net benefit for the project and still miss the political problem.

Net benefit does not tell us who is netting whom.

This is why abundance is political.

The phrase sounds strange because abundance is usually presented as an escape from politics. If there is enough energy, enough intelligence and enough wealth, why fight over allocation? Many technological visions promise precisely this release: make the pie so large that distribution stops mattering.

Growth can make conflict easier to manage. It does not abolish distribution.

A larger pie still has slices.

More importantly, abundance is never uniform across every dimension at once. Cheap electricity can coexist with scarce land. Cheap machine intelligence can coexist with scarce trusted attention. Abundant generation can coexist with limited transmission. A wealthy country can contain households that cannot afford basic services.

The remaining scarcity becomes political because people bargain over it.

Consider the data-center boom.

From the perspective of a technology company, a new campus is infrastructure for future services. From the perspective of a utility, it is a large load requiring generation and network planning. From the perspective of a state, it may be investment and tax revenue. From the perspective of a nearby resident, it is a construction site, a water user, a set of transmission upgrades and a claim on local power capacity.

All four descriptions can be accurate.

Conflict begins when one description is presented as the whole.

Technology companies naturally emphasize innovation and economic growth. Critics naturally emphasize local costs. Governments may exaggerate job creation. Utilities may understate uncertainty because investment earns regulated returns. Residents may overestimate some harms or resist infrastructure because they prefer the benefits of growth without nearby construction.

Politics is the mechanism through which these partial perspectives collide.

Good politics does not make everyone happy.

It creates procedures capable of distinguishing legitimate harm from strategic obstruction, then allocating costs in a way that preserves enough consent to build.

This is becoming one of the central capabilities of the energy transition.

The technical case for more transmission is strong in many regions. Lines can connect cheap generation to demand, improve reliability and reduce congestion. The political case is harder because a regional benefit is created by a corridor crossing specific places.

The beneficiaries are dispersed. The disruption is concentrated.

That asymmetry is a classic infrastructure problem.

A person hundreds of miles away may receive slightly lower electricity prices and never know the line exists. A landowner beneath the line sees it every day.

Markets struggle with this because the transaction is not a simple voluntary exchange between one buyer and one seller. Governments use eminent domain, permitting and regulated cost allocation because networks require collective coordination.

Those powers need legitimacy precisely because they override some individual preferences.

Faster permitting is therefore not synonymous with weaker rights.

A competent system can be faster because it makes decisions clearly, resolves disputes promptly, coordinates agencies and rejects frivolous repetition. A slow system can be unjust because delay itself becomes a weapon available to actors with resources.

The relevant variable is decision quality per unit of time.

Intelligence should improve that conversion.

AI systems could analyze applications, compare routes, detect conflicts in rules, summarize public comments and help agencies process larger volumes. That can reduce administrative bottlenecks.

It also raises a democratic danger.

If public participation is cheap to generate, comment periods can be flooded by synthetic submissions. Organized interests can create the appearance of mass sentiment. Agencies can respond by using AI to filter comments, which creates an escalating contest over what counts as genuine civic participation.

Cheap intelligence changes politics by changing the cost of voice.

The same technology that allows one citizen to understand a 500-page environmental filing can allow a lobby to generate ten thousand tailored objections.

Verification becomes political infrastructure.

Who is speaking? On whose behalf? Is the submission original? Does that matter if the argument is good? How much weight should a million identical machine-generated comments receive?

Old participation systems assumed that producing a detailed comment was costly enough to signal some level of effort.

That assumption is disappearing.

Abundance changes democratic signals just as it changes market signals.

Distribution also matters inside the labor market.

Suppose AI raises national productivity substantially. The aggregate gain can be real while a specific group loses bargaining power. A worker whose task is automated may experience the transition as unemployment, not as a statistic showing higher total output.

Telling the worker that consumers receive cheaper services is economically relevant and politically inadequate.

Transitions need institutions for sharing gains and absorbing shocks.

This can include unemployment insurance, wage insurance, retraining, portable benefits, geographic mobility support, stronger labor bargaining, tax policy or public investment. Reasonable people disagree about the mix. The important point is that the distribution does not arrange itself automatically toward social stability.

Markets reward ownership and scarcity, not fairness as an independent objective.

If AI makes capital more productive and labor less scarce in certain tasks, owners of capital can capture larger rents. Competition can transfer some gains to consumers. New industries can increase labor demand elsewhere. Tax systems can redistribute. Workers can own capital through pensions or savings.

Technology opens the distributional question. Institutions answer it.

Energy transitions have always done the same.

Coal regions gained employment and political power during industrialization. Oil-producing regions accumulated wealth and strategic importance. Environmental regulation later imposed costs on some industries to reduce harms borne by others. Renewable-energy development creates new winners in landowners, manufacturers, developers and construction while threatening incumbent businesses.

There is no energy policy without constituency formation.

Once a subsidy exists, companies organize around it. Once a plant employs thousands of workers, closure becomes political. Once homeowners rely on cheap electricity, price increases become politically difficult.

Value signals create institutions that then defend the signals.

This path dependence explains why optimal policy cannot simply be redesigned from scratch every year.

People invest based on rules. A sudden change can destroy legitimate expectations. Stability has value.

So does adaptation.

The tension between them is governance.

Artificial intelligence can accelerate change enough to make this tension severe. If capabilities improve on annual or monthly cycles while education, licensing, regulation and infrastructure move over decades, societies experience repeated mismatch.

Policy can become permanently late.

One response is to write broader principles rather than technology-specific rules. Privacy rules can govern data use regardless of which model architecture is fashionable. Liability can focus on consequence and control rather than naming one technique. Energy tariffs can define obligations for large loads without assuming a particular server.

Principles age better than product names.

They can also be vague.

Institutions need enough technical intelligence to translate principles into decisions without becoming captured by whichever industry supplies the expertise.

Regulatory capacity becomes a scarce complement to technological abundance.

This is an underappreciated form of state capacity.

A weak regulator does not necessarily mean fewer rules. Weak systems can produce more paperwork because they substitute procedure for judgment. They may require every applicant to satisfy cumbersome steps while lacking the expertise to identify which risks matter.

Good regulation is selective intelligence.

It knows where to impose friction.

The same is true of taxation.

A government seeking to capture some value from data centers can offer incentives to attract investment and then discover it has bid away most of the tax base. Another can impose high taxes and lose the project to a competing jurisdiction. A third can structure incentives around actual performance: investment, jobs, grid upgrades, clean generation or local procurement.

The policy is a negotiation over mobile capital and immobile geography.

Data centers are more mobile than mines and less mobile than software.

Once built, the facility is fixed. Before construction, developers can compare jurisdictions. This gives the company bargaining power during site selection and the host government more leverage after sunk capital appears.

Both sides know the game.

Long-term contracts attempt to stabilize it.

Energy infrastructure contains similar bargaining. A factory may demand a favorable electricity rate in exchange for locating. A utility may justify new generation based on the load. If the factory closes early, other customers can inherit costs.

Who bears stranded risk is political economy.

The problem will grow as AI investment becomes more capital-intensive.

The April 2026 International Energy Agency review of energy and AI reported that capital spending by five large technology companies exceeded four hundred billion dollars in 2025 and was expected to rise sharply again in 2026. Numbers at that scale change local negotiating power.

A county government and a hyperscale technology company do not arrive at the table with equal analytic resources.

Machine intelligence can narrow that gap if public institutions use it well.

A small government can model contracts, compare deals and analyze energy impacts with tools that once required expensive consultants. This is a democratic benefit of cheap intelligence rarely emphasized in automation debates.

Capacity can diffuse downward.

The same tools are available to the company.

Relative capability matters.

If both sides become smarter, bargaining power still depends on alternatives. A locality with unique access to power and fiber can negotiate. One of many interchangeable sites has less leverage.

Scarcity determines the negotiating position.

Abundance also creates cultural politics.

If synthetic media becomes unlimited, societies may disagree over whether human-created work deserves labeling, compensation or special protection. Artists may demand control over training uses of their work. Consumers may want disclosure. Platforms may resist obligations that increase cost.

These disputes are not simply fear of technology.

They concern property, identity and bargaining power.

The technical fact that a model can learn from an enormous corpus does not determine what legal rights creators should possess over that use. Copyright law, contract and new statutes define the boundary.

Value is institutional again.

The same conflict appears in data.

A medical model can become better with more patient data. The social benefit of improved diagnosis can be large. Patients may still value privacy. Hospitals may treat data as an asset. Researchers may seek broad access. Commercial firms may want exclusive rights.

More intelligence is available if more data is shared.

That does not make consent irrelevant.

A system that treats every privacy constraint as an inefficiency will optimize itself out of legitimacy.

This is the political version of a technical local optimum.

The machine finds a better score while society withdraws permission.

Energy developers have seen this pattern repeatedly. A project can be cost-effective, low-carbon and technically sound, then fail because local opposition was treated as a communications problem rather than a value conflict.

Some opposition is based on misinformation. Some is strategic NIMBYism. Some reflects genuine distributional harm.

Intelligence means telling the difference.

Compensation can resolve some conflicts.

Landowners can be paid. Communities can receive tax revenue, discounted electricity, infrastructure or direct benefits. Workers can receive transition support. Revenue-sharing can align incentives.

Not every value is compensable.

A sacred site is not necessarily made acceptable by a larger check. An ecosystem can have irreversible value. A community may reject a project because the process violates autonomy.

Markets are powerful because many values can be traded.

Rights matter because some values should not depend on willingness to pay.

The future will require clearer distinctions between the two.

Technological abundance increases the pressure because more projects become feasible. Cheap energy makes more industrial uses economic. Cheap intelligence produces more designs, businesses and applications. Capital finds opportunities faster.

The number of proposals can rise faster than society's ability to decide.

Governance becomes throughput.

A state that cannot approve good projects or reject bad ones predictably becomes a bottleneck in its own development. Investors price delay. Citizens lose trust. Infrastructure ages while paperwork accumulates.

A state that approves everything quickly can create the opposite failure: environmental damage, corruption, unsafe systems and political backlash.

The valuable capability is not speed alone.

It is competent decision-making under contested value.

That is intelligence in its most difficult form because there is no single objective function.

The optimizer wants one number.

Politics exists because there are several.

Cost. Reliability. Jobs. Emissions. Privacy. Property. Security. Equality. Growth. Beauty. Heritage. Speed. Local autonomy. National strategy.

These values can sometimes be translated into prices or weights. Sometimes the act of translating them changes what people believe is being decided.

A democratic society cannot escape the argument by pretending the spreadsheet is neutral.

The spreadsheet is useful.

It should be used.

Then people still have to choose.

Abundance makes those choices more important because capacity magnifies consequence.

A poor society may have few options and painful constraints. A rich, energy-abundant, intelligence-rich society can choose among many futures.

That sounds like freedom.

It is.

Freedom creates politics because people want different things.

The promise of technology is not that the argument ends.

It is that we can afford better arguments and more generous outcomes.

Whether we choose them is outside the machine.

Rate design is where this abstract argument becomes a monthly bill.

A data center can increase a utility's sales and justify new investment. It can also require generation, substations and transmission that last far longer than a server fleet. If the customer receives a discounted tariff to locate in the region while the utility socializes network upgrades across households and small businesses, abundance can arrive as a subsidy from people with little bargaining power to a company with enormous bargaining power.

The opposite design can be harmful too. Charge a new industrial customer for every system upgrade regardless of who benefits, require inflexible terms and assign it risks created by the wider network, and a useful project may locate elsewhere. The grid loses the customer that might have financed expansion.

There is no politics-free tariff because every tariff allocates risk.

Large-load contracts increasingly grapple with minimum bills, long commitments, collateral, exit fees and curtailment terms for exactly this reason. They are attempts to prevent one fast-moving customer from leaving a long-lived asset stranded. The principle is straightforward: if a private forecast induces public infrastructure, the party making the forecast should carry enough downside to care whether it is true.

That principle does not require hostility to growth. It is what makes growth durable.

The same issue appears on the supply side. A government can subsidize new generation, a semiconductor fab or a transmission corridor because the social return exceeds what private investors can capture. That is a coherent economic argument. But subsidies are politically sticky. Once firms, workers and local governments organize around them, a temporary bridge can become a permanent claim.

The test should be whether the public problem that justified the subsidy is still present.

A manufacturing subsidy designed to overcome first-mover risk should not automatically survive after the industry is mature. A tax credit intended to accelerate deployment should be judged against the new cost curve, not the one from the year the law was written. A resilience payment should be tied to actual resilience rather than the label on the recipient.

Value systems need maintenance just as infrastructure does.

This is difficult because the beneficiaries of an old rule are organized and the beneficiaries of reform are often diffuse. The factory knows what it receives. Millions of taxpayers each pay a fraction. The same asymmetry that complicates transmission siting complicates subsidy removal in reverse.

Political economy has directionality.

That is why abundance can produce a paradoxical feeling of scarcity. A society may become richer in energy and cognition while citizens feel that rents, housing, insurance, healthcare or electricity bills remain punishing. The technological surplus is real, but it may be landing in categories people do not experience as relief.

A cheaper model cannot compensate for an unaffordable apartment.

A megawatt of new generation does not help a household if network costs and fixed charges keep its bill high. A productivity gain does not feel like abundance to a worker whose bargaining power falls. The political legitimacy of growth depends partly on whether people can identify some of the gain in their own lives.

This is not an argument that every innovation needs a visible rebate. Consumer surplus often arrives quietly through better products and lower quality-adjusted prices. It is an argument that aggregate abundance and experienced affordability are separate variables.

The difference can decide elections, permitting battles and public trust.

There is a constructive version of this politics. Communities can negotiate benefits before projects arrive. Utilities can publish transparent cost-allocation rules. Governments can build analytic capacity so they are not negotiating blind. Tax incentives can include expiration dates and measurable conditions. Workers can share in productivity through ownership, bargaining or broader fiscal systems. Infrastructure planning can make regional benefits visible enough that local burdens are compensated rather than dismissed.

None of these choices eliminates conflict.

They change conflict from a veto game into a bargaining problem.

That may be the most realistic political promise of abundance.

Not that distribution stops mattering, but that there is enough surplus to make fairer bargains possible.
