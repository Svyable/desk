# A 2030 Scorecard

A book about the future should eventually have to face the future.

The chapters in this manuscript make many arguments that are deliberately conditional. Data-center electricity demand may rise quickly, but efficiency can change the slope. Machine intelligence may push value toward verification, but only if raw capability really does commoditize. Semiconductor geography may diversify, but industrial concentration can survive expensive attempts to dilute it. Human accountability may become more valuable in some systems, but institutions may discover ways to assign responsibility without preserving the professional roles we know today.

Conditional language is honest. It can also become a hiding place.

So here is a scorecard.

These are not forecasts with decimal-point confidence. They are claims strong enough that a reader in 2030 should be able to say that this book saw something important, missed something important, or protected itself with too much ambiguity.

The baseline is the world visible in 2026: global data-center electricity use rising rapidly; AI infrastructure absorbing exceptional capital; inference costs falling quickly at fixed capability; U.S. interconnection queues measured in terawatts of proposed generation, storage and large loads; leading-edge semiconductor production still concentrated despite major geographic diversification programs; and firms only beginning to redesign organizations around persistent machine intelligence.

The scorecard asks what should be different four years from now if the triumvirate is a useful description rather than an attractive story.

**First bet: AI will make electricity planning more important before it makes electricity economically trivial.**

By 2030, the strongest data-center regions should still treat large computational loads as a distinct planning category rather than as ordinary commercial demand. Utilities and grid operators should have more specialized large-load tariffs, interconnection procedures, capacity requirements or curtailment arrangements than they did in 2026.

The reason is not that AI must consume some enormous fixed share of global electricity. The reason is concentration and timing. A workload can be modest in national percentage terms and still be large relative to a constrained substation, transmission corridor or regional planning forecast.

This bet weakens if data-center connection times fall sharply across major markets, if workloads move so fluidly that local power scarcity rarely changes site selection, or if hardware and algorithmic efficiency improve fast enough that electricity becomes a small and uninteresting part of AI infrastructure decisions.

If the average frontier-compute executive in 2030 rarely asks about power availability because the market simply supplies it on demand, several chapters of this book gave the socket too much weight.

**Second bet: the unit cost of adequate machine cognition will keep falling faster than organizations learn to use it.**

The exact capability threshold will change, so the right comparison is not the price of a fixed model name. It is the cost of obtaining an output that meets a useful task threshold.

By 2030, competent drafting, extraction, translation, routine coding, classification and first-pass analysis should be markedly cheaper than in 2026. The larger productivity difference should come from whether organizations rebuilt workflows around that abundance.

Some firms will have cheap intelligence embedded deeply in operations. Others will still use frontier systems as elaborate autocomplete because permissions, data quality, incentives, accountability and legacy software remain harder to change than the model.

This bet fails if model access remains expensive enough that raw inference cost is still the main barrier for ordinary firms, or if organizational adoption becomes so standardized that complementary process redesign stops explaining much of the productivity gap.

If every competent company can buy an “AI operating model” off the shelf and obtain similar results, this book overstates conversion capacity as a differentiated asset.

**Third bet: more cognition will be produced, not merely cheaper cognition.**

The Jevons-for-minds claim should become visible in workload volume.

By 2030, organizations using advanced AI should perform categories of analysis, monitoring, personalization, testing and simulation that they simply did not perform at human scale in 2026. The interesting statistic will not be how many old tasks were automated. It will be how many cognitive operations became routine because their marginal cost collapsed.

Software should run more tests. Businesses should generate more forecasts. Systems should inspect more transactions. Agents should perform more background work. Researchers should search wider spaces before selecting experiments.

The book loses this bet if demand saturates quickly: if companies discover that a small amount of cheap cognition satisfies most valuable tasks, agentic workloads remain narrow, and efficiency gains translate mainly into lower total compute rather than new uses.

Rebound should be observed, not assumed.

**Fourth bet: verification, provenance and authorization will capture more value as generation becomes abundant.**

By 2030, producing a plausible artifact should be substantially less differentiating than proving that the artifact is reliable, authorized and connected to consequence.

The shift should be visible in software testing, audit trails, identity, provenance systems, professional review, warranties, liability structures and products that sell trusted outcomes rather than raw generation.

This does not require every market to pay a “human premium.” In many low-stakes domains, automated output will be accepted without ceremony. The claim is that the economic value of verification rises most where generation becomes cheap while error remains costly.

This bet weakens if models become reliable enough that verification costs fall in parallel with generation, institutions routinely accept autonomous authorization, and users show little willingness to pay for stronger provenance or accountable review.

If cheap generation is followed by cheap trust, the value migration described here will be smaller than expected.

**Fifth bet: leading-edge semiconductor geography will diversify without becoming geographically ordinary.**

By 2030, more advanced fabrication and packaging capacity should operate outside the most concentrated 2026 locations. Projects in the United States, Japan and Europe should add meaningful capability, and supply-chain policy should continue to encourage alternatives.

But the frontier should still exhibit powerful concentration because process learning, specialized equipment, supplier ecosystems, capital intensity and tacit manufacturing knowledge compound.

This bet is not “Taiwan remains dominant forever.” It is that industrial capability moves more slowly than political desire.

The bet fails if advanced fabrication becomes broadly replicable across many jurisdictions with modest yield or cost penalties, or if architectural change makes leading-edge process concentration much less strategically important than it was in 2026.

If a frontier fab becomes something rich countries can reproduce on a predictable schedule merely by financing it, this book romanticized tacit industrial knowledge.

**Sixth bet: strategic technology policy will move toward portfolios of options rather than pure self-sufficiency.**

By 2030, serious national AI and semiconductor strategies should look increasingly like dependency management: multiple suppliers, allied capacity, stockpiles where sensible, portability, domestic competence in selected layers and contingency plans for critical workloads.

Countries will still use the rhetoric of sovereignty, and some will pursue national champions. But complete domestic replication of the stack should remain too expensive and technically difficult for most states.

The strongest strategies should therefore distinguish critical dependencies from ordinary imports rather than treating every foreign component as a sovereignty failure.

This bet loses if geopolitical fragmentation becomes so severe that large economic blocs actually reproduce most critical layers internally and interdependence shrinks sharply. It also loses in the other direction if markets prove so resilient that governments retreat from semiconductor and compute strategy because substitution after shocks is easy.

Either outcome would make the portfolio framing less useful.

**Seventh bet: the most valuable AI adopters will increasingly be distinguished by organizational capital rather than model exclusivity.**

By 2030, many useful model capabilities should be available from several providers, open systems or specialized services. Competitive advantage should therefore shift toward proprietary context, integration, workflow design, distribution, trusted customer relationships, physical execution and the ability to improve operations continuously.

A company saying “we have access to model X” should sound less strategic than it did in the first half of the 2020s.

The important question should be what the company can convert the capability into that competitors cannot easily reproduce.

This bet fails if frontier model capability remains persistently scarce and vertically concentrated enough that access itself continues to dominate the economics of downstream AI products.

If the best model provider captures most of the surplus because the capability gap remains overwhelming, value migration upward will have beaten value migration into complements.

**Eighth bet: apprenticeship will become an explicit design problem in at least some knowledge professions.**

By 2030, firms in law, software, finance, medicine, engineering or other expertise-heavy fields should be talking more openly about how juniors gain experience when machines perform a larger share of the first drafts, routine cases and low-risk repetitions that once trained them.

Some organizations will respond badly by assuming senior judgment will remain abundant forever. Others will create simulations, rotations, supervised exception handling and deliberate practice systems because they recognize that production work had been carrying an educational function.

This bet is weaker than a prediction of mass deskilling. It says only that the training pipeline becomes visible as automation removes some of its old hiding places.

The bet fails if junior learning proves easy to replace automatically, if AI mentorship accelerates expertise enough to offset lost routine work, or if the occupations themselves reorganize so thoroughly that the old senior skill pipeline no longer matters.

That would be a welcome failure.

**Ninth bet: infrastructure contracts will increasingly assign forecast risk to large new loads.**

By 2030, utilities serving fast-growing data-center regions should be less willing to build long-lived infrastructure solely on optimistic load requests without stronger customer commitments.

Minimum bills, collateral, exit charges, direct upgrade contributions, staged connection rights, curtailment obligations or similar mechanisms should become more common where one customer's forecast can induce large public-system investments.

This is a value-ledger prediction. The physical system lasts longer than the server forecast, so contracts should evolve to keep the party creating the forecast exposed to enough downside that it cares whether the forecast is real.

This bet fails if load forecasts become accurate and stable enough that utilities can socialize expansion with little stranded-asset risk, or if competitive generation and modular infrastructure make large-load growth cheap enough that risk allocation stops being contentious.

If nobody is arguing about who pays for the grid, abundance will have arrived faster than this book expects.

**Tenth bet: carbon accounting for computation will become more granular rather than less.**

By 2030, serious environmental claims about data centers should rely more on location, timing, marginal system effects, additional generation and lifecycle boundaries than on a single annual matching percentage.

The shift need not produce one universal standard. The point is that the old abstraction—annual consumption matched with annual renewable procurement—will increasingly be recognized as insufficient for some decisions.

Flexible workloads should also be treated more explicitly as an environmental variable because when and where computation runs can change the emissions consequence.

This bet weakens if grids decarbonize fast enough that temporal matching becomes economically and environmentally unimportant in most major compute regions. That would be another good way for the book to become wrong.

**Eleventh bet: the AI infrastructure boom will create both durable capability and disappointing capital.**

By 2030, some projects and companies financed during the mid-2020s buildout should have produced poor investor returns even while the underlying infrastructure remains useful.

The mechanism is ordinary. Real demand attracts many competitors. Capital arrives. Capacity expands. Prices fall. Some forecasts prove too optimistic. Assets change hands or are refinanced. The technology keeps spreading.

The book does not predict a universal crash. It predicts separation between technological importance and investment return.

This bet fails if infrastructure scarcity remains so persistent that most well-executed AI data-center, chip and power investments from the 2025-2027 buildout earn unusually strong returns through 2030.

In that case the market may have underbuilt rather than overbuilt despite the extraordinary capital wave.

**Twelfth bet: “conversion capacity” will either become measurable or deserve to be retired.**

This is the bet on the framework itself.

By 2030, organizations and governments that repeatedly turn new technical capability into useful deployment should be distinguishable through observable mechanisms: shorter infrastructure delivery times, better procurement, higher diffusion of good practices, faster correction of failed policies, stronger maintenance, more reliable execution, lower integration friction and the ability to move resources when bottlenecks change.

If those mechanisms do not predict much, then “conversion capacity” is only a flattering label applied after success.

The concept should be reduced or discarded.

Likewise, the three ledgers should not be forced into every explanation. Some domains may become so abundant that one ledger genuinely stops mattering for the decision. Some bottlenecks may vanish without creating a consequential replacement. Some markets may coordinate the relevant tradeoffs so well that a broader value analysis adds little.

The framework should shrink where reality makes it unnecessary.

That is the final scorecard item because it is the one that keeps the others honest.

A reader in 2030 should not ask whether every prediction landed exactly. Four years is short for grids and factories and long for model capabilities. Shocks will occur that no chapter anticipated. Policy can change abruptly. A new hardware architecture can alter energy economics. A recession can delay infrastructure that technology would otherwise justify. A geopolitical rupture can make redundancy valuable overnight.

The better grading question is whether the book correctly identified the moving interfaces.

Did cheap cognition make verification, action and context relatively more important?

Did the physical buildout of intelligence remain industrial enough to matter strategically?

Did prices and institutions redirect investment as scarcity moved?

Did abundance create new useful activity rather than merely lower the cost of old activity?

Did the places that could convert resources reliably outperform places that merely possessed them?

And when one of these mechanisms failed to appear, did the framework admit defeat cleanly enough to learn from it?

The point of a prediction is not to look impressive when it comes true.

It is to make error visible.

A civilization that is becoming better at producing intelligence should be willing to spend some of that intelligence keeping score.
