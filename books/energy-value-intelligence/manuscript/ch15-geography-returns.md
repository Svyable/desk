# Geography Returns

The cloud has always had an address.

For years, the metaphor worked because most users did not need to know it. A file went “to the cloud.” A company rented “cloud compute.” A video streamed from somewhere. The abstraction was useful precisely because geography had been pushed down the stack.

Artificial intelligence is pulling it back up.

A large computational workload cares where electricity is available. It cares where fiber is dense, where water or other cooling options exist, where land can be assembled, where tax treatment is favorable, where skilled workers can build and maintain facilities, where latency to customers is acceptable, and where a regulator will allow a load of extraordinary scale to connect.

The digital economy has not escaped geography.

It has become sophisticated enough to choose geography strategically.

The choice begins with power.

In 2024, the United States accounted for the largest share of global data-center electricity consumption. Yet the national total obscures extraordinary concentration. The International Energy Agency noted that nearly half of U.S. data-center capacity was located in five regional clusters. Those clusters did not appear randomly.

They accumulated around complements.

Northern Virginia is the obvious example because the concentration became large enough to turn a local industry nickname—Data Center Alley—into a global reference point. Connectivity helped. Federal and enterprise customers helped. Land and business conditions helped. Most importantly, success became self-reinforcing. Once a region contains data centers, network infrastructure, experienced developers, contractors, utilities familiar with the load profile, and customers accustomed to the location, the next facility has reasons to arrive too.

Clusters are intelligence embodied in place.

They contain knowledge that does not appear on a map: which contractor can deliver, which county understands the permitting process, which utility engineer has solved the interconnection problem before, which supplier keeps spare equipment nearby, which recruiter knows the labor pool.

This is why economic geography persists even when the product can move at the speed of light.

Silicon Valley did not become irrelevant when software distribution became global. Financial firms still cluster. Film production still has centers. Biotech companies still gather near universities and laboratories. Ideas move cheaply, but people, trust, infrastructure and tacit knowledge do not move with zero friction.

AI adds a more explicitly physical reason to cluster: power infrastructure.

That can eventually become a reason to disperse.

Once a cluster's transmission system becomes constrained, electricity prices rise or connection schedules lengthen, the value of locating elsewhere increases. A new region with abundant generation, faster permitting or unused grid capacity can compete.

Agglomeration meets congestion.

Cities have always contained this tension. Density creates productivity through proximity, matching and shared infrastructure. Density also produces high land prices, traffic, crowding and political resistance. The best location becomes expensive precisely because it is best.

Data-center geography follows a similar pattern.

A region may be desirable because it has power, fiber and customers. Growth consumes the available power, land and political tolerance. The cluster generates its own diseconomies.

Then the map widens.

Texas attracts interest because of energy resources, land and a large power market, though its grid has its own reliability and congestion challenges. Parts of the Midwest can offer land and energy. Hydropower-rich regions can offer low-carbon electricity. Regions near new generation projects can market power availability as an industrial asset.

The relevant geography differs by workload.

Interactive inference cares about latency. A voice assistant should respond quickly. Financial applications may care about milliseconds. Sovereignty rules may require data or computation to remain within a jurisdiction. Sensitive enterprise workloads may need specific security guarantees.

Training is more movable.

A large training run can tolerate greater physical distance from the end user if data, chips and networking are available. Batch inference, scientific simulations and background agents can also be more flexible than interactive services.

This creates a hierarchy of computational mobility.

The most flexible workloads can chase cheap or abundant energy. The least flexible stay close to users, networks or legal jurisdictions.

Software schedulers can exploit the difference.

A cloud operator with facilities in several regions can route nonurgent work toward lower-cost locations. In principle, workloads can move toward periods of renewable surplus or away from grid stress. The ability to move computation becomes a substitute for moving electricity.

This is an extraordinary property.

A factory cannot teleport its blast furnace from Ohio to Arizona for the afternoon. A computational task sometimes can.

Bits can arbitrage place.

That does not eliminate physical constraints. The destination needs spare hardware and network capacity. Data may be expensive to move. State can be difficult to synchronize. Users may have contractual or privacy requirements. An idle accelerator in another region is useful only if the software stack can route work to it without creating larger costs.

Mobility must be designed.

The same is true for energy flexibility. A data center may have schedulable workloads, but if business units are rewarded only for completion speed they will not voluntarily wait for a low-price hour. A platform can theoretically shift demand among regions, but customer contracts may pin it.

Technical flexibility that has no economic interface is stranded flexibility.

This is a recurring theme of the book: the physical, cognitive and value layers have to line up.

Water illustrates the geographic stakes particularly well.

Data centers generate heat. Some cooling systems use water directly or indirectly. Water consumption varies substantially by climate, cooling technology, facility design and electricity source. A facility in a cool climate may have very different requirements from one in a hot, dry region.

A global statement that “AI uses water” therefore tells us little about local consequence.

The important question is whether the additional demand is significant in the watershed where the facility sits, during the season when water is scarce, relative to alternative cooling technologies and other users.

This is where geography turns externalities into politics.

A liter of water in a wet region during a surplus period does not have the same value as a liter in a drought-stressed basin. The physical molecule is identical. The opportunity cost is not.

The same applies to land.

A large facility on low-value industrial land near transmission may create little conflict. The same facility on land valued for agriculture, housing, conservation or cultural significance can create intense opposition.

Location changes the value ledger.

This should make policymakers skeptical of national averages when planning industrial strategy. “The country has enough electricity” is not an interconnection study. “The state has abundant water” is not a local water balance. “The region has cheap land” says nothing about where transmission exists.

Capacity is granular.

The energy transition makes this granularity more important because new generation is often located far from the highest demand. Wind and solar resources have geography. Hydropower has geography. Gas pipelines have geography. Nuclear plants need suitable sites and regulatory support. Transmission connects the map, imperfectly.

A future rich in electricity will still have locational prices.

This creates an incentive to move energy-intensive industries toward generation rather than build unlimited transmission to historic centers of demand.

Aluminum did this. Fertilizer and petrochemicals followed feedstocks. In a more electrified economy, data centers, hydrogen production, synthetic fuels and some industrial processes may follow cheap power.

The result could be a new industrial map.

Regions that were peripheral to the software economy could become central to the intelligence economy because they possess the physical complement.

That sounds like an opportunity for regional development.

It is, with caveats.

A data center can represent billions of dollars of capital investment while employing relatively few permanent workers compared with a labor-intensive factory. Construction jobs can be substantial. Tax revenue can be meaningful. Suppliers can cluster. But the local economic multiplier depends on ownership, tax structure, procurement and whether the facility becomes part of a broader ecosystem.

A region should not confuse capital intensity with community value.

The project may still be worthwhile. It should be judged on the actual terms.

Cheap power can be sold in several forms. A region can export electricity if transmission exists. It can convert electricity into a transportable commodity such as aluminum, hydrogen-derived products or data-center computation. It can use electricity domestically to improve households and local industry.

Each conversion has different value capture.

A megawatt used for a data center may produce high-value digital exports and little local employment. The same megawatt in a manufacturing plant may support more workers but generate lower margins. A household electrification program may create broad welfare without producing an export revenue stream.

There is no technically correct allocation independent of social priorities.

Energy abundance becomes industrial policy.

Climate adds another geographic layer.

Cooling demand rises with heat. Extreme weather can threaten power infrastructure. Drought can reduce hydropower and constrain thermal plant cooling. Wildfire affects transmission. Flood risk matters to sites. A data center expected to operate for decades is making a climate bet about its location.

So is every new factory.

The old industrial map was shaped by resource deposits, waterways, railroads, ports and labor pools. The emerging map adds renewable resource quality, grid carbon intensity, data connectivity, climate exposure and semiconductor supply chains.

Geography is not returning because it ever left.

Our abstractions simply got good enough that many decision-makers forgot to ask where the physical layer sat.

Latency provides a beautiful counterexample to that forgetting.

Light is fast. It is not infinitely fast. A signal crossing a continent or ocean takes time even under ideal conditions. Networks add routing and processing delays. For many applications, tens or hundreds of milliseconds are fine. For others they matter.

Physics puts distance back into software.

This means the intelligence system will likely develop layers.

Some computation will remain concentrated in hyperscale centers where hardware utilization and power economics are attractive. Some will move to regional facilities closer to users. Some will move onto phones, vehicles, factories and edge devices where latency, privacy or resilience matter more than central efficiency.

The future is not simply “the data center gets bigger.”

It is a topology problem.

Which intelligence belongs centrally? Which locally? What data should move? What model should run at the edge? How much redundancy is worth paying for?

Energy efficiency interacts with the answer. A small model running locally may consume less total network and data-center energy for a simple task, but local hardware can be underutilized. A giant centralized model can be more capable and efficiently batched while requiring communication and more compute.

System value again.

Sovereignty adds borders to the topology.

Governments increasingly care where data is stored, where chips are available and who controls cloud infrastructure. A country may want domestic or allied compute capacity for national-security workloads. Data-protection law can require particular handling of personal information. Export controls can restrict hardware movement.

The map becomes political.

A cloud region is not merely a technical zone. It can be a jurisdictional promise.

This matters especially for countries without frontier-scale infrastructure. They face a strategic choice between relying on foreign cloud and AI services, subsidizing domestic capacity, building regional alliances or accepting that some layers of the intelligence stack will remain imported.

There is no shame in import dependence. Modern prosperity is built on specialization.

The question is which dependencies are tolerable.

A country may import coffee without anxiety and worry deeply about military communications. It may be comfortable using foreign consumer software and insist that health or defense data remain domestic. It may value access to multiple providers more than owning one national champion.

Strategic geography is selective.

The same is true for companies.

A business does not need to own every data center. It may need multiple suppliers, portable software and contractual guarantees. A model provider does not need to own every power plant. It may need credible access to enough electricity across enough locations that one regional constraint cannot stop growth.

Resilience is geographic optionality.

This is where grids, chips and fiber begin to look like a portfolio.

Too much concentration creates efficiency and fragility. Too much dispersion sacrifices scale economies and increases coordination cost. The right architecture balances them under uncertainty.

The answer changes as intelligence improves because better software can make distributed systems easier to operate. It changes as energy improves because cheap generation can make remote sites competitive. It changes as value changes because users may pay more for sovereignty, low carbon intensity or low latency.

The map is not background.

It is an active economic variable.

For the individual user, a model answer still arrives without a location attached. That experience will become smoother as the infrastructure beneath it becomes more geographically complex.

Good systems hide complexity.

The responsibility of strategy is to remember the complexity anyway.

A cloud is weather.

A cloud service is real estate, power, fiber, machines, law and people arranged so well that you can forget where they are.

Until geography sends the bill.

There is a reason economic geographers never accepted the late-twentieth-century fantasy that telecommunications would make place irrelevant. Distance became cheaper for some transactions, but cheaper communication changed what could cluster as often as it encouraged dispersion. When routine information can travel freely, the residual reasons to be near other firms—specialized labor, tacit knowledge, supplier depth, trust and serendipitous matching—can become more important.

The empirical literature on cities makes the point without requiring romance about urban life. Antonio Ciccone and Robert Hall found a positive relationship between employment density and productivity in the United States; later surveys placed plausible agglomeration gains in a range large enough to matter but far from magical. Dense places are not productive because concrete itself thinks. They are productive because proximity changes matching, specialization, learning and the availability of intermediate services.

The same logic applies to an industrial cluster that happens to contain fewer pedestrians and more substations.

A data-center cluster shares workers, fiber routes, equipment suppliers, permitting knowledge and utility experience. A contractor who has already built a hundred megawatts of critical-load electrical infrastructure is not interchangeable with a contractor encountering the problem for the first time. A utility that has modeled the load shapes of multiple hyperscale campuses has institutional memory. An equipment supplier can justify local inventory because several customers sit within trucking distance.

Those are agglomeration economies.

Then the electricity system adds a harsher boundary than a city usually faces. Office workers can squeeze into a denser district at higher rents. A transmission line has a thermal rating. A substation has transformers of finite capacity. A region may possess abundant generation in the aggregate and still lack the wires to serve one more two-hundred-megawatt campus at the requested node.

The International Energy Agency's 2025 work on energy and AI made the concentration unusually visible. Around forty-five percent of global data-center electricity consumption in 2024 was in the United States, and nearly half of U.S. data-center capacity sat in five regional clusters. Virginia had reached a point where data centers accounted for roughly a quarter of metered electricity supply in the state. More strikingly, the IEA estimated that about half of U.S. data centers then under development were still targeting existing large clusters.

The congestion signal was already blinking. The capital kept following the cluster.

This is not irrational. A place can remain the best available location after becoming expensive. Leaving Northern Virginia may reduce grid pressure but increase latency to customers, complicate network architecture, move a project away from a supplier ecosystem and force a developer through an unfamiliar permitting regime. A competing region has to offer enough advantage to pay those switching costs.

The result resembles the economics of a successful city. High rents do not prove New York has become economically pointless; they prove many people still value access to what is there. Congestion is both a cost and evidence of demand.

For power systems, however, the consequence becomes national planning. PJM, the regional transmission organization serving a large part of the eastern United States, has had to incorporate large-load adjustments from data centers into long-term forecasts. Its 2025 planning materials projected unprecedented load growth across the footprint, and subsequent reviews pointed to tens of gigawatts of expected data-center demand growth over only a handful of years.

That is no longer an office-park story. It is a generation-and-transmission story.

The geographic choice made by private developers can therefore create public infrastructure obligations. A utility may need new substations and lines. A regional market may procure more capacity. Rate design has to decide how much of the cost is assigned to the new load and how much is socialized. Existing customers reasonably ask whether their bills will rise to support a campus whose output is sold globally.

The question is not solved by declaring the data center “good for growth.”

Location determines incidence.

If a developer builds where generation and transmission are abundant, a large load can improve utilization of existing assets and provide a stable customer for new supply. If it builds behind an already constrained interface, the same nominal megawatts can trigger expensive upgrades. If it brings dedicated generation but still relies on the grid for backup, reliability planning changes again.

Megawatts have neighborhoods.

This is also why the ability to move compute can become an industrial advantage. The IEA has noted the underused possibility of locating new facilities where grid capacity is available and operating some workloads more flexibly. A model training job does not care about a county line. If two data centers have compatible hardware and network access, software can sometimes route the job toward the site where electricity is less constrained.

That is geographic arbitrage performed by intelligence.

The obvious temptation is to imagine this will make transmission unnecessary. It will not. Computation is only one load, and even computational mobility depends on fiber, replicated data, spare servers and contractual freedom. But the direction is important: for the first time, one of the largest new industrial loads can move a meaningful share of its productive activity without moving the physical plant.

An aluminum smelter cannot do that. A steel mill cannot do that. A chemical plant cannot do that.

This creates new bargaining power for places that can offer the right bundle rather than simply the cheapest electricity. A remote hydro-rich region with weak fiber may lose to a somewhat more expensive site with excellent connectivity. A low-cost solar region may be unattractive if evening reliability requires expensive backup. A jurisdiction with clean power may gain customers who value low-carbon claims, unless its permitting process makes delivery too slow.

The location decision becomes multicriteria optimization with politics attached.

It also changes regional-development strategy. Local officials naturally see a multibillion-dollar investment and want the project. The investment figure is real. So is the possibility that the tax concessions, infrastructure costs and permanent employment profile produce less local value than the headline suggests.

A useful negotiation asks what remains after the ribbon cutting.

Do local utilities gain assets that benefit other customers? Does the campus pay the full incremental network cost it creates? Are construction and operating jobs local? Does the project attract suppliers, research activity or adjacent firms? Are tax abatements temporary while the infrastructure is durable? Does the company fund new generation or merely claim existing clean supply that somebody else was already using?

There is no universal answer. That is the point.

Geography turns an abstract growth industry into a contract among particular people in a particular place.

The same is true at national scale. A country may possess spectacular solar resources and still fail to turn them into an intelligence industry because the grid is unreliable, capital is expensive, fiber connectivity is poor or legal institutions make customers nervous. Another country with mediocre renewable resources may build a formidable compute sector because everything else in the bundle works.

Comparative advantage is rarely one variable.

Energy abundance can redraw the map, but only where intelligence and institutions know how to capitalize on it.

The frontier may therefore look less like a handful of permanent “AI capitals” and more like a portfolio of specialized regions. Dense metropolitan areas host research, customers and highly interactive services. Giant campuses sit near power and transmission. Sovereign facilities serve regulated workloads. Edge systems handle latency-sensitive tasks. Batch compute migrates toward cheap hours and uncongested nodes. Fiber ties the pieces together.

The cloud becomes geographically differentiated even as the interface becomes more seamless.

That is not a return to a pre-digital world. It is a more mature digital world finally acknowledging the physics it rents.
