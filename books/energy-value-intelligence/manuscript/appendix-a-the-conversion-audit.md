# The Conversion Audit

A framework becomes useful when it changes what you inspect before you spend money.

The easiest way to misuse this book is to turn energy, intelligence and value into three boxes on a slide. Put the project in the middle. Draw arrows. Declare the system understood.

Nothing has been understood.

The purpose of the three ledgers is not to make complexity look tidy. It is to catch category errors before they become expensive. A proposal can be rich in intelligence and poor in physical capacity. It can be physically feasible and economically pointless. It can be profitable for the sponsor and destructive for the system around it. It can have all the required inputs and still fail because no institution can convert them on the required schedule.

The conversion audit is a way to ask the awkward questions early.

Begin with an outcome that can fail.

Not a technology. Not an input. Not an aspiration.

“Deploy AI” is not an outcome. “Add ten gigawatts of generation” is not an outcome. “Train one hundred thousand workers” is not an outcome. “Spend two billion dollars on industrial policy” is not an outcome.

An outcome describes a changed condition in the world.

A hospital reduces the time between an abnormal scan and a treatment decision without increasing preventable errors. A manufacturer raises output per worker while maintaining quality and apprenticeship. A region connects enough reliable low-carbon electricity to support new industry without shifting unreasonable costs onto existing customers. A government processes benefit applications faster while preserving due process and appeal. A person increases the fraction of her work spent on consequential judgment rather than routine production while preserving a credible path to future expertise.

The first sentence of an audit should therefore be brutally concrete: what is supposed to be better, for whom, by when, and what would count as failure?

This is harder than it sounds because organizations prefer nouns.

Nouns are safe. Innovation. Resilience. Transformation. Sovereignty. Productivity. AI strategy. Energy abundance.

Outcomes contain verbs, and verbs create accountability.

Once the outcome is clear, trace the conversion chain backward.

If the desired result is faster treatment, what must happen immediately before treatment? A clinician needs a decision. What must happen before the decision? The relevant information must be available, interpreted and trusted. What must happen before that? Imaging, records, laboratory systems and model outputs must be accessible. What must happen before that? Equipment, staffing, electricity, software, data rights and workflows must be functioning.

Keep walking backward until the glamorous technology is surrounded by ordinary dependencies.

This is where projects become real.

A company says it wants an AI coding agent. Fine. The agent needs repository access. It needs tests capable of rejecting bad changes. It needs permissions that are broad enough to be useful and narrow enough to be safe. It needs a deployment path. It needs somebody to own the result. It needs a way to measure whether generated code improves the product rather than merely increasing commit volume.

The model may be the most sophisticated component in the system and still not be the binding constraint.

A data-center developer says it has secured a gigawatt of capacity. The audit asks what kind of capacity. Is it an interconnection approval, a signed power contract, energized substation capacity, completed building capacity, installed IT load, or operating compute? Each stage is real. They are not interchangeable.

A government says it has allocated money for a factory. The audit asks what the money can actually command. Is suitable land available? Is the grid connection on schedule? Can the equipment be purchased? Are enough construction crews available? Does the customer exist? Is there an operator who knows how to run the process after the ribbon cutting?

Claims settle at interfaces.

That is the first habit of the conversion audit: convert every impressive noun into a sequence of handoffs.

The second habit is to separate stocks from flows.

Stocks are inventories. Flows are what the system can move through time.

A nation may have enormous gas reserves and limited pipeline capacity. A company may employ brilliant engineers and have a review process that allows only a few consequential decisions to reach production each week. A university may contain extraordinary knowledge and transfer very little of it into commercial or public use. A grid may have abundant annual generation and too little power at the node and hour a new factory needs it.

The distinction prevents a common form of optimism: pointing at a large stock and assuming high throughput.

Warehouses teach the lesson plainly. Ten thousand units of inventory do not help a customer if the picking system can ship only one hundred per day. A reservoir is not a water service if pumps and pipes cannot reach the neighborhood. A model's benchmark capability is not organizational productivity if the workflow cannot make use of the answer.

For every important resource, ask two questions. How much exists? How fast can it be converted into the service the outcome requires?

The second number is often more valuable.

The third habit is to identify the binding constraint without falling in love with it.

A bottleneck is not whatever looks scarce. It is the constraint that materially limits the outcome now.

That last word matters.

A shortage can be dramatic and irrelevant. A company may have only two lawyers, but legal review is not the bottleneck if customer demand is weak. A region may have a shortage of advanced chips, but chips are not binding if the proposed data center has no power connection. A team may complain about access to a larger model when the actual failure is that its source data are stale.

The audit should therefore ask a counterfactual question: if this constraint disappeared tomorrow, would the outcome improve substantially?

If not, you have named a scarcity, not the bottleneck.

Then ask the more dangerous question: if the bottleneck disappeared, what would become binding next?

This is where “abundance moves the bottleneck” becomes operational instead of decorative.

Suppose a utility accelerates interconnection studies. Projects move faster until transformer availability becomes limiting. Manufacturers expand transformer capacity. Construction labor becomes limiting. Training expands. Rights of way become limiting. Permitting improves. Financing costs become limiting.

The sequence is not proof that scarcity can never be defeated. Some constraints vanish permanently enough that users stop caring. The purpose of the audit is to anticipate the next expensive interface while there is still time to prepare for it.

The best investment is often in the complement that becomes scarce after your current plan succeeds.

That is a different question from fixing today's problem.

The fourth habit is to measure time as a resource.

Every layer has a clock.

Software can change in hours. Hiring may take months. A factory takes years. A transmission corridor can take longer. A professional reputation can take a career. A political coalition can evaporate in an election. A semiconductor generation can become commercially old while the building around it remains useful.

Projects fail when they combine assumptions from incompatible clocks.

A five-year infrastructure project justified by a six-month product forecast contains a timing risk even if every individual number is sensible. A ten-year tax abatement attached to equipment with rapid technological depreciation contains a different one. A company that eliminates junior tasks this quarter while assuming senior expertise will remain abundant a decade from now is borrowing from an apprenticeship clock it may not be maintaining.

Draw the clocks before drawing the forecast.

Ask which commitments are reversible and which become sunk. Software architecture can often be changed. Concrete cannot be unpoured cheaply. A contract may be renegotiated; a destroyed wetland cannot be restored by changing a spreadsheet cell. A worker can learn a new tool in weeks and may need years to rebuild a professional identity after displacement.

The longer the clock, the more valuable options become.

This is why modularity, interoperability, staged investment and credible exit paths matter. They let a slow asset survive a fast forecast.

The fifth habit is to distinguish value creation from value capture.

A project can make society better off and lose money for the investor. It can make the investor rich and impose costs on everyone else. It can create enormous consumer surplus while commoditizing the supplier that made the improvement possible.

The audit should name the beneficiaries and the payers separately.

Who receives the service? Who pays the invoice? Who owns the asset? Who bears the downside if the forecast is wrong? Who receives the upside if it is right? Who absorbs costs that do not appear in the contract?

This is especially important when public and private systems meet.

A data center may pay for electricity while households pay part of the network expansion through regulated rates. Or the data center may finance infrastructure that improves reliability for everyone. A semiconductor subsidy may protect a strategically valuable capability while transferring public money to shareholders. Or it may create a domestic option whose value becomes visible only during a disruption.

The words “good investment” are meaningless until the point of view is specified.

Good for whom?

The sixth habit is to inspect the missing prices.

Markets are powerful because prices compress information. The audit begins by respecting that power. If something is expensive, assume there is information in the price before deciding the market is foolish. If a skilled trade commands higher wages, perhaps the labor is genuinely scarce. If a grid connection becomes valuable, perhaps connection time is telling you more than the average wholesale power price.

Then inspect what the price cannot know because the institution did not put it there.

Pollution is the familiar example. Strategic dependence can be another. So can unpaid care, political legitimacy, systemic risk, privacy, loss of apprenticeship, or the option value of redundancy.

Do not add these casually.

An unpriced cost is not whatever somebody dislikes. It should have a mechanism, a bearer and a plausible consequence.

The discipline matters because “externality” can otherwise become a word for smuggling preferences into an economic argument without admitting the choice.

Ask who experiences the consequence, why the transaction does not already price it, and what institutional response could include it more accurately.

Sometimes the answer is a price: a tax, tariff, insurance premium, congestion charge or liability rule.

Sometimes the answer is a standard or right because the value should not depend entirely on willingness to pay.

Sometimes the answer is to do nothing because the cost of correcting the imperfection would exceed the imperfection itself.

The audit is not a machine for producing intervention.

It is a machine for exposing the decision.

The seventh habit is to test common dependencies.

Redundancy is easy to fake accidentally.

Two cloud providers may share one data center. Two chip suppliers may depend on one foundry. Two electrical feeders may pass through one substation. Two investment models may use the same historical assumptions. Ten managers may appear to provide independent judgment while every one relies on the same AI system for the first draft.

Count failure modes, not logos.

Ask what single event can break several supposedly independent paths at once.

This is the reliability engineer's contribution to the triumvirate. Diversity has value when it creates genuinely different ways to fail.

The same logic applies to intelligence.

A model that improves average decisions can increase systemic risk if it makes everyone react alike. A hiring model used across an industry can create common blind spots. Trading agents trained on similar data can discover the same strategy. Grid devices responding to identical signals can synchronize into a new peak.

Local intelligence can produce global stupidity.

The audit should therefore include a crowding test: what happens if everyone adopts the same smart strategy?

The answer is often different from what happens when only you adopt it.

The eighth habit is to attack the metric.

Every conversion system eventually chooses a score.

Issues resolved per hour. Cost per token. Megawatts connected. Jobs created. Processing time. Revenue per employee. Carbon intensity. Model accuracy. Graduation rate.

The score is necessary. Without compression, organizations cannot coordinate.

The score is also an invitation.

Ask how an intelligent actor could improve the number while making the underlying outcome worse.

If the metric is code produced, generate useless code. If it is applications processed, reject difficult applications quickly. If it is renewable energy purchased annually, buy certificates while operating during carbon-intensive hours. If it is jobs announced, count temporary construction labor as though it were permanent employment. If it is model accuracy on a benchmark, optimize for the benchmark.

The attack should happen before deployment, not after the scandal.

A useful metric survives an adversarial imagination test.

It does not have to be impossible to game. Nothing important is. It needs enough surrounding measures, audits and consequences that gaming becomes less attractive than achieving the real objective.

The ninth habit is to define the stopping rule.

Abundant intelligence creates a peculiar danger: there is always another analysis available.

Another scenario. Another model call. Another committee. Another optimization. Another report. Another forecast.

More thought is not automatically better.

The audit should ask how much uncertainty the decision can afford to leave unresolved and how much more information is actually worth buying.

A reversible software experiment may justify acting with substantial uncertainty because failure is cheap and feedback arrives quickly. A nuclear plant, dam, medical approval or irreversible environmental intervention deserves more evidence because the cost of error is larger and correction is slower.

Match the depth of thought to the reversibility of action.

This is one of the cleanest places where energy, value and intelligence meet. The physical commitment determines the cost of being wrong. The value at risk determines how much verification is justified. Intelligence should be spent until the expected benefit of another unit of analysis falls below its cost or delay.

The stopping rule prevents cognition from becoming bureaucracy.

The tenth habit is to define what would prove the story wrong.

This book has already attacked its own framework for being too flexible. The conversion audit should inherit that suspicion.

If you believe electricity will be the binding constraint on AI growth, name the evidence that would make you downgrade the claim: falling connection times, rapidly expanding generation, workload migration, large efficiency improvements, or electricity becoming a trivial fraction of delivered value.

If you believe human accountability will command a premium, name what would refute it: institutions accepting machine authorization broadly, liability shifting away from identifiable professionals, customers showing no willingness to pay for human responsibility, or automated systems achieving trusted legitimacy without a human signature.

If you believe a particular industrial subsidy is purchasing resilience, name the shock it protects against and the cheaper substitute that would make the subsidy unnecessary.

A forecast without a retirement condition becomes identity.

The audit should end with a date at which its assumptions will be challenged again.

This makes the framework useful under change rather than merely persuasive at the moment of writing.

A conversion audit can now be run on almost anything.

Take a corporate AI program.

The stated outcome is not “use AI.” It is perhaps to reduce the elapsed time from customer request to reliable resolution by thirty percent without increasing severe errors or reducing retention.

Trace backward. Resolution requires a worker or agent with authority to act. Authority requires access to systems. Access requires security and audit. Good recommendations require context. Context requires usable data. Deployment requires integration. Integration requires engineering time. The model is one component.

Separate stocks and flows. The company may have terabytes of data and a terrible path for retrieving the right record during a live case. It may have hundreds of engineers and one overloaded security review team. It may have a strong model and no automated tests.

Find the binding constraint. Perhaps model quality is not it. Perhaps the expensive queue is permission to deploy.

Look one constraint ahead. If security review becomes automated, production observability may become the next bottleneck. Prepare it before celebrating the first improvement.

Inspect value capture. Customers receive faster service. The company may save labor. Workers may gain leverage from better tools or lose bargaining power if staffing falls. The provider may capture subscription revenue. Regulators and customers carry some tail risk from errors.

Attack the metric. If the team is rewarded for tickets closed, the machine may learn to close difficult cases prematurely. Pair speed with recontact rate, severe error rate and customer outcome.

Define the stopping rule. A low-stakes support reply may be automated after light testing. A refund above a threshold may require stronger verification. The depth of control follows consequence.

Define falsification. If the model does not improve resolution after integration and data cleanup, stop blaming complements and reconsider the model or use case.

That last sentence is essential.

The framework does not get infinite chances.

Now take a regional energy strategy.

The outcome is not “build renewables.” It may be to supply an additional five gigawatts of reliable low-carbon industrial load by a target year without degrading household reliability or producing unacceptable rate increases.

Trace backward from energized customer load. Sites need substations. Substations need equipment and construction. Transmission needs corridors and approval. Generation needs financing and interconnection. Customers need long-term demand. Tariffs need to allocate risk.

Separate annual energy from deliverable power. A region can generate enough terawatt-hours and still fail the peak or locational requirement.

Find the bottleneck. If interconnection studies dominate the schedule, reform them. If studies become fast and transformer deliveries dominate, the intervention changes. If equipment expands and rights of way dominate, politics becomes the constraint.

Inspect value capture. Who pays for network expansion? Who receives the industrial tax base? Are households subsidizing mobile customers? Are developers funding assets that benefit the wider grid?

Attack the metric. Gigawatts announced are not gigawatts delivered. Use energized capacity, reliability performance, cost incidence and actual load served.

Define falsification. If expected industrial demand fails to materialize, stop building against stale forecasts merely because projects have become institutional commitments.

Now take a national AI strategy.

The outcome is not “AI sovereignty.” It may be to preserve the ability of critical health, defense, public-administration and research systems to operate through a specified class of external disruption.

Trace the dependency graph. Chips, fabrication tools, cloud providers, electricity, fiber, model weights, software libraries, data, people, financing and allies.

Separate ownership from control. A domestic company using one foreign cloud and one foreign chip supply chain may be less resilient than a foreign service that can move among several allied providers under enforceable contracts.

Find common dependencies. Three national champions relying on the same accelerator source are not three independent paths.

Price the option. Redundancy costs money. Make the risk it buys down explicit. If substitution after a shock is cheap, resilience spending may be waste. If substitution takes five years, the option may be valuable.

Attack the metric. Domestic compute installed is not sovereign capability if critical workloads cannot use it or the electricity system cannot support it during stress.

Define falsification. If open standards, allied supply and rapid substitution make domestic duplication unnecessary, retire the subsidy.

Finally, take a career.

The outcome is not “be AI-proof.” Nothing deserves that promise.

The outcome may be to increase your ability to create useful results under changing technology while preserving income, learning and agency.

Trace your own conversion chain. Which part of your work produces an artifact? Which part requires access? Which part requires trust? Which part requires physical presence? Which part exists because you are authorized to decide? Which part trains you for the next level?

Identify the task whose unit cost is falling fastest.

Then ask what becomes more valuable when it does.

If drafting becomes cheap, maybe direct observation, client trust, judgment, distribution or execution becomes more valuable. If analysis becomes cheap, maybe choosing the question matters more. If software creation becomes cheap, maintaining systems and understanding users may gain relative importance.

Do not merely run toward the new scarcity.

Ask whether you want the life attached to it.

That is where the value ledger becomes personal rather than economic.

A career is not a portfolio optimized only for wages. Geography, family, craft, autonomy, status, health and meaning belong in the choice. The three ledgers are not an instruction to become maximally marketable. They are a way to see the trade you are actually making.

The conversion audit ends where the book began: with three ledgers that refuse to collapse into one.

Can the world physically do what the plan requires?

Can the system turn available capability into a reliable result on the required schedule?

Is the result worth enough, to the relevant people under the relevant rules, to sustain the conversion?

Then add the questions the book earned along the way.

What becomes scarce if the plan works?

Who is carrying a cost that the headline number hides?

Which supposedly independent paths fail together?

Which clock is slowest?

Which metric will an intelligent optimizer learn to game?

What evidence would make us stop believing the story?

Those questions do not guarantee a good decision.

They make certain bad decisions harder to hide.

That is enough to justify the audit.

Civilization does not suffer from a shortage of plans.

It suffers from plans that stop one interface too early.

The purpose of the conversion audit is to follow them one interface farther.
