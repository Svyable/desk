# Chips Are Frozen Decisions

A semiconductor is a library of engineering choices that electricity can read at extraordinary speed.

The phrase is imperfect, but it captures something important. A modern processor embodies decisions about physics, materials, architecture, instruction flow, memory, packaging, manufacturing tolerances, thermal limits and software interfaces. Years of human intelligence are compressed into a physical object that can then perform trillions of operations without reconsidering the design each time.

Intelligence becomes matter.

That conversion is one reason the semiconductor industry is such a useful place to study the triumvirate. Chips transform electrical energy into information processing. Their economic value depends on what useful computation they enable. Their production depends on a supply chain whose complexity makes many claims about a “digital economy” sound pleasantly naive.

The leading edge begins long before the wafer enters a fab.

Chip designers specify architectures. Electronic design automation software helps manage structures too complex for any person to draw manually. Intellectual-property blocks can be licensed and reused. Manufacturing equipment firms build tools for deposition, etching, inspection and lithography. Chemical suppliers provide materials of extraordinary purity. Foundries transform designs into silicon. Packaging firms connect dies, memory and substrates. Memory manufacturers produce high-bandwidth components that can become bottlenecks in their own right. Data-center builders then integrate the finished systems with networking, power and cooling.

No single company “makes the chip” in the everyday sense.

The product is a coordination achievement across specialized firms, countries and generations of accumulated know-how.

This specialization creates efficiency because each layer can become exceptionally good at one difficult problem. It also creates concentrated dependencies.

At the frontier, a small number of firms occupy positions that are extraordinarily difficult to replace quickly. Advanced lithography equipment comes from a narrow supplier base. Leading-edge fabrication is concentrated. High-performance memory and advanced packaging have their own capacity constraints. Critical materials and equipment contain further chokepoints.

The exact map changes. The structural point does not.

Specialization lowers the cost of coordination until a disruption reveals how much coordination the system assumed.

This is the semiconductor version of the abstraction trap. A software company sees an accelerator available from a cloud provider and experiences compute as a service. The cloud provider sees a server from an original equipment manufacturer. The server vendor sees chips, memory and boards. The foundry sees designs, wafers, tools and process recipes. The equipment maker sees precision components and specialized suppliers.

Every layer hides the one below.

The hiding is productive. Nobody could build modern technology if every user had to understand the full stack.

But hidden dependency is still dependency.

The 2026 Stanford AI Index noted that the United States hosts the largest concentration of AI data centers while most leading AI chips are fabricated by one Taiwanese foundry. That juxtaposition describes a strange industrial geography: computational power can be physically located in one country while its most critical components depend on manufacturing capacity concentrated thousands of miles away.

Value, energy and intelligence are geographically separated before being recombined.

This separation creates strategic questions that ordinary price signals may underweight during calm periods. If a supplier is reliable and capacity expands, concentration can be efficient. Duplicate capacity elsewhere may look expensive. But the value of diversification rises sharply under geopolitical risk, natural disaster, trade conflict or industrial accident.

Resilience is an option that appears wasteful until the state of the world changes.

Governments have therefore returned to semiconductor industrial policy. The United States, European Union, Japan and other jurisdictions have offered subsidies, tax incentives and strategic support for fabrication and related supply chains. These programs are often criticized, with reason, for their cost, political allocation risk and the possibility of subsidizing capacity that markets might have built anyway.

They also respond to a real problem: private project returns and national strategic value are not identical.

A government may value domestic capacity because it reduces exposure to a geopolitical chokepoint even if the local fab has a higher production cost than the global leader. That difference is an insurance premium.

The difficult part is knowing how much insurance to buy.

Too little and the country remains vulnerable. Too much and industrial policy becomes an expensive attempt to recreate every layer domestically, sacrificing the productivity gains from specialization.

Strategic autonomy is not self-sufficiency.

The better concept is option value: maintain enough alternative capacity, inventories, alliances, knowledge and substitution pathways that a disruption does not become paralysis.

Chips make this concrete because fabrication capacity cannot be summoned quickly. A leading-edge fab requires enormous capital, specialized equipment, skilled labor, process knowledge, infrastructure and customers. Even after construction, yields have to improve. Manufacturing at the frontier is not a matter of buying machines and pressing start.

Knowledge lives in process.

This is an underappreciated form of intelligence.

Some knowledge is explicit: designs, manuals, patents, recipes. Some is tacit: the accumulated ability of teams to run a process near the edge of physical tolerances, diagnose defects, tune equipment and improve yields. Tacit knowledge is difficult to transfer because it is distributed across people and routines.

A machine-learning model can help preserve and extend such knowledge. It cannot instantly create decades of manufacturing experience.

This limits a common fantasy about AI and industrialization. Better design intelligence can shorten some development cycles. It can help optimize layouts, detect anomalies, plan construction and automate documentation. But intelligence has to meet physical learning curves.

A fab teaches through production.

The same is true of batteries, reactors, aircraft, factories and grids. There are things a society learns only by building and operating.

Intelligence can compress search. It cannot eliminate every feedback cycle from reality.

Semiconductor economics also illustrate how value moves through a stack.

A chip designer can capture extraordinary margins when its architecture is uniquely valuable and supply is constrained. A foundry can gain bargaining power when leading-edge capacity is scarce. Memory suppliers can benefit when high-bandwidth memory becomes a bottleneck. Packaging capacity can suddenly matter more as chiplet architectures and large accelerators increase demand for advanced integration.

The glamorous layer is not always the profitable layer forever.

Scarcity migrates.

The AI boom has made this migration visible. At one moment, accelerator availability dominates discussion. Then power becomes the constraint. Then networking. Then packaging. Then transformers. Each bottleneck attracts capital and attention until capacity expands or demand changes.

This is why static value-chain analysis fails in fast-growing industries.

The future winner is not necessarily the firm currently earning the highest margin. High margins are an invitation. Competitors invest. Customers design around the bottleneck. Suppliers expand. Governments intervene. Technology substitutes.

Value attracts intelligence and capital toward its own destruction.

That is one of the healthiest features of competitive markets.

It is also why monopoly positions matter. If entry is blocked by natural scale, intellectual property, regulation, network effects or extraordinary technical barriers, scarcity can persist long enough to generate durable rents. Semiconductor fabrication at the leading edge has some of these properties: capital requirements are vast, process knowledge compounds, equipment access is specialized and customers are reluctant to risk important designs on an unproven process.

Difficulty becomes defensibility.

Yet even here, value depends on demand. A fab full of obsolete capacity is not valuable because it was hard to build. A chip no workload needs is expensive sand.

Necessary and difficult must meet desired.

The materiality of chips also complicates the environmental accounting of intelligence.

Electricity consumption receives attention because it is continuous and measurable. Manufacturing footprint is less visible to the user. Semiconductor fabs use water, chemicals and substantial energy. Advanced data-center systems require metals and complex supply chains. Rapid hardware replacement can create embodied emissions and waste.

This does not mean older hardware should always be kept longer. A new accelerator may be so much more energy-efficient that replacement reduces lifecycle impact for heavily utilized workloads. The answer depends on utilization, manufacturing footprint, energy mix, performance improvement and what happens to the old equipment.

Lifecycle optimization is a calculation, not a slogan.

The physical constraints inside chips are becoming more important as well.

For decades, the semiconductor industry benefited from remarkable scaling. Smaller transistors and architectural improvements delivered more computing performance at lower cost. The historical pattern taught software developers to expect hardware beneath them to improve almost as a natural law.

The scaling regime has become more complex.

Performance gains now rely increasingly on specialized accelerators, advanced packaging, parallelism, memory bandwidth and system-level design rather than a simple continuation of one transistor trend. Power density and heat removal constrain how much computation can be packed into a system. Moving data between memory and processors consumes energy. Networking large clusters introduces communication overhead.

The frontier is no longer just “more transistors.”

It is an energy and architecture problem.

This gives intelligence a new task: co-design the system around physical limits.

A model architecture that uses hardware efficiently can be more valuable than a larger model that wastes computation. A chip tailored to a dominant workload can outperform a general-purpose design on cost per useful result. Software that reduces memory movement can save energy. Data-center cooling can allow denser systems.

The layers co-evolve.

This co-evolution makes forecasting treacherous. A projection based on current chips can overstate future energy demand if hardware and algorithms improve rapidly. A projection based on efficiency trends can understate demand if cheaper computation creates larger workloads. A projection based on one supply chain can miss substitution.

We should be wary of single curves.

The more durable question is which decisions become frozen into infrastructure.

A chip tape-out freezes an architecture long enough to manufacture it. A fab freezes a process bet and location. A data center freezes a power connection and building geometry. A transmission project freezes assumptions about where generation and demand will grow. A software platform freezes interfaces that other developers begin to depend on.

Once other layers build around a decision, changing it becomes expensive.

This is path dependence.

The cost of switching creates value for incumbents and risk for everyone else.

Standards can reduce that risk by making components interoperable. Open software ecosystems can reduce dependence on one vendor. Multi-cloud architecture can create redundancy. Chiplet designs can allow components from different processes to be combined. Yet every abstraction has performance costs and new interfaces.

There is no free modularity.

Integration can be more efficient because the system is optimized end to end. Modularity can be more resilient and competitive because pieces can be replaced. The right architecture depends on how much the environment is expected to change.

Fast change increases the value of options.

That is why the most important asset in the AI hardware race may not be a particular chip generation. It may be the capacity to keep making good decisions as the bottleneck moves.

A firm with strong chip design, software, networking, customer relationships and capital can adapt. A country with energy, engineers, allied supply chains, research institutions and functioning infrastructure can respond to shocks. An investor with exposure only to today's scarce layer may discover that the scarcity was temporary.

Intelligence is the ability to navigate the moving constraint.

The chip itself is yesterday's navigation made physical.

When electricity passes through it, those frozen decisions become active again as computation.

That is the extraordinary conversion at the heart of the AI economy.

Human intelligence designs a physical object. Industrial systems manufacture it. Energy flows through it. The object produces machine intelligence. Machine intelligence then helps design the next object.

The loop can accelerate.

But every turn still passes through factories.

That final sentence becomes less abstract when a factory is given an address. TSMC's first Arizona fab entered volume production in the fourth quarter of 2024, making 4-nanometer products in a desert state better known for copper, retirees and relentless summer heat than for leading-edge logic. By the company's 2025 annual report, the second Arizona fab was being pulled forward toward high-volume production in the second half of 2027, the third had begun construction, and TSMC had announced a much larger American expansion that included additional fabrication and advanced-packaging capacity. At the same time, the company was preparing multiple 2-nanometer phases in Taiwan, expanding in Japan and building a specialty fab in Germany.

The map did not flatten. It became more complicated.

That distinction matters. Diversification is often narrated as if one jurisdiction simply copies the industrial capability of another. Frontier manufacturing does not move like a spreadsheet cell. It moves as equipment deliveries, construction schedules, water rights, electrical substations, technician training, supplier qualification, process recipes, customer commitments and thousands of small operating habits. A new fab can reproduce a process node without instantly reproducing the industrial ecology that made the original cluster so effective.

This is why geography and learning interact. The first wafer out of a new location is not the end of the story. Yield, cycle time, defect detection, maintenance and supplier responsiveness become a continuing education. The factory accumulates memory by operating. A country that wants semiconductor capacity is therefore buying more than a building. It is buying the chance to keep learning how to run one.

The economics are correspondingly awkward. If geographic redundancy were free, every customer would want it. It is not. Duplicate factories can lower utilization. New locations may have higher labor or construction costs. Suppliers may have to build parallel footprints. Government support can shift losses from shareholders to taxpayers without changing the underlying cost. A resilience policy can be sensible and still be expensive; calling it strategic does not repeal arithmetic.

The more useful question is what failure the extra capacity is meant to insure against. A temporary shipping disruption calls for inventory. A single-equipment bottleneck may call for supplier diversification. A geopolitical rupture may justify duplicated fabrication. A long-term technological rivalry may require education, research institutions and domestic tool capability, because a fab without engineers or equipment access is an impressive shell.

Different risks demand different redundancies.

Trade policy adds another layer of frozen decisions. An accelerator is designed to a performance envelope, but the commercial value of that design can change when an export-control threshold moves. In January 2026 the U.S. Bureau of Industry and Security revised its licensing policy for certain advanced processors bound for China, allowing applications for products including Nvidia's H200 and AMD's MI325X to be reviewed case by case when specified conditions were met. The significance was larger than the immediate list of chips. It demonstrated that the usable market for a semiconductor can depend on rules rewritten after the architecture was chosen and after wafers were committed.

The chip carries politics it did not contain at tape-out.

That makes semiconductor design partly an exercise in regulatory option value. A supplier may create variants for different markets. Customers may stock permitted hardware before rules change. Cloud providers may become an alternative route to computation when physical shipment is restricted, inviting new rules around remote access. Governments try to write controls that remain meaningful while performance metrics, packaging techniques and architectures evolve around them.

Regulation becomes another participant in the co-design problem.

This is not an argument for or against a particular export restriction. National-security policy has objectives that commercial optimization does not. It is an argument against treating hardware as a neutral commodity whose destiny is fixed once manufactured. The same physical device can be abundant in one market, prohibited in another, strategically subsidized in a third and indispensable everywhere.

Value is jurisdictional.

The semiconductor industry also shows why a narrow measure of intelligence can mislead. The brilliant architecture attracts attention because it is legible: benchmark charts move, model training accelerates, product announcements arrive. The less legible intelligence is scattered through toolmakers, process engineers, construction managers, chemical suppliers, packaging specialists, reliability teams and utility planners. Their decisions determine whether the architecture exists in sufficient quantity to matter.

A civilization that celebrates invention while allowing production capability to atrophy can become excellent at imagining machines it cannot reliably make. The opposite failure is possible too: a country can build factories without enough design, software or demand to keep them at the frontier. Production and invention are complements, not rival religions.

The loop between machine intelligence and semiconductor manufacturing may tighten this relationship. Better models can search design spaces, help place circuits, predict defects, schedule fabs and diagnose equipment. Those gains could make physical learning faster. But the output of the model still has to survive a wafer, a package, a thermal envelope and a customer workload. Reality retains veto power.

The strategic advantage, then, is not to escape the factory. It is to make the factory more intelligent while keeping enough factories, suppliers and skills alive that intelligence has somewhere to land.

The chip is frozen decision. The industry around it is the capacity to thaw, test and refreeze decisions again.
