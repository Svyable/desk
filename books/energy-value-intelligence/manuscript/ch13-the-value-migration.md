# The Value Migration

When a bottleneck disappears, the money does not disappear with it.

It goes looking for the next constraint.

Picture a production line with one machine that determines the output of the whole plant. Improving every other station does little while work continues to pile up at that machine. Improve it, and throughput rises until something else starts limiting the line.

Economic systems are less obedient, but the motion is recognizable.

Scarcity moves. Bargaining power moves with it. Value capture follows.

A technological breakthrough can therefore make one layer less profitable while creating fortunes next door. Cheap storage weakens storage as a differentiator and raises the importance of search, security and interpretation. Cheap solar modules make interconnection, land, financing and permitting loom larger in project economics. Cheap first-pass analysis can make trusted context, execution or authorization more valuable than producing another draft.

The useful claim is not the textbook observation that scarce things command a price. It is dynamic: successful innovation changes the location of bargaining power.

That movement surprises incumbents because organizations often define themselves by whatever used to be scarce.

Newspapers once owned scarce printing and distribution. Digital publishing made distribution abundant and attention became harder to command. Television networks controlled scarce broadcast slots; online video multiplied supply and shifted power toward recommendation, brand, audience loyalty and platform access. Professional firms sold expensive access to human expertise; cheap machine drafting can push their economic center toward trust, proprietary context, negotiation and responsibility.

The business does not necessarily disappear. Its center moves.

The movement is rarely clean because technologies substitute unevenly. A model may draft beautifully and negotiate badly. It may understand public information and lack the client's tacit context. A robot may dominate a controlled factory task and fail in a cluttered house.

There is therefore no permanent list of “AI-proof” complements. There is only today's residual.

As capability improves, the residual moves again.

That is why career advice built around supposedly safe tasks ages badly. The more durable question is structural: what remains necessary when this capability becomes cheap, and why is that remainder difficult to substitute?

Sometimes the answer is physical execution. A model can design a kitchen renovation. Someone still has to enter the house, measure, demolish, carry, wire, plumb, tile and discover what the previous owner hid behind the wall. Robotics will narrow that gap, but embodiment has a different capital, energy and safety profile from digital cognition. The path from a language model to a document draft is shorter than the path from a language model to autonomous construction.

Sometimes the answer is permission. A model can produce an engineering design while a licensed professional remains legally responsible for signing it. A power project can be financially attractive and wait on interconnection. A drug candidate can look promising and still require trials and regulatory approval.

Rights to act can become more valuable as proposals become cheaper to produce.

That does not make every permit or license wise. Some protect incumbents long after their original justification has faded. Others embody a deliberate judgment that certain mistakes are costly enough to justify friction. The economic point is narrower: if proposal generation scales faster than approval capacity, the approval interface acquires bargaining power.

Sometimes the answer is context.

A general model can be highly capable and still lack the facts that matter inside one company: customer histories, equipment logs, contract terms, pricing rules, process measurements and the undocumented reason a workflow looks strange. Calling all of this “data” hides the real scarcity. More data is not automatically better. Old records can encode obsolete practice; bad records can make a capable model confidently wrong; sensitive records carry rights and liabilities.

The scarce asset is usable, governed context.

Sometimes the answer is distribution. A brilliant capability without customers can capture less value than a merely good capability embedded in software people already use. Operating systems, productivity suites, cloud platforms, commerce networks and established customer relationships all reduce adoption friction.

The strategic fight then moves to routing: who decides which intelligence receives the request?

If interfaces standardize and models become substitutable, routing can weaken model-provider power. If one platform controls the customer relationship, it can capture value without producing every underlying capability itself.

Sometimes the answer is capital. Cheap ideas make selection and endurance more important. A thousand generated business plans do not finance themselves. A thousand computationally proposed drug targets still compete for laboratory throughput and clinical budgets. A factory design still has to survive construction risk and a period in which the future is uncertain.

Capital is stored option value.

Sometimes the answer is reputation. Surface polish can be copied quickly; evidence accumulated through time cannot. A company that has delivered reliably for twenty years possesses a history a generated identity lacks. Reputation can collapse in a day, but it cannot usually be manufactured in one.

Sometimes the answer is attention.

Machine generation can expand the supply of articles, videos, messages, proposals, advertisements and entertainment far faster than human waking time expands. Recommendation systems gain power because they mediate that scarcity. Intelligence then gets deployed not merely to create media but to predict and influence a finite nervous system.

The economic problem becomes a political one: which forms of influence are legitimate, and who gets to optimize the scarce attention?

Sometimes the answer loops back to energy. Cheap intelligence expands usage; data centers grow; power connections become valuable in constrained regions; technology companies sign generation contracts. Then intelligence is applied back to energy through better cooling, workload scheduling, chips, forecasting and grid control.

The three ledgers rotate because no layer owns the surplus permanently.

A practical market analysis can therefore begin with five questions.

What just became cheaper or more abundant? Which complements are required to turn that abundance into the outcome customers actually value? Which of those complements materially constrains the outcome? Who controls it? What will capital, technology, customers or regulators do to erode that scarcity?

The last question prevents a temporary shortage from masquerading as a permanent moat.

Scarcity attracts its own destruction. High electricity prices attract generation and transmission. High chip margins attract fabrication investment and architectural substitution. Expensive licensed services invite software, regulatory reform or new business models. Valuable datasets attract competitors, collection efforts and synthetic alternatives.

A durable scarcity needs protection stronger than today's imbalance of supply and demand. Physics can provide it. Geography can. Law, network effects, accumulated trust, tacit knowledge and scale can too.

A copper deposit cannot be copied. A transmission corridor through dense land is difficult to recreate. A payments network becomes more useful when both sides already accept it. A trusted institution accumulates legitimacy through time. A manufacturing process improves through repeated learning that a new factory cannot buy from a catalog.

These durable complements are where technological surplus often gets divided.

David Teece's classic work on profiting from technological innovation made the distinction explicit: inventing something valuable and capturing the value it creates are different activities. An innovator can lose the economic prize to owners of manufacturing, distribution, service, standards or other complementary assets when those assets hold the stronger bargaining position.

The personal computer is a familiar example precisely because the box became less interesting than several layers around it. Durable value accumulated in operating systems, processor architectures, developer ecosystems, enterprise relationships and distribution. The physical machine mattered. The location of scarcity inside the system changed.

William Nordhaus approached the problem from the other side by estimating how much technological surplus producers historically captured. His result should not be turned into a universal constant, but the implication matters here: consumers can receive enormous gains from innovation even when the pioneering producer captures only a fraction.

Capability, company revenue and social value do not rise in lockstep.

A model improvement can create enormous consumer surplus while competition pushes inference prices toward cost. A model provider can capture extraordinary rents for a period while customers fail to turn the capability into productivity. A company can create little frontier capability and still capture a large share of the gains because it owns distribution or a trusted workflow.

Value migration is not a moral verdict on who deserved the money. It is an account of bargaining position.

That position can be engineered. A firm facing commoditization can integrate downstream, build an ecosystem or strengthen distribution. A customer fearing dependence can insist on open standards, portable data and multi-provider routing. A government can invest in a complementary asset such as transmission or semiconductor fabrication because controlling it changes the value captured elsewhere.

But a predicted scarcity can be crowded before it arrives. If every investor decides data is the moat, money floods into data acquisition and synthetic alternatives. If every enterprise decides power is the bottleneck, connection queues swell and equipment manufacturers expand. If everyone decides distribution is the winning layer, customer-acquisition costs rise.

A moat suggests a fixed castle. Value systems are closer to river deltas: the channels move as sediment accumulates and storms open new paths.

This is especially important in the AI transition because access to capable models can become more equal while outcomes become less equal.

Research on “digital capital” has tried to measure the firm-specific complements that allow information technology to become productive: reorganized workflows, trained people, data, management practices and accumulated know-how. The important observation is simple. The server can be purchased. The organization capable of using it well cannot be purchased in one transaction.

Two firms can buy the same model at the same API price and receive radically different value. One has clean context, clear processes, managers empowered to change the workflow, tests capable of rejecting bad output and systems that can observe what happens after deployment. The other adds a chatbot to a broken process and calls it transformation.

Equal access to intelligence does not imply equal conversion efficiency.

As model access gets cheaper, organizational capital can therefore become more valuable. The question shifts from who has the model to who can embed it into operations without destroying reliability, accountability or customer trust.

Countries face the same asymmetry. Importing a model is easier than importing a functioning administrative state. Cheap AI can give civil servants better drafting, analysis and translation tools. If records are corrupt, procurement is dysfunctional and nobody can act on the analysis, the intelligence remains decorative. Another government can convert a less impressive model into much more public value because its institutions can execute.

This is not an argument for a mystical national “conversion score.” The mechanisms need names: procurement competence, grid reliability, construction speed, diffusion of good practice, contract enforcement, technical workforce, maintenance, the ability to reverse a failed policy. If the broad phrase adds nothing beyond those mechanisms, discard the phrase.

The labor market will experience value migration at a finer resolution than job titles suggest. Suppose a model reduces the time needed to prepare a competent financial analysis. Spreadsheet mechanics may command less value. Demand can rise for people who obtain reliable source data, choose assumptions, explain uncertainty, negotiate with counterparties and accept responsibility for a decision. Then models improve at extraction and scenario generation. The premium moves again.

A worker cannot guarantee safety by naming the next complement once.

One useful clue is price compression. When a previously expensive capability gets cheap quickly, ask who still has pricing power around it. Another is queue formation: if customers can cheaply generate more projects, which approval, capital or physical resource starts accumulating a backlog? Another is vertical integration: when companies suddenly begin buying suppliers, land, power or distribution, they may be revealing where they expect bargaining power to move.

Corporate capital expenditure is a map of anticipated scarcity.

The current AI buildout makes that map unusually visible. Model companies sign long-term compute agreements. Hyperscalers acquire land and power. Chip designers deepen software ecosystems. Utilities receive data-center connection requests. Firms whose product appears virtual keep reaching downward into physical complements because the physical layer can ration the digital one.

The direction can reverse. If hardware becomes plentiful while model capability remains differentiated, value can move back upward. If models converge in capability and price, applications and customer relationships gain relative importance. If open systems become good enough for many workloads, proprietary providers may compete on reliability, integration or safety rather than raw intelligence.

There is no final layer.

That matters for antitrust and public policy. Concentration can reflect genuine productivity and control of a bottleneck at the same time. High margins alone do not tell us which. The more useful question is whether the structure keeps the migration process alive.

Can new firms enter when a complement becomes valuable? Can customers switch? Are standards open enough to prevent unnecessary lock-in? Are scarce public resources such as spectrum, rights of way and grid connections allocated toward useful deployment rather than incumbency? Does intellectual property protect real investment without freezing an obsolete control point forever?

Competition is one mechanism that turns today's rent into tomorrow's invitation.

When it works, high returns say: solve this scarcity. Capital and intelligence respond. Capacity grows. Substitutes emerge. Prices fall. Value migrates.

When it fails, scarcity hardens into a tollbooth.

The distributional consequences follow the same logic. If routine cognition becomes abundant while ownership of capital, land, distribution, energy infrastructure or trusted data remains concentrated, owners of those complements can capture more of the surplus. Strong competition can pass gains to consumers. Worker ownership, bargaining institutions and fiscal policy can spread them differently. New scarce human roles can raise wages for some people.

Technology changes the bargaining terrain. It does not write the final contract.

The same is true between countries. A country with abundant clean energy, strong grids, semiconductor access, skilled labor and stable institutions may capture more of the AI buildout. Another may possess talent but weak power infrastructure and export people or remote services instead. A state controlling a chokepoint in fabrication, minerals or equipment may gain strategic leverage.

But owning a complement does not guarantee capturing the surrounding value. A country can export cheap electricity embodied in computation while most profit, intellectual property and high-value services accrue elsewhere. Another can use the same physical investment to build a local ecosystem of research, suppliers and operating expertise.

Conversion and capture are separate problems.

For individuals, the lesson is less grand and more useful.

Do not ask only whether AI can perform your current task. Ask what becomes more valuable when it can.

If writing is cheap, firsthand access may matter more. If analysis is cheap, choosing the question may matter more. If software creation is cheap, customer understanding and maintenance may matter more. If plans are cheap, execution may matter more. If synthetic media is abundant, provenance may matter more.

Then keep watching those layers too. The machine is coming for some of them next, and investment is coming for the rest.

The aim is not to stay permanently ahead of technology. Nobody does.

It is to notice when value has stopped living where your old map says it lives.
