# The Value Migration

When a bottleneck disappears, the money does not disappear with it.

It goes looking for the next constraint.

This is easiest to see in a simple production line. Suppose one machine determines the output of the entire plant. Improving every other machine has little effect because work still piles up at the bottleneck. Improve the bottleneck and throughput rises until some other stage becomes limiting.

Economic systems behave similarly, though the bottlenecks are less obedient.

Scarcity moves. Bargaining power moves with it. Value capture follows.

This is why a technological breakthrough can simultaneously make one industry less profitable and create fortunes in an adjacent layer.

If data storage becomes cheap, storing data is less differentiated. The value may move toward software that organizes, searches, secures or interprets the data. If generic computation becomes cheap, advantage moves toward specialized software, distribution or proprietary workflows. If solar modules become cheap, project economics become more sensitive to interconnection, land, financing and permitting.

If intelligence becomes cheap, the valuable layer will often be whatever intelligence cannot cheaply substitute for.

This chapter is about that migration.

The idea sounds close to saying that scarcity determines price, which is basic economics. The more useful claim is dynamic: technological progress creates its own next scarcity.

A capability can be so successful at one layer that it makes another layer strategically important.

Cheap machine-generated code increases the value of knowing what software should do. Cheap design increases the value of manufacturing capacity. Cheap analysis increases the value of trusted data. Cheap generation increases the value of verification. Cheap electricity can increase the value of transmission. Cheap transportation can increase the value of attractive land.

Progress redistributes attention to the interfaces it used to hide.

This can surprise incumbents because organizations often define themselves by the layer that was historically scarce.

A newspaper once had scarce printing presses and distribution. Digital publishing made distribution cheap, and attention became the bottleneck. A television network once controlled scarce broadcast spectrum and scheduling. Online video expanded supply, and recommendation, brand, audience loyalty and platform access became more important.

A professional service firm historically sold access to expensive human expertise. If competent first-pass analysis becomes abundant, the firm may discover that clients value trust, accountability, proprietary context and the ability to execute more than the raw production of text.

The business has not disappeared. Its economic center has moved.

This migration is rarely clean because technologies substitute unevenly.

A model may be excellent at drafting and poor at negotiation. It may analyze public information and lack the client's tacit context. It may produce options and be unable to persuade a regulator. A robot may perform a factory task and fail in an unstructured home.

The “AI-proof” complement is therefore not a permanent category.

It is today's residual.

As capability improves, the residual moves again.

This is one reason career advice based on a fixed list of safe skills ages badly. People are told to move toward creativity, then models become creative in some senses. They are told to move toward coding, then code generation improves. They are told to move toward management, then agents handle portions of coordination.

The more durable strategy is to understand the structure of complementarity.

What remains necessary when this capability becomes cheap?

Sometimes the answer is physical execution.

A model can design a kitchen renovation. Someone must enter the house, measure, demolish, carry, wire, plumb, tile and deal with the surprise behind the wall. Robotics may automate portions over time, but the physical environment is variable and mistakes are costly.

This creates a temporary and potentially durable premium for skilled trades in an economy where office cognition becomes cheaper.

The premium is not because physical work is more noble. It is because the substitution frontier moves at a different speed.

Energy, intelligence and robotics are intertwined here. Cheap intelligence can improve robotics, but robots require energy, actuators, sensors, materials and safe physical operation. The path from language model to autonomous construction is longer than the path from language model to document drafting.

Embodiment is a bottleneck.

Sometimes the answer is permission.

A model can produce an engineering design, but a licensed professional may need to sign it. A developer can find land, but zoning may prohibit the use. A power project can be financially attractive and wait years for interconnection. A drug candidate can look promising and still require clinical trials and regulatory approval.

Rights to act become valuable.

Permits, licenses and standards can be abused as barriers to entry. They can also embody society's decision that some errors are costly enough to justify friction.

When intelligence makes proposal generation cheap, approval systems face pressure. More applications arrive. Regulators must either scale their own intelligence, simplify rules or create queues that become economically valuable.

The permit becomes a scarce complement to cheap planning.

Sometimes the answer is trusted data.

A general model trained on public information can be capable and still lack the details that matter inside a particular company. Customer histories, equipment logs, pricing data, process measurements, contract terms and internal decisions can make generic intelligence contextually useful.

Data then becomes an asset.

But “data is the new oil” is too crude. Data has quality, rights, freshness and context. More is not automatically better. A bad database can make a capable model confidently wrong. Sensitive data creates privacy and security liabilities. Historical data can encode obsolete practices and bias.

The scarce asset is not raw data. It is usable, governed context.

That requires intelligence to maintain.

Sometimes the answer is distribution.

A model developer can build an extraordinary capability and fail to reach customers. A mediocre capability embedded in a product people already use can capture more value. Distribution reduces adoption friction.

This is why incumbents with operating systems, productivity suites, cloud platforms, search engines, commerce networks or large customer bases hold strategic advantages in AI. They can place intelligence inside existing workflows.

The countervailing force is low switching cost. If AI interfaces become standardized and models become substitutable, users may route among providers and erode platform power.

The battle is over the interface.

Who decides which intelligence receives the request?

That routing layer can become valuable because it allocates demand across the underlying models. It is analogous to a market or an operating system: the allocator does not need to produce every capability if it controls access to the user.

Sometimes the answer is capital.

Cheap ideas can make financing more important.

If AI makes it easy to generate thousands of business plans, the scarce input is not the plan. It is the ability to distinguish a credible team, fund the operation and endure uncertainty. If scientific models propose many promising drug targets, laboratory budgets and clinical capital become limiting. If engineering tools make factory design easier, construction finance matters more.

Capital is stored option value.

It allows a project to consume resources now for uncertain future value.

In an abundance of proposals, capital allocators face a harder selection problem. Intelligence can help them analyze. It can also generate more polished nonsense.

Trust returns.

Sometimes the answer is reputation.

A brand is economically valuable when it reduces uncertainty or carries meaning. If AI makes the visible quality of marketing materials easy to imitate, the history behind the brand may matter more. A company that has delivered reliably for twenty years has evidence that a newly generated identity lacks.

Reputation is costly because it requires time.

Time is difficult to synthesize.

This does not make brands invulnerable. Reputation can collapse quickly. New entrants can build trust through transparency, guarantees, communities or third-party verification. But when surface-level polish becomes abundant, accumulated evidence can gain weight.

Sometimes the answer is attention.

This may be the most universal migration.

Machine generation expands the supply of things that can ask for human attention. Articles, videos, messages, software notifications, proposals, ads and entertainment can all multiply.

Human waking time does not.

The competition for attention becomes more intense.

Recommendation systems therefore gain power because they mediate the scarcity. A platform that decides what billions of users see can capture value even when the content itself is abundant.

This creates a dangerous incentive loop. If attention is the scarce resource, intelligence gets deployed to capture it. Models personalize content, timing and persuasion. The system becomes very good at the metric that pays.

Value directs intelligence toward the bottleneck.

The bottleneck is a human nervous system.

This is why the economics of attention cannot be treated as a side issue in the AI era. Energy and compute may become abundant enough to generate effectively unlimited media. The scarce good is a person's willingness to care.

Markets will pay handsomely for systems that predict and influence that willingness.

Society will then need to decide which forms of influence are legitimate.

Sometimes the answer is energy itself.

This is the loop returning to its starting point.

As machine intelligence gets cheaper, usage expands. Data centers grow. The value of power connections rises in constrained regions. Technology companies invest in generation. Suddenly an industry whose product is information competes for transformers and gas turbines.

Cheap intelligence makes energy scarce locally.

Then intelligence is applied to energy: better cooling, better chips, workload scheduling, grid optimization, new generation technologies.

The triumvirate rotates.

This dynamic suggests a practical way to analyze any fast-changing market.

First, identify the newly abundant layer.

Second, identify the complements required to turn that abundance into a valued outcome.

Third, ask which complement is both necessary and difficult to substitute.

Fourth, ask who controls it and how long the scarcity can last.

Fifth, ask what capital and intelligence will do in response.

This is not a framework to be worshipped. It is a habit of looking one step beyond the visible breakthrough.

The fifth step is especially important because scarcity attracts its own erosion.

If electricity is scarce, developers build generation and grids. If chips are scarce, manufacturers expand. If a profession charges high rents because of a restricted license, political pressure may grow. If a platform captures too much value, competitors and regulators respond. If a dataset creates advantage, others collect alternatives or generate synthetic data.

The new bottleneck is usually temporary unless protected by something stronger than current demand.

That protection may be physics, geography, law, network effects, accumulated trust, tacit knowledge or scale.

These are the durable scarcities.

A copper deposit cannot be copied. A transmission corridor through dense land is difficult to recreate. A global payments network benefits from acceptance on both sides. A trusted institution accumulates legitimacy over time. A manufacturing process improves through learning.

The future will be shaped by contests over these durable complements.

This is also where inequality enters the story.

When a formerly scarce productive input becomes abundant, owners of the remaining complements can capture a larger share of the surplus. Cheap AI may reduce the value of routine cognitive labor while increasing the value of ownership of capital, distribution, land, data and energy infrastructure.

That outcome is not inevitable, but it is structurally plausible.

If workers own little capital and bargaining institutions are weak, productivity gains can accrue disproportionately to asset owners. If competition is strong and services become cheaper, consumers can capture enormous value. If governments tax rents and invest in public goods, gains can spread further. If workers use AI to become more productive in scarce human roles, wages can rise.

Distribution is institutional.

Technology changes the bargaining terrain. It does not write the final contract.

The same is true between countries.

A country with abundant clean energy, strong grids, semiconductor access, skilled labor and stable institutions may capture more of the AI buildout. A country with talent but weak power infrastructure may export people or remote services rather than host frontier compute. A country controlling a chokepoint in minerals, fabrication or equipment may gain strategic leverage.

Energy and intelligence become terms of trade.

Value decides what the leverage is worth.

This is why the future will not be divided cleanly into “technology winners” and “energy winners.” The advantage belongs to systems capable of converting one strength into the other while retaining enough of the surplus.

A state can export cheap electricity embodied in computation and capture little if the data center is foreign-owned, lightly taxed and weakly connected to the local economy. Another can use the same infrastructure to build a domestic ecosystem of research, services and industry.

Conversion determines capture.

For individuals, the lesson is similar but less grand.

Do not ask only whether AI can perform your current task.

Ask what becomes more valuable when it can.

If writing is cheap, perhaps firsthand access is valuable. If analysis is cheap, perhaps judgment is valuable. If software is cheap, perhaps customer relationships are valuable. If plans are cheap, execution is valuable. If synthetic media is abundant, provenance is valuable.

Then watch those layers too, because the machine is coming for some of them next.

A moving future requires moving strategy.

The point is not to stay permanently ahead of technology. Nobody does.

The point is to understand that value is not attached forever to a skill, object or industry.

It migrates toward whatever the new abundance cannot yet replace.
