# The Three Ledgers

A modern data center keeps at least three kinds of score.

The first is brutally physical. Electricity enters through substations and switchgear. Power is converted, conditioned, routed, used by processors, memory, storage and networking equipment, then used again to remove the heat created by all that computation. Operators watch load, temperature, voltage, utilization, water, backup capacity, battery state, equipment failures and the gap between the energy delivered to the site and the energy that actually reaches computing equipment. Whatever abstractions are running inside the machines, the building answers to thermodynamics.

The second ledger is financial. Somebody bought or financed the land. Somebody paid for transformers that may have taken months or years to procure. Chips arrive with prices attached. The utility sends a bill. Debt has a cost. Engineers have salaries. Customers pay for reserved capacity, cloud services, software subscriptions, advertising, transactions or outputs. A facility can be a technical marvel and still be a bad investment if the revenue never covers the claims made on it.

The third ledger is harder to see. It records what the machines know how to do.

Not consciousness. Not metaphysics. Capability. How much useful uncertainty can the system remove? How well can it classify, predict, generate, search, compress, plan, translate, diagnose, optimize or control? How much human attention can it save? Which decisions can it improve, which tasks can it perform, and how reliably can it tell the difference between an answer and a convincing mistake?

Energy, value, intelligence.

The three ledgers are usually kept by different people. Facilities teams watch the electrical one. Finance watches the monetary one. Researchers and product teams watch the capability one. That separation is sensible inside an organization. It is dangerous as a way of thinking about the future.

The three ledgers settle against one another.

If a new model requires ten times the computation for a modest increase in useful capability, the intelligence ledger may look better while the energy and capital ledgers deteriorate. If a new chip performs far more useful work per watt, the physical ledger improves, but that efficiency may reduce the price of computation enough to create so much new demand that total electricity use rises. If a model becomes cheap enough to automate a task, value may leave the task and migrate toward whatever remains scarce: customer access, trusted data, legal responsibility, physical execution, taste, capital, permits, energy, or time.

This is the central claim of the book: the future is not being shaped by energy, value and intelligence as three independent forces. It is being shaped by their exchange rates.

We already have a language for some exchange rates. Engineers measure efficiency. Economists measure productivity. Investors calculate returns on capital. Managers calculate labor hours saved. Computer scientists compare benchmark performance per dollar, per parameter or per unit of compute. Grid operators compare generation, demand, capacity and reliability. Households compare wages with prices and time with convenience.

But the boundaries between these measures matter. A process can become technically efficient without becoming economically valuable. A valuable service can be physically wasteful. A highly capable intelligence can make a poor decision because the objective it was given was wrong. A profitable industry can impose costs outside its own accounts. A public investment can create enormous value that never appears as revenue to the agency that funded it.

The ledgers do not add neatly.

That is why arguments about technology so often pass one another without meeting. One person points to an extraordinary capability curve. Another points to capital expenditure. A third points to electricity demand. A fourth points to revenue. A fifth points to a social benefit that cannot easily be monetized. A sixth points to a harm that the market price ignores. Everyone can be describing something real while drawing incompatible conclusions.

Consider a machine that produces a legal draft in thirty seconds. A capability benchmark may show that the system can now produce work that once required an hour of a junior lawyer's time. The energy used for that particular inference may be small relative to the human labor displaced. The software provider may charge pennies, dollars, or bundle the capability into a subscription. The law firm may save money, increase output, lower prices, raise margins, or simply perform more iterations before a partner signs off. The client may receive faster service. The junior lawyer may lose a training task that once built judgment. The partner may become more valuable because responsibility cannot be delegated as cheaply as text generation. Courts may receive more filings because drafting became cheaper. Regulators may demand new verification procedures because plausible errors multiplied.

What was the value of the intelligence?

There is no single answer. The answer depends on whose ledger we inspect and on what happens next.

This is not an argument against measurement. It is an argument for knowing what a measurement leaves out.

Energy has the advantage of physical discipline. A joule does not care about a business model. There is a minimum amount of work required to lift a mass, separate materials, heat water, cool a room or move electrons through resistance. Real systems can approach or retreat from theoretical limits, but they cannot negotiate with them. The world of energy contains hard constraints that make many technological fantasies easy to falsify.

Yet energy alone does not explain why one use happens and another does not. A society may possess enough energy to desalinate seawater and still fail to build the plant because financing is unavailable, permitting is slow, governance is weak, the water is politically contested, or a cheaper alternative exists. A country can produce enormous quantities of fuel while citizens experience unreliable electricity because extraction and grid performance are different systems. A household can live in an energy-rich nation while being unable to afford adequate heating.

Physical abundance is not the same as access.

Value has the opposite character. It can move with startling speed because human judgment can change faster than infrastructure. A company can lose billions in market value while its factories remain exactly where they were. A painting can become more valuable without changing chemically. A neighborhood can become expensive because enough people decide they want to be there. A software service can create enormous consumer surplus while being sold at a price close to zero. A scarce object can become nearly worthless when fashion turns.

This flexibility makes value powerful and slippery.

Economists use price because it is observable, comparable and extraordinarily useful. A price can coordinate strangers who know almost nothing about one another. It can pull supply toward demand. It can ration scarcity. It can communicate that something has changed without requiring every participant to understand why. But price is not identical to value, and market value is not identical to social value. A polluted river can be absent from a product's price until law, liability or scarcity forces the cost into the transaction. Unpaid care can create enormous human value without appearing as market output. A monopoly can capture more money while producing less total benefit. An invention can improve millions of lives while its inventor captures only a small fraction of the gain.

So the value ledger is partly a ledger of preferences and partly a ledger of institutions.

Who owns what? Who may exclude whom? Which harms create liability? Which benefits can be charged for? Which claims receive legal protection? Which risks are insured, socialized, ignored or pushed onto people with less bargaining power? Markets answer questions inside rules. The rules help determine what the answers mean.

Intelligence sits awkwardly between the physical and the social.

Brains are physical systems. Computers are physical systems. Thinking requires energy. Memory requires matter. Communication requires channels. Yet the outputs of intelligence often behave like information: they can be copied, combined and reused at very low marginal cost. A design may take years to discover and seconds to transmit. A theorem can guide an unlimited number of calculations. Software can control millions of machines after the expensive work of creating it has been done once. A scientific insight can change how society uses existing energy and capital without adding a new raw material.

Intelligence therefore has leverage.

A better turbine blade does not create energy. It converts available energy into useful work more effectively. A better logistics plan does not create trucks. It uses the trucks differently. A good forecast does not create electricity, but it can help a grid operator commit generation more efficiently. A new chemical process may lower the temperature or pressure required to make a product, changing the energy economics of an industry. A machine-learning model may discover patterns in equipment data that allow maintenance before a costly failure.

In each case intelligence changes the conversion rate between physical resources and valued outcomes.

That makes intelligence look almost magical if we focus only on the output. The magic disappears when we include the cost of discovering, deploying, verifying and governing the intelligence. A design that saves energy in use may require difficult manufacturing. A forecast can become harmful when actors change behavior in response to it. A model can save a worker ten minutes and cost the organization an hour of review because its errors are subtle. A recommendation system can optimize engagement while damaging something the objective failed to represent.

Intelligence is not synonymous with wisdom. It is a capacity for finding and selecting patterns, plans and actions. The quality of the objective still matters.

This distinction becomes more important as machine intelligence gets cheaper. Human societies have long been constrained by the cost of cognition. Expertise takes time to acquire. Attention is finite. Meetings are expensive. Analysis is delayed because the analyst is busy. A small business may not hire a statistician, lawyer, translator, designer, programmer or research assistant because the need is real but not valuable enough to justify the full cost of a specialist. A government agency may leave forms unread because there are too many of them. A scientist may not test a hypothesis because searching the relevant space is tedious.

Lower the cost of cognition and these dormant demands appear.

Some replace existing work. Many do not. They create work that previously was not worth doing at all.

This is familiar from energy history. Efficiency often expands demand because it changes the feasible set. When lighting becomes dramatically cheaper, society does not necessarily purchase the same amount of illumination and pocket the savings. It lights streets, offices, factories, signs, stadiums, parking lots, warehouses and decorative facades. When transport becomes cheaper, people and goods travel farther. When computing becomes cheaper, we put computers into watches, thermostats, cars, toys, cameras, factories and doorbells.

The same logic is likely to apply to intelligence.

A world in which a competent first-pass analysis costs a few cents will contain more analysis. A world in which software can be generated cheaply will contain more software. A world in which personalized tutoring is inexpensive will generate more tutoring interactions, assuming institutions and users value them. A world in which thousands of candidate molecules can be screened computationally will generate more candidates for physical validation. A world in which agents can monitor systems continuously will generate more monitoring.

More intelligence can therefore require more energy even while each unit of intelligence becomes more energy-efficient.

This is already visible in the expansion of data-center infrastructure. The International Energy Agency estimated that data centers consumed roughly 415 terawatt-hours of electricity globally in 2024, around one and a half percent of global electricity consumption, and projected rapid growth through 2030. The number matters less here than the shape of the problem. Computation has become more efficient for decades. That efficiency helped make computation cheap enough to spread everywhere. Now a new category of demand is scaling quickly enough to become a material planning issue for grids in particular regions.

Efficiency did not fail. It succeeded so well that it helped create a larger market.

The financial ledger responds immediately. Power contracts become strategic. Land near substations changes value. Transformer lead times matter to software companies. Natural-gas generation, nuclear projects, batteries, solar farms and transmission lines enter conversations that a decade earlier could have remained mostly within energy companies and public utilities. Technology firms that liked to describe themselves as weightless discover that growth has a footprint measured in acres, megawatts and years.

This is one reason the triumvirate matters now. Intelligence is becoming infrastructure at the same moment that electricity is becoming the preferred carrier for more of the economy.

Transport is electrifying. Heating is electrifying. Industrial processes are experimenting with electrification. Data centers are expanding. Air conditioning demand is rising in many regions. Electricity's share of final energy use is likely to increase. The grid is being asked to absorb new generation, new loads, new storage, new control systems and new expectations for reliability while much of its physical and regulatory architecture was built for a slower-moving era.

In other words, intelligence is arriving at the socket during an already consequential electricity transition.

The interaction will not be uniform. Some regions have abundant power and weak demand growth. Others have strong demand and congested transmission. Some have cheap gas. Some have excellent solar resources. Some have nuclear fleets. Some have hydropower. Some can permit infrastructure quickly; others can debate a line for a decade. Some have deep capital markets. Some have stranded energy but poor connectivity. Some have skilled construction labor and equipment supply chains; others do not.

The intelligence economy will therefore acquire a geography.

So will value.

A megawatt in a place with no transmission, fiber, cooling, political stability or customer demand is not equivalent to a megawatt beside a dense network of complementary assets. A brilliant model without distribution may capture little. A data center with power but no useful workload is an expensive heater. A grid upgrade that looks costly in isolation may unlock generation, industrial capacity and regional growth that exceed the direct return visible to the utility.

The conversion matters more than the inventory.

This principle reaches far beyond AI. A barrel of oil underground is an energy resource, but turning it into useful mobility requires extraction, refining, transport, engines, roads, capital, law and demand. Human intelligence is abundant in every country, but turning it into scientific or economic output requires nutrition, health, education, institutions, tools, networks, freedom and opportunity. Money can finance productive capacity, but money cannot substitute indefinitely for engineers, copper, equipment, trust or competent administration.

Civilization is a conversion machine with losses at every interface.

Some losses are physical. Heat escapes. Friction wastes motion. Resistance dissipates electricity. Chemical processes produce unwanted by-products.

Some losses are cognitive. Organizations misunderstand problems, fail to share information, ignore warnings, optimize the wrong metric, or repeat work because knowledge is trapped in a department.

Some losses are economic and institutional. Capital is misallocated. Monopolies block entry. Prices omit externalities. Useful projects die in coordination failures. Corruption turns public budgets into private rents. Regulations protect incumbents long after the original justification has disappeared. Markets race into bubbles because a true technological opportunity is mistaken for a guarantee of investment returns.

The future can improve by reducing any of these losses.

That creates a useful way to think about progress. We often imagine progress as discovering more resources, inventing a more capable machine or becoming richer. But much progress comes from better conversion: using the same energy more effectively, using the same knowledge more widely, directing the same capital toward better opportunities, coordinating the same people with less friction, turning waste into input, or recognizing a valuable possibility that was present but invisible.

Intelligence is especially important because it can improve the other conversions. It can search for better materials, routes, schedules, designs, contracts, drugs, policies and explanations. But that recursive quality also creates danger. Intelligence can optimize a harmful objective with equal enthusiasm. It can increase the scale at which a bad incentive operates. It can make manipulation, surveillance, fraud or extraction more efficient. Better conversion is not automatically better civilization unless the valued outcome deserves to be valued.

We therefore need all three ledgers at once.

Energy asks: can this happen in the physical world, at what resource cost, and at what scale?

Intelligence asks: can we find a better way to do it, understand what is happening, and choose effectively under uncertainty?

Value asks: is the result wanted, by whom, under what rules, with which costs counted, and who receives the gains?

A serious strategy for the future has to survive all three questions.

The rest of this book follows the ledgers backward before following them forward. Energy came first in the obvious physical sense: life existed before markets, and metabolism before money. Value emerged socially long before modern prices. Intelligence preceded both formal economics and digital computation. The Industrial Revolution then multiplied humanity's command of energy. Electrification changed the architecture of production. Markets and firms developed new ways to coordinate enormous systems. Computing lowered the cost of information processing. Machine learning is now lowering the cost of particular forms of cognition.

Each transition created abundance. Each abundance moved scarcity somewhere else.

That is the pattern worth watching.

At the data center, the three ledgers continue to update. The meter turns. The invoices accumulate. The models improve. A new generation of chips arrives and changes all three columns at once. Somewhere outside the fence, a utility planner studies a queue of proposed loads. An investor studies expected returns. A researcher studies a capability curve. A regulator studies emissions, reliability or consumer prices. A local community studies land, tax revenue, water and noise.

None is looking at the whole future.

Neither are we, yet.

But we can at least keep the ledgers on the same desk.
