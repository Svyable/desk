# Electrify Everything

A steam-powered factory had architecture.

Mechanical power arrived through shafts, belts and pulleys. The arrangement of machines was constrained by the need to distribute motion from a central source. Power had geometry. A machine placed in the wrong part of the building was not merely inconvenient; it could be mechanically awkward or impossible to drive.

Electricity loosened that geometry.

An electric motor could be placed near the machine that needed it. Power could arrive through wires instead of a long mechanical train. The motor could be switched independently. Factories could reorganize workflows around production rather than around the location of the prime mover.

This sounds obvious in retrospect because we inherited the reorganized factory. It was not obvious at the beginning.

General-purpose technologies are often disappointing when first used as replacements for the technology they are destined to reorganize. An early factory owner could install an electric motor and use it to drive the same central shaft that steam had driven. The energy source changed, but the factory logic did not. Some benefits appeared. The larger benefits came when designers understood that electricity allowed a different layout altogether.

The improvement was partly electrical and partly cognitive.

Engineers had to notice what the new capability made possible. Managers had to redesign process flows. Equipment makers had to produce appropriate motors and controls. Utilities had to generate and distribute reliable power. Workers had to adapt. Capital had to finance the transition.

Electricity became valuable through a system of complements.

This is why the economic effect of a technology can arrive later than the technical invention. A device may exist before organizations learn how to use it well. Complementary capital has to turn over. Standards have to settle. Skills have to diffuse. Infrastructure has to reach enough users. Business practices built for the previous regime have to be rewritten.

The phenomenon is sometimes called a productivity lag. It should also be understood as an intelligence lag.

Economic historian Paul David made this point famous by comparing the late twentieth-century computer productivity puzzle with the earlier electrification of factories. The dynamo existed before its largest productivity effects appeared in aggregate data. Industrial plants first had to acquire motors, change layouts, replace old capital, train workers, learn new production methods and build managerial routines around a different power architecture.

The lag was not evidence that electricity had failed.

It was evidence that the complement was the organization.

Factory-level history reinforces the point. Electrification did more than replace a steam engine with a motor. Distributed electric drive reduced dependence on long line shafts. Machines could be arranged around process flow rather than mechanical transmission. Portable tools became practical. Lighting, ventilation and cleanliness improved. Expansion became easier. Studies of early twentieth-century manufacturers have found that electrifying plants increased output and labor productivity relative to plants that did not electrify, with part of the gain coming from more efficient production processes rather than simply from adding more energy or capital.

This matters because it changes the unit of analysis.

If we ask whether an electric motor was cheaper than a steam engine at one task, we can miss the value created by changing the whole factory. The important productivity gain can appear in shorter material movement, fewer transmission losses, more flexible machine placement, better working conditions, faster expansion and tighter control.

The new technology alters the cost of coordination inside the plant.

That is why productivity statistics can lag invention. The installed base of old factories embodies yesterday's assumptions. Managers have careers invested in yesterday's workflow. Accounting categories measure yesterday's units. Suppliers produce equipment compatible with yesterday's system. Workers know how to operate yesterday's process. Reorganization destroys some existing capital and some existing expertise before the new system's advantage becomes visible.

A general-purpose technology often asks society to pay for two things at once: the new tool and the abandonment of the old architecture.

The world can possess a new capability before it possesses the organizational knowledge required to harvest its full value.

That lesson matters for artificial intelligence because companies currently face a similar temptation: bolt a new intelligence layer onto an old process and count the minutes saved. A clerk once copied information from one form to another, so a model now copies it faster. A programmer once wrote boilerplate, so a model now writes the boilerplate. A call-center worker once searched a knowledge base, so a model now finds the answer.

These uses can be valuable. They may also be the equivalent of attaching an electric motor to the old line shaft.

The deeper productivity gain may require redesigning the process around the assumption that some cognition is cheap, fast and continuously available. If software can inspect every incoming document, perhaps the organization no longer needs the same queue. If models can generate and test many design alternatives, perhaps product development changes sequence. If agents can monitor equipment continuously, maintenance may become more predictive. If translation is cheap, the boundary of the market changes. If drafting is cheap, review and accountability become the bottlenecks.

The new technology reveals itself by changing architecture.

There is an uncomfortable implication.

Organizations that measure AI adoption only through task-level time savings may systematically underinvest in redesign. The easiest return comes from making an old step cheaper. The larger return may require deleting the step, moving the decision, changing who has authority, rebuilding a database, exposing a tool interface, altering incentives or accepting a temporary productivity dip while the organization relearns its own workflow.

This makes adoption look slower than capability progress.

A model can improve every few months. An organization may need years to change budgeting, risk controls, software, training and job design. The gap between those clocks can produce alternating disappointment and overreaction. One year the technology appears overhyped because productivity has not moved. The next year a redesigned competitor suddenly operates with a cost structure the old organization cannot match.

The lag is where strategy lives.

Electricity did something else that makes it a useful model for the present. It became a general carrier.

Coal is a fuel. Electricity is not a primary source in the same sense. It is an energy carrier produced from other sources and delivered in a form that can power many different end uses. A grid can accept generation from coal, gas, nuclear, hydro, wind, solar, geothermal and other sources, then deliver electricity to motors, lights, computers, pumps, heaters, trains, compressors and appliances.

That flexibility is one reason modern decarbonization strategies emphasize electrification.

If transport, building heat and some industrial processes shift from directly burning fossil fuels to electricity, the upstream generation mix can change without replacing every end-use device again. A car driven by an electric motor does not need to know whether the marginal electron came from wind, gas, nuclear or hydro. A heat pump can become cleaner as the grid becomes cleaner.

Electricity separates source from service.

This is economically powerful because it creates modularity. The generator and the appliance can evolve on different schedules. A new solar farm can feed an existing fleet of electric devices. A new motor design can use the existing grid. Standards at the interface allow innovation on both sides.

The same principle appears in computing. A cloud service separates the user from the physical server. An application calls an API without needing to know every detail of the model or hardware behind it. A software developer can switch to a better infrastructure provider if the interface remains compatible.

Interfaces create freedom by hiding implementation.

They also create dependency.

A household that heats with electricity becomes more dependent on the grid. A factory that replaces on-site fuel with electric equipment may need a larger connection. An economy that runs more transport, heat, industry and computation through electricity increases the consequence of grid failure. Electrification can improve efficiency and reduce emissions while making reliability and transmission more strategically important.

Abundance moves the bottleneck again.

This is visible in the current language of energy policy. The International Energy Agency describes an emerging “Age of Electricity,” with demand growth driven by industry, electric vehicles, air conditioning and data centers. The phrase captures a real structural trend. Electricity is being asked to carry a larger fraction of economic activity.

But electricity is not a fluid that can simply be poured into demand wherever a forecast shows growth.

A power system must balance supply and demand continuously within operating tolerances. Generation has location. Transmission has capacity. Distribution networks have limits. Weather changes both supply and demand. Some generators can be dispatched when needed; others depend on wind or sunlight. Storage can shift energy through time but has cost, duration and efficiency limits. Large loads can require years of planning and construction before they are connected.

Electricity is therefore an energy system that behaves partly like an information system.

Grid operators must know what is happening across a network and act quickly enough to preserve stability. Forecasts matter. Control systems matter. Markets matter. Protection schemes matter. A physical flow is continuously coordinated by intelligence.

The power grid may be the clearest existing example of the book's triumvirate.

Energy flows through wires. Intelligence forecasts, schedules and controls it. Value appears in wholesale prices, retail tariffs, capacity payments, congestion costs, investment decisions and regulatory judgments about reliability and fairness.

The three do not always agree.

A generator with very low marginal operating cost may produce when electricity is not especially valuable. A transmission constraint can make electricity cheap on one side of a bottleneck and expensive on the other. A reliability resource may sit idle most of the year and still be valuable because it is available during a rare emergency. A household may face a flat retail price even when the system's marginal cost changes sharply by hour. A utility may be unable to earn the full social return from a transmission project that lowers prices across a region.

One price cannot describe the whole grid.

This is why energy debates become confusing when participants use the word “cheap” without specifying the boundary. A solar panel can be cheap. Electricity from a solar farm can be cheap at the point of generation. Delivering reliable electricity to a specific load at a specific hour may require transmission, storage, backup, balancing and distribution whose costs belong to the system rather than the panel.

The reverse mistake is also common. Because integration costs exist, critics sometimes treat variable generation as though its falling production cost were irrelevant. It is not. Cheap generation changes the optimization problem even when complementary infrastructure is required.

The right question is system value.

What combination of generation, networks, storage, demand flexibility and control delivers the desired service at acceptable cost and reliability?

This systems view is essential for AI infrastructure.

A data center does not buy a theoretical average megawatt. It needs power at a site, on a schedule, with reliability characteristics compatible with its equipment and contracts. A region can have abundant annual renewable generation and still face local constraints. Another can have reliable generation but insufficient transmission to the proposed campus. A third can offer cheap power but lack fiber or construction labor.

The energy ledger becomes spatial.

Electricity also reveals the difference between average cost and marginal value. A kilowatt-hour generated at noon in a solar-rich region may have a different system value from a kilowatt-hour available during a winter evening peak. As grids add more generation with similar production profiles, the value of additional output at those same hours can decline unless storage, transmission or flexible demand expands.

This is not unique to renewable energy. Every system encounters changing value at the margin. A gas plant may be valuable for flexibility even if it runs infrequently. Nuclear can provide high-capacity-factor low-carbon generation but faces high construction cost, long lead times and project risk in many jurisdictions. Batteries can respond quickly but their economics depend on duration, cycling, price spreads and service markets.

The physical unit is the same. The system value is not.

That distinction is a preview of what happens when intelligence becomes abundant.

A generated answer will not have a constant value merely because its marginal production cost falls. The first useful diagnosis, code review or translation may be extremely valuable. The thousandth automatically generated report may be noise. Intelligence delivered at the right moment, attached to authority and grounded in good information can matter greatly. Intelligence generated where nobody can act on it may be worthless.

The quality of connection matters as much as the quantity of supply.

Electricity became transformative because society built an enormous connection system. Power stations, wires, substations, standards, meters, appliances, motors, regulators and markets created a platform upon which millions of uses could develop. The grid lowered the transaction cost of obtaining power. Users did not need to build a dedicated generator for every machine.

Cloud computing did something similar for computation. A firm no longer needed to own enough servers for its peak demand. It could rent capacity. Model APIs are attempting the same abstraction for intelligence: request a capability, pay by usage or subscription, and let someone else own the infrastructure.

The analogy is useful but incomplete.

Electricity is highly standardized at the interface. A refrigerator does not usually care which power plant produced the electricity. Intelligence services differ in capability, reliability, latency, privacy, model behavior and governance. The output is not fungible in the same way. A megawatt-hour from two sources can be physically interchangeable after it enters the grid even if its externalities differ. Two model answers can have radically different quality.

Intelligence is a messier commodity.

Still, the economic aspiration is similar: convert a complex underlying system into an easy-to-consume service.

Abstraction encourages demand because it reduces friction. Once electricity became a reliable service, appliance makers could assume it. Once cloud computing became easy to rent, startups could build without constructing data centers. If capable AI becomes available through standardized services, people will build workflows that assume intelligence can be summoned on demand.

That assumption will feed back into the grid.

This is where the historical comparison stops being metaphor and becomes infrastructure planning. AI services require computation. Computation requires electricity. The more society designs processes around continuously available machine intelligence, the more the reliability, price and geography of electricity become part of the intelligence economy.

At the same time, AI may help operate the energy system. Better forecasting can improve scheduling. Machine learning can detect equipment anomalies. Optimization can coordinate flexible loads. Models can assist permitting analysis, engineering design, maintenance documentation and customer service. Scientific models may accelerate materials discovery.

The loop closes.

Electricity powers intelligence that can improve electricity systems.

This recursive relationship creates the possibility of compounding improvement. It also creates correlated dependence. If critical infrastructure increasingly relies on digital control and machine intelligence, failures, cyberattacks, software bugs or model errors can propagate into physical systems. The intelligence layer becomes both a tool and an attack surface.

Electrification teaches us to respect interfaces.

A good interface can make a complex system usable. It can also hide fragility until the interface fails. The wall socket gives no hint of generator availability, transmission congestion, fuel inventories, transformer temperature or balancing reserves. That invisibility is the achievement. It is also why a blackout feels like the sudden disappearance of a natural law.

Modern civilization has made electricity feel ambient.

Machine intelligence may become ambient in the same way. People may stop experiencing it as a separate product and encounter it instead inside software, vehicles, appliances, medical systems, schools, factories and public administration. The most important economic effects may not appear in “AI companies” any more than the most important effects of electricity remained inside electric utilities.

The value moves into the uses.

This is another warning for investors and policymakers who search for the future by looking only at the producer of the enabling technology. The power company enabled electric manufacturing, but much of the value appeared in manufacturers, consumers and entirely new industries. Semiconductor firms enabled software, but much of the value appeared in businesses that used cheap computation to reorganize media, commerce, finance and communication.

If machine intelligence is a general-purpose technology, its largest effects may appear far from the model provider.

They may also take time.

Organizations have installed decades of procedures, software, job categories and regulatory assumptions around the cost structure of human cognition. Reorganizing them will be harder than buying a subscription. Some tasks exist only because information was expensive to move. Some management layers exist partly because attention was scarce. Some professional workflows assume that drafting is costly and review follows. Some educational systems evaluate students by producing artifacts that machines can now generate cheaply.

The electrical factory suggests the right question.

Do not ask only what the new motor can replace.

Ask what the building would look like if the architect had always known the motor was available.

For AI, ask what an organization would look like if competent machine assistance had always been cheap. Which queues disappear? Which controls become continuous? Which roles move from production to judgment? Which services become personalized? Which tasks explode in volume because they were previously too expensive to perform? Where does responsibility sit when generation is cheap but consequences remain costly?

Then ask the physical question beneath all of them.

What does that organization require from the grid?

The answer will differ by use. A small local model may consume little. A hyperscale service may support millions of users. Training may be flexible in time; latency-sensitive inference may be less so. Efficiency improvements may reduce energy per task while demand grows faster. Hardware may shift. Algorithms will improve. Workloads may move geographically in response to energy cost, regulation or latency.

No single forecast will hold.

What will hold is the architecture of the problem.

Electricity is becoming more central to final energy use. Intelligence is becoming more dependent on large-scale electricity infrastructure at its frontier. Intelligence is also becoming more useful in managing that infrastructure. Value will determine which loads receive investment, which generation gets built, who pays for networks, how externalities are treated, and how reliability is priced.

The industrial future will be argued through all three ledgers.

Electrification once allowed factories to stop organizing themselves around a shaft.

The next phase may allow organizations to stop organizing themselves around some of the old costs of thought.

That will not make energy disappear.

It will make the socket more important.
