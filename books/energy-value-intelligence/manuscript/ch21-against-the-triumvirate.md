# Against the Triumvirate

A framework earns its keep when it survives an intelligent enemy.

So far, this book has treated energy, value and intelligence as three ledgers that repeatedly settle against one another. It has argued that abundance in one layer usually moves scarcity toward another, that the decisive unit is often conversion rather than inventory, and that physical, cognitive and institutional systems should be analyzed together.

There is an obvious danger in a framework this broad.

It can become impossible to lose.

If energy is scarce, the framework says energy matters. If energy becomes abundant, it says the bottleneck moved. If intelligence is scarce, intelligence matters. If intelligence becomes abundant, verification or authority matters. If markets work, value signals coordinated the system. If markets fail, the value ledger omitted something.

A theory that can explain every outcome after the fact may explain nothing before it.

That objection deserves more than a footnote.

The strongest case against this book begins with a simpler proposition: perhaps the future is not governed by a triumvirate at all. Perhaps one force becomes so dominant that the other two mostly follow.

The most obvious candidate is intelligence.

Imagine that machine cognition improves far faster than physical infrastructure. Models become better at engineering, science, negotiation, software, forecasting and organizational design. Robotics makes construction more automated. Better materials reduce the amount of copper, concrete, cooling and fuel required for a unit of useful work. AI designs chips that consume less power, schedules grids more efficiently, discovers substitutes for scarce materials, automates permitting analysis and compresses years of engineering into weeks.

In that world, intelligence does not merely sit beside energy and value.

It eats them.

Not literally. Physics remains. But many constraints we currently describe as energy, capital or institutional constraints turn out to have been intelligence constraints in disguise.

A transformer shortage may look physical until better forecasting prevents the shortage, modular designs reduce variation, automated factories expand production and software routes around the remaining bottleneck. A permitting delay may look political until better tools make impacts legible, alternatives easier to compare and public bargaining more precise. A fuel constraint may disappear because intelligence discovers a process that needs less fuel.

The book would then have mistaken today's interface problems for permanent categories.

This is a serious possibility.

History contains many constraints that looked stubborn until an invention dissolved them. Whale oil was scarce until petroleum changed lighting. Long-distance communication was expensive until telegraphy and later digital networks changed the cost structure. Human calculation was a bottleneck until computers made arithmetic effectively free for most purposes.

Not every bottleneck migrates into another valuable bottleneck.

Some bottlenecks die.

That point matters because the phrase “abundance moves the bottleneck” can become too pessimistic if it is heard as a conservation law of scarcity.

Scarcity is not conserved.

A technological system can reduce the total amount of scarcity relevant to a human goal.

Indoor lighting became cheaper without society inventing an equally painful complementary scarcity that canceled the gain. Digital photography eliminated film-processing costs for billions of images without requiring an offsetting bottleneck of comparable burden. Software distribution made copying a program nearly free. The new constraints—attention, storage, discovery, trust—were real, but the user still received an enormous net expansion of possibility.

Abundance can move a bottleneck and improve life at the same time.

More provocatively, sometimes the new bottleneck barely matters.

A person who can take ten thousand photographs does not care much that she lacks time to inspect every one. The attention constraint exists in a formal sense, but it may not bind the value she wanted from the camera. A family streaming music is constrained by listening hours, but that does not mean the abundance of recorded music failed to create something close to post-scarcity access for its practical purpose.

The framework therefore risks overdiagnosing scarcity simply because some finite complement always remains.

Everything finite can be called a bottleneck if the system boundary is wide enough.

A useful theory needs a stronger test: does the complement actually constrain the outcome people care about?

That is the first challenge to the triumvirate.

The second comes from energy.

This book repeatedly returns to electricity because machine intelligence has acquired an industrial footprint. Data centers need power. Electrification increases load elsewhere. Grid queues are long. Transmission and transformers are slow.

But perhaps this is a transitional picture.

Computing has a long history of becoming dramatically more efficient. Hardware, algorithms, quantization, distillation, sparsity, specialized accelerators, better compilers and model architectures can reduce the energy required for a given capability. If those improvements remain rapid while frontier scaling becomes less economically attractive, total AI electricity demand could grow much more slowly than current infrastructure plans imply.

Workloads can also move.

A steel mill is tied closely to its physical plant. Computation is unusually portable. Data, latency, law and network capacity create friction, but many workloads can be shifted across regions or time. A training run does not necessarily care whether the marginal megawatt comes from Texas, Quebec, the Gulf, Scandinavia or somewhere else with suitable infrastructure. If energy prices diverge enough, compute has an incentive to migrate toward abundance.

That mobility could make the grid constraint less durable than this book expects.

A software industry that can move demand toward power may not need every city to rebuild itself around data centers.

The strongest version of this objection says that energy will matter as an input but not as a strategic bottleneck. Electricity becomes a commodity whose supply expands in response to price. Generation, storage and transmission investment rise. Hardware efficiency improves. Data centers locate where the combined package is attractive. The market clears.

Under that scenario, today's power scramble resembles an ordinary investment cycle rather than a deep structural change.

The book would have promoted a temporary shortage into a theory of civilization.

This is testable.

If data-center connection times shrink, power-price differences narrow, workloads move easily, generation expands faster than load and energy cost becomes a small fraction of the value produced by machine intelligence, then the emphasis on the socket should be reduced in later editions.

A framework should not defend itself by declaring that some other physical complement must now be the bottleneck.

It should admit that the energy leg became less important.

The third challenge comes from value.

This book uses “value” broadly: prices, wages, budgets, law, legitimacy, status, social welfare and institutional selection. That breadth is useful because human systems do not choose through one mechanism.

It is also dangerous.

If value means everything that causes a society to choose one outcome over another, then the value ledger becomes close to a synonym for human preference plus institutions.

A concept that broad can hide more than it reveals.

The market-oriented critic can sharpen the complaint. Why not simply say that prices and property rights coordinate scarce resources reasonably well, then analyze the specific market failures where they do not? Why elevate value into a third civilizational force?

Most infrastructure does not require society to agree on a moral theory before it gets built. Buyers pay. Sellers invest. Lenders finance. Insurers price risk. Workers choose jobs. Governments enforce contracts and correct externalities where politics demands it.

Prices do an extraordinary amount of work precisely because they avoid the need for a shared definition of value.

The strongest Hayekian objection to this manuscript is that it keeps looking for a ledger that nobody needs to maintain.

The market is already the distributed ledger.

A higher electricity price tells the data center to become more efficient, move, contract for generation or stop expanding. A high chip margin attracts competitors and capital. Expensive housing attracts construction where rules allow it. A shortage creates a price signal before a committee has finished naming the bottleneck.

From this perspective, “abundance moves the bottleneck” is mostly a verbose description of substitution and relative prices.

Economics already has those tools.

This criticism should sting because it is partly right.

A book about value should not rebrand supply and demand as philosophy.

The defense has to be narrower. Prices coordinate only what enters the market under existing rights, rules and information. Strategic dependence, pollution, public goods, unpaid care, political legitimacy and distribution can matter without being fully reflected in the transaction price. But if those omissions prove small in the domains discussed here, then the broader value ledger is unnecessary.

That too is testable.

If ordinary market signals consistently cause firms to build enough resilience, internalize environmental costs, preserve socially valuable human capabilities and distribute infrastructure burdens in politically durable ways without much institutional correction, then this book has overstated the importance of value outside price.

We should hope for evidence in that direction.

The fourth challenge is empirical vagueness.

“Conversion capacity” is an appealing phrase because everyone has seen resources fail to become results. A government announces money but fails to build. A company owns data but cannot use it. A country has energy but weak institutions.

But how do we measure conversion capacity before success occurs?

If we define it by successful conversion, we have built a tautology.

Successful countries convert well because they have high conversion capacity. We know they have high conversion capacity because they succeeded.

That is not analysis.

A serious version of the framework must identify observable intermediate variables: interconnection times, construction productivity, permitting duration, procurement competence, capital cost, technical workforce depth, equipment lead times, grid reliability, firm entry rates, contract enforcement, diffusion speed, maintenance quality, research output, infrastructure utilization and the ability to revise a failed policy.

These variables can be measured imperfectly.

They can also disagree.

A country may permit factories quickly and perform poorly at innovation. Another may have world-class universities and painfully slow infrastructure. One firm may excel at software deployment and fail at hardware operations. “Conversion capacity” should not wash those differences into one score.

If the concept cannot make useful predictions without decomposing into dozens of ordinary measures, perhaps the concept is only a narrative device.

Narrative devices are allowed in books.

They should not pretend to be equations.

The fifth challenge is that the three ledgers may not be independent enough to deserve equal billing.

Energy is physical.

Intelligence is a capability of physical systems.

Value is a judgment made by intelligent agents.

One could argue that the whole triumvirate collapses into a hierarchy.

Energy enables intelligence. Intelligence produces valuation. Valuation directs energy.

Or start elsewhere: intelligence is the master variable because intelligent agents discover energy sources and invent value systems. Or value is the master variable because nobody builds an energy system or intelligence system unless some agent values the outcome enough to act.

The circularity that this book celebrates can be interpreted as conceptual muddle.

If every variable causes every other variable, what have we learned?

The answer has to come from asymmetry.

At particular moments, one constraint should dominate enough that identifying it changes action.

A data center with no grid connection has an energy-and-infrastructure constraint even if model demand is enormous. A grid with abundant generation and no customer willing to pay has a value constraint. A factory with equipment and customers but no process knowledge has an intelligence constraint.

The framework is useful only when it helps locate the binding interface.

If it merely reminds us that “everything is connected,” discard it.

Everything is connected is true and useless.

The sixth challenge is speed.

The book repeatedly contrasts fast information with slow matter. Software updates in hours. Grids take years. Factories take years. Institutions take longer.

But AI itself may attack that speed mismatch.

Robotic construction, automated engineering, digital twins, faster design review, machine-assisted procurement, autonomous equipment, predictive maintenance and better logistics could compress the time needed to build physical systems. Governments can digitize permitting. Manufacturers can use AI to reduce defects and ramp factories faster. Standardized modular designs can turn bespoke infrastructure into repeatable products.

If physical buildout starts moving much closer to software speed, several chapters in this book become period pieces.

The gap between idea and infrastructure is not a law of nature at its current size.

Some delays are physical. Concrete cures. Mines take time. Transmission crosses land. Nuclear plants contain extraordinary safety requirements.

Other delays are organizational residue.

Intelligence can remove residue.

The optimistic counter-thesis is therefore stronger than “AI will help a little.” It is that machine intelligence will increase the conversion capacity of civilization so rapidly that the very framework used here loses importance. Bottlenecks become visible sooner, alternatives are generated automatically, institutions learn faster and capital moves toward the answer before scarcity becomes painful.

A triumvirate that works perfectly disappears from attention.

Nobody thinks about the logistics system when the package arrives.

Perhaps the future is like that.

The seventh challenge comes from demand saturation.

Jevons-style reasoning is seductive because it explains why efficiency can coexist with rising total use. Make something cheaper, create more applications, expand the market.

But demand is not infinitely elastic.

People do not want infinite lighting, infinite miles of travel or infinite legal memos. At some point another activity becomes more appealing than another unit of the same service. Machine-generated content already demonstrates the problem: increasing the supply of text does not guarantee anyone wants to read it.

Intelligence can become abundant faster than useful questions do.

If most economically valuable cognitive tasks are satisfied by modest amounts of inference, efficiency gains may eventually reduce aggregate resource needs. Models could become good enough that users stop paying for larger ones. Companies could discover that more agents generate more coordination cost than benefit. The frontier could remain scientifically interesting while the mass market stabilizes around cheaper systems.

In that world, cognitive rebound weakens.

The “Jevons for minds” analogy would need revision.

This is another place where the book should resist its own metaphor.

The eighth challenge is political overreach.

Once we say energy, intelligence and value are strategic, governments can justify intervention almost anywhere. Subsidize chips. Subsidize power. regulate models. control exports. plan grids. protect workers. tax externalities. review foreign investment. preserve domestic capacity.

Some of those policies may be sensible.

Together they can become a permission structure for expensive nationalism and industrial favoritism.

A state that worries about every dependency can destroy the gains from specialization. A government that tries to preserve every job can prevent productive reallocation. A regulator that treats every new load as a systemic risk can make infrastructure impossible to build. A country that insists on domestic ownership of every strategic layer may become poorer and less innovative than one that trusts allies and markets.

Resilience can become autarky with better branding.

This book has tried to distinguish option value from self-sufficiency, but the distinction is easy to say and hard to govern. Every incumbent supplier can describe itself as strategic. Every subsidy recipient can describe redundancy as national security. Every agency can call its delay a safeguard.

The burden of proof belongs on intervention.

If the private system can adapt cheaply after a shock, public redundancy may be wasteful. If substitution is fast, strategic dependence is less dangerous. If consumers capture most of the benefit through lower prices, protecting domestic producer rents may reduce rather than create value.

A framework about hidden dependencies should be equally alert to hidden costs of resilience.

The ninth challenge is moral.

The language of conversion can make people sound like inputs.

Human capital. Labor supply. Skills pipelines. Adaptation. Retraining.

These concepts are useful to economists and managers because they describe real mechanisms. They can also flatten a life into an asset that should move toward whatever complement became scarce.

A person is not obligated to become the bottleneck the economy currently rewards.

A community may prefer continuity over maximum productivity. A worker may value mastery in a craft whose market price is falling. A society may preserve inefficient practices because they carry identity, dignity, beauty or independence.

Value is plural partly because human beings are not components in an optimization problem.

If the triumvirate becomes a management doctrine—identify scarcity, route resources, maximize conversion—it has failed morally even if it succeeds economically.

The framework should help people see tradeoffs, not declare one objective superior.

That constraint belongs inside the theory, not outside it.

After all these objections, what survives?

Less than a law.

More than a slogan.

The triumvirate is useful if it behaves like a diagnostic lens with clear conditions for retirement.

Retire the energy emphasis where physical supply becomes cheap, portable and rapidly expandable enough that it rarely changes decisions.

Retire the intelligence emphasis where adequate know-how is fully commoditized and differences in judgment, coordination or learning no longer explain outcomes.

Retire the broader value ledger where prices and contracts capture nearly all the consequences relevant to the decision.

Retire “conversion capacity” where it cannot be decomposed into observable mechanisms that predict something before the result is known.

Retire bottleneck migration where the new complement does not materially constrain the outcome people value.

Retire rebound analogies where demand saturates.

Retire strategic-resilience arguments where substitution after a shock is demonstrably cheap.

A theory should become smaller as the world teaches us more.

That is the opposite of protecting a framework.

It is how the framework stays alive.

There is a practical test for the reader.

Take a project that failed.

Before saying “the bottleneck moved,” ask whether the original premise was simply wrong.

Perhaps nobody wanted the product.

Perhaps the technology was worse than advertised.

Perhaps the project was badly managed.

Perhaps the market correctly killed it.

Not every failure is a deep systems lesson.

Take a project that succeeded.

Before praising conversion capacity, ask whether success came from one overwhelming advantage: exceptional geology, a monopoly, a breakthrough patent, cheap capital, lucky timing.

Not every success proves institutional excellence.

Take a new scarcity.

Before calling it durable, ask what investment it is attracting, what substitutes exist and whether customers can change behavior.

Scarcity is an invitation to its own destruction.

This is where the strongest critic of the book becomes its most useful editor.

The critic asks whether the framework predicted the constraint before everyone saw it.

Whether it identified a real complement rather than any finite variable.

Whether it distinguished temporary shortage from structural scarcity.

Whether it admitted when technological progress removed the bottleneck rather than moving it.

Whether it improved a decision.

Those are fair demands.

The chapter before this one put the triumvirate on a real site in Abilene. The test there is not whether every sentence can be assigned to energy, value or intelligence after the fact. The test is whether examining the interfaces helps explain what actually has to happen for announced gigawatts to become productive computation and then durable value.

If a simpler model does the job better, use the simpler model.

There is no prize for keeping all three ledgers open.

The framework exists to prevent category errors, not to create categories for their own sake.

That is the version of the triumvirate worth carrying into the conclusion.

Not three eternal forces demanding equal weight in every problem.

Three questions that are often useful and sometimes unnecessary.

Can the world physically do this?

Can we intelligently convert the available capability into a reliable result?

Does the result create enough value, under rules we are willing to live with, to keep doing it?

Sometimes the answer to one question will dominate so completely that the other two can wait.

Sometimes a new technology will make an old question disappear.

Sometimes the market will solve the coordination problem before anyone names it.

Sometimes intelligence will dissolve a physical constraint.

Sometimes energy abundance will make optimization irrelevant.

Good.

A framework should celebrate the problems that no longer require it.

The future will be better if this book becomes wrong for the right reasons.
