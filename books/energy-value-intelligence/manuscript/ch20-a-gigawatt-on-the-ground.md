# A Gigawatt on the Ground

The easiest way to misunderstand artificial intelligence is to look at a screen.

The screen is clean. A prompt goes in. An answer comes back. Nothing about the exchange tells you that a serious attempt to scale this capability has started to resemble heavy industry.

For that, it helps to go to Abilene, Texas.

In June 2024, construction began on what would become the first large physical site of the Stargate buildout: an AI data-center campus at Lancium's site outside Abilene. The first announced phase was 200 megawatts. By March 2025, Crusoe said construction had begun on a second phase that would bring the campus to eight buildings, roughly four million square feet and 1.2 gigawatts of total power capacity. OpenAI later described Abilene as the first Stargate site and paired it with a much larger program of additional capacity. By 2026, independent analysts tracking the site from company disclosures, permits and satellite imagery treated it as partially operational and still expanding toward the published campus scale.

There are many numbers in that paragraph, and they are easy to turn into spectacle.

A gigawatt sounds large because it is large. But scale alone is not the interesting part.

The interesting part is how many different systems have to agree before that gigawatt becomes intelligence anyone can use.

Start with the land.

A data center needs physical space for buildings, substations, cooling equipment, backup systems, roads, security, fiber and the machinery that allows a dense concentration of servers to operate continuously. The buildings are not the expensive point by themselves. They are containers for a chain of commitments that stretches far beyond the property line.

The grid has to deliver power.

The semiconductor supply chain has to deliver accelerators, memory, networking equipment and servers.

The capital markets have to finance construction and hardware that depreciate on different schedules.

A customer has to value the computation enough to pay for it.

Engineers have to turn electricity into useful machine work rather than heat, outages or stranded capacity.

Local government has to decide what sort of bargain it wants with an unusually large industrial project.

State and federal policy have to tolerate the chips, fuels, transmission, emissions and financial arrangements required to make the project possible.

Then all of those systems have to remain compatible while the technology underneath them changes quickly.

This is the triumvirate on one property.

Energy arrives most visibly.

Lancium describes the Abilene campus as having a 1.2-gigawatt interconnection approved through the ERCOT process. That does not mean 1.2 gigawatts were instantly available to running servers on the day the approval existed. A grid connection is permission and infrastructure, not an operating load. Buildings have to be completed. Electrical equipment has to be energized. Cooling has to work. Server halls have to be fitted out. Chips have to arrive and survive commissioning. Software has to make use of them.

The gap between an approved interconnection and productive compute is the book's conversion problem in unusually pure form.

Capacity exists in stages.

There is planned capacity.

There is permitted capacity.

There is connected capacity.

There is energized building capacity.

There is installed IT power.

There is utilized compute.

There is useful intelligence produced from that compute.

There is economic value captured from the useful intelligence.

Those stages should never be collapsed into one number.

The public discussion of AI infrastructure often does exactly that. A company announces gigawatts and readers imagine servers running. An analyst counts chips and assumes utilization. A utility sees an interconnection request and treats it as future demand. An investor sees a lease and assumes the customer will still want the same configuration when the building is ready.

Every step contains attrition.

ERCOT's own large-load queue makes the point dramatically. In its 2025 annual report, published in 2026, ERCOT said its large-load interconnection queue contained roughly four hundred requests totaling about 239 gigawatts in February 2026. That was about 2.8 times the grid operator's record system peak.

Nobody serious believes all 239 gigawatts will arrive as simultaneous load on the schedule implied by the queue.

The queue is a map of desire, not destiny.

Some projects will die because financing changes. Some will move. Some customers will contract elsewhere. Some will fail maturity requirements. Some will shrink. Some will discover that transmission upgrades take too long. Some will be replaced by projects we cannot see yet.

But even a queue full of uncertainty has consequences.

Grid planners cannot ignore a request merely because it might disappear. A load measured in hundreds of megawatts can affect transmission planning, stability studies, generation assumptions and the economics of neighboring projects. By 2026, ERCOT was developing a new batch process for large loads and publishing specialized work on how large electronic loads such as data centers behave dynamically on the grid.

That is a remarkable institutional change.

A grid built around homes, factories, oilfields, air conditioners and ordinary commercial loads now has to model facilities whose electrical behavior is tied to enormous fleets of power electronics and computers.

The data center has become a grid object.

The grid has become an AI input.

This is the point where the clean software story ends.

Suppose a model developer discovers an algorithmic improvement that makes a training run twenty percent more efficient. That can save a great deal of electricity and chip time. It may also make the next training run larger because the effective price of capability has fallen. If the competitive frontier moves faster than grid construction, the company may still want every megawatt it can obtain.

The engineer sees efficiency.

The utility sees demand.

The investor sees a scarce connection.

All three can be correct.

Abilene is valuable partly because the connection itself became an asset.

A data-center campus with land but no power is a real-estate project waiting for a reason to exist. A site with a large approved interconnection, fiber, construction activity and a committed customer is different. The ability to draw power at scale on a useful timeline can become more strategically valuable than the concrete shell.

This is value migration into infrastructure.

The chips sharpen the effect.

OpenAI said in July 2025 that its additional Oracle partnership, together with Abilene, would put more than five gigawatts of Stargate capacity under development and eventually run more than two million chips. The statement was about a program rather than only the Abilene campus, but it gives a sense of the hardware scale that the commercial ambition implied.

Again, the number is less important than the chain behind it.

An accelerator is not procured in isolation.

It requires advanced fabrication, packaging, high-bandwidth memory, boards, networking, racks, power supplies, cooling and software. If one layer is constrained, the expensive part behind it can wait idle. An additional transformer is not useful if the building has no servers. A building full of servers is not useful if the network fabric cannot support the workload. A cluster with extraordinary theoretical throughput is not useful if model training repeatedly fails or customer demand does not justify the run.

A gigawatt campus is a synchronization problem disguised as construction.

That synchronization happens across clocks that barely resemble one another.

Software can change today.

A model architecture can change within months.

A chip generation may turn over within a few years.

A server can be replaced faster than a substation.

A transmission asset may last for decades.

Tax agreements can run for a decade.

The community will still be there after several generations of accelerators have become obsolete.

One piece of the project is living in model time. Another is living in utility time. Another is living in municipal time.

The capital structure has to bridge them.

This is where financial abstraction can become dangerous.

Imagine a spreadsheet that treats the campus as one asset with one useful life. The physical campus may retain value for decades because power access, land and network connections can serve later generations of hardware. The accelerators inside it may lose economic value far more quickly. Cooling systems may need retrofits. Rack densities can change. The customer may demand a different architecture. The power connection can outlive the computing equipment several times over.

Different clocks imply different risks.

A financier cares about the durability of the contract.

A model company cares about not being trapped on obsolete hardware.

A data-center developer cares about keeping the site useful across successive hardware generations.

A utility cares about whether the load remains long enough to justify network investment.

A city cares about whether the promised economic base survives after tax concessions and construction activity fade.

There is no single return calculation that makes those interests identical.

The local value ledger is especially revealing.

Abilene did not encounter the project as an abstract national race for AI leadership. It encountered companies, parcels, assessed values, tax-abatement agreements and public decisions.

The city's own records show multiple tax-abatement agreements associated with Lancium and the eight data-center phases. In an August 2025 public agenda summary, the city described agreements offering an 85 percent abatement on the taxable value of qualifying new improvements, repairs and tangible personal property for ten years, with two consecutive abatements available per site under the specified structure. The details matter because a data center can be nationally strategic and locally negotiated at the same time.

A tax abatement is a price signal created by government.

The city gives up some near-term tax claim because it expects enough value in return: investment, employment, future taxable value, infrastructure, economic diversification, prestige, follow-on development or some combination of these.

Whether the bargain proves wise cannot be answered by the data center's technical performance alone.

The servers can run beautifully and the local bargain can still disappoint.

The project can create national strategic value while capturing much of its financial return elsewhere.

It can employ thousands during construction and far fewer people during steady operations.

It can enlarge the local tax base while also increasing demands on roads, housing, emergency services, utilities or public administration.

It can make the region more attractive to suppliers and skilled workers. It can also make local politics more dependent on a small number of unusually capital-intensive taxpayers.

These are empirical questions, not talking points.

By the 2025-2026 city budget, Abilene DC1 and Lancium Abilene already appeared among the city's largest property taxpayers by assessed valuation. That is a concrete way to see the project entering municipal reality. The campus was no longer merely a technology announcement. It had become part of the city's fiscal map.

The distribution of value now matters.

Who owns the chips?

Who receives the model revenue?

Who owns the land and buildings?

Who finances the debt?

Who receives construction wages?

Who receives permanent jobs?

Who pays for grid upgrades?

Who receives the property taxes after abatements?

Who bears environmental or reliability risks if they appear?

Who captures the upside if the campus becomes a durable strategic hub?

A single project can generate many correct answers to the question, "Is this valuable?"

That is why the value ledger cannot be reduced to the private return of the company making the largest investment.

The energy ledger also has more than one column.

A large load creates demand for generation, but the generation mix changes through time. Texas has added extraordinary quantities of solar and battery storage while retaining substantial natural-gas generation and other resources. ERCOT reported roughly 62 gigawatts of new generation synchronized from 2021 through 2025, the majority solar and batteries.

A large data center entering that system does not consume an abstract average electron.

Its effect depends on when it draws power, which resources are marginal, whether it can shift work, whether new generation is built, whether transmission is constrained, how much backup capacity is maintained and how the load behaves during grid stress.

This makes flexibility economically interesting.

Some computation is delay-sensitive. A user waiting for an interactive response does not want the service to pause until wholesale electricity is cheap. Other computation may be schedulable across hours or locations. Training, batch inference, data processing and nonurgent background tasks can sometimes move more easily than an aluminum smelter or hospital.

If compute can become a flexible industrial load, intelligence may help energy absorb intelligence.

That sentence sounds circular because the system is circular.

Software can decide where and when to run workloads. Better forecasts can anticipate grid conditions. Storage can smooth power demand. On-site generation or contracted resources can change the campus's relationship with the grid. Hardware can become more efficient. Cooling can adapt to weather and load.

The data center is therefore not merely demand imposed on the energy system.

It can become a controllable participant in it.

But flexibility should not be romanticized.

A flexible gigawatt is still a gigawatt-scale project. Transmission, substations and generation capacity do not disappear because software can move some workloads. A company may claim the ability to curtail and discover that its commercial obligations make curtailment expensive precisely when the grid would most value it. A workload may be movable in theory but pinned by data location, network capacity, customer latency or software architecture.

Optionality has to be engineered before it can be sold as a virtue.

Water produces another example of boundary confusion.

Public debates about data centers often jump from electricity to water as though every campus has the same cooling system and every gallon has the same local value. They do not. Cooling design, climate, water source, reuse systems, equipment density and operating practice change the footprint substantially.

The correct question is local.

What cooling system is used here? What water source does it depend on? Is the water potable or reclaimed? How does the demand compare with local supply? What happens during drought? What alternatives exist, and what do they cost in electricity or capital?

The triumvirate keeps turning environmental questions into conversion questions.

Saving water can require more energy.

Saving energy can require more capital.

Reducing emissions can require more transmission.

Reducing transmission needs can require moving compute.

Moving compute can create latency or data-governance costs.

There is rarely one resource to minimize.

The goal is a system that creates enough value to justify its complete set of costs.

That brings us to the most uncertain ledger: useful intelligence.

A gigawatt of computation is not a gigawatt of wisdom.

It is an input into a system that may train models, serve users, run agents, search design spaces, generate software or perform work that does not yet exist. The economic case for the campus ultimately depends on whether those activities create enough valuable outcomes to support the physical investment.

This is where enthusiasm can outrun evidence.

A company can have extraordinary confidence that model capability will keep improving. It can still overpay for capacity.

A data-center developer can correctly predict that AI infrastructure demand will grow and still choose the wrong site, customer or financing structure.

A utility can correctly forecast rising electricity demand and still build the wrong asset if the load later shifts geography.

A city can correctly predict construction activity and still misprice the long-run public bargain.

Technological importance does not immunize investment against bad returns.

Railroads should have taught us this.

The tracks transformed economies. Many investors still lost money.

AI infrastructure can produce the same separation between social usefulness and private capture. A campus may eventually provide cheap computation that supports enormous consumer surplus while early investors earn mediocre returns. Or the opposite may happen: a bottleneck may remain scarce long enough for infrastructure owners to capture substantial rents.

The question is not whether AI matters.

The question is where durable scarcity remains after everyone agrees it matters.

Abilene helps because the scarcity keeps moving as you walk through the project conceptually.

Start with models: capability is scarce.

Fund more research and buy more chips: accelerators become scarce.

Secure chips: packaging and memory matter.

Secure hardware: power matters.

Secure power: the interconnection matters.

Secure the interconnection: transformers, substations and transmission matter.

Build those: construction labor and equipment schedules matter.

Finish the building: cooling, networking and commissioning matter.

Run the cluster: useful workloads and reliability matter.

Serve the workloads: customers' willingness to pay matters.

Generate profits: legitimacy, taxes and public bargaining still matter.

At no point does the system become post-scarcity.

It becomes better at moving the constraint.

This is why a one-gigawatt project is more useful intellectually than another chart of AI benchmark performance.

The chart tells us what a model can do.

The campus tells us what civilization must do to make the capability persistent.

It needs industrial firms that can build quickly.

It needs utilities and grid operators that can study unprecedented loads without treating every application as guaranteed demand.

It needs capital willing to finance assets whose components age at different speeds.

It needs semiconductor supply chains that can produce equipment at scale.

It needs municipalities capable of negotiating with companies whose capital budgets may dwarf local government spending.

It needs rules for reliability, emissions, water, taxes, labor and land.

It needs customers whose demand lasts longer than the construction cycle.

It needs enough technical progress that today's expensive infrastructure does not become tomorrow's stranded monument.

The intelligence economy is not leaving the physical economy behind.

It is colonizing it.

That is a different claim from saying AI will consume all electricity or that every announced data center will be built. The evidence does not support either certainty.

In fact, the queue data should make us skeptical of announcement arithmetic. ERCOT's hundreds of gigawatts of requested large loads cannot simply be added to a forecast as though every project were equally mature. OpenAI's own multi-gigawatt development statements describe capacity under development, not a meter reading. Independent analysts tracking Abilene distinguish campus power, IT power, installed compute and operational timing for exactly this reason.

Maturity is a variable.

A serious forecast discounts by it.

That is also true of intelligence itself.

A model can demonstrate a capability in an evaluation without the capability being reliable enough for widespread deployment. An agent can succeed in a benchmark without an organization trusting it with money. A product can attract users without generating durable margins. A company can book capacity without knowing how efficiently it will monetize the hardware several years later.

The same discipline should govern both the physical and cognitive frontier.

Ask what is real now.

Ask what is committed.

Ask what is merely planned.

Ask what has to happen in between.

Abilene is impressive because much has already happened in between.

Construction started. Buildings rose. Power infrastructure was built. The first phase entered operation. The project became visible in municipal tax records. Grid institutions adapted to a broader wave of large-load requests. A national AI program acquired a physical address.

But the unfinished parts are just as informative.

The full campus scale still depends on completion and commissioning. Future hardware generations will change the economics. Demand will determine utilization. ERCOT will continue deciding how large loads should connect without destabilizing the system or forcing speculative infrastructure onto other customers. Local officials will eventually be able to compare the tax and employment reality with the bargain they approved.

The project will teach by operating.

That is the kind of intelligence no model can fully produce in advance.

There is always a point where the plan has to touch steel, copper, concrete, weather, accounting and people.

A useful future-facing book should therefore resist two temptations at once.

The first is software mysticism: the belief that once intelligence becomes powerful enough, matter becomes an implementation detail.

The second is energy determinism: the belief that whoever has the most cheap electricity necessarily wins.

Abilene argues against both.

Electricity matters enormously, but electricity without chips, customers, financing, construction competence and software is not an AI industry.

Intelligence matters enormously, but intelligence without a place to run at scale is a capability waiting on infrastructure.

Value matters enormously, but a high valuation cannot energize a rack or shorten a transformer lead time by itself.

The advantage belongs to the system that converts among them with the least destructive friction.

That may be the durable meaning of the AI infrastructure race.

Countries are not merely racing to own models.

Companies are not merely racing to own chips.

Regions are not merely racing to advertise cheap power.

They are racing to shorten the path from idea to energized compute to valuable output while keeping enough resilience, legitimacy and optionality that the path does not break under stress.

The winner will not necessarily be the place with the largest announcement.

It may be the place where fewer things get stuck.

This is a quieter standard than raw gigawatts.

It is also harder to fake.

A campus either obtains power or it does not. Equipment either arrives or it does not. The cluster either runs or it does not. Customers either pay or they do not. The grid either remains reliable or it does not. The public bargain either produces durable local value or becomes a lesson for the next negotiation.

The ledgers settle eventually.

That is what makes Abilene such a useful final case before the conclusion.

For most of the book, energy, value and intelligence have been separated so we could understand the mechanisms.

Here they are difficult to separate at all.

The model needs the chip. The chip needs the grid. The grid needs investment. Investment needs expected value. Expected value depends on capability. Capability improvement increases demand for the grid. The city changes the value calculation through taxes. The grid changes it through connection timing. Semiconductor policy changes it through hardware availability. Engineering changes it through efficiency. Competition changes it through price.

The loop closes on a patch of Texas land.

A gigawatt on a press release is a number.

A gigawatt on the ground is an institution.
