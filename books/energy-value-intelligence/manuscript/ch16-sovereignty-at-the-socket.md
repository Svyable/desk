# Sovereignty at the Socket

A country can own a flag, a currency and an army while depending on another country for the machines that increasingly shape its decisions.

This is not new. States have always depended on foreign resources, technologies and trade routes. Britain worried about timber and grain. Industrial powers worried about coal, oil and shipping lanes. Modern states worry about semiconductors, rare materials, telecommunications equipment, cloud infrastructure and energy supply.

Artificial intelligence adds a new strategic layer because advanced computation is becoming part of national capacity.

A government that can train, deploy and securely operate capable models may improve intelligence analysis, logistics, scientific research, cybersecurity, administration and military systems. A country that cannot access advanced chips or reliable electricity may remain a consumer of capabilities produced elsewhere.

The resulting political language is sovereignty.

Sovereignty is an easy word to misuse.

Taken literally, technological sovereignty can become an argument for autarky: every country should own every layer of every critical supply chain. That is neither realistic nor necessarily desirable. Modern semiconductors exist because specialization allows firms and countries to become extraordinarily capable at particular stages. Rebuilding the entire stack independently can waste enormous resources and still produce inferior technology.

The practical question is narrower.

Which dependencies can a state tolerate, and which dependencies give another actor unacceptable power during stress?

Energy policy has asked this question for decades.

A country importing oil can prosper during normal trade and become vulnerable when supply is disrupted. Diversified suppliers, strategic reserves, domestic production, fuel substitution and alliances reduce the risk without requiring complete self-sufficiency.

The same logic applies to compute.

A state can depend on foreign chips while holding inventories, cultivating multiple suppliers, maintaining domestic packaging or fabrication capabilities, securing allied commitments and ensuring that critical workloads can operate if trade is disrupted.

Strategic autonomy is a portfolio of options.

The semiconductor supply chain makes this difficult because some options are expensive and slow.

Leading-edge fabrication requires enormous capital and accumulated process knowledge. Lithography and other manufacturing equipment depend on specialized firms. Advanced memory and packaging introduce additional concentrated layers. A country cannot decide during a crisis that it would like a mature domestic ecosystem and have one next quarter.

Capability has a gestation period.

That creates a national version of the reliability tax. Redundant capacity may look inefficient in peacetime. The social value appears when a disruption occurs.

Governments therefore subsidize semiconductor factories partly for reasons private investors do not price fully. A new fab may have lower financial returns than producing in the established cluster while still creating strategic option value.

This logic can justify policy.

It can also justify almost anything if left unbounded.

Every industry can argue that it is strategic. Every subsidy can be called resilience. Political systems are good at converting legitimate exceptions into permanent constituencies.

The discipline is to identify the failure mode.

What specific disruption are we insuring against? How severe would it be? Which layer is truly hard to substitute? Could inventories, contracts or allies provide cheaper resilience? How quickly would technology make the subsidized capacity obsolete?

National strategy should face the same opportunity-cost tests as corporate strategy, even if the objectives differ.

AI sharpens these questions because the technology itself changes quickly while the physical infrastructure is durable.

A government can subsidize a data center whose servers age within a few years. The power connection may remain valuable for decades. A semiconductor fab can be strategic at one process node and less so after technology advances. A training cluster can be important now while future models become more efficient or distributed.

The durable asset may not be today's machine.

It may be the capability to replace the machine.

That means engineers, research institutions, energy infrastructure, capital markets, supply relationships, standards and a functioning industrial base matter as much as inventory.

Sovereignty is adaptive capacity.

The United States and China offer the most visible contemporary contest because advanced semiconductors and AI have become explicit subjects of trade and national-security policy.

Since 2022, the United States has repeatedly revised export controls on advanced computing chips and semiconductor manufacturing technology headed to China and other restricted destinations. The rules have changed as technology and policy changed. In 2025, the U.S. Commerce Department rescinded the previous administration's AI Diffusion Rule before its compliance requirements took effect while maintaining and revising other chip-related restrictions. In January 2026, the Bureau of Industry and Security moved to case-by-case review for licenses involving certain high-performance chips such as Nvidia's H200 and AMD's MI325X for approved customers in China under specified conditions.

The policy has not been one static wall.

It is an evolving attempt to price security risk into access to computation.

That evolution demonstrates the difficulty of technology controls. Restrict too little and the policy may not change the target's capabilities. Restrict too much and domestic suppliers can lose revenue, foreign competitors can gain incentives, allies can resist and the target can accelerate substitution.

Controls create intelligence on the other side.

The constrained actor searches for workarounds, stockpiles, domestic production, alternative architectures, intermediaries and efficiency gains. A restriction can slow capability while also increasing the value of becoming independent of the restriction.

This is strategic rebound.

The same dynamic has appeared throughout history. Blockade encourages substitution. Sanctions encourage evasion. Tariffs encourage local production when the market is large enough. None of this means controls are futile. It means the response belongs in the forecast.

An intelligent policy models the adversary as intelligent.

AI makes that requirement almost embarrassingly literal.

A country with limited access to the most advanced chips may invest more heavily in software efficiency, model compression, domestic accelerators or alternative computing architectures. The energy cost per unit of capability can become part of geopolitical competition.

Efficiency is strategic because it stretches scarce compute.

So is electricity.

A country cannot translate semiconductor access into AI capability without power. Large clusters need reliable generation and grids. Energy-rich countries therefore possess an input to the intelligence economy that may become more strategically valuable than it appeared during the software era.

This does not mean every energy-rich state becomes an AI power.

Power has to meet chips, fiber, talent, capital, law and organizational competence.

The Gulf states are a useful example of the opportunity. Hydrocarbon wealth created capital and energy advantages. Governments in the region have invested in data centers, AI firms and technology partnerships as part of efforts to diversify their economies. The strategic possibility is clear: convert energy wealth and financial capital into computational capability.

The difficulty is equally clear: buying hardware is easier than building an innovation ecosystem.

Research culture, talent attraction, educational institutions, entrepreneurship, openness, governance and trusted international relationships matter.

A cluster is not a warehouse full of GPUs.

Europe faces a different configuration. It possesses wealthy markets, research institutions, industrial companies and substantial technical talent, but has worried about dependence on foreign cloud platforms, chips and AI models. Its response includes industrial policy, cloud initiatives, regulation and investment in compute capacity.

The question is not whether Europe should reproduce the American or Chinese stack.

It is which layers it must control to preserve meaningful strategic choice.

This is the right sovereignty question for most countries.

Small states cannot own the frontier. They can build leverage through specialization and alliances.

The Netherlands illustrates this through advanced semiconductor equipment. Taiwan through leading-edge fabrication. South Korea through memory and semiconductors. Japan through materials, equipment and manufacturing expertise. Countries with hydropower or renewable resources can offer low-carbon electricity. Others can specialize in software, science or trusted data regimes.

Interdependence can create resilience when dependencies are mutual and diversified.

It can create vulnerability when one side controls a chokepoint the other cannot replace.

The distinction is bargaining power.

Energy markets learned this through pipelines.

A pipeline is efficient because it creates a durable connection between producer and consumer. It is also a political relationship. The seller depends on revenue. The buyer depends on supply. If alternative routes are weak, either side can gain leverage under the right conditions.

Compute infrastructure creates analogous relationships.

A country relying on one foreign cloud provider for critical government systems may obtain excellent service at low cost. It also creates switching risk. A company relying on one model API may become exposed to pricing, policy or availability changes. A military relying on foreign components needs assurance that supply continues during conflict.

The solution is not necessarily domestic ownership.

Portability can be sovereignty.

Open standards, interoperable software, multiple suppliers, escrow arrangements, local backups and contractual rights can reduce dependence. A system that can switch among providers may be more sovereign than one built around a subsidized national champion that performs poorly.

Architecture is foreign policy.

The same principle applies to energy. Interconnected grids can increase resilience by allowing regions to share generation during stress. They also create dependencies. Gas interconnectors can diversify supply or transmit shocks. Strategic autonomy often requires more connection, not less, provided the connections are diverse.

Sovereignty is the ability to choose under pressure.

This definition moves the concept away from ownership and toward option value.

A country is more sovereign when a single supplier cannot veto an essential action. It is more sovereign when it has substitutes, reserves, trusted partners and the knowledge to adapt.

Machine intelligence could improve this adaptive capacity.

Models can help map supply-chain dependencies, forecast energy demand, identify component substitution and support crisis logistics. They can translate technical documents, accelerate engineering and help smaller countries access expertise.

They can also create new common-mode risks.

If many governments, companies and militaries rely on similar models, software stacks or cloud providers, a failure or compromise can propagate across nominally independent systems. Diversity at the institutional level can be undermined by homogeneity at the model layer.

This is a subtle form of concentration.

Two organizations may buy from different application vendors whose products call the same underlying model. Ten countries may operate separate systems whose critical chips come from the same foundry. Redundancy that shares a dependency is not full redundancy.

The reliability engineer asks about common cause.

Geopolitics should too.

A strategic map of AI therefore needs more than national borders. It needs dependency graphs.

Which fabs produce the chips? Which tools maintain the fabs? Which energy systems power the data centers? Which fiber routes connect them? Which software libraries are shared? Which model weights are controlled by whom? Which payment systems clear the contracts?

Power hides in the edges of the graph.

The same graph reveals opportunities for cooperation.

No allied country needs to dominate every layer if the group can maintain trusted access across the stack. One country can fabricate, another produce equipment, another supply energy, another host data centers, another contribute research and software.

This is industrial alliance as distributed intelligence.

It can be more efficient than national duplication and more resilient than dependence on one jurisdiction.

Alliances introduce politics, of course. Trust can change. Governments change. Export rules change. Firms pursue their own interests. Strategic planning has to model those uncertainties rather than assume allies are permanent extensions of the domestic state.

There is no final architecture.

That is why national advantage increasingly belongs to countries that can reconfigure.

A rich energy endowment is useful. A semiconductor industry is useful. Frontier models are useful. Deep capital markets are useful. None guarantees sovereignty if the surrounding system is brittle.

The strongest position is the ability to convert resources into new capability faster than shocks destroy old arrangements.

That is a form of intelligence.

It also changes how we should think about national wealth.

GDP measures economic output. Financial reserves measure claims. Resource reserves measure physical potential. Military inventories measure one form of coercive capacity.

In an intelligence-intensive era, a country's latent ability to solve novel problems becomes increasingly important.

Can it permit and build infrastructure? Can it train people? Can agencies use good information? Can firms scale? Can universities generate knowledge? Can the grid connect new demand? Can the legal system resolve disputes? Can the state attract allies rather than frighten them away?

These are conversion capacities.

They are not purchased all at once.

A government can announce a billion-dollar AI initiative faster than it can create the institutions required to use the money well. This mirrors the energy system exactly. Capital can be appropriated. Infrastructure has to be built.

Sovereignty at the socket therefore begins with an unglamorous question.

When the machine is needed, will the power be there?

Then another.

Will the chip be there?

Then the most difficult one.

Will the society know what to do with them?

The semiconductor industry's actual expansion plans show why allied capacity is a better unit of thought than national self-sufficiency. TSMC's 2025 annual report described leading-edge expansion in Taiwan, additional capacity in Arizona, specialty production in Japan and Germany, and advanced packaging alongside fabrication. The first Arizona fab was already in volume production; subsequent facilities were being accelerated. A single company was becoming geographically more redundant while remaining technologically integrated.

That is not autarky. It is diversification inside an interdependent system.

The company still relies on equipment from Europe, materials from several countries, customers concentrated in other markets and a workforce whose expertise was built over decades. A U.S. fab can reduce one geographic concentration without making the United States independent of the semiconductor world. The reduction in risk is real precisely because it is partial.

This is the useful way to measure sovereignty: by the number of credible paths that remain open after a shock.

A strategic reserve creates one path. An allied fab creates another. A domestic packaging line may create a third. Software portability can create a fourth by allowing workloads to run on less-preferred hardware. Energy reserves and redundant transmission create still more. None is sufficient alone. Together they change the probability that one broken link becomes a national veto.

The cost of those options should be visible.

If a country pays a large premium for local fabrication, citizens should know which risk the premium buys down. If a government requires sovereign cloud capacity, it should specify the workloads that actually need the jurisdictional guarantee. If a national model initiative is justified on security grounds, it should explain why access to multiple commercial systems would not provide equivalent resilience.

Strategic language should sharpen cost-benefit analysis, not exempt policy from it.

The 2026 U.S. revision for certain advanced-chip exports to China is a good example of policy adjusting rather than pretending the first rule was eternal. BIS moved specified products such as the H200 and MI325X into case-by-case licensing for approved customers subject to conditions. Whatever one thinks of the balance, the revision acknowledged that technology control is a moving optimization problem: security benefit, commercial leakage, enforceability, allied alignment and substitution all change over time.

A sovereign strategy that cannot revise itself is merely brittle in patriotic clothing.

The energy side has the same lesson. The International Energy Agency's work on AI and electricity emphasized that data-center demand is geographically concentrated and that growth can move faster than grids. A government may possess national generation capacity and still discover that its strategic computing cluster cannot connect where planned. In that moment, sovereignty is constrained by a transformer, a transmission corridor or a permitting queue.

The socket is not metaphorical.

This is especially important for countries trying to leap directly into the intelligence economy. A minister can announce an AI campus, sign a partnership and order servers. The local grid may then require years of reinforcement. Imported diesel backup can keep machines alive but undermine cost and climate goals. Foreign technicians can install hardware but do not automatically create domestic operating capability. A national champion can own the building while the most important software, chips and financing remain external.

Ownership can overstate control.

The reverse is also true. A country can exercise meaningful control without owning every asset. Denmark does not need to manufacture every wind-turbine component to have energy policy. Singapore does not need to own every ship to be strategically important to maritime trade. A small state with excellent interconnection, multiple cloud providers, strong cyber institutions and trusted alliances may have more usable digital sovereignty than a larger state with a nominally domestic stack that nobody competitive wants to use.

Usable sovereignty is performance under stress.

That suggests a more demanding test for national AI strategies. Imagine the favored supplier disappears for six months. Imagine the leading chip becomes unavailable. Imagine electricity prices triple. Imagine a model provider changes terms. Imagine a neighboring ally elects a government less willing to cooperate. Which critical services keep functioning, and which adaptations are already rehearsed rather than improvised?

The answer reveals whether the strategy purchased capability or symbolism.

This is the same question a reliability engineer asks of any complex system: not whether every component is domestic, but whether failure has somewhere safe to go.

Countries need graceful degradation too.

A defense application may fall back to a smaller local model. A hospital system may preserve human operating procedures when cloud services fail. A grid operator may keep critical control systems isolated from general-purpose AI. An agency may maintain data exports in standard formats so vendors can be replaced. Research programs may cultivate several accelerator architectures rather than optimizing every tool around one supplier.

These measures can look inefficient because duplication always does.

The value appears when options matter.

The strategic competition around AI will therefore reward countries that are good at maintaining options without becoming paralyzed by redundancy. Too little resilience creates hostage points. Too much self-sufficiency consumes resources that could have bought better capability through trade.

Between those extremes lies a network of allied specialization, reserves, interoperability and domestic competence.

That network is not a retreat from sovereignty.

It is what sovereignty looks like in a civilization too complex for any one country to reproduce alone.
