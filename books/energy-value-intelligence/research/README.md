# Research Brief

## Thesis under test

*Energy, Value & Intelligence* argues that the most useful unit for understanding technological and economic change is not energy, intelligence, or value in isolation, but the conversion among them.

The strong version of the thesis — “cheap energy plus powerful AI automatically creates abundance and prosperity” — is deliberately rejected. The version worth testing is conditional:

> Energy expands the physically reachable set of outcomes. Intelligence improves the search, coordination, design, and control used to reach them. Value signals select which outcomes receive capital, permission, attention, legitimacy, and continued effort. When one input becomes substantially cheaper or more abundant, scarcity and bargaining power tend to migrate toward the complementary inputs that remain difficult to supply, substitute, coordinate, or authorize.

The manuscript treats this as a diagnostic framework, not a natural law. Scarcity can disappear rather than migrate. Competition can destroy rents quickly. Institutions can deliberately preserve constraints because the constrained activity carries harms. A new bottleneck can be temporary. Some “constraints” are merely bad rules. Some are physics. Some are rights. The point is to identify which case applies before treating abundance as sufficient.

The book should remain useful if several forecasts in it are wrong. Its durable method is to ask five questions:

1. What physical capability exists?
2. What intelligence converts that capability into useful action?
3. What value signal or institution directs the action?
4. What becomes scarce after the conversion improves?
5. Who bears the costs and who captures the resulting value?

## Evidence lanes

### 1. Preindustrial energy, access, and entitlement

The opening chapters use food and preindustrial energy to separate resource stock from access. Amartya Sen's famine work is central because it demonstrates that aggregate availability and individual command over a resource are different variables. His entitlement approach does not imply that food availability never matters; it shows that famine cannot always be explained by aggregate supply decline alone. Wages, prices, employment, production rights, public relief, and other claims determine whether particular groups can obtain food.

The manuscript generalizes this carefully. A power system can possess adequate generation in aggregate while a specific load lacks transmission or a connection. Compute can exist globally while a small firm cannot afford or legally access it. Money can be appropriated while administrative capacity to convert the appropriation into infrastructure is absent.

**What would weaken this lane:** evidence that access constraints are generally trivial once aggregate physical supply is adequate, or that institutional conversion costs contribute little to real-world scarcity.

### 2. Steam, efficiency, rebound, and market expansion

The steam chapter rests on two claims. First, early steam engines were valuable in mines despite low efficiency because they solved a high-value local constraint where coal was cheap. James Watt's separate condenser then sharply reduced fuel consumption, widening the contexts in which steam power was economical. Science Museum histories provide a useful engineering anchor for this transition.

Second, the manuscript uses Jevons as a mechanism rather than a slogan. Better efficiency lowers the effective price of an energy service, which can increase use directly and through broader economic responses. Modern rebound research is heterogeneous. Direct rebound for one household service, economy-wide rebound, and full “backfire” are different claims. Reviews and econometric studies find material rebound but do not justify treating a 100% rebound as a universal constant.

**What would weaken this lane:** consistent evidence that efficiency improvements translate almost one-for-one into lower aggregate resource use across general-purpose technologies, with little response through prices, income, new applications, or sectoral reorganization.

### 3. Electrification and organizational complementarity

The electrification chapter uses Paul David's historical comparison between dynamos and computers, together with economic history of factory electrification. The key mechanism is complementary reorganization. Replacing a steam engine with one large electric motor could leave the old line-shaft factory logic intact. Distributed electric drive allowed machines to be placed according to production flow, changed control, made plant expansion easier, and enabled new equipment and working conditions.

The analogy to AI is conditional. It does not prove that every AI deployment will eventually produce a delayed productivity boom. It suggests that task-level substitution can understate the effect of a general-purpose technology if the larger gain requires workflow redesign, new capital, new skills, and new organizational architecture.

**What would weaken this lane:** evidence that AI's value is almost entirely captured by isolated task acceleration and that organizations gain little from redesigning processes around cheap machine cognition.

### 4. Prices as distributed intelligence — and incomplete accounting

The value chapters draw on Hayek's account of dispersed knowledge and Coase's account of transaction costs. Prices can coordinate adaptations without any participant possessing the complete causal story. Firms exist partly because repeated market contracting also has costs, so hierarchy and markets are alternative and complementary coordination systems.

The manuscript pairs this with externalities, market power, rights, and distribution. A price is not treated as a moral score. It reflects the institution in which exchange occurs. Carbon emissions, strategic dependence, unpaid care, local environmental harm, or the welfare of a person without purchasing power can be weakly represented or absent unless law, liability, taxation, regulation, bargaining power, or other institutions bring them into the decision.

William Nordhaus's work on producer capture of innovation surplus grounds the distinction between value creation and value capture. A technology can generate large social surplus while early investors or producers capture only a fraction of it.

**What would weaken this lane:** evidence that private prices consistently internalize the major long-run external, strategic, and distributional consequences relevant to infrastructure and AI decisions without institutional correction.

### 5. AI capability, cost, and physical footprint

Stanford's 2026 AI Index is the principal current source for the manuscript's AI infrastructure claims. It reports rapid growth in global AI compute capacity, high concentration of frontier hardware and data-center infrastructure, continued model progress, and growing concern about energy and environmental footprint. The report also shows that inference energy consumption varies widely across models and workloads; there is no timeless joules-per-query constant.

The 2025 AI Index remains useful for the earlier measured collapse in the price of reaching a fixed benchmark capability. The manuscript treats this as a dated observation, not an immutable exponential curve. Frontier systems can retain high costs even while older capability becomes dramatically cheaper.

**What would weaken this lane:** sustained increases in the cost of useful machine cognition, rapid saturation of demand, or technical progress that makes AI's infrastructure footprint negligible before power, chips, or construction become meaningful constraints.

### 6. Data centers, electricity, and the grid as constraint

The International Energy Agency's *Energy and AI* work and its 2026 electricity outlook provide the main physical-system evidence. The IEA projects data-center electricity consumption rising rapidly through 2030 in its base case while emphasizing large uncertainty from efficiency, adoption, and energy-sector bottlenecks. It also stresses the difference between fast data-center development cycles and slower power-system construction.

Berkeley Lab's *Queued Up 2026* data show more than 2,060 GW of proposed U.S. generation and storage actively seeking interconnection at the end of 2025. Most queued projects will not be built; the queue is not a forecast of realized capacity. It is evidence of a conversion problem between proposed projects and deliverable connections.

The manuscript therefore distinguishes energy availability from deliverable power at a specific location, time, reliability level, and connection date.

**What would weaken this lane:** grid expansion, hardware efficiency, and geographic workload flexibility keeping concentrated AI load easy to serve with little effect on connection schedules, local prices, generation mix, or network investment.

### 7. Chips, supply chains, and frozen decisions

The semiconductor chapter treats a chip as accumulated design and manufacturing intelligence embodied in matter. Stanford's 2026 AI Index provides a current concentration snapshot: the United States hosts the largest AI data-center base while leading AI chips remain heavily dependent on fabrication by TSMC. The manuscript uses this as a strategic-dependency example, not as a prediction that today's supplier shares remain fixed.

U.S. export-control policy is evidence that advanced computing has become a strategic resource. The Bureau of Industry and Security's January 2026 policy moved certain H200-, MI325X-, and similar chip licenses for approved Chinese customers to case-by-case review under security conditions. The policy's evolution is important: technology controls are adaptive instruments, and constrained actors respond through substitution, efficiency, domestic investment, stockpiling, and redesign.

**What would weaken this lane:** rapid diversification of leading-edge fabrication and equipment such that no small set of firms, countries, or tools creates meaningful substitution delays during shocks.

### 8. Productivity, labor, and the human complement

Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond's customer-support study provides concrete evidence that generative AI can raise measured productivity and that gains can be heterogeneous, with especially large effects for novice and lower-skilled workers in that setting. The manuscript does not generalize the percentages across occupations. It uses the study to support a mechanism: machine systems can diffuse patterns of high-performing work and change the relative value of experience.

The labor chapters remain conditional. Jobs are bundles of tasks, and task automation can produce substitution, complementarity, demand expansion, wage compression, or new roles depending on elasticity, institutions, and the rest of the workflow. The book's “human premium” is therefore economic rather than mystical: accountability, embodied action, relationship, scarce attention, legitimacy, and trusted responsibility may become relatively more valuable when generic production becomes cheap.

**What would weaken this lane:** broad evidence that AI affects workers uniformly, that task bundles remain unchanged, or that accountability and human interaction have negligible value once machine output reaches high average accuracy.

### 9. Cheap energy as option abundance

The abundance chapters deliberately avoid “free-energy” rhetoric. Reverse-osmosis desalination, electrolysis, industrial heat, storage, and flexible computation are examples where lower electricity prices expand the feasible set without eliminating capital cost, transport, maintenance, materials, finance, water, land, or conversion losses.

DOE desalination material grounds the point that reverse osmosis requires high-pressure pumping and produces concentrated brine that must be managed. The IEA's *Global Hydrogen Review 2026* shows both growth and constraint: installed electrolysis capacity expanded rapidly, yet low-emissions hydrogen remains a small share of production and projects face cost, demand, infrastructure, and policy barriers.

**What would weaken this lane:** evidence that electricity cost dominates total system cost so completely across most proposed abundance technologies that complementary capital, logistics, materials, and institutions become secondary.

### 10. Carbon and the changing electricity mix

The climate chapter treats carbon as a physical externality rather than a reason to minimize energy use. IEA's *Global Energy Review 2026* shows that low-emissions electricity grew rapidly in 2025 and that renewables plus nuclear increased by more than total electricity generation growth, while global energy-related CO2 emissions still reached a new high. Both facts belong in the same ledger.

The manuscript distinguishes average and marginal electricity emissions, annual contractual matching and hourly physical supply, operational emissions and lifecycle impacts, and efficiency from total demand. It treats additional clean supply, grids, flexibility, and demand response as system questions rather than labels attached to individual technologies.

**What would weaken this lane:** reliable evidence that electricity decarbonization stalls structurally while electrification continues, making the book's complementarity between abundant electricity and lower-carbon growth much less plausible.

### 11. Sovereignty, geography, and option value

The sovereignty chapters define strategic autonomy as the ability to choose under pressure rather than literal self-sufficiency. The relevant assets can include inventories, multiple suppliers, allied capacity, domestic skills, portable software, generation, grids, and institutions capable of rapid substitution.

The World Bank's AI-foundations framework — connectivity, compute, context, and competency — supports the claim that model access alone does not produce national capability. Geography matters because power, fiber, water, climate exposure, law, chips, and talent are unevenly distributed.

**What would weaken this lane:** rapid commoditization and portability across the entire AI stack such that location, power systems, compute ownership, data regimes, and technical capability contribute little to national bargaining power or resilience.

## Falsification notes for the whole manuscript

The book's framing would need substantial revision if several of the following become true at once:

- Useful AI inference stops getting cheaper across a broad range of capability levels.
- Total machine-cognition demand saturates quickly enough that efficiency improvements consistently reduce aggregate compute and electricity use.
- AI productivity gains remain confined to narrow tasks and produce little process or firm-level reorganization.
- Grid expansion and workload flexibility make data-center power access routine even in the fastest-growing clusters.
- Leading-edge semiconductor production becomes geographically and technologically easy to substitute within short time periods.
- Cheap clean electricity makes desalination, hydrogen, industrial heat, and other conversion technologies economical with little dependence on complementary capital or infrastructure.
- Energy efficiency routinely produces near-proportional reductions in economy-wide energy use with minimal rebound.
- Prices and ordinary contracts consistently capture major externalities and resilience value without regulation, liability, taxation, standards, or collective investment.
- Human accountability, relationship, embodiment, and legitimacy lose economic value across high-consequence domains faster than the manuscript expects.
- Global AI capability converges rapidly despite large differences in connectivity, power, compute, data, skills, and institutions.
- Physical infrastructure construction begins moving on software-like timescales, eliminating the book's recurring mismatch between fast information and slow capital.

Evidence in the opposite direction should strengthen specific mechanisms without being treated as proof of the entire triumvirate.

## Source discipline

- Prefer primary research, official datasets, original regulations, institutional reports, company filings, and historical scholarship.
- Keep dated technology statistics dated. AI prices, benchmark performance, chip shares, data-center counts, and policies can move quickly.
- Distinguish installed capacity from announced projects and active queues from completed infrastructure.
- Distinguish average electricity supply from the marginal consequence of adding a new load.
- Distinguish efficiency per task from total demand after rebound.
- Distinguish physical abundance from access, connection, authority, and entitlement.
- Distinguish value creation from value capture.
- Distinguish descriptive prices from moral valuations.
- Treat scenario projections as scenarios rather than realized facts.
- Preserve contrary empirical estimates, especially in rebound, productivity, labor substitution, and energy demand.
- Do not convert a case study into a universal coefficient.
- When a claim depends on a changing policy, record the date and jurisdiction.
- Prefer uncertainty ranges and boundary conditions over false precision.
