# Appendix Evidence Map

The two appendices are deliberately different from the main chapters.

Appendix A, **The Conversion Audit**, is procedural. It distills mechanisms already argued and sourced in the manuscript into a reusable diagnostic. It introduces no new historical or quantitative claim that requires a separate evidence base. Its claims about bottlenecks, time horizons, value capture, common-cause failure, metrics, reversibility, and falsification are derived from the chapter evidence already mapped in `chapter-briefs.md` and `source-ledger.csv`.

Appendix B, **A 2030 Scorecard**, is prospective. Its purpose is to expose the manuscript to future disconfirmation. The bets are therefore tied to dated 2026 baselines and should be re-scored against later evidence rather than silently rewritten.

## Scorecard evidence anchors

### Bet 1 — AI keeps electricity planning strategically important

Primary anchors: S13, S14, S15, S32, S37.

Baseline: data-center electricity demand is rising quickly, large-load queues are material, and data-center construction can move faster than generation, grid, and equipment delivery.

Evidence against: connection times collapse across major clusters; large-load planning becomes operationally routine; workload mobility or efficiency makes power availability largely irrelevant to site and capacity decisions.

### Bet 2 — Adequate machine cognition gets cheaper faster than organizations reorganize

Primary anchors: S06, S11, S12, S17.

Baseline: fixed-capability inference costs have fallen sharply, while historical general-purpose technologies and current workplace evidence both suggest complementary organizational change is slower than technical capability change.

Evidence against: useful inference cost remains stubbornly high for ordinary firms, or standardized deployment practices cause organizations with similar access to converge rapidly on similar productivity gains.

### Bet 3 — Cognitive rebound expands total machine work

Primary anchors: S04, S05, S12, S37.

Baseline: energy rebound literature establishes the mechanism and boundary conditions; AI task cost is falling while energy use associated with AI-focused data centers is rising as adoption and workload depth expand.

Evidence against: demand saturates; cheaper inference is used mainly to reduce cost for a stable workload; total machine-cognition demand falls materially as efficiency improves.

### Bet 4 — Verification, provenance, and authorization gain relative value

Primary anchors: S17, S18, S36 and the manuscript's accountability analysis.

Baseline: current evidence supports assistance and productivity gains but does not remove institutional responsibility, verification, or professional consequences.

Evidence against: high-consequence systems broadly accept autonomous authorization; verification becomes as cheap and reliable as generation; users and institutions show little willingness to pay for provenance or accountable review.

### Bet 5 — Semiconductor geography diversifies without becoming ordinary

Primary anchors: S11, S16, S29.

Baseline: leading AI hardware depends on concentrated fabrication and equipment ecosystems while TSMC and governments are expanding production geographically.

Evidence against: frontier fabrication becomes rapidly reproducible across many jurisdictions with modest yield, cost, equipment, or learning penalties; or architecture changes make leading-edge fabrication far less strategically important.

### Bet 6 — Sovereignty becomes a portfolio of options more than a program of autarky

Primary anchors: S16, S19, S29.

Baseline: advanced computing is already shaped by export controls, allied manufacturing, domestic subsidies, and geographically distributed supply chains.

Evidence against: major blocs reproduce most critical layers domestically and interdependence falls sharply; or market substitution becomes so fast that governments retreat from compute and semiconductor resilience policy.

### Bet 7 — Organizational capital matters more as model exclusivity falls

Primary anchors: S06, S09, S17, S19.

Baseline: historical ICT productivity and current generative-AI evidence both support complementarity between technical access and organizational capability.

Evidence against: frontier model access remains persistently scarce enough that the provider captures most downstream differentiation and firms with comparable access show relatively little productivity variance from workflow, data, or management quality.

### Bet 8 — Apprenticeship becomes an explicit design problem

Primary anchors: S17, S18, S36.

Baseline: generative AI can diffuse some expert patterns toward less experienced workers, while automation research and aviation guidance show that removing routine practice can create skill-maintenance questions.

Evidence against: AI tutoring and simulation replace lost practice so effectively that expertise pipelines accelerate; or professional task bundles reorganize enough that the old apprenticeship path becomes irrelevant.

### Bet 9 — Large-load contracts assign more forecast risk to the requester

Primary anchors: S14, S30, S32, S33, S34.

Baseline: large AI campuses can induce long-lived grid and municipal commitments while server and customer forecasts move on much faster clocks.

Evidence against: demand forecasting becomes stable enough, infrastructure becomes modular enough, or competitive supply expands enough that stranded large-load infrastructure ceases to be a material policy problem.

### Bet 10 — Computation carbon accounting becomes more granular

Primary anchors: S13, S20, S21, S26.

Baseline: grid carbon intensity is changing, data-center load is geographically concentrated, and the manuscript distinguishes annual matching from hourly physical consequence.

Evidence against: major compute grids decarbonize so thoroughly and reliably that temporal and locational accounting rarely changes the decision.

### Bet 11 — The infrastructure boom creates durable capability and disappointing capital

Primary anchors: S10, S27, S28, S30, S31.

Baseline: social surplus can greatly exceed producer capture, while current AI infrastructure capex and revenue are large enough to attract broad competitive investment.

Evidence against: infrastructure scarcity remains durable enough that most competent mid-2020s AI compute, chip, and power investments earn exceptional returns through 2030 with little evidence of overcapacity or repricing.

### Bet 12 — Conversion capacity becomes measurable or should be retired

Primary anchors: S15, S19, S24, S32 and the cross-chapter evidence on implementation delays, institutional competence, and common-cause failure.

Baseline: countries and organizations with similar nominal resources show large differences in connection times, procurement, reliability, deployment, and institutional throughput.

Evidence against the concept: those observable mechanisms fail to predict useful deployment; the label adds no information beyond existing measures; or successful conversion is routinely explained by a single dominant advantage rather than cross-layer capability.

## Scoring discipline for 2030

Do not grade the scorecard by finding one anecdote that fits a bet. Use broad evidence where the claim is broad and local evidence where the claim is explicitly local.

A bet can be partly right. Record the mechanism that appeared, the mechanism that did not, and any event that changed the boundary conditions.

Do not edit the 2026 wording before scoring it. If a later edition changes a bet, preserve the earlier version in Git history and state why the update was made.

The scorecard succeeds if it makes the book easier to falsify, not if it produces a high batting average.
