# Chapter Briefs and Falsification Notes

## Ch 1 — The Three Ledgers

**Question:** Is it more useful to analyze technological change through the conversion among energy, intelligence, and value than through any one as a master variable?

**What would make this false?** If one input reliably explains outcomes without material dependence on the other two, or if the three-way framing adds description without improving predictions about bottlenecks, adoption, or distribution.

**Primary sources:** S13, S17, S20; conceptual synthesis across the ledger.

## Ch 2 — Before the Price Tag

**Question:** How often does apparent scarcity come from access and institutional conversion rather than aggregate physical shortage?

**What would make this false?** If aggregate supply alone explains most severe allocation failures once measurement error is removed, with entitlement, transport, rights, and administrative capacity contributing little.

**Primary sources:** S01.

## Ch 3 — The Engine and the Market

**Question:** Can a large efficiency improvement expand the market for an energy service enough that total demand rises while value migrates toward complementary infrastructure?

**What would make this false?** If steam history and modern efficiency evidence show little demand response, little complement formation, and near-proportional reductions in total resource use after efficiency gains.

**Primary sources:** S02, S03, S04, S05, S09.

## Ch 4 — Electrify Everything

**Question:** Do general-purpose technologies deliver their largest productivity gains only after complementary capital and organizations are redesigned around the new capability?

**What would make this false?** If historical electrification gains were mostly immediate equipment substitution or if AI adoption shows that isolated task acceleration captures nearly all durable productivity value.

**Primary sources:** S06, S07, S14, S20.

## Ch 5 — What a Price Knows

**Question:** How much useful distributed knowledge can prices compress, and which important costs remain outside the price without institutional intervention?

**What would make this false?** If market prices consistently internalize externalities, resilience, distributional effects, and strategic dependence without law, liability, taxation, regulation, or bargaining institutions.

**Primary sources:** S08, S09, S10.

## Ch 6 — Intelligence Is a Productive Force

**Question:** When does cheaper cognition raise productive capacity rather than simply generate more information?

**What would make this false?** If AI-assisted analysis rarely changes actions, throughput, quality, coordination, or organizational structure after accounting for review and integration costs.

**Primary sources:** S09, S17, S18.

## Ch 7 — The Cost of a Thought

**Question:** What is the economically relevant physical cost of machine cognition when training, inference, hardware, data centers, energy, and workload composition all change at once?

**What would make this false?** If a stable per-query energy or financial constant proves sufficiently accurate across models and use cases that system-level accounting adds little explanatory value.

**Primary sources:** S11, S12, S13.

## Ch 8 — Chips Are Frozen Decisions

**Question:** How much strategic value sits in the accumulated engineering, tacit process knowledge, fabrication, packaging, and equipment embodied in AI hardware?

**What would make this false?** If frontier chip production becomes rapidly substitutable across firms and countries, or if software efficiency makes leading hardware and manufacturing concentration economically unimportant.

**Primary sources:** S11, S16.

## Ch 9 — The Grid Becomes a Computer Constraint

**Question:** When AI infrastructure scales, does deliverable power — connection date, transmission, substations, transformers, generation, and reliability — become a binding compute input?

**What would make this false?** If data-center loads are routinely connected on software-compatible timelines with little congestion, queue delay, network reinforcement, or willingness to pay for faster power access.

**Primary sources:** S13, S14, S15, S25.

## Ch 10 — Cheap Intelligence, Expensive Consequences

**Question:** Does cheap generation shift scarcity toward verification, authority, maintenance, trust, and the capacity to absorb more decisions?

**What would make this false?** If verification and maintenance costs fall at least as quickly as generation costs, leaving little new pressure on attention, accountability, software estates, or decision rights.

**Primary sources:** S17, S18; S11 for scaling context.

## Ch 11 — Cheap Energy, New Worlds

**Question:** Does cheaper electricity primarily create option abundance by making more conversion pathways worth evaluating rather than making downstream products automatically cheap?

**What would make this false?** If energy cost dominates total cost across desalination, hydrogen, industrial heat, flexible compute, and similar processes so strongly that capital, transport, materials, siting, and institutions become minor considerations.

**Primary sources:** S22, S23, S25.

## Ch 12 — Jevons for Minds

**Question:** If machine cognition becomes much cheaper per task, will demand expand through more queries, retries, agents, personalization, monitoring, and machine-to-machine activity?

**What would make this false?** If cognitive demand saturates quickly and falling unit cost consistently reduces aggregate compute, inference spending, and electricity use.

**Primary sources:** S04, S05, S12, S13.

## Ch 13 — The Value Migration

**Question:** When intelligence becomes abundant, does bargaining power migrate toward the complements that remain necessary and difficult to substitute?

**What would make this false?** If economic rents remain concentrated in the newly abundant capability itself despite competition, or if complementary scarcity is short-lived and easily arbitraged away across most important markets.

**Primary sources:** S10, S11, S17.

## Ch 14 — The Human Premium

**Question:** Does machine abundance increase the relative value of scarce human accountability, relationship, embodiment, trusted taste, and legitimacy?

**What would make this false?** If users and institutions place negligible incremental value on human responsibility or presence once machine systems reach adequate accuracy, including in high-consequence and relational settings.

**Primary sources:** S17, S18.

## Ch 15 — Geography Returns

**Question:** Does the physical geography of power, fiber, water, land, climate, law, and latency become more economically important as AI becomes more infrastructure-intensive?

**What would make this false?** If compute becomes sufficiently mobile and energy-light that local infrastructure and jurisdiction have little influence on deployment, price, resilience, or national capability.

**Primary sources:** S11, S13, S14, S19.

## Ch 16 — Sovereignty at the Socket

**Question:** Is technological sovereignty better understood as option value and adaptive capacity than as national self-sufficiency?

**What would make this false?** If concentrated dependencies create little leverage during stress, or if complete domestic ownership reliably outperforms diversified alliances, inventories, portability, and substitution pathways at reasonable cost.

**Primary sources:** S11, S16, S19.

## Ch 17 — The Carbon Constraint

**Question:** Can growing useful electricity consumption coexist with decarbonization if low-emissions supply, grids, flexibility, and electrification expand fast enough?

**What would make this false?** If electricity demand growth structurally forces fossil generation upward despite clean-capacity deployment, or if lifecycle impacts erase most emissions advantages of the low-carbon pathways discussed.

**Primary sources:** S20, S21, S26.

## Ch 18 — Abundance Is Political

**Question:** Why do projects with positive aggregate net benefits still fail politically, and how much of the conflict comes from concentrated costs, legitimacy, bargaining power, and rights?

**What would make this false?** If distribution and procedure contribute little to infrastructure opposition once technical benefits are established, or if compensation and aggregate gains almost always resolve conflict.

**Primary sources:** S14, S19, S20; institutional analysis across the manuscript.

## Ch 19 — Failure Modes of the Triumvirate

**Question:** What recurring failures appear when energy, intelligence, and value signals are individually strong but poorly coupled?

**What would make this false?** If failures are well explained by isolated component weakness and interaction effects such as correlated strategies, hidden dependencies, measurement capture, or missing resilience add little.

**Primary sources:** S24, S15, S16; cross-chapter synthesis.

## Ch 20 — A Gigawatt on the Ground

**Question:** Does a real gigawatt-scale AI campus behave like a conversion system in which power, chips, construction, finance, customers, grid rules, and local public value must become simultaneously compatible before announced capacity becomes useful intelligence?

**What would make this false?** If published campus capacity maps quickly and predictably into productive compute with few losses between interconnection, construction, hardware installation, utilization, customer demand, and local value capture; or if those interfaces add little explanatory value beyond the headline gigawatt number.

**Primary sources:** S30, S31, S32, S33, S34, S35.

## Ch 21 — Against the Triumvirate

**Question:** Can the framework survive its strongest alternatives: intelligence dissolving physical constraints, energy becoming an ordinary commodity input, markets coordinating enough value without a broader ledger, bottlenecks disappearing instead of migrating, and conversion capacity proving too vague to predict?

**What would make the book's framework false?** Sustained evidence that these alternatives dominate: useful cognition becomes energy-light and demand-saturated; infrastructure moves on near-software timescales; prices internalize the important consequences with little institutional correction; strategic dependencies substitute cheaply under stress; and measured conversion institutions add little predictive power once ordinary prices, technology quality, and management are controlled for.

**Primary sources:** S04, S05, S08, S13, S14, S19; counter-thesis synthesis rather than a separate empirical claim set.

## Ch 22 — The Conversion Civilization

**Question:** Is a civilization's adaptive advantage better described by its ability to convert among physical capacity, intelligence, and valued outcomes than by raw inventories of any one input?

**What would make this false?** If societies with large raw energy, financial, or cognitive inventories reliably outperform societies with stronger conversion institutions but fewer raw resources, or if the mechanisms identified in Ch 21 make the conversion lens redundant.

**Primary sources:** S09, S14, S19, S20; synthesis of the full source ledger.

## Revision rule

A later edition should not merely add newer statistics. For each chapter, first test the falsification note against the newest evidence. If the evidence weakens the mechanism, revise the mechanism before refreshing the example. If the evidence strengthens it, preserve the boundary conditions that made the original claim falsifiable.
