# THE SOUND INSIDE YOUR HEAD

## When a real bioeffect becomes an intelligence mystery

Sven Hardy Benson

---

This book is about a promotion problem.

A person can hear a sound produced by pulsed radio-frequency energy without an ordinary acoustic source. That proposition belongs to experimental literature going back more than sixty years.

A person heard a strange sound in Havana, therefore a microwave weapon attacked them, therefore a foreign intelligence service operated the weapon, therefore the same actor caused hundreds of later anomalous health incidents: those are different propositions. They do not inherit proof from the first one.

The book keeps four evidence classes visible:

**1. Verified / documented.** Public government records, declassified material, peer-reviewed experiments, official reports and reproducible physical effects. The existence of the microwave auditory effect belongs here. So do the existence of reported anomalous health incidents and the contradictory public assessments issued by U.S. institutions.

**2. Credible but incomplete.** Clinically serious reports, witness testimony, investigative findings and technical judgments that deserve weight but lack enough public evidence to close causation or attribution.

**3. Disputed / unconfirmed.** Claims of a deployed directed-energy weapon, specific adversary responsibility, covert operational programs or a single cause spanning the full AHI population where independent public confirmation is absent.

**4. Speculative synthesis / open conjecture.** The book's own models for understanding how a real mechanism can become an evidentiary attractor: the mechanism-attribution gap, the subset trap, and causal promotion. These are analytical tools, not discoveries.

A patent proves that somebody filed a patent. A classified-looking acronym proves nothing unless the program can be documented. A technical mechanism proves possibility under its demonstrated conditions, not use in a particular event. A government assessment is evidence of an assessment and the evidence described in it; confidence language is not a substitute for the underlying data.

The strongest conclusion may be untidy: some people were ill; some incidents may have ordinary explanations; a smaller subset may remain unresolved; pulsed RF can produce real human perceptual effects; and none of those facts may identify an attacker.

That possibility is not a compromise. It is the problem.
