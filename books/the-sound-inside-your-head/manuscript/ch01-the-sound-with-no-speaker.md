# The Sound With No Speaker

The first problem with a sound inside your head is that the sentence sounds metaphorical.

In this case it is engineering.

In 1961, Allan H. Frey published a short paper with an almost aggressively unromantic title: *Auditory System Response to Radio Frequency Energy*. People exposed under particular conditions to pulsed radio-frequency energy could perceive sounds without an ordinary loudspeaker putting acoustic waves into the room. The phenomenon would acquire Frey's name, which is unfortunate in the usual scientific way. Naming a thing after a person makes it feel more settled than it is and more mysterious than it needs to be.

The useful fact is narrower.

Electromagnetic energy can couple into a human head and, under appropriate pulsed conditions, produce an auditory perception.

That sentence is real science.

It is also where a great deal of bad reasoning begins.

More than half a century later, American diplomats and intelligence personnel in Havana began reporting something frightening. Some described a sudden sound or pressure with directional qualities. Some became dizzy. Some developed headaches, tinnitus, visual difficulties, cognitive complaints or persistent vestibular problems. The cases were not all alike. The medical literature became contested. The vocabulary changed. What the press called Havana syndrome became, inside government, anomalous health incidents: AHIs.

Then the old radio-frequency literature returned.

You can see why.

A person says a sound seemed to appear in or around the head. A laboratory literature says pulsed RF can produce an auditory percept without an ordinary acoustic source. The two facts have a shape that wants to lock together.

Human beings love a mechanism that arrives carrying a plot.

But mechanism is not plot.

The microwave auditory effect establishes that one class of physical energy can be transduced into an auditory experience. It does not establish that anybody in Havana was exposed to it. Exposure would not by itself establish injury. Injury would not establish a weapon. A weapon would not establish an attacker. An attacker would not establish Russia. Russia would not establish a worldwide campaign. Every one of those promotions requires new evidence.

This book is about what happened when those promotions were made in public, resisted inside institutions, revived by technical panels, weakened by intelligence collection, and never quite killed because the first physical fact remained true.

That is what makes the case interesting.

If microwave hearing were nonsense, the story would be easy. If a Russian weapon had been recovered from a hotel room, the story would also be easy. Instead we have the worst possible material for a civilization trying to think clearly: a real effect attached to an unresolved event.

The real effect becomes an evidentiary magnet.

Everything sticks to it.

## The click

Frey's early work belongs to a period when radio-frequency biological effects were being investigated without the cultural packaging they would later acquire. Radar was no longer exotic. Microwave engineering had become militarily important. Exposure was an occupational and physiological question. The possibility that RF energy might produce a perceptible effect in humans was not, by itself, paranormal.

Later experiments explored the phenomenon further. In 1973 Frey and Robert Messenger published a psychophysical study in *Science* reporting that perception induced by pulse-modulated ultrahigh-frequency electromagnetic energy depended primarily on peak power and secondarily on pulse width. Average power, in their experiment, was not the decisive variable.

This matters because ordinary intuition about heating is not enough. People hear “microwave” and imagine a kitchen oven. Bulk heating is a different claim. Pulsed energy can create rapid, tiny thermal expansions that generate pressure waves in tissue: a thermoelastic or thermoacoustic route from electromagnetic pulse to mechanical wave to auditory perception.

The head becomes part of the transducer.

Not an antenna in the cartoon sense. Not a radio receiver decoding speech out of the ether. A physical object in which deposited energy produces a mechanical consequence that the auditory system can detect.

There is a difference between strange and supernatural. We routinely lose it.

The phenomenon has limits. Frequency matters. Pulse structure matters. energy density matters. Geometry matters. Anatomy matters. The perceived result is not whatever an operator wishes to insert into consciousness. The existence of clicks or buzzes does not give us mind control. A demonstrated auditory effect does not automatically provide a practical long-range weapon, and a practical weapon would not automatically produce the chronic clinical picture reported by AHI patients.

Those distinctions sound obvious when written in a row.

They were much harder to preserve once people got sick.

## Havana

The original Havana cases did not arrive as a physics problem. They arrived as sick people inside an embassy.

That changes everything.

Institutions have to act before causation is solved. A security officer cannot tell an employee with acute symptoms to return in five years after peer review. A physician does not need a geopolitical theory to treat vertigo. An ambassador does not get to wait for an elegant mechanism before deciding whether personnel are safe. Intelligence services are paid to investigate low-probability hostile possibilities precisely because the cost of dismissing one can be enormous.

This creates an asymmetry between action and knowledge.

You can rationally evacuate people before you know why they are ill.

You can rationally investigate a weapon hypothesis before you know a weapon exists.

You can rationally collect against a foreign adversary before you have evidence that the adversary caused the event.

The mistake comes later, when precaution is rewritten as proof.

The State Department's public record from 2018 described personnel and family members with symptoms and clinical findings, including dizziness, headaches, tinnitus, fatigue, cognitive issues, visual problems, ear complaints and sleep difficulty. It also said something less memorable: the precise nature of the injuries and whether a common cause existed had not been established.

That sentence should have survived every headline.

Whether a common cause exists.

A named syndrome creates pressure toward unity. Once a population has a label, the label starts behaving like an explanation. Cases separated by geography, time, symptoms, exposure circumstances and evidentiary quality become rows in the same spreadsheet. The spreadsheet then appears to prove the thing it was built to investigate.

This is not unique to Havana. Intelligence categories do it. Medical syndromes do it. UFO databases do it. Fraud investigations do it. The category is necessary for collection and dangerous for inference.

The category asks us to gather similar-looking things.

Causation asks whether they belong together.

Those are not the same job.

## The panel that said maybe

In 2020, the National Academies published an assessment commissioned by the State Department. Its conclusion was not that a microwave weapon had attacked American diplomats. The committee said that, among the mechanisms it considered, directed pulsed radio-frequency energy appeared the most plausible for explaining many of the distinctive acute signs, symptoms and observations in the cases it reviewed. It also discussed persistent postural-perceptual dizziness and possible additive or reinforcing factors.

The phrase “most plausible” did extraordinary cultural work.

It was frequently received as “the cause.”

But a ranking is not an identification.

If four explanations are considered and one fits better than the others, the winning explanation can still be wrong. If the data supplied to the panel are incomplete, the ranking inherits those limits. If cases are heterogeneous, a mechanism that fits a distinctive subset may fail on the rest. If no exposure was measured, the physical bridge between mechanism and event remains inferential.

None of this makes the National Academies judgment trivial. Quite the opposite. It means the serious version of the directed-RF hypothesis cannot be dismissed by saying microwaves cannot produce auditory effects. They can. A serious expert committee examined the clinical pattern and found the mechanism plausible enough to put first among the possibilities it considered.

Now the hypothesis has earned another question.

Where is the exposure evidence?

Not: can RF do something to a human?

Not: did Soviet scientists study RF bioeffects?

Not: can a pulsed field produce a click?

Where is the exposure evidence for this event?

That question is brutally simple and surprisingly difficult to answer after the fact. A transient exposure leaves no helpful label. If nobody had the right detector operating at the right place and time, the field itself is gone. Symptoms can outlast a pulse. Memory can outlast symptoms. Buildings are electromagnetically complicated. Devices move. Personnel move. Months pass. The forensic scene is partly biological and partly vanished.

This is why mechanism can become seductive. When the event itself was not instrumented, the literature starts standing in for the missing measurement.

A paper from 1961 becomes a ghost detector.

It tells us what could happen under demonstrated conditions. It cannot tell us what did happen in a Havana room in 2016.

## The panel that said a subset

The U.S. intelligence community later convened an expert panel to examine potential mechanisms. Its declassified 2022 summary made an important move that should have received more attention than it did.

It stopped talking as if every AHI had to be one thing.

The panel judged that a subset of incidents could not be easily explained by known environmental or medical conditions and that pulsed electromagnetic energy, particularly in the radio-frequency range, could plausibly explain the core characteristics, although information gaps remained. Ultrasound was also considered plausible in more constrained close-access scenarios. Several other proposed mechanisms were judged implausible for the core characteristics under the conditions considered.

Subset is the most dangerous word in anomaly research.

It can rescue a real signal from a noisy population.

It can also rescue a dying hypothesis from falsification.

Suppose one hundred reported incidents have twenty different causes. Averaging them together can erase the unusual cluster. The correct scientific move is to identify the cluster with defensible criteria and study it separately.

Now suppose the hypothesis performs badly across one hundred incidents. An advocate selects the seven strangest cases after seeing the data and declares that the phenomenon survives in the “core” subset. The hypothesis has been protected by selection.

The difference is prospective definition.

What makes a case part of the subset before we know whether it helps the theory?

Sudden onset? Directionality? A particular sound? A measurable vestibular sign? Location? Duration? Biomarker? Contemporaneous environmental data? Multiple people affected at the same place and time? Some combination?

If the criteria move after every contradiction, subset analysis becomes a machine for manufacturing immortality.

If the criteria are fixed and a distinctive group persists, the subset may be the only place the real signal lives.

That is the statistical knife edge under the entire AHI argument.

## The intelligence community that said probably not

In March 2023 the Office of the Director of National Intelligence released an updated Intelligence Community Assessment. Most participating agencies concluded that it was very unlikely a foreign adversary was responsible for the reported AHIs. The assessment said intelligence collection and investigation pointed away from adversary involvement and away from a global campaign.

This was widely framed as a reversal of the directed-energy theory.

It was a reversal of something more specific: attribution.

The distinction matters.

The National Academies can be right that pulsed RF is physically capable of explaining parts of a reported acute presentation and the intelligence community can simultaneously be right that it found no persuasive evidence of a foreign campaign causing the reported incidents.

Those propositions are not contradictory.

A mechanism is not an adversary.

The public argument kept wanting one institution to defeat the other because conflict is easier to narrate than conditional inference. “Scientists say microwaves; spies say no Russia” feels unstable. It is only unstable if you have quietly inserted an equals sign between microwaves and Russia.

Remove the equals sign and the evidence becomes legible again.

There is a physical question: can a specified exposure produce the reported acute effects?

There is a clinical question: what happened to the patients?

There is a forensic question: was such an exposure present at the time and place?

There is an engineering question: could a device deliver it with the required range, directionality, concealment and repeatability?

There is an operational question: was such a device used?

There is an attribution question: who used it?

There is a campaign question: do geographically distributed cases belong to the same operation?

Seven questions. One label.

That is how mysteries become ideological.

## The weapon-shaped hole

A peculiar feature of the public Havana debate is that the hypothetical weapon often becomes more detailed as the direct evidence for it becomes thinner.

It is directional. Portable. Concealable. Long-range. Selective. Capable of penetrating structures. Powerful enough to cause the relevant biological effect. Quiet enough not to announce itself conventionally. Operated without being caught. Repeated across locations. Perhaps derived from decades of Soviet research. Perhaps disguised as something ordinary.

Maybe.

But each adjective is a bill.

Directionality costs aperture, frequency, geometry or proximity. Range costs power and propagation. Concealment constrains size, cooling, power supply and operation. Penetration depends on materials and frequency. Biological effect depends on exposure at the target, not output at the device. Repeatability creates opportunities for detection. Deployment creates logistics. Operators create communications and travel. A global campaign creates a larger signature than a single event.

A weapon hypothesis is not strengthened merely because each component is individually imaginable.

The components have to coexist in one system.

This is where classified technology becomes a rhetorical escape hatch. We know states hide capabilities. Therefore, absence of a public device cannot settle the question. True.

But secrecy does not repeal engineering.

A classified transmitter still requires energy. A beam still propagates. An operator still needs access or geometry. A biological target still receives some exposure. A repeated operation still generates opportunities for collection. Classification can hide specifications. It cannot make physical constraints optional.

The right question is not whether a government could keep a device secret.

It is what traces the claimed operation would have to leave outside the secret.

## The sound that was recorded

Another trap waits in the famous recordings associated with Havana.

Some personnel recorded unusual sounds. Acoustic analysis produced hypotheses involving insects, including calling crickets. That evidence became ammunition for two incompatible simplifications.

One side: crickets explain Havana syndrome.

The other: sick people were not attacked by crickets, therefore the acoustic analysis is irrelevant.

Both are too fast.

If a recording is well explained by an insect, that explains the recording. It may also explain a person's contemporaneous auditory experience if the recording captured what they heard. It does not automatically explain persistent symptoms. Conversely, if a directed RF exposure can produce an internally perceived sound, it does not automatically explain an external microphone recording. The microphone and the nervous system are different detectors.

This gives us an unexpectedly powerful rule.

Never let two observations merge merely because the story wants them to.

A recorded sound is one observation.

An auditory percept is another.

A vestibular injury is another.

A headache is another.

A location near an intelligence target is another.

A foreign service's historical research is another.

The work is to connect them without inventing the connective tissue.

## The causal ladder

The rest of this book will use one ugly ladder because ugly ladders are harder to worship.

Reported event.

Exposure.

Biological coupling.

Acute effect.

Persistent injury.

Device.

Operator.

Adversary.

Campaign.

The microwave auditory literature gets us somewhere around biological coupling and acute perception under defined experimental conditions.

That is not nothing.

It is also not the top of the ladder.

The Havana story became culturally explosive because different institutions were answering different rungs while the public kept asking for one verdict. Physicians wanted to know how to treat people. Physicists wanted to know what mechanisms were plausible. intelligence officers wanted to know whether an adversary was acting. diplomats wanted to know whether posts were safe. journalists wanted to know what happened. patients wanted an explanation equal to their suffering. politicians wanted accountability. adversaries had incentives to deny, exploit, confuse or simply watch the United States argue with itself.

There may never have been one question called Havana syndrome.

There may have been a stack of questions hidden under one name.

That possibility is less satisfying than a weapon and more interesting than a debunking.

Because the hardest anomaly is not a thing science says cannot happen.

It is a thing science says can happen, attached to an event for which we cannot show that it did.
