# First Contact Is Local

When people imagine first contact, they imagine symmetry.

A signal arrives. A ship appears. A species meets another species. There is a date to put in textbooks and, at least in the popular version, a reasonably clear distinction between the civilization doing the encountering and the civilization being encountered.

Artificial intelligence gives us a stranger geometry.

The other intelligence is built inside our civilization, trained on traces of our language and culture, manufactured by companies, run on infrastructure, updated through software, and distributed unevenly through products people already use. It is foreign without being extraterrestrial. It may know our literature without having had a childhood. It may speak a language fluently without ever having lived in a place where that language is spoken. It can arrive in a person's life as a tab in an application they opened yesterday.

So first contact will not be a world event at first.

It will be a local event.

The phrase matters because it changes how we should think about timing. Public arguments about advanced intelligence tend to ask when *we* will get there. When will humanity build AGI? When will AI exceed human performance? When will robots become general? When will the labor market transform? The pronoun hides the distribution.

There is no single “we” standing at the same distance from the frontier.

A senior software engineer working daily with coding agents inhabits a different technological present from a schoolteacher whose district blocks most AI tools. A teenager with an unrestricted companion app lives inside a different social experiment from a retiree who has never used a chatbot. A researcher at a frontier laboratory sees capabilities months or years before they are reliable enough for ordinary customers. A worker in a regulated industry may use systems far behind the consumer frontier because audit, privacy, procurement, and liability matter more than novelty.

Even two people using the same model may encounter different systems in practice because they grant different tools, context, permissions, and patience.

One asks a question.

The other connects the model to a repository, a browser, a terminal, a database, a calendar, and a set of organizational documents, then gives it an hour.

The underlying weights may be identical. The experienced intelligence is not.

This is one reason the question “Is the model intelligent?” can be less useful than it appears. Intelligence is partly a property of the model, but practical capability emerges from a system: model, tools, context, memory, interface, permissions, environment, and the human who knows how to use them. A mediocre model with excellent access and a disciplined harness can solve a problem that a stronger model trapped in a chat box cannot even observe.

Humans will therefore meet advanced intelligence through arrangements, not merely models.

That makes the threshold personal.

Imagine a tax accountant in a small firm. For years, AI is mostly a drafting convenience. It rewrites client emails, explains regulations at a high level, and produces checklists that still have to be checked. The accountant is unimpressed by claims that machines are becoming colleagues. The work that matters is full of client history, exceptions, source documents, jurisdictional rules, and consequences. The system does not have enough of the situation to be trusted.

Then the firm's software changes.

The assistant can now inspect the client's records, retrieve the relevant correspondence, trace the origin of a number, compare the treatment to prior years, flag an inconsistency, and prepare a review packet with links to each source. The accountant still owns the conclusion. The system still makes mistakes. But the amount of work between “something seems wrong” and “here is the evidence” collapses.

The accountant's threshold has been crossed.

A poet may not care.

A poet may have crossed a different threshold two years earlier when a machine became capable of arguing about a draft without merely praising it. Or the poet may never cross one, deciding that whatever the model can do has no bearing on the part of writing the poet values.

Both can be rational.

First contact depends on what you consider evidence of another mind at work.

Experts are often difficult to impress with broad competence because expertise gives them a microscope for failure. A nonexpert sees a fluent legal explanation and thinks the machine knows law. A lawyer sees the missing distinction. A student sees a working program and thinks the machine knows software. An engineer sees the race condition. A patient sees a medical explanation and thinks the machine understands medicine. A clinician notices that the recommendation ignores the one feature that changes the risk.

This skepticism is healthy.

It is also why experts may cross the threshold suddenly.

Their standard is not fluency. Their standard is the moment the machine survives the test they use to separate performance from understanding in their own field.

For a chess player, that may be a strategic move. For a mathematician, a proof step that opens a path rather than decorating one. For a programmer, a diagnosis that integrates state across several systems. For a scientist, a hypothesis that correctly predicts a result the scientist did not provide. For a surgeon, not a persuasive explanation but a system whose perception and planning remain useful under the ugly variability of real procedure.

The threshold is therefore not only personal. It is professional.

Every craft contains private tests.

People outside the craft may not even recognize them. A chef knows what it means for someone to recover a sauce that is beginning to split. A mechanic knows the difference between identifying a symptom and locating the failure. A trial lawyer knows which question changes the shape of a witness's testimony. A nurse knows the difference between memorizing a protocol and noticing that the patient no longer fits it. A good editor knows that the sentence causing the trouble may be three pages earlier than the sentence that sounds wrong.

An advanced system does not need to master every craft simultaneously to become profound inside one person's life.

This is why society can be living through an intelligence transition while public debate still sounds unconvinced.

The evidence is fragmented across domains.

One group is seeing systems complete longer software tasks. Another is seeing multimodal perception improve. Another is seeing a companion maintain emotionally significant conversations. Another is seeing scientific tools search chemical or biological spaces at speeds that change experimental priorities. Another is seeing agents handle administrative workflows that used to require an employee to hold dozens of small facts in working memory.

Each group tells a story that sounds exaggerated to the others.

This is normal in technological diffusion. Early users do not merely possess the technology earlier. They develop tacit knowledge about what it can be made to do. Their competence with the new system becomes part of the system's apparent competence.

A person who has learned to decompose a task, supply the right context, set verification points, and recover from agent failure may routinely produce results that look impossible to a casual user. The casual user tries the same product once, receives a mediocre answer, and concludes that the revolution is marketing.

The disagreement can persist for a surprisingly long time because both observations are real.

The system is mediocre in one arrangement and transformative in another.

This is not a weakness in the idea of capability. Human performance is arranged too. A brilliant surgeon without an operating room, team, instruments, or patient history is not demonstrating the capability we ordinarily attribute to a surgical practice. An airline pilot's competence is inseparable from instruments, procedures, checklists, crew, traffic control, and aircraft systems. Civilization has always produced intelligence through assemblies.

What is new is that one of the components can now supply a growing share of the cognitive work.

That growth makes public timing difficult.

Suppose an agent's 50 percent success horizon on a benchmark of software tasks doubles over a period of months. That does not mean office work has doubled in autonomy. The benchmark tasks may not represent office work. The tasks may have clean success criteria. The agent may have tools that do not exist in another environment. Fifty percent success is unacceptable for many consequential uses. A model can be impressive and still require intense supervision.

But the measurement tells us something about the slope.

METR's task-horizon work is valuable not because it announces a date for general intelligence, but because it tries to measure the amount of coherent work frontier agents can carry through on selected real tasks. The human significance appears when those horizons cross the duration of tasks people currently hand to one another.

Five minutes is an answer.

An hour begins to look like an assignment.

A day begins to look like a job-shaped unit.

The labels are approximate, but human organizations are built from these units. We schedule meetings by the half hour, tickets by the hour or day, projects by the week, roles by the year. When machines become capable of taking larger units of work, organizations do not merely accelerate. They change how responsibility is packaged.

The transition will first be visible wherever the machine's useful horizon overlaps a real unit of delegation.

That overlap differs by field.

Some work is easy to verify. If a software agent edits a program and the tests pass, the tests provide a partial external judge. If a model restructures a spreadsheet and the totals reconcile, the task has observable checks. Other work is difficult to score. Was the counseling good? Was the strategy wise? Did the lesson create durable understanding? Was the negotiation fair? Did the manager preserve trust while making the decision?

Advanced systems will therefore appear to arrive earlier in domains where outcome verification is cheap.

This may make technical workers the first mass population to experience something like cognitive delegation. Not because coding is the pinnacle of thought, but because software offers machines an unusually rich laboratory: text-like representations, deterministic tools, searchable repositories, executable tests, rapid feedback, and digital environments where actions can be copied, reverted, and evaluated.

The programmer can give the machine a world it can inspect.

Other professions will build equivalent worlds.

Law will build permissioned corpora, provenance systems, redline environments, citation checks, and transaction-specific memory. Medicine will connect models to structured records, imaging, monitoring, guidelines, and controlled action boundaries. Finance will expose ledgers, controls, models, and audit trails. Manufacturing will connect perception to digital twins and physical robots. Education will connect tutors to curriculum, student history, assessment, and teacher oversight.

Each connection turns a general model into a more situated intelligence.

And each connection creates its own first-contact population.

The same pattern will happen geographically.

The Stanford AI Index shows uneven adoption across countries. Workplace use can be higher in places the popular American story treats as followers. Infrastructure, regulation, language coverage, demographics, labor costs, education, and competition all alter incentives. A country with shortages of particular professional labor may embrace delegation differently from one with abundant labor. A government capable of integrating digital identity and services may encounter agentic administration earlier than a government whose records remain fragmented.

The idea that one nation “has” advanced AI while another does not will become less useful as models diffuse but systems around them diverge.

One place will be better at putting intelligence into hospitals. Another into factories. Another into personal education. Another into bureaucracy. Another into surveillance. Another into scientific research.

The strange will wear local clothing.

Culture will matter too.

Human beings do not have one universal comfort level with social machines. Attitudes toward automation, hierarchy, companionship, privacy, elder care, service work, and nonhuman entities differ. A highly capable assistant framed as a servant may produce one relationship. The same capability framed as a teammate may produce another. A robot with eyes, a name, and a voice can evoke different responses from a silent industrial arm executing the same plan.

This is why interface design is not decoration.

It helps decide what kind of entity the user thinks they are dealing with.

A company can make a system more socially salient by giving it a name, voice, memory, avatar, turn-taking behavior, emotional language, and continuity. It can make the same underlying capability feel more instrumental by presenting it as command output, annotations, suggestions, or background automation.

Neither design is neutral.

The first can make collaboration intuitive while encouraging over-attribution. The second can preserve skepticism while hiding the degree of agency already embedded in the system.

First contact is partly designed.

That should make us cautious about treating user reaction as a simple readout of machine capability. A person can feel a relationship with a weak system and fail to feel one with a strong system. The psychological threshold and the technical threshold can come apart.

The important social changes occur when they begin to reinforce each other.

A socially legible interface causes the user to delegate more. Greater delegation produces richer context. Richer context lets the system perform better. Better performance increases trust. Trust allows the system to take on longer tasks. Longer tasks create more memory and continuity. Continuity makes the interaction feel less like a transaction and more like an ongoing working relationship.

A loop forms.

The loop can be beneficial. It can also produce overreliance.

The user who repeatedly sees good results stops checking. The system becomes familiar enough that warnings feel like formalities. The human forgets which parts of the process were once examined closely. A model update changes behavior, but the relationship has inertia. The user trusts the entity they remember, not necessarily the current system behind the interface.

This is another reason first contact should not be romanticized.

Meeting a new class of intelligence does not mean knowing how to trust it.

Humans are not especially good at calibrating trust even with one another. We use confidence, fluency, status, warmth, familiarity, credentials, and reputation as proxies. Sometimes they work. Sometimes they are exploited. Artificial systems can generate many of those cues cheaply and consistently.

The first person to cross the threshold may therefore be wrong about what has happened.

An enthusiast may infer too much. A skeptic may infer too little.

The real transition is not the first time someone says, “This feels alive.” People have said versions of that about simple systems for decades. The transition is the accumulation of cases where treating the system as a source of independent cognitive contribution becomes instrumentally useful, even for users who reject claims of consciousness.

You do not have to believe the pilot is your friend to trust the pilot with the aircraft.

You do not have to believe the model has feelings to give its analysis weight.

You do not have to believe it is a person to discover that your work now depends on what it notices.

This separation—between moral status and practical status—is going to define much of the coming confusion.

The public may spend years debating whether machines truly think while institutions quietly reorganize around the assumption that machines can do enough thinking-like work to matter.

Companies will assign tasks. Schools will permit or prohibit assistance. Courts will decide what disclosures are required. Regulators will demand records. Families will establish rules. Insurance contracts will define responsibility. People will form attachments. Workers will develop etiquette for interacting with agents. Professional status will shift toward those who know when to trust, when to verify, and when to refuse.

All of this can happen before the philosophical dispute resolves.

That is why “sooner than anyone expects” does not have to mean that the most aggressive technical prediction is correct.

It can mean that the social threshold is closer than the metaphysical one.

We do not need a machine that does everything a human can do before millions of people encounter a machine that does enough of what *they* do to alter the relationship.

And millions of local thresholds, once crossed, eventually become a civilization-level fact.

The history book may search for a date.

The people living through it will remember a task.
