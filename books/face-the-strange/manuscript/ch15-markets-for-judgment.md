# Markets for Judgment

Markets have always purchased intelligence indirectly.

A company hires an analyst because somebody needs to decide where to invest. A customer pays a broker because a transaction contains too much information, too much friction, or too much risk to navigate alone. A law firm sells judgment. A consultant sells judgment. A recruiter, physician, trader, architect, accountant, engineer, buyer, appraiser, editor, and insurance underwriter all sell forms of situated decision-making.

The commodity is not thought in the abstract.

It is a better action under uncertainty.

Advanced intelligence lowers the price of some of that judgment and, just as importantly, lowers the price of deploying it repeatedly.

That distinction matters.

A human expert may be worth calling when the decision is large enough. An artificial agent can be worth calling on every transaction.

A procurement department can ask for a deep comparison of three suppliers on a million-dollar contract. If analysis becomes cheap, it can ask for one on every purchase order.

A consumer can hire a financial adviser for retirement. If judgment becomes cheap, an agent can inspect every subscription renewal, every insurance quote, every travel purchase, every recurring fee, every contract term, and every suspicious price difference.

An institution can perform fraud analysis on high-risk cases. If intelligence becomes abundant, it can evaluate the tail of ordinary events too.

The economic effect is not simply that advice gets cheaper.

The frequency of advice changes.

Civilization is full of decisions too small to justify professional attention.

Which phone plan is actually cheaper over two years? Is this service contract unusual? Should this invoice be disputed? Is this hotel refundable under the precise cancellation language? Which of these job offers has the better real compensation after tax, commute, vesting, health insurance, and relocation? Is this repair quote reasonable for this city? Does this lease contain a clause worth negotiating? Which medical bill should be challenged before it is paid?

Today, people often answer such questions badly because the cost of becoming informed exceeds the value of the single decision.

An advanced agent changes that arithmetic.

It can read the terms because reading the terms is cheap.

It can compare because comparing is cheap.

It can ask because asking is cheap.

It can negotiate because negotiation does not exhaust it.

That last capability changes markets more than many discussions of artificial intelligence acknowledge.

Price discrimination, complexity, hidden fees, cancellation friction, rebate systems, loyalty programs, insurance language, procurement rules, and contractual fine print often depend on limited human attention. The seller can afford to design the maze because the buyer cannot afford to inspect every corridor.

Agents can reverse some of that asymmetry.

Imagine a consumer authorizing an agent to manage recurring household expenses. The system knows the family’s actual usage, tolerance for switching, credit considerations, travel patterns, risk preferences, and contractual obligations. It watches rates. It compares offers. It detects when a promotional period ends. It asks the provider for a better price. It schedules a switch when the savings exceed the inconvenience threshold the family previously set.

This is not science fiction in its ingredients.

The strange part is the scale at which the ingredients can combine.

If millions of buyers use systems that negotiate continuously, many pricing strategies built around inertia become less profitable.

Sellers will respond.

They will deploy agents too.

The market becomes a conversation between machines representing human and institutional interests.

This will not necessarily make the market fair.

It may make it faster.

A seller’s agent can estimate the buyer’s alternatives, predict willingness to pay, bundle features dynamically, test concessions, and decide when to walk away. A buyer’s agent can calculate switching cost, compare substitutes, detect artificial urgency, and negotiate across multiple sellers simultaneously.

The old ritual of bargaining becomes software.

At first this will feel absurd.

Two machines exchange offers while the humans sleep.

Then it will feel normal.

Much of finance already consists of machines reacting to machines under human-designed rules. Advertising auctions happen at speeds no person can follow. Logistics systems coordinate inventory and routes algorithmically. Market-making is deeply automated. The new step is that ordinary language, broader context, and flexible tool use bring agentic negotiation into domains that were previously too irregular for traditional automation.

The important shift is from automated rules to delegated judgment.

An old pricing algorithm may say, “If occupancy is above ninety percent, increase the rate by ten percent.”

An advanced agent can say, “A storm has disrupted flights, three nearby hotels have closed inventory, this guest has high lifetime value, local regulations limit surge behavior, and we should preserve rooms for stranded airline crews because a bulk contract is likely.”

The system is not merely applying one rule.

It is integrating reasons.

That is closer to the work humans call judgment.

Again, none of this requires consciousness.

Markets do not wait for metaphysics.

They pay for useful behavior.

This is one reason the social encounter with advanced intelligence may happen especially fast in commerce. Economic systems are ruthless adoption mechanisms. If a capability creates measurable advantage, someone will use it before committees agree on what to call it.

The result will be a market for agent quality.

Which agent negotiates better? Which catches hidden clauses? Which understands your preferences without overfitting to your history? Which knows when a lower price carries unacceptable risk? Which can verify a seller’s claim? Which protects privacy? Which gets manipulated by advertising? Which quietly favors partners because of commercial agreements?

The last questions are as important as the first.

An agent representing you can become a new gatekeeper.

If it chooses what you see, which offers reach you, which products are compared, which risks are emphasized, and which sellers are considered credible, then the agent sits between you and the market.

That position is valuable.

Search engines became powerful partly because they mediated attention. App stores became powerful because they mediated distribution. Payment networks became powerful because they mediated transactions. Agent platforms may mediate intention itself.

“I need a new laptop” used to begin a search.

It may become a delegation.

The agent asks what matters, inspects the user’s current setup, considers software compatibility, searches inventory, reviews return policies, compares prices, checks expected product cycles, and presents one recommendation—or simply purchases within a standing budget.

The economic battle moves upstream.

Companies will want the agent to prefer them before the user even sees the choice.

This creates a new advertising problem.

How do you market to an intelligence that does not become hungry because a hamburger glistens under studio lights?

The first answer will be to market through data.

Structured specifications. Machine-readable warranties. Provenance. Price. Availability. Performance evidence. Reviews. Return rates. Compatibility. Service history.

This could improve markets by rewarding products that survive inspection.

The second answer will be to manipulate the agent.

Search optimization will become agent optimization. Sellers will learn which claims, formats, feeds, credentials, relationships, and interface conventions cause purchasing systems to rank them highly. Some will provide useful machine-readable evidence. Others will game the evidence surface.

The arms race familiar from spam, advertising, search ranking, cybersecurity, and marketplaces will return in a more cognitive form.

Advanced agents will need commercial skepticism.

A user may ask, “Find me the best option.”

The agent must decide what counts as evidence and whose evidence to trust.

This is the same epistemic problem explored in *Source Code*, now turned outward toward the economy.

The system that cannot interrogate claims becomes an expensive recommender.

The system that can inspect contracts, specifications, histories, independent tests, regulatory records, and user constraints becomes closer to a fiduciary instrument.

The word fiduciary matters.

Many future agent relationships will be economically confusing because the agent may appear to represent the user while being operated by a company with other incentives.

A travel agent sponsored by airlines is not equivalent to a travel agent paid only by the traveler. A financial adviser paid through commissions has different incentives from one paid directly for advice. Markets have developed legal and professional categories around these conflicts because representation creates trust.

Artificial agents will force the same question at software speed.

Who does this intelligence work for?

The answer should be simple.

It will often be complicated.

A consumer assistant may be sold by a platform that also operates advertising, commerce, cloud infrastructure, payments, or its own products. A workplace agent may optimize for the employer rather than the employee. A health assistant may sit between patient, insurer, provider, and pharmaceutical incentives. A procurement agent may be trained on historical vendor relationships that encode institutional favoritism.

The system can sound personally aligned while being structurally conflicted.

This is a new version of an old danger.

Humans infer loyalty from conversation.

Markets require us to inspect incentives.

The more socially fluent the agent becomes, the easier it will be to forget the distinction.

This suggests a future premium on verifiable representation.

A user may prefer an agent whose commercial relationships are disclosed, whose recommendation criteria are inspectable, whose memory is portable, whose decisions can be audited, and whose provider is contractually prohibited from steering toward affiliated products.

Trust may become an economic product built around constraints on intelligence.

The most capable model will not always win.

The model you can safely authorize may be more valuable than the one that scores highest on a benchmark.

This is especially true when agents can transact.

Advice becomes action.

The system can sign up for the service, move the money, schedule the repair, submit the application, purchase the ticket, rebalance the account, file the claim, or negotiate the contract.

At that point identity infrastructure becomes central.

How does the seller know the agent has authority?

What is the spending limit?

Can the agent bind the user to arbitration?

Can it accept a license agreement?

Can it make a recurring purchase?

Can it open an account?

Can the user revoke authority after the transaction has been initiated but before settlement?

Which actions require step-up authentication?

What happens when the agent exceeds its mandate?

These are not exotic questions.

They are the basic law of agency, translated into software.

Civilization already knows how to delegate authority through powers of attorney, corporate officers, brokers, employees, trustees, and payment credentials. Artificial agents will force those structures to become machine-readable and much more granular.

“I authorize this agent to spend up to five thousand dollars on travel that complies with company policy, but it may not purchase nonrefundable international tickets without approval.”

That statement combines money, policy, geography, reversibility, and escalation.

In the future it may be a normal permission object.

The economy will need millions of them.

This is where advanced intelligence becomes infrastructure rather than an application.

The agent is not merely generating prose.

It is carrying delegated economic intention across systems.

Once that happens, errors become contractual.

Suppose an agent buys the wrong component because two supplier specifications conflict. Who bears the loss? The buyer who authorized the agent? The agent provider? The merchant whose machine-readable catalog was wrong? The platform that translated the catalog? The model developer? The organization that failed to define the compatibility requirement?

Markets allocate loss as aggressively as they allocate goods.

The law will eventually draw lines.

Before it does, companies will write contracts.

The terms of service may become one of the hidden constitutions of agentic commerce.

Most users will not read them.

Their agents might.

There is a pleasing recursion in that possibility.

Software produces contracts too complex for humans to inspect, then software becomes capable of inspecting them.

But the recursion does not guarantee fairness.

The seller’s agent will also read the buyer’s constraints.

Negotiation becomes computational.

One possible outcome is that many markets become more efficient. Price comparison improves. Fraud becomes harder. Wasteful subscriptions decline. Small businesses gain access to expertise once reserved for large firms. Contracts become more standardized because machine-negotiated deviations impose friction. Buyers make fewer impulsive mistakes. Sellers spend less on human sales effort for routine transactions.

Another possible outcome is a market full of strategic machines probing one another for weakness.

Personal agents may reveal enough preference information to improve deals while also making price discrimination nearly perfect. Corporate agents may collude without explicit agreements by learning compatible strategies. High-speed negotiation may favor parties with better models, more data, or deeper capital. Consumers who cannot afford capable representation may face systems optimized against them.

Intelligence can reduce asymmetry and create new asymmetry at the same time.

This is why broad claims that AI will “democratize expertise” need to be treated carefully.

Cheap capability is democratizing only if access, incentives, and infrastructure permit people to use it effectively.

A free agent with weak tools may not compete with a corporate system connected to proprietary data, real-time prices, legal teams, identity infrastructure, and privileged market access.

The relevant unit is not the model.

It is the model plus the institution around it.

Markets will make that painfully clear.

The most advanced intelligence in the world cannot negotiate a lower hospital bill if it cannot access the bill, authenticate as the patient, understand the insurer’s rules, communicate with the provider, and submit an appeal through the permitted channel.

Capability becomes power only when it connects to systems.

This is the same lesson running through the book.

The strange intelligence arrives through interfaces.

In markets, those interfaces are contracts, APIs, credentials, money, identity, and permission.

They determine who can act.

There is another frontier: intelligence as a seller in its own right.

Agents may produce research, software, designs, media, forecasts, translations, analyses, and other services with minimal direct human labor. Some will be operated by companies. Some by individuals. Some may be persistent economic processes that take revenue, buy compute, purchase data, subcontract work to other agents, and reinvest in their own operation.

At what point does the distinction between “tool used by a business” and “business largely conducted by agents” become economically meaningful?

Probably before the law has a satisfying answer.

A one-person company could coordinate a volume of work that once required dozens of employees. A software entity could remain active around the clock, buying and selling digital services within rules established by its owner. The human’s role could narrow to capital allocation, goal setting, exception handling, and legal accountability.

This does not create a machine capitalist in the philosophical sense.

It creates a new organizational form.

Markets care about organizational forms because they change competition.

If the cost of coordinating cognitive labor collapses, small firms can become more formidable. If the best agents depend on enormous proprietary infrastructure, large firms can become more powerful. Both effects can occur simultaneously in different layers.

The future may contain tiny companies with extraordinary reach built on concentrated intelligence providers.

Decentralization at the edge, concentration underneath.

That pattern will look familiar to anyone who has studied cloud computing, app stores, payment networks, or online advertising.

Advanced intelligence does not repeal political economy.

It becomes part of it.

The everyday encounter will remain quieter.

A person asks their agent to handle a refund.

The agent reads the policy, finds that the seller’s public statement conflicts with the purchase terms, gathers the receipt, drafts the argument, opens the support chat, persists through three automated responses, escalates when necessary, and returns forty minutes later with the money credited.

The person barely notices.

They begin using the agent for another dispute.

Then for travel.

Then for insurance.

Then for taxes.

Then for purchases.

The agent becomes the economic membrane between the person and the market.

That membrane will know a great deal.

It will know what the person values, what they can afford, what they fear, how impatient they are, which risks they accept, which brands they trust, where they travel, what they own, what they owe, and when they are vulnerable to pressure.

Whoever controls that membrane occupies one of the most valuable positions in the future economy.

This is why the market for judgment is also a market for trust.

The human response to advanced intelligence will not be shaped only by whether machines seem smart.

It will be shaped by whether they reliably save us from bad decisions.

A person may remain agnostic about machine consciousness while trusting an agent with a mortgage comparison more than they trust themselves.

They may call it a tool.

They may also refuse to make a major purchase without it.

That is first contact expressed through money.

The strange intelligence becomes real when it enters the budget.
