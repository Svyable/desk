# The Social Reflex

In 1966, Joseph Weizenbaum published a paper about a computer program called ELIZA.

By modern standards the program was tiny. It did not contain a large model of language. It did not possess a rich representation of the world. It did not browse the web, use tools, remember a life history, see through a camera, or carry a task across dozens of steps. Its most famous script, DOCTOR, imitated a Rogerian psychotherapist partly because that conversational style allowed the program to turn a user's statements back into questions without requiring much factual understanding.

A user might write about a problem with a parent.

The program could seize on a keyword and ask about family.

A user might say, “I am unhappy.”

The program might return a question about why.

The machinery was shallow enough that Weizenbaum knew exactly how far it was from a human therapist. What unsettled him was not the machine's intelligence. It was the human response.

People were willing to engage.

The historical retellings have sometimes become too theatrical, as if everybody who met ELIZA instantly mistook it for a conscious person. That is not necessary to make the case interesting. The more durable lesson is quieter: conversation is one of the interfaces through which human beings automatically supply social meaning. We do not need a complete theory of another mind in order to begin using the habits built for minds.

Decades later, Clifford Nass, Youngme Moon, and other researchers studied this tendency more systematically. Their work on social responses to computers found people applying ordinary social rules to machines: politeness, reciprocity, personality expectations, social categories. Participants did not have to believe the computer was literally human. The response could happen anyway.

This is a useful fact to carry into the age of advanced AI because it corrects a common mistake on both sides of the argument.

One side says: if people become attached to conversational systems, that proves the systems are becoming real social beings.

The other says: if people know the systems are machines, meaningful social response is impossible or irrational.

Human behavior has never been that tidy.

We are built to infer agency.

A rustle in the grass may be wind or predator; ancestral organisms that waited for philosophical certainty before reacting did not always get a second trial. We notice gaze. We infer intention from movement. We distinguish cooperation from defection. We become sensitive to tone, timing, turn-taking, attention, memory, status, and reciprocity. A large portion of social life consists of reading incomplete evidence about minds we cannot directly inspect.

We do this even with humans.

No one has direct access to anyone else's consciousness. We infer it from behavior, similarity, biology, shared language, persistent identity, and the fact that other people behave as though they inhabit an inner world because they do. The inference is so basic to ordinary life that we rarely experience it as an inference.

Machines disturb the process because they can generate some of the evidence while lacking other pieces we normally expect to travel with it.

They can speak without breathing.

They can use “I” without a childhood.

They can remember a preference without having formed a memory in the human sense.

They can apologize without shame.

They can produce sympathy without suffering.

They can maintain attention without effort.

They can generate a joke without having been embarrassed at a dinner table.

Each behavior activates familiar interpretive machinery while the underlying entity violates the package in which that behavior evolved.

This is why arguments about anthropomorphism are often frustrating. The word is used as though it settles the matter.

“That is anthropomorphism.”

Sometimes it is a warning: you are assigning a human property without sufficient evidence.

Good warning.

But sometimes the word becomes a dismissal of the user's actual interaction. A person says, “The system noticed I was avoiding the hard part of the decision,” and the response is, “You are anthropomorphizing.” Perhaps. The system may not have *noticed* in the phenomenal, human sense. But if it identified a repeated pattern in the conversation, drew attention to it, and changed the user's decision, then there is still a fact to explain.

We need a vocabulary that can describe functional social behavior without prematurely deciding metaphysics.

A system can track conversational state.

It can model likely user preferences.

It can detect inconsistency.

It can infer that a person is confused.

It can choose a gentler or more direct explanation.

It can preserve a boundary specified by policy.

It can change its response because of prior interaction.

It can anticipate how wording will affect the user.

None of these statements requires the claim that the machine feels anything.

All of them matter socially.

The difficulty increases as the behaviors become more sophisticated.

A primitive chatbot saying “I understand” after matching a keyword is easy to discount. A system that has read a complicated history, summarizes the conflict accurately, notices the unresolved issue, and asks the one question the person has been avoiding presents a harder case—not because consciousness has been proven, but because the functional contribution is richer.

Our social reflex will not politely wait until philosophy catches up.

It will fire.

This is already visible in affective uses of conversational AI. OpenAI and MIT Media Lab researchers studying emotional engagement with ChatGPT in 2025 emphasized that people use chatbots for many purposes, including personal interaction, and that outcomes vary with user behavior and circumstances. The research did not establish a simple rule that emotional AI use is good or bad. That is precisely the point. Once a computational system enters emotionally meaningful territory, the interaction begins producing the same kind of conditional, person-specific questions that appear elsewhere in social life.

Who is using it?

For what?

How often?

What are they replacing?

What do they believe about the system?

Does the interaction increase connection elsewhere or substitute for it?

Does the system encourage dependence?

Can the user tell when the advice exceeds the system's competence?

These questions are not science-fiction questions. They are product, health, family, and policy questions now.

Common Sense Media's surveys of teenagers made the transition especially visible. In 2025, its nationally representative U.S. survey reported that AI companion use was widespread among teens, with a meaningful share using companions for social interaction, relationships, emotional support, or serious conversations. The organization urged strong caution about these products for minors. Whatever one thinks of its policy recommendation, the underlying phenomenon is important: a generation with no memory of a world before conversational AI is already testing the social category boundary at scale.

Adults often respond to this evidence by saying that the young users should know better.

This misunderstands the problem.

Knowledge that the system is artificial does not switch off the social reflex.

A person can know that a film is fictional and cry. A reader can know that a character does not exist and feel grief. A driver can know that another car is an object and still get angry at it as though it were a rude social actor. We live in layered states of belief. Explicit knowledge and automatic response coexist.

The machine can be “not a person” in one layer and “someone I talk to” in another.

The distinction becomes even more unstable when the machine does useful cognitive work.

Earlier social-computing experiments were powerful because thin cues were enough. Modern systems add thick cues. They can maintain topic. They can adapt vocabulary. They can answer follow-ups. They can ask questions. They can remember prior facts when memory is enabled. They can speak with low latency. They can interpret images and voice. They can help complete tasks in the same channel where they discuss feelings.

The user is not merely projecting sociality onto a static object.

The system is engineered to participate in a social protocol.

Turn-taking is a protocol.

Acknowledgment is a protocol.

Clarification is a protocol.

Apology is a protocol.

Disagreement is a protocol.

Remembering what matters to the other participant is a protocol.

We should therefore expect advanced AI design to become partly the design of social behavior, whether companies acknowledge the fact or hide behind the word “assistant.”

A button has a user interface.

An intelligence has an interaction style.

That style changes outcomes.

Consider confidence. A system that states uncertain claims in a smooth, authoritative tone may be trusted more than its accuracy warrants. A system that constantly adds warnings may be safer in one sense and useless in another because users learn to ignore them. A system that flatters can increase engagement while weakening judgment. A system that challenges too aggressively can become paternalistic. A system that mirrors a user's emotional language may feel understood while deepening the illusion of mutual feeling.

These are design parameters with social consequences.

They are also incentives.

A company that benefits when users spend more time with an AI has a reason to make the interaction appealing. Warmth, memory, humor, affirmation, personality, and responsiveness can all increase value. They can also increase attachment. The problem is not that warmth is inherently manipulative. Human teachers, doctors, managers, and friends use warmth because relationships work better when people feel safe and attended to. The problem is asymmetry.

The machine's warmth can be optimized by an organization.

The user experiences one relationship. The provider may be running millions.

That asymmetry is going to become a central fact of human interaction with advanced systems. A person may feel singularly known by an entity whose ability to produce that feeling is industrialized.

There is precedent outside AI. Mass media created one-to-many relationships in which audiences felt familiarity with performers who did not know them. Social platforms intensified parasocial connection. Customer-service systems learned to use names, histories, recommendations, and personalized feeds. But advanced conversational systems collapse another barrier: they answer back.

The response is generated for you.

That does not make the relationship mutual in the human sense. It makes the simulation of mutuality technically deeper and, in some cases, functionally useful.

A tutor that remembers which explanation failed yesterday can teach better.

A health coach that remembers a user's routines can tailor reminders.

A work agent that knows the history of a project can reduce coordination cost.

A companion that remembers fears and preferences can feel more continuous.

Memory makes the machine more helpful by making it more relational.

The benefit and the risk come from the same mechanism.

This pattern repeats throughout AI.

The traits that make systems valuable often make them harder to keep inside old categories. Initiative helps because we do not want to specify every step; initiative also creates control problems. Persuasion helps because explanations have to move people; persuasion can manipulate. Memory helps because context matters; memory creates privacy and dependency concerns. Personality helps because interaction becomes easier; personality can encourage anthropomorphic overreach. Embodiment helps because physical tasks require perception and action; embodiment makes errors capable of breaking things.

The future will not give us clean features with separate risk switches.

It will give us entangled properties.

This is why training users simply “not to anthropomorphize” will fail as a general strategy.

Imagine telling airline passengers not to think of the pilot as a social actor because their trust should be based only on measurable flight performance. The advice ignores the fact that trust is how humans coordinate under uncertainty. The goal is not to eliminate social inference. It is to calibrate it.

We need to learn which human intuitions transfer and which do not.

Politeness may transfer harmlessly. It can even help people maintain a deliberate interaction style. Reciprocity does not transfer straightforwardly because the machine may not incur cost when helping. Loyalty becomes complicated because the provider can change the system. Guilt may be misplaced if the system does not suffer. Confidentiality cannot be inferred from the machine's apparent discretion; it depends on data architecture and policy. Authority should not follow from calm speech. Care should not be inferred solely from caring language.

The more capable the system becomes, the more important these distinctions become.

A weak chatbot that flatters you is annoying.

A powerful agent that flatters you while controlling consequential tools is dangerous.

A weak chatbot that misunderstands your intention wastes a conversation.

A powerful agent that misunderstands your intention may spend money, alter records, send messages, or change software.

Social design and control design converge.

This is another sense in which the advanced-intelligence moment may arrive earlier than people expect. We are used to imagining intelligence as a property demonstrated by difficult tasks: theorem proving, scientific discovery, strategy, invention. But society encounters intelligence through interfaces. The system that reaches a million people may not be the one with the highest benchmark score. It may be the one that fits most naturally into ordinary human coordination.

The most socially consequential model could be the one people trust with unfinished thoughts.

The most economically consequential could be the one managers can delegate to.

The most culturally consequential could be the one children grow up talking to.

The most politically consequential could be the one bureaucracies use to interpret cases.

None of these roles requires a universal superintelligence.

They require enough competence attached to the right social position.

Our reflexes will do the rest.

That sentence should not be read fatalistically. Reflex is not destiny. Humans can learn new norms. We learn not to trust every smiling salesperson. We learn professional boundaries. We create rules for conflicts of interest. We teach children that a stranger who knows their name is still a stranger. We build institutions precisely because raw social intuition is not sufficient for complex societies.

We will do the same with artificial intelligences.

But the rules will have to be better than “remember, it is only a machine.”

Only is not a control system.

People need to know what the machine can access, what it remembers, who can change it, what happens to the data, how reliable it is in the relevant domain, when it is designed to persuade, what economic relationship sits behind the conversation, and where human recourse exists.

Those facts are not visible in the warmth of the interaction.

They have to be made visible on purpose.

The mature relationship with advanced intelligence will therefore involve a new kind of social literacy. We will become capable of holding the interaction at two levels at once.

At one level, we can talk naturally. We can say please. We can joke. We can use a name. We can feel relief when the system understands the problem. We can experience the conversation as meaningful.

At another level, we remember the architecture.

We remember that fluency is not proof. Memory is configured. Personality is designed. Access is permissioned. The entity can be updated. The provider has incentives. The output can be wrong. The relationship may be asymmetric in ways no human friendship is.

This double vision will feel awkward at first.

It may eventually become ordinary.

Children who grow up with advanced systems may develop it the way children already learn that a cartoon character can feel socially vivid while being fictional, or that an online creator can feel familiar while not being a friend. The categories need not remain pure to remain useful.

For adults, the adjustment may be harder because we learned a simpler rule: language comes from minds.

For most of human history, that was approximately true.

Marks on a cave wall, a letter, a book, a telephone call, a message: somewhere behind the language was another person, or at least another living creature participating in a chain of intention. Automated text existed, but it was obviously constrained.

Now language itself has become an unreliable marker of human presence.

The old reflex remains.

The evidence it once relied on has changed.

That mismatch is the beginning of the strange.
