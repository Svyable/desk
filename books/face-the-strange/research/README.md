# Research Brief

## Thesis under test

*Face The Strange* asks what it will feel like for ordinary people to encounter artificial systems that are no longer experienced merely as software tools but as advanced intelligences: entities capable of sustained reasoning, contextual understanding, tool use, planning, memory, initiative, disagreement, and surprising problem-solving across enough of a person's life that the relationship changes category.

The book does not require a claim that current or near-future AI systems are conscious, sentient, persons, or moral patients. Those are separate philosophical and scientific questions. The narrower and more empirically tractable claim is about human experience and institutional behavior. People do not wait for metaphysical certainty before assigning social meaning, trust, authority, responsibility, intimacy, or status. Human-computer interaction research has shown for decades that social responses can be triggered by surprisingly thin cues. As AI systems become more capable, persistent, multimodal, agentic, and embodied, those cues arrive attached to genuine competence rather than simple conversational mimicry.

The central prediction is therefore not “AGI will be announced on a particular date.” It is that advanced-intelligence encounters will arrive locally, unevenly, and earlier than consensus. A programmer may cross the threshold when an agent can independently investigate and repair an unfamiliar system. A scientist may cross it when a machine proposes a useful line of inquiry she did not foresee. A child may never experience conversational AI as strange at all. A manager may cross it when she stops checking every delegated task. A person using a companion system may cross it when continuity and memory turn occasional interaction into an ongoing relationship. A government may cross it when automated systems become actors inside administrative processes rather than tools outside them.

The book must distinguish several claims that are easy to blur:

1. **Capability is not consciousness.** A system can be strategically or socially consequential without evidence that it has subjective experience.
2. **Social response is not proof of personhood.** Humans can treat machines socially while knowing they are machines.
3. **Autonomy is not reliability.** Longer tasks and greater tool use increase both usefulness and the surface area for error.
4. **Anthropomorphism is not always a mistake, but it can be.** Human social instincts can help us coordinate with machines while also encouraging misplaced trust.
5. **Diffusion is not uniform.** Adoption, access, skill, regulation, language, wealth, infrastructure, and culture shape who meets advanced systems first and on what terms.
6. **A local threshold can matter before a global threshold exists.** Social change does not require universal agreement on a definition of AGI.
7. **Advanced intelligence need not be singular.** Different models, agents, institutions, tool environments, memories, incentives, and embodiments can produce materially different machine counterparts.
8. **Foreignness can be useful.** The value of advanced intelligence may come partly from strategies and representations that do not imitate human thought, so human-likeness should not be mistaken for the capability target.

The strong version of the book's argument—that nearly everyone will soon regard AI systems as persons—is too brittle and too speculative. The version worth testing is more durable: the human category boundary between “tool” and “other intelligence” will become behaviorally unstable well before society has settled the philosophical question, because systems are becoming more capable and more socially situated faster than institutions can update their norms.

## Core empirical tests

1. **The social reflex:** How readily do humans apply politeness, reciprocity, personality, authority, and relationship scripts to computational systems even when they know the systems are not human?
2. **The alien-solution effect:** What happens when a machine produces useful moves, strategies, designs, or hypotheses outside the human tradition that trained the expert's intuition?
3. **Task-horizon growth:** Are agents becoming capable of completing tasks that would take human experts progressively longer, and what are the methodological limits of those measurements?
4. **Delegation:** Are workplace interactions shifting from question-answer assistance toward longer-running delegated execution, and how do organizations preserve human accountability as that happens?
5. **Memory and persistence:** How does an ongoing record of preferences, history, and unfinished work change the user's perception of an AI system from session-bound tool to continuing counterpart?
6. **Affective use:** How common is emotional or companion-like use, and what evidence exists about benefits, harms, dependence, loneliness, disclosure, and well-being?
7. **Embodiment:** How rapidly are multimodal and robotic systems gaining the ability to perceive, plan, converse, and act in physical environments, and where do current systems remain brittle?
8. **Institutional lag:** Are education, corporate governance, law, and public policy adapting at the same rate as capability and adoption?
9. **Generational normalization:** Are children and teens incorporating AI into ordinary life quickly enough that experiences adults call uncanny become baseline expectations for younger users?
10. **Plural arrival:** Does observed change look more like many domain-specific thresholds than one clean “AGI day”?
11. **The skeptical baseline:** Do reliability failures, economic constraints, security limits, and weak real-world performance remain large enough that being unimpressed is often the rational response?
12. **Plural machine ecology:** Does deployment produce one convergent general counterpart, or a population of differentiated systems shaped by owners, permissions, memories, tools, objectives, and environments?

## Evidence lanes

### I. The old social machinery

Use Joseph Weizenbaum's 1966 ELIZA paper and the later Computers Are Social Actors research to establish that people can respond socially to machines without literally believing the machine is human. Treat this as a psychological starting condition, not as evidence of machine understanding. The important question is what happens when a social interface is paired with substantially stronger competence, continuity, and initiative.

### II. Intelligence that feels foreign

Use AlphaGo's 2016 match against Lee Sedol, especially the historical significance attached to Move 37, as a concrete example of a machine producing a high-value action that did not look like the obvious extension of expert human convention. Avoid mythologizing one move as proof of general intelligence. The case matters because it shows that machine competence can become most culturally significant precisely when it is not simply human imitation.

### III. From chat to agents

Use METR's task-completion time-horizon work, the Anthropic Economic Index, OpenAI enterprise research, Microsoft workplace research, and the Stanford AI Index to track the shift from conversational assistance toward delegation and tool-using agents. Preserve methodological caveats: software-task benchmarks are not the whole economy, self-reported productivity is not the same as measured output, successful demonstrations do not establish robust autonomy, and enterprise telemetry reflects selected users and products.

### IV. Intimacy and affective use

Use the 2025 OpenAI/MIT Media Lab work on affective use and the Common Sense Media teen surveys as evidence that some users already employ conversational systems socially and emotionally. Do not universalize from these populations. Separate systems designed as companions from general assistants used as companions. Do not equate user satisfaction with healthy outcomes, and do not infer mental-health efficacy from conversational fluency.

### V. Childhood and education

Use UNESCO guidance, Stanford AI Index education material, and Common Sense Media's 2026 census to examine the speed at which young people are encountering AI relative to schools' and parents' ability to establish norms. The question is not whether AI is “good” or “bad” for education in the abstract. It is what happens when students grow up with a patient, interactive, often persuasive cognitive system available before institutions have agreed how to teach judgment about it.

### VI. Embodiment

Use Google DeepMind's robotics releases as developer evidence of current research direction: multimodal systems connecting perception, language, planning, tool use, and physical action. Balance these claims with independent reporting and the persistent gap between controlled demonstrations and economical, robust operation in unconstrained environments. The social transition caused by embodiment may begin before humanoid robots are generally capable; voice, cameras, vehicles, appliances, and specialized machines can provide physical presence without a human-shaped body.

### VII. Work and the price of judgment

Use ILO exposure estimates, Anthropic usage studies, Microsoft work research, and Stanford's economic chapter to distinguish job transformation, task automation, productivity, organizational redesign, and employment effects. Avoid assuming that exposure equals displacement. The book's interest is the human relationship to delegated cognition: what people stop doing, what they still verify, how status changes, and which forms of judgment become more valuable when execution becomes cheap.

### VIII. Governance and category strain

Use NIST's AI Risk Management Framework and Generative AI Profile and the EU AI Act as examples of institutions trying to govern systems through risk, transparency, documentation, and accountability rather than settling whether they count as minds. This supports the book's claim that practical governance can precede philosophical consensus. Avoid presenting any regulatory framework as settled, globally representative, or technically sufficient.

### IX. Skepticism, plurality, and useful foreignness

The closing chapters deliberately reuse the strongest evidence above rather than pretending each philosophical synthesis requires a new statistic. METR's bounded task-horizon measurements, Reuters' robotics counter-reporting, ILO's distinction between exposure and displacement, affective-use caveats, NIST's risk framing, and the AlphaGo case form a combined pressure test. Together they support three narrower conclusions: capability should be judged behaviorally rather than ceremonially; real limitations deserve continued attention even during rapid progress; and the most valuable machine intelligence may differ across systems and from human cognitive style. These chapters are synthesis and counterargument, not claims of a newly measured universal property.

## Falsification and counter-evidence

Every chapter should preserve evidence that weakens the thesis.

- People may continue to treat even highly capable AI as sophisticated software, especially when interfaces emphasize tools rather than personalities.
- Capability improvements may slow, become uneconomical, or fail to generalize outside benchmark-rich digital tasks.
- Agent reliability may remain too low for deep delegation; human supervision may stay intensive for important work.
- Persistent memory may prove undesirable because of privacy, security, or user discomfort.
- Companion use may remain a minority behavior among adults even if it is culturally visible.
- Embodied AI may lag digital intelligence for many years because physical reliability, cost, safety, maintenance, and data constraints are harder than conversational performance.
- Organizations may deliberately restrict agent autonomy in order to preserve accountability, auditability, and security.
- Strong social responses to machines may be shallow, temporary, playful, or context-specific rather than evidence of a durable category shift.
- Different cultures may adopt very different norms for personifying, trusting, or delegating to AI.
- The word “intelligence” may become less socially important as capability becomes ordinary; advanced systems could disappear into infrastructure rather than becoming recognized counterparts.
- Model ecosystems may converge around a few providers and interaction styles, making plurality less important than the manuscript expects.
- Human-like interfaces may prove practically superior for communication even when the underlying reasoning is foreign, reducing the visible strangeness of the encounter.

The book should survive these possibilities. Its durable claim is not that one anthropomorphic future is inevitable. It is that humans will have to renegotiate the boundary between instrument and interlocutor because machines are acquiring combinations of competence and social presence that older categories did not anticipate.

## Source discipline

- Prefer original papers, official datasets, regulator texts, and primary developer documentation for claims about measured capability or product design.
- Label company surveys and developer benchmarks as such; do not silently convert marketing evidence into neutral fact.
- Do not infer consciousness, subjective experience, desire, suffering, or personhood from language behavior.
- Separate demonstrated capability from reliable deployment.
- Separate task exposure from job loss.
- Separate companionship usage from therapeutic efficacy.
- Separate user attachment from mutual relationship.
- Preserve dates and vintages because AI capability, product behavior, and regulation are changing rapidly.
- Never invent dialogue, quotations, private reactions, or biographical details to make the future feel vivid.
- Future-facing scenes should be explicitly framed as scenarios, not reported events.
- For synthesis chapters, identify which earlier evidence lanes are being recombined instead of manufacturing spurious chapter-specific evidence.
- For every chapter, keep a note titled “What would make this false?”
