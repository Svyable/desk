# Mars in Moscow

At the Institute of Biomedical Problems in Moscow, the NEK facility and the SIRIUS program place international crews into long-duration isolation and confinement analogs. NASA describes research there on psychology, physiology, team dynamics, sensory deprivation, monotony, and limited communication.

There is an elegant historical reversal here. Cold War analysts once read Soviet chamber studies because they feared what an adversary might know about manipulating humans. Decades later, American and Russian institutions cooperated in chambers because both needed to know how humans behave when Earth becomes operationally distant.

The room survived the ideology that made it sinister.

Mars forces the practical question: not whether isolation can break a generic human, but what environmental design, crew composition, autonomy, workload, lighting, sleep protection, private space, communication practices, and meaningful novelty keep a specific crew capable for years.
