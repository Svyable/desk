# The Room at McGill

In 1951, the interesting thing to do to a human being was almost nothing.

At McGill University, Donald Hebb and colleagues built conditions in which subjects received less patterned sensory input than ordinary life provides. Translucent goggles reduced vision to diffuse light. Sound became monotonous. Touch was constrained. The subjects were not being tortured in the theatrical sense. They were being paid to remain in an impoverished environment and do very little.

A declassified 1972 U.S. intelligence assessment later summarized this research with unusual frankness. It said the work had been motivated partly by questions raised by Korean War “brainwashing” and partly by the mundane problem of attention lapses under monotonous conditions such as prolonged radar watching. The juxtaposition matters. One road leads to coercive interrogation folklore. The other leads to ergonomics.

Subjects reported vivid perceptual phenomena. Cognitive and perceptual performance changed. Some became more susceptible to persuasive material in experiments. These observations were real enough to provoke an international research literature. What they did not establish was a reliable technology for installing beliefs in unwilling people.

That distinction—destabilization versus control—will survive every chapter of this book.

The room also contains a second lesson. The nervous system is not a camera waiting passively for photons. When patterned input falls away, perception does not simply switch off. Internally generated structure becomes harder to distinguish from externally driven structure. That can produce imagery, temporal distortion, boredom, anxiety, and the strange inflation of minor sensations. It also makes the experiment a useful boundary case for understanding ordinary perception.

The Cold War noticed the spectacular implication first. Spaceflight would eventually notice the boring one: people require an environment with enough variation, timing, social contact, meaningful work, sleep stability, and perceptual structure to remain reliable operators.
