# Brainwashing Comes Home

The Korean War gave American researchers a frightening explanatory problem. Some prisoners made statements that appeared to contradict prior loyalties. Public discussion supplied a new word—brainwashing—and the word supplied an imagined technology.

The temptation was to assume an adversary possessed a precise method. That assumption could motivate useful research while also corrupting its interpretation. If one begins by believing there must be a machine for remaking conviction, every effect of sleep loss, isolation, fear, dependency, interrogation, social pressure, and ordinary coercion begins to look like a component in the machine.

The declassified record shows U.S. interest in sensory isolation partly in this context. It does not show that the feared machine was found.

This is a recurring intelligence pathology: technological surprise can be real, so the institution must investigate; but the act of investigation is later cited as evidence that the technology existed. Funding becomes efficacy by retelling.

The book will refuse that conversion. A research contract proves interest. A memorandum proves somebody wrote a memorandum. A disturbing experiment proves that a disturbing experiment occurred. Capability requires its own evidence.
