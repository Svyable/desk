# The Expiration Constitution

We began with a packet that disappears because nobody renews it.

We end with a society that has to decide what deserves the same fate.

The examples have been wildly different.

A DNS answer.

A certificate.

A password.

An emergency power.

A patent.

A drug exclusivity.

An option contract.

A lease.

A passport.

A food date.

A medicine lot.

A gift balance.

A benefit determination.

A green card.

A personal-data record.

An archive.

An AI agent's mandate.

A consent.

The clocks are not interchangeable.

That is the conclusion.

The expiration principle is not that everything should expire.

It is that persistence should be designed rather than inherited.

Every institution already has a constitution of time whether it admits it or not. Some states disappear automatically. Some continue automatically. Some require renewal. Some can be revoked. Some are practically permanent because nobody remembers how to end them. Some are formally permanent because permanence is the protection.

The defaults allocate power.

A temporary right places the burden of continuation on the holder.

A permanent right places the burden of termination on the challenger.

An auto-renewing contract makes inaction favor continuity.

A sunset clause makes inaction favor ending.

A retention schedule makes inaction dangerous unless deletion is automated.

An archive makes deletion dangerous unless preservation is deliberate.

The clock decides who has to move.

That is why expiration belongs beside ownership, consent, due process, and delegation as a fundamental design question.

We need an expiration constitution.

Not one law prescribing one lifetime for everything.

A set of rules for deciding what kind of lifetime a state deserves.

The first rule is to name the thing that expires.

This sounds trivial and is routinely neglected.

Does the card expire or the status?

The password or the account?

The certificate or the domain?

The patent or every barrier to competition?

The food's quality guarantee or its safety?

The medicine's evidence-supported stability period or the molecule itself?

The consent receipt or the underlying relationship?

The agent's token or its mandate?

If the institution cannot answer precisely, the expiration date is carrying more meaning than the design can support.

The second rule is to identify the stale-state harm.

Why is persistence dangerous?

A credential can be stolen.

A permission can outlive a job.

A benefit determination can outlive eligibility.

An emergency can outlive the conditions that justified extraordinary power.

Personal data can outlive its purpose and become a source of surveillance or breach.

A market exclusion can outlive the period society is willing to grant in exchange for innovation.

If nobody can describe the harm caused by stale continuation, expiration may be ritual rather than protection.

The third rule is to identify the termination harm.

What happens if the state ends while it is still valid?

A website goes offline.

A patient loses medicine.

A family loses coverage.

A tenant loses housing.

A scientist loses irreplaceable data.

A historical record disappears.

An ecosystem loses protection.

A basic right becomes contingent on political renewal.

The cost of false termination belongs in the design, not in an appeal process added later.

The fourth rule is to ask whether the state can be reconstructed.

Renewable information can tolerate aggressive expiry.

A cache can query the source again.

A short-lived access token can be reissued after fresh authentication.

A machine can reacquire a routine capability after proving current authorization.

Irreplaceable information deserves a different presumption.

A destroyed archive cannot be refreshed.

An extinct species cannot reauthenticate.

A lost opportunity for due process cannot always be replayed after the harm.

Reversibility is one of the strongest arguments for short lifetimes.

Irreversibility is one of the strongest arguments for continuity.

The fifth rule is to match the clock to the rate of change.

Children's passports are shorter-lived than ordinary adult passports because appearance changes faster.

Machine credentials can be extremely short-lived because renewal can be automated.

Some medical conditions are stable enough that constant recertification adds little information.

Some financial or security states can change in minutes.

A uniform interval is administratively convenient and epistemically lazy when the underlying processes move at different speeds.

The sixth rule is to prefer event-driven revalidation when reliable events exist.

Change a password when compromise is detected rather than because the calendar is bored.

Review access when a role changes.

Ask for new consent when the capability or purpose materially changes.

End an incident mandate when the incident closes.

Revalidate a vendor when ownership changes.

Calendar expiry remains valuable as a backstop for events we fail to detect.

The strongest systems combine both.

The seventh rule is that renewal must produce information.

A renewal that merely updates a timestamp is churn.

A manager who approves all permissions without review has not made the access list fresh.

A worker who changes `Summer2026!` to `Autumn2026!` has not necessarily improved security.

A legislature that mechanically extends a temporary authority may have complied with the sunset without reconsidering the power.

A benefits recipient who proves the same permanent condition for the tenth time has not created meaningful new knowledge.

Ask what the renewal learned.

If the answer is nothing, redesign the renewal.

The eighth rule is to budget attention.

Every recurring review competes with every other recurring review.

The system should not demand human judgment where objective evidence can safely automate continuation. It should not bury consequential decisions among hundreds of low-risk approvals. It should not convert consent into wallpaper or security warnings into reflexes.

Freshness consumes attention.

Spend it where attention can change the outcome.

The ninth rule is to preserve a credible path across the boundary.

Renew before expiration when continuity matters.

Use overlap.

Use grace periods where appropriate.

Use receipt notices, replacement credentials, bridge documents, safe holding states, retries, and escalation.

A well-designed expiration should not require the world to fail for a moment merely to prove the date was real.

Hard boundaries are sometimes necessary.

Cliff failure should be intentional, not accidental.

The tenth rule is to make revocation independent of expiration.

A bad state should not have to survive until its birthday.

A stolen credential should be revocable now.

A compromised agent mandate should end now.

A dangerous product should be recalled now.

A professional who loses required qualifications should not necessarily retain authority until renewal.

A law can be amended before sunset.

Expiration is a maximum presumption of validity, not immunity during the term.

The eleventh rule is to make renewal authority explicit.

Who gets to decide continuation?

The holder?

The issuer?

Both parties?

A court?

A legislature?

A machine test?

A human reviewer?

An independent control plane?

An agent should not renew its own high-risk mandate merely because it can produce a plausible argument for continuing. A regulated entity should not be the sole judge of its own compliance. A vendor should not be able to make customer dependence the only evidence needed for renewal.

The boundary redistributes bargaining power.

Say who holds it.

The twelfth rule is to protect durable rights from accidental temporariness.

Not every capability should become a subscription because software makes subscriptions easy.

Not every status should require recurring proof from scratch.

Not every constitutional protection should face a sunset.

Not every archive should be subject to ordinary deletion logic.

Sometimes permanence is the safeguard against future power.

The expiration principle needs this rule to avoid becoming a philosophy of precarity.

The thirteenth rule is to let evidence extend a date when evidence is better than the original approximation.

Medicine shows the model.

A labeled shelf life is supported by stability data. Under controlled federal stockpile conditions, fresh testing can support extension for specific lots.

The old date was not fake.

New evidence justified a new date.

Other institutions should aspire to that honesty. Do not cling to a duration merely because it was printed first. Do not extend merely because continuation is convenient. Let better information move the boundary.

The fourteenth rule is to distinguish memory from authority.

An institution may need to remember that permission once existed without treating the permission as current.

An archive can preserve the emergency declaration after the emergency ends.

An agent can remember that a manager once approved a purchase without assuming the approval covers a new purchase.

A company can retain a legally necessary audit record after operational access expires.

History should survive more often than power.

This distinction is essential for accountable AI systems.

The fifteenth rule is that the expiration rule itself must expire into review.

A thirty-day retention period can become obsolete.

A ten-year document term can become too long after security threats change.

An annual recertification can become pointless after better data exchange exists.

A short emergency cadence can become permanent bureaucracy after the emergency ends.

No expiration schedule deserves exemption from the principle merely because it is the schedule doing the expiring.

Review the clock.

These rules do not produce one answer.

They produce better disagreements.

A policymaker can argue that a surveillance authority deserves a one-year sunset because stale power is dangerous and political reconsideration is worth the cost.

Another can argue that yearly renewal will become ritual and that stronger event-driven oversight would be better.

Now the disagreement is about stale-state harm, renewal quality, default power, and attention—not about whether sunsets are generically good.

A security team can argue for fifteen-minute credentials because automation makes renewal cheap and compromise costs are high.

An operations team can respond that recovery infrastructure is not mature enough and that the shorter lifetime will cause outages.

Now the trade is visible.

A privacy team can propose deleting raw user data after ninety days.

A research team can argue that a de-identified archive has durable scientific value.

The answer may be layered retention rather than keep-or-delete absolutism.

An AI product team can propose persistent agent permissions for convenience.

A governance team can ask whether the user's original intent will remain fresh as capabilities expand.

The answer may be durable preferences, short-lived action mandates, and fresh confirmation for new powers.

This is what a constitution does.

It does not decide every future case.

It establishes what questions must be answered before power can continue.

The most important change is cultural.

Today, persistence often feels neutral.

If nobody deletes the data, it remains.

If nobody revokes the permission, it remains.

If nobody repeals the law, it remains.

If nobody closes the account, it remains.

If nobody removes the scheduled job, it runs again tomorrow.

Continuation inherits the moral innocence of inaction.

It should not.

Persistence is a decision made by defaults.

Sometimes the default is right.

A person's citizenship should not evaporate because a clerk forgot to renew a record.

A constitutional protection should not disappear because a legislature missed a deadline.

An archive should not be destroyed because nobody could predict its future value.

But a privileged machine permission, emergency power, old personal-data copy, temporary monopoly, or abandoned agent mandate should not gain immortality merely because ending it requires somebody to notice.

The world needs both durable foundations and perishable authority.

The art is knowing which is which.

We have spent the digital age making memory cheap.

We are entering an age that makes action cheap too.

That combination is unprecedented in practical scale.

A system can remember an old instruction perfectly and act on it instantly years later. It can preserve data nobody intended to remain operational. It can renew credentials without human friction. It can delegate authority to descendants that continue after the original task disappears.

The old human safeguards—forgetting, inconvenience, staff turnover, lost paperwork, finite attention—were terrible systems.

They also accidentally caused things to end.

As machines remove those frictions, deliberate expiration becomes more important.

We should not recreate the paperwork.

We should recreate the boundary.

The best future is not one in which everything constantly asks permission again.

That would be unbearable.

It is one in which systems understand the difference between what may safely persist, what should renew invisibly after objective checks, what deserves a fresh human decision, and what must never become contingent merely because technology can put a timer on it.

A civilization that remembers forever needs rules for forgetting.

A civilization that automates action needs rules for stopping.

A civilization that can renew almost anything needs to know when renewal is the wrong goal.

The expiration date was never about time.

It was about how long the past gets to govern the future without being asked again.
