# Money That Vanishes

A gift card begins as a small act of trust.

Someone gives a merchant money now and accepts a promise that goods or services can be claimed later. The card may be plastic, electronic, branded, reloadable, transferable, or tucked into an email. The economic sequence is the same.

Cash moves first.

Consumption comes later.

That gap creates a temptation.

What if later never arrives?

If the promise can expire quickly, a merchant can receive money and eventually be released from the obligation because the customer forgot, lost the card, delayed too long, or failed to notice a date in the terms.

The product has become more profitable because memory failed.

U.S. federal law puts limits around that model. Under 15 U.S.C. § 1693l-1, covered gift certificates, store gift cards, and general-use prepaid cards generally cannot be issued with an expiration date earlier than five years after issuance or, for certain reloadable cards, after funds were last loaded. The law also restricts dormancy and service fees and requires disclosures. The implementing rules distinguish between the expiration of a physical card and the expiration of the underlying funds in certain cases.

The statute contains exclusions, including for some loyalty, award, and promotional cards where no money or value was exchanged.

That difference is the heart of the issue.

Paid value and promotional value are not treated as the same promise.

If a merchant gives you a coupon for ten dollars off next Tuesday, nobody imagines the merchant has taken ten dollars from you and placed it on deposit. The deadline is part of the promotion. The offer is valuable because it is temporary and designed to change behavior.

If you hand the merchant one hundred dollars for a gift card, the moral intuition is different. The merchant has received value. Letting the obligation disappear next week because you did not spend it would look less like a promotion ending and more like the seller keeping your money.

Expiration law follows that intuition.

The clock depends on what was exchanged at the beginning.

This is another reason not to treat all expiry as one category. Two balances can look identical in an app and carry different moral claims.

One was purchased.

One was awarded.

One may deserve long persistence because it represents prepaid value.

The other may reasonably exist only inside a marketing campaign.

Digital systems increasingly blur the difference.

A platform can show cash balance, store credit, loyalty points, promotional credit, referral rewards, compensation for a service failure, and purchased gift value on the same screen. Each balance may have different rules. Some expire. Some do not. Some can be withdrawn. Some can only be spent. Some disappear when an account closes. Some are subject to state unclaimed-property law. Some are plainly promotional.

The interface makes them all look like numbers.

The legal and economic reality underneath can be radically different.

This is expiration as accounting ontology.

What kind of thing is the balance?

The answer decides whether the issuer is extinguishing a debt, ending an offer, closing an account, enforcing a contractual term, or removing a benefit that was never purchased.

Consumers often do not know.

They learn at the boundary.

Your credit expired.

The phrase is maddening because it compresses the whole history into two words. Was the credit purchased? Earned? Awarded after a canceled flight? Granted as a promotion? Issued because the company failed to provide a service already paid for? Was the expiration disclosed? Did the user have a realistic opportunity to redeem it?

The date cannot answer those questions.

It can only enforce the classification chosen earlier.

Gift cards are also interesting because expiration competes with another phenomenon: breakage.

Businesses know that some fraction of prepaid value will never be redeemed. Cards are lost. Email addresses change. Recipients forget. Small residual balances remain. Companies account for the expected nonredemption under applicable financial rules.

This means inattention has economic value even where the underlying funds do not expire quickly.

A card can remain technically redeemable for years and still produce profit because the customer never returns.

Expiration is only one way a promise can die.

Forgetfulness can do the work without a formal date.

That distinction matters in policy. Extending the legal minimum lifetime of a gift card protects consumers from abrupt contractual extinction, but it does not guarantee redemption. A right can persist on paper and remain unused in practice.

This is the inverse of a familiar problem in access systems, where a permission persists on paper and creates risk because nobody remembers it exists.

Forgotten value benefits the issuer.

Forgotten authority threatens the issuer.

Institutions therefore have opposite incentives depending on what persistence costs them.

A company wants dormant customer credit to disappear.

The same company wants dormant employee access to disappear.

In one case, expiry reduces a liability.

In the other, it reduces a security risk.

The mechanism is neutral to motive.

This is why expiration should never be praised merely because it cleans up old state.

Old state belongs to someone.

Deleting it transfers value.

Suppose an airline issues a travel credit after a passenger cancels a paid ticket. If the credit expires before the passenger can reasonably use it, the airline may retain value without transporting the traveler. The company can argue that open-ended credits create accounting, pricing, and capacity-management problems. The passenger can argue that the cash was real and should not evaporate because travel was inconvenient during the specified period.

Neither side is talking about time in the abstract.

They are negotiating who bears the cost of delay.

Expiration allocates that cost.

The same happens with unused vacation days, commuter benefits, flexible-spending accounts, promotional credits, data allowances, mobile minutes, and loyalty points. “Use it or lose it” is an expiration design attached to stored capacity or value.

Sometimes the rule serves a legitimate operational purpose.

Vacation is a good example. An employer may want workers to actually take time off rather than accumulate enormous liabilities indefinitely. A rule that limits carryover can encourage rest and keep accounting manageable. Yet a strict year-end forfeiture can punish employees whose managers made it difficult to take leave or whose workload prevented use.

The expiration has a behavioral objective.

It can still distribute the cost unfairly.

“Use it or lose it” works by making loss credible.

That makes expiration a tool for creating urgency.

Retail promotions have understood this forever. Sale ends Sunday. Coupon expires tonight. Points expire this month. Limited-time credit. Early-bird price until Friday.

The consumer is being told that future delay has a cost.

The urgency can solve procrastination. It can also manufacture it.

A shopper who would not buy today may buy because the option to wait is deliberately being removed. The seller is using expiration not to keep information fresh or authority bounded, but to change the timing of demand.

This is a different branch of the expiration principle.

The date is persuasive architecture.

The technique works because people value avoiding losses and because an open-ended choice is easy to postpone. A deadline converts a vague future intention into a present decision.

That can be helpful. People use self-imposed deadlines for the same reason.

It can also be manipulative when the deadline is fake, repeatedly extended, or designed to exploit confusion. An online store that claims a deal expires in six minutes and silently resets the timer for every new visitor is not using expiration as a real boundary. It is using the appearance of expiration to induce urgency.

A false deadline is an impersonation of scarcity.

This is why real expiration creates trust when the rule is stable.

If an option contract says it expires Friday, the exchange does not normally reset the date because the buyer hesitated. If a patent term ends, the owner cannot simply announce a new private term against the world. If a public tender closes, late bids are not ordinarily accepted merely because the bidder regrets waiting. The credibility of the deadline comes from the fact that it binds the designer too.

Promotional expiration is weakest when only the consumer is bound.

The company can extend the offer whenever it wishes.

The customer cannot extend the credit.

Asymmetry turns the clock into leverage.

Gift-card law pushes in the opposite direction by limiting the issuer’s ability to make paid value disappear quickly. It recognizes that the consumer already performed the difficult side of the exchange: the money was paid.

The five-year federal floor is not eternity. It still treats administration as having limits. Cards can be replaced. issuers can change systems. State law can add protections. Companies can fail. Unclaimed-property rules can become relevant. No consumer promise is literally immortal.

But the law shifts the default toward preserving purchased value.

That is what expiration policy often does at its best.

It chooses which party should bear the burden of attention.

For a short-lived security token, the holder bears the burden of getting renewed authority because stale permission is dangerous.

For a paid gift card, the issuer bears more of the burden of keeping the promise redeemable because disappearing value is unfair.

For a food-quality date, the consumer bears more judgment after the producer’s quality window ends.

For a medical product, the user is told not to rely on old stability evidence.

The right direction depends on the underlying relationship.

This is why “everything should expire” is such a bad principle.

Some states become dangerous when they persist.

Other states become predatory when they disappear.

A good system needs to know which kind it is managing.

Money is especially revealing because value leaves a trace.

If you pay one hundred dollars for something and receive nothing yet, the seller has an obligation that is easy to describe. Expiring that obligation transfers wealth.

Other forms of expiration hide the transfer.

A lapsed permit transfers freedom from the holder back to the regulator. An expired lease transfers control of property back toward the owner. An expired patent transfers freedom to practice the claimed invention outward. An expired data-retention period transfers control from the collector toward the subject by requiring deletion or anonymization, at least in theory.

Every expiry changes a balance sheet of rights.

The balance sheet may not be denominated in dollars.

Gift cards simply make it impossible to miss.

The phrase “money that vanishes” therefore contains the wrong subject.

Money does not vanish.

The claim to it does.

The issuer’s liability falls. The consumer’s asset disappears or becomes harder to use. Value moves through a legal rule disguised as a date.

Once you see that, expiration stops looking like housekeeping.

It becomes distribution.

This is a useful lens for the next chapter, where the expiring thing is not stored value but eligibility for public help.

A benefits recipient who misses a renewal deadline may remain just as poor, disabled, unemployed, elderly, or medically vulnerable as the day before.

The underlying need does not vanish.

The administrative claim can.

That is a far more consequential kind of breakage.
