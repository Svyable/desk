# The Archive That Must Not Expire

An expiration principle that cannot defend permanence is only a fashion for deletion.

The strongest case against this book begins in an archive.

A file reaches the end of its agency usefulness. The program that created it has closed. The official who signed it retired. The dispute it concerned ended decades ago. The storage system is expensive. Nobody inside the originating office expects to open the record again.

By the logic of ordinary data minimization, the file looks old.

By the logic of history, it may be just becoming valuable.

The United States National Archives and Records Administration formalizes this distinction through records schedules. Federal records can be designated temporary or permanent. Temporary records may eventually be destroyed according to approved disposition instructions. Permanent records have enough historical, evidential, informational, legal, or other value to warrant preservation and transfer to the National Archives.

The label “permanent” is deliberate.

NARA’s guidance warns that a merely lengthy retention period is not the same thing.

Some records are meant to outlive the business process that created them.

This is the inverse of almost every system we have examined so far.

A short-lived security token loses value as it ages because the old permission can become dangerous.

A historical record can gain value as it ages because temporal distance reveals consequences no one could see at creation.

The same document that was mundane administrative evidence in 1970 can become irreplaceable evidence of how government worked in 2070.

Age is not always staleness.

Sometimes age is provenance.

This is why the language of “data hygiene” can become reckless when applied to archives. Clean databases remove duplication, obsolete fields, dead accounts, stale permissions, and unnecessary records. A historian often wants exactly the mess the cleaner would remove: drafts, contradictions, abandoned plans, marginal notes, procedural debris, records showing what people believed before the final decision made everything look inevitable.

Efficient organizations prefer final versions.

Accountability often lives in the versions before final.

The same conflict appears in science.

A dataset collected for one research question can become valuable decades later when new methods, new theories, or new comparison data exist. Astronomical observations, climate measurements, genomic samples, epidemiological records, ecological surveys, and long-term cohort studies gain power from continuity across time.

Destroying the raw material because the original grant ended can make future science impossible.

The problem is especially severe for observations of events that cannot be repeated.

A hurricane passes once.

A species goes extinct once.

A glacier retreats through a particular state once.

A pandemic unfolds in one historical sequence.

An election, war, migration, industrial accident, market crash, or government decision creates a record that no future researcher can regenerate by asking the world again.

The source has expired.

The record must not.

This is where the DNS analogy fails completely. A resolver can drop a cached answer because an authoritative server still exists. History has no authoritative server.

Once the evidence is gone, the query cannot refresh it.

That gives archival memory a different moral status.

Permanent preservation is not a refusal to clean up. It is recognition that some knowledge is nonrenewable.

This distinction should shape every retention policy.

Before deleting, ask whether the source can be reconstructed.

If yes, expiration is easier to justify.

If no, the burden for destruction should be higher.

A short-lived access token can be recreated after fresh authentication.

A customer’s old session can be discarded.

A cache can be refilled.

A historical witness cannot be resurrected to repeat testimony after the only recording is deleted.

Reversibility changes the value of expiration.

This is true outside archives.

A temporary discount can expire because a seller can create another offer later.

A conservation protection can be harder to let expire because ecological damage during the gap may not be reversible.

A medical credential can be reinstated after paperwork is corrected.

A missed dose of a critical medicine cannot always be undone later.

A deleted customer profile may be recreated.

A deleted record of misconduct may leave victims unable to prove a pattern.

Expiry is safest where mistakes are recoverable.

This brings us to Antarctica.

In 2016, Stanford researchers described a debate within the international body responsible for conserving Antarctic marine living resources. Some member states were pressing for proposed marine protected areas to include sunset clauses of roughly twenty to thirty years. The researchers argued that such limits sat awkwardly with the ecological time scales involved; one noted that twenty years was shorter than the lifespan of many Antarctic predators the protections were intended to help.

The argument is a powerful challenge to the instinct for periodic reauthorization.

A twenty-year sunset sounds long in politics.

It can be short in ecology.

The ecosystem does not organize itself around human committee cycles. A whale, seabird population, deep-sea coral, or slow-changing food web can require protection across periods much longer than the officials negotiating the rule expect to remain in office.

A sunset clause shifts the future burden of proof.

If protection expires automatically, conservation supporters must rebuild political agreement later. If protection is permanent unless affirmatively changed, extractive interests must make the case for reopening.

Which default is more appropriate depends on what happens if government guesses wrong.

If a temporary restriction persists too long, some fishing opportunity may be lost.

If protection disappears too soon and a vulnerable ecosystem is damaged, the loss can be irreversible on human time scales.

The asymmetry argues for continuity.

This is the same logic that can justify conservative safety limits in medicine. When the cost of a false continuation and the cost of a false termination are radically different, the expiration default should reflect the harder-to-repair mistake.

Permanence is not always institutional laziness.

It can be a hedge against irreversibility.

Constitutional rights offer an even more important case.

Imagine a bill of rights with a twenty-year sunset. Every generation would be required to reenact freedom of speech, due process, equal protection, protection against unreasonable searches, or other foundational constraints on state power.

The arrangement would satisfy the strongest version of the expiration principle. No generation could rely forever on the political judgment of the past.

It would also make basic liberty contingent on recurring political majorities.

That is exactly what constitutional entrenchment is meant to resist.

Some rules are made difficult to change because their purpose is to protect people during the moments when change would be easiest and most dangerous.

Permanence can bind future power in favor of durable principle.

This is the mirror image of a sunset on emergency authority.

Emergency power is dangerous when temporary panic becomes permanent permission.

Constitutional protection is dangerous when temporary panic can make permanent rights disappear.

The correct direction of inertia depends on what is being preserved.

This is why no general rule can say “power should expire.”

Some power should.

Some restraints on power should not.

The distinction matters in contracts too. Ownership of a purchased object is not usually designed as a renewable annual license from the seller. The consumer pays and acquires durable rights. Converting every purchase into a subscription would make the seller the continuing gatekeeper of the buyer’s use.

Software has made that conversion increasingly possible.

A physical machine can depend on cloud authentication. Features can be remotely enabled or disabled. Media can be licensed rather than owned. A device bought outright can lose functionality when servers shut down. The object remains in the home while the permission to use part of it expires elsewhere.

The technology can turn permanence into rent.

Sometimes that enables useful service, updates, financing, or safety controls. It can also transfer long-term power from owner to vendor.

The archive chapter therefore has a broader purpose than defending old documents.

It defends the social value of irreversible grants.

Some commitments need to be reliable precisely because one party should not have to keep asking.

A property title, court judgment, citizenship status, pension right, academic degree, historical record, or conservation commitment can derive value from being difficult to unsettle.

Renewal would not make them fresher.

It would make them more contingent.

This is the same insight that ended routine password expiration as a universal best practice. Movement is not improvement.

The archive adds another reason.

Sometimes the cost of erroneous termination is permanent.

That should make us wary of automated deletion systems whose elegance exceeds our understanding of future value.

A retention rule is written by people who know present purposes. Archives exist for people who will ask questions we cannot predict.

This creates a genuine conflict with privacy law’s suspicion of keeping personal data “just in case.”

The phrase can justify almost unlimited hoarding.

Every company can invent a hypothetical future use.

Every agency can claim unknown historical value.

If possibility alone defeats deletion, storage limitation collapses.

The solution is not to choose privacy or history once for all data.

It is to separate commercial optionality from public-interest memory.

A retailer wanting to keep every customer click forever because future marketing models might find it useful is different from a public archive preserving records of a major government decision. A police agency retaining broad personal data for indefinite speculative use is different from an archival institution preserving a carefully selected historical record under access safeguards.

Purpose, governance, access, and power matter as much as retention duration.

The same information can become less dangerous when moved from an operational database into a controlled archive.

Operational data acts on people.

Archival data documents what acted on people.

The boundary is not perfect. Archives can expose sensitive information. Historical records can harm living people. Access restrictions, closure periods, redaction, declassification rules, privacy protections, and ethical research practices exist because permanent preservation does not mean immediate universal publication.

Again, the layers can have different clocks.

Preserve permanently.

Restrict access for a period.

Release later.

An archival system can protect memory and privacy by separating existence from availability.

This is more sophisticated than deletion because it admits that the future may value the record while the present still needs safeguards.

The same pattern can apply to corporate and scientific records. Cold storage, legal holds, limited access, anonymization, and controlled research environments can preserve selected evidence without leaving it in everyday operational circulation.

Expiration can act on access before it acts on existence.

That is a crucial design option.

We tend to ask whether data should be kept or deleted.

The better question may be who should be allowed to use it, for what, and for how long before another level of permission is required.

The archive itself can remain.

The authority to exploit it can expire aggressively.

This distinction will become increasingly important as artificial intelligence makes old archives easier to search and activate. Permanent preservation need not imply permanent unrestricted machine access.

History should survive.

Permissions can still rotate.

The expiration principle therefore arrives at its boundary not as defeat but as refinement.

Some things should end because persistence is dangerous.

Some things should persist because ending is dangerous.

The decision depends partly on reversibility, partly on power, partly on purpose, and partly on the time scale of the thing being protected.

A twenty-year sunset can be a lifetime in software.

It can be a moment in an ecosystem.

A ten-year document can be sensible evidence refresh.

A ten-year right to basic status can be intolerable precarity.

A thirty-day data retention period can be privacy protection.

A thirty-day archive can be historical amnesia.

The calendar does not know the difference.

We have to.
