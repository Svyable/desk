# The Right to Be Asked Again

Consent is often treated as a receipt.

The organization asks once.

The person clicks yes.

The receipt is stored.

Years later, somebody opens the database and finds the old approval. The record is clean. The timestamp is precise. The language may even have been legally sufficient when it was collected.

The institution concludes that permission exists.

The person may not remember giving it.

This is the consent version of stale authority.

The problem is not that old consent is automatically invalid. Some permissions are meant to endure. A patient may authorize a durable arrangement. A customer may choose an ongoing service. A person can make a long-term contractual commitment. Requiring fresh consent every morning would turn autonomy into harassment.

The problem is that the object of consent can change while the stored yes remains identical.

The company changes ownership.

The data is combined with new sources.

A harmless analytics system becomes an AI training corpus.

A wearable collects new categories of information.

A child's account becomes an adult's account.

A private community becomes searchable.

A research project discovers a new use for old samples.

A trusted assistant becomes an autonomous agent capable of acting without supervision.

The original yes persists.

The meaning of yes has drifted.

This is why consent needs a theory of expiration.

Not every consent needs a date.

Every consent needs a boundary.

Sometimes the boundary is time.

Sometimes purpose.

Sometimes context.

Sometimes a material change in risk.

Sometimes a change in who controls the system.

The institution should know what event makes the old permission insufficient.

We already accept this intuitively in ordinary life.

If a friend says, “You can borrow my car this afternoon,” nobody treats the sentence as permanent vehicle authorization. The purpose and time are embedded in the social context.

If a colleague says, “Yes, send that draft to the team,” the approval does not authorize publishing every future draft to the internet.

If a patient agrees to one procedure, the consent does not become a general medical power over unrelated interventions.

Human consent is scoped even when the scope is not written in database fields.

Digital systems prefer durable booleans.

`consent = true`

The boolean is administratively beautiful and philosophically disastrous.

A better representation would ask: consent to what, by whom, for whom, under which conditions, until what changes?

This sounds cumbersome because it is more information than a checkbox.

The checkbox was hiding the complexity rather than eliminating it.

Expiration can help by forcing the system to confront change.

Consider a service that stores intimate voice recordings to provide transcription. The user consented when recordings were processed only to return transcripts and then deleted after thirty days. Years later, the company develops a model that can infer emotion, health signals, identity, relationships, and behavioral patterns from voice. It wants to use the old archive to train the model.

The data is the same audio.

The capability surrounding the data is not.

Should the original consent automatically authorize the new use?

A legal answer depends on jurisdiction, contract, notice, purpose, and many other facts. The institutional-design answer is clearer.

When technological change materially alters what information can reveal or what a system can do with it, the old permission deserves re-examination.

Capability growth can expire consent.

This is a profound shift because traditional consent systems assume the permission ages while the object remains relatively stable.

AI reverses that assumption. The same stored dataset can become more revealing over time because analysis improves. The same connected account can become more consequential because the assistant gains new actions. The same camera can become more invasive because recognition software improves.

The object of consent evolves after collection.

A timestamp alone cannot protect autonomy.

The system needs change detection.

This resembles event-driven credential renewal. Do not ask for a new password merely because ninety days passed. Ask for fresh authorization when something important changed.

The same principle can reduce consent fatigue.

People are already drowning in requests to accept terms, cookies, privacy notices, permissions, updates, and policies. If every consent simply expired every thirty days, users would click through even faster. The renewal would create no information.

A meaningful consent system should ask again less often and at better moments.

The camera is about to be used for facial recognition for the first time.

The assistant is about to gain purchasing authority.

The health dataset is about to be shared with a new category of recipient.

The research project is moving from aggregate analysis to individual recontact.

The child's account is reaching an age where the person can make decisions independently.

The organization is changing the purpose in a way a reasonable person would experience as materially different.

Those are renewal events.

The clock can remain as a backstop where change is hard to detect, but the event carries the information.

This suggests a right that digital systems rarely name explicitly: the right to be asked again when the question changes.

The phrase matters because consent is often framed as the user's burden.

You agreed.

You clicked.

You should have read.

The receipt becomes a defense against future objection.

A right to be asked again shifts some burden back to the institution. If the institution materially changes the proposition, it cannot rely forever on the fact that the person once answered a narrower question.

This is not a right to rewrite history.

The original consent happened.

The institution may have lawfully acted on it.

The right concerns continuation under changed conditions.

This distinction mirrors the entire book.

Expiration does not say the old decision was wrong.

It says the old decision has reached the end of the period or context in which it deserves to control the future without another look.

Consent is especially sensitive because the person can change too.

Preferences change.

Relationships change.

Risk tolerance changes.

A teenager becomes an adult.

A patient receives a diagnosis that changes how health data feels.

A person leaves an abusive relationship and suddenly old location-sharing permissions become dangerous.

A public figure becomes private.

A private person becomes public.

A user who once wanted persistent memory may later value forgetting.

The institution cannot continuously predict these changes.

That is why revocation matters as much as expiration.

A good consent system gives the person a practical way to end permission before any scheduled review.

The existence of an expiry date is not an excuse for making revocation difficult.

A five-year consent that can be revoked in seconds may protect autonomy better than a one-year consent buried behind customer support.

Again, duration is not the whole design.

Exit quality matters.

The strongest consent systems may combine several layers:

A durable relationship-level permission for the basic service.

Shorter-lived permissions for sensitive capabilities.

One-time confirmation for unusually consequential actions.

Event-driven reconsent after material changes.

Immediate revocation where feasible.

Records of what proposition the person actually saw.

The structure resembles modern security because consent and security are both authorization problems.

The difference is that consent protects agency rather than merely systems.

That difference should make designers more careful, not less.

There is a temptation to automate consent renewal through inferred behavior.

The user kept using the service, therefore the user accepted the new terms.

The person did not turn off the feature, therefore consent continues.

The customer opened the email, therefore notice was sufficient.

In some contractual contexts, continued use can have legal significance. But as a general design principle, inferred renewal is dangerous when the institution controls the environment in which the inference occurs.

A person may continue because leaving is costly.

A worker may continue because the employer requires the tool.

A patient may continue because alternatives are unavailable.

A tenant may continue because moving is expensive.

A user may never have noticed the change.

Behavior under dependence is weak evidence of enthusiastic renewal.

This returns us to the rented life.

Temporary systems can preserve choice only when the person can realistically refuse.

Consent expiration without a viable refusal path is theater.

The system asks again and receives the only answer the person can afford to give.

That does not mean the question is useless. Notice can matter even when alternatives are limited. But the institution should not confuse procedural assent with substantive freedom.

The right to be asked again is therefore only one piece of autonomy.

The right to say no matters.

The right to leave matters.

The right to preserve already-earned status matters.

The right to challenge an automated decision matters.

Expiration can create moments for those rights to operate. It cannot manufacture them by itself.

There is another reason to expire some consents: future selves deserve representation.

A person can make a decision today that affects the informational environment of the person they will become in twenty years. The current self may be comfortable with a company preserving intimate data indefinitely. The future self may live under different politics, technology, family circumstances, health conditions, or social norms.

We allow people to make long-term commitments because autonomy includes the freedom to bind oneself.

We also limit some commitments because the future matters.

Bankruptcy law can discharge debts. Employment law limits certain waivers. Family law restricts some private agreements. Consumer law can make some rights nonwaivable. Legal systems routinely decide that not every present yes should dominate the future forever.

Consent design belongs in the same family of questions.

How much authority should the present self have over the future self?

There is no universal answer.

A tattoo is deliberately durable.

A marriage is intended to be serious but can end.

An organ donation can be irrevocable once performed because the act cannot be reversed.

A marketing permission has little claim to eternity.

A scientific contribution may be intended to support research for decades, but governance can still constrain future uses.

The nature of the act determines how much expiration makes sense.

Digital services have often chosen indefinite consent not because permanence is philosophically justified but because databases make it convenient.

Store the flag.

Never ask again.

The expiration principle exposes that convenience as a design choice.

If the company can remember the permission forever, it can also remember the conditions under which the permission was given.

If it can detect a material product change, it can decide whether the old consent covers the new capability.

If it can personalize advertising in real time, it can probably surface a meaningful reauthorization at the moment it matters.

Technical sophistication should not be available only for persuasion.

It can be used for restraint.

This becomes critical for agents because an agent can act on old consent rather than merely process old data.

A user once authorized an assistant to book restaurants.

Later the assistant gains the ability to spend from a connected bank account, invite contacts, negotiate prices, and sign agreements. The historical instruction “handle my reservations” cannot simply expand with the product's capabilities.

Authority should not inflate automatically when capability inflates.

New power requires a new boundary.

The safest default is that permission attaches to the capability set the person reasonably authorized, not every future capability that can be described with the same verb.

“Book” once meant reserve a table.

Tomorrow it may mean commit funds, accept cancellation terms, share dietary information, coordinate transportation, and message guests.

The word stayed the same.

The action surface grew.

Ask again.

Not constantly.

Not ritualistically.

When the question changed.

That is the difference between consent as a receipt and consent as a living boundary.
