# Chapter 10 — Blood on the Night Shift

Romanian physicians who later described the late communist period often returned to blood: women arriving at night, severe hemorrhage, sepsis, fear, police questioning. Such testimony is vivid and therefore dangerous for a historian. Memory can compress years into one scene. The scene can become representative because it is unforgettable.

The statistical record gives those memories a harder frame. Research on Romanian maternal mortality shows abortion-related deaths rising to dominate maternal mortality by the end of the 1980s, while mortality from other obstetric causes did not follow the same path. By 1989, abortion-related deaths accounted for the great majority of maternal deaths.

That divergence is one of the strongest causal signals in the book. If the country's general maternity system alone were deteriorating uniformly, one would expect broader obstetric mortality to move in tandem. Instead, the component linked to unsafe abortion behaved differently.

Numbers still require caution. Classification under a punitive system can be distorted. Families and clinicians had incentives to conceal abortion. Some deaths may have been miscoded; some suspected abortions may have been misclassified. The safest claim is therefore directional and comparative: restrictive policy coincided with extraordinarily high abortion-related maternal mortality, and the burden fell rapidly after legal access returned.

The human mechanism is easy to understand. Criminalization pushes procedures later, farther from formal care, or into methods with greater complication risk. Fear can delay treatment. Incriminating information can be withheld. The same medical system that might prevent death becomes entangled with enforcement.

The night shift is where demographic policy turns back into anatomy.
