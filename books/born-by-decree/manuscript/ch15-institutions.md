# Chapter 15 — Institutions

When Western cameras entered Romanian child institutions after 1989, the images were devastating. Children were found in conditions of severe neglect, deprivation and disability. It is tempting to draw a straight line from Decree 770 to every bed.

The line is real but not sufficient.

Pronatalism increased births, including births in families unable or unwilling to care for additional children. Restrictive reproductive policy therefore plausibly increased pressure on abandonment and institutional care. But Romanian institutions also reflected poverty, disability policy, medical classification, social stigma, chronic underfunding and a state model that separated children labeled unrecoverable from ordinary family life.

Causal discipline matters here because horror encourages monocausal stories. If Decree 770 becomes the sole explanation, the specific institutions and decisions that produced neglect disappear from view.

Romanian investigations into communist-era child homes have documented deaths, malnutrition and systemic abuse. Those records deserve their own causal chain: admission rules, disability categories, staffing, budgets, medical care, food, heating, mortality reporting and accountability.

The reproductive regime belongs upstream, not everywhere.

This distinction strengthens rather than weakens the indictment. A state can create one harmful policy and then meet its downstream consequences with other harmful institutions. The archive should be allowed to show each mechanism separately.
