# Chapter 9 — The Informal Clinic

Once safe legal abortion narrows while demand persists, expertise does not disappear. It migrates.

The Romanian shadow system included people with very different levels of skill: physicians operating outside authorization, nurses, intermediaries, acquaintances, self-induced methods and improvised techniques. It is misleading to treat all illegal abortion as medically identical. Some clandestine procedures were likely competent but legally risky. Others were catastrophically unsafe.

The state saw only fragments. A successful illegal abortion could leave no official trace. A failed one might appear as hemorrhage, infection, uterine injury, infertility or death. Police and medical records therefore overrepresent failure by design. That does not reduce the seriousness of the mortality; it means the denominator is difficult to reconstruct.

The informal clinic also had its own price system. Scarcity creates rents. A provider willing to take legal risk can charge for access. A patient with money may buy safer expertise. A patient without money may substitute physical danger for financial cost.

This makes coercive reproductive policy regressive even when the statute is formally universal. The law may apply equally while the ability to evade its worst consequences does not.

Romania's experience is unusually valuable because the post-1989 reversal was abrupt. Legal abortion returned within days of the regime's collapse, and abortion-related maternal mortality then fell dramatically over the following years. That before-and-after pattern does not answer every causal question, but it sharply weakens explanations that treat Romanian maternal mortality as primarily a fixed feature of medical capacity or culture.

The informal clinic was not outside the state. It was produced by the state-defined boundary between acceptable and unacceptable reproductive medicine.
