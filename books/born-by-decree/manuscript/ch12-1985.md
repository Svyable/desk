# Chapter 12 — 1985

On December 26, 1985, Decree 411 tightened the gate again. The age threshold returned to over forty-five. The parity exception moved from women who had borne four children to women who had borne five.

By then the early baby-boom effect was long gone. Fertility had fallen despite nearly two decades of restriction. The regime's response was not to infer that families preferred fewer children. It narrowed legal escape.

This is what happens when a metric becomes politically sacred. Disappointing results can be interpreted not as evidence against the policy but as evidence that implementation is too weak. The target protects the theory from falsification.

The 1985 measure also came during Romania's severe economic austerity. Families were being asked to raise more children in a period of shortages and declining living standards. Pronatalism therefore collided with household economics. The state could prohibit abortion more easily than it could make another child affordable.

The gap between national demographic ambition and family-level constraint widened. That gap was filled by risk.

The 1985 decree is an important counterweight to narratives that reduce late Ceaușescu pronatalism to propaganda about motherhood. The machinery remained legal and specific. Age forty-four meant something different from age forty-six. Four children meant something different from five. Bureaucracy translated ideology into thresholds.

And thresholds create cliff edges in human lives.
