# Chapter 18 — Reproductive States

Romania's history is often summoned in present political arguments as a warning. Warnings are useful only when they are specific.

Decree 770 was not simply any restriction on abortion. It was a twenty-three-year authoritarian pronatalist system with narrow legal exceptions, criminal sanctions, weak contraceptive access, medical authorization, prosecutor involvement, later tightening, and extensive state capacity to pressure institutions and individuals. Comparisons that ignore those features turn history into a slogan.

The case nevertheless reveals mechanisms that travel. Restricting a demanded medical service does not eliminate demand. It changes where the service occurs, who can obtain it safely, what information patients disclose, and what risks clinicians face. If the policy's objective is demographic rather than merely legal, officials also acquire incentives to monitor pregnancies and outcomes more closely.

That is the book's central claim. Coercive pronatalism is an information state. It must know who is pregnant, who qualifies for exception, which doctors are helping, where pregnancies disappear, and why births are still below target. Every adaptation creates a new blind spot. Every blind spot invites another sensor.

Romania also shows that this machinery can achieve real short-run outcomes. The baby boom was not propaganda. That is precisely why the case matters. Harmful policy can work on its chosen metric before failing on the larger human system.

The most important question is therefore not whether the state can move the birth rate. It can.

The question is what it has to become in order to keep moving it.
