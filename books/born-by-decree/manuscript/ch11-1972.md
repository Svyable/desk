# Chapter 11 — 1972

In February 1972 the regime lowered the age exception for abortion from over forty-five to over forty. The amendment was small on paper and large as evidence.

A rigid ideological story would predict one direction only: ever tighter control. The 1972 change shows policy responding to consequences and pressures. Older pregnancies carry different medical risks. A growing number of births among older women also had implications for congenital anomalies and institutional care. Romanian later reporting connects the change to the burdens produced by the earlier threshold.

The amendment did not renounce pronatalism. It recalibrated it. That distinction matters because authoritarian policy is often more adaptive than retrospective accounts allow. A state can modify a system to preserve it.

The most useful way to read 1972 is therefore not as mercy or retreat, but as feedback. The regime had learned something about the cost structure of the 1966 rule. It reopened one category while keeping the architecture intact: prohibition remained the default; commissions remained; criminal sanctions remained.

This is also a warning against treating Decree 770 as one immutable law from 1966 to 1989. The system had versions. A woman's legal options depended on the year, her age, her number of children, her diagnosis, and the discretion of institutions around her.

Any serious reconstruction must therefore be time-indexed. Stories from 1968 cannot automatically describe 1987. The policy hardened and softened at different points. Its continuity lay not in one threshold but in the principle that reproductive permission belonged to the state.
