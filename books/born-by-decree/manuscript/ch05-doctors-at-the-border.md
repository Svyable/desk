# Chapter 5 — Doctors at the Border

A doctor under Decree 770 occupied a border between two obligations. One was clinical: treat the patient in front of you. The other was legal: do not facilitate an unauthorized abortion, and document interventions that might later require justification.

The original decree made this tension explicit by routing authorization through commissions and requiring emergency notification to prosecutors. Later enforcement practices, described in Romanian historical reporting and scholarship, could place police and investigators near the bedside when clandestine abortion was suspected.

The point is not that every Romanian physician became an enthusiastic enforcer. The archival and testimonial record contains variation: compliance, fear, circumvention, bribery, informal help, rigid adherence and moral disagreement. A system does not require uniform enthusiasm. It requires enough enforceable risk that professional judgment changes.

This is where reproductive policing damages information. A patient who expects questioning may delay seeking care. A physician who fears prosecution may hesitate before acting or record events strategically. Families conceal methods and helpers. The state then receives worse medical information precisely because it has made that information dangerous.

The feedback is perverse. Poor information can be interpreted as evidence that illegal abortion is under control. Rising emergency complications can be interpreted as evidence that enforcement is not strict enough. Either reading can justify more intrusion.

Romanian accounts of women arriving at hospitals after unsafe procedures often describe a terrifying sequence: hemorrhage, delay, questioning, treatment. Individual memories must be handled carefully, but the structure that made such scenes possible is documented in law. The prosecutor was not an accidental visitor to reproductive medicine. The legal system had been designed to know.

The deeper story is not medicine captured by politics in one dramatic moment. It is a gradual change in what a clinical fact meant. A pregnancy, a miscarriage, a fever, a hemorrhage, a missing fetal heartbeat: each could become medically urgent and administratively suspicious at once.
