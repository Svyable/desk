# Chapter 14 — Children of the Decree

The children born after 1966 eventually acquired a name: decreței. The word is useful and dangerous.

Useful, because the cohort bulge is visible and the policy genuinely altered who was born and when. Dangerous, because a demographic cohort is not a uniform psychological category. Being born in 1967 does not reveal whether one's parents wanted the pregnancy, feared the law, used no contraception, welcomed a larger family, or never considered abortion.

The decree's effects followed the cohort into institutions. Schools absorbed unusually large classes. Later labor markets encountered a wave of similarly aged entrants. Researchers have studied long-run educational, labor and health effects of the cohort discontinuity, using the sharp policy shock as a natural experiment.

Those studies are powerful precisely because the law changed cohort size so abruptly. They are also limited. A policy that changes fertility composition may alter which parents have children, birth order, maternal age and household resources at the same time. A simple comparison of people born just before and after the decree can therefore bundle several mechanisms.

The label decreței should remain a historical clue, not a destiny.

The strongest claim is structural: the state created a cohort large enough to leave measurable traces decades later. That is an extraordinary demonstration of how reproductive policy can alter a country's demographic geometry long after the politicians who designed it are gone.
