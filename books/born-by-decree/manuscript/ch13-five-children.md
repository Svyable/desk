# Chapter 13 — Five Children

The difference between four children and five can look trivial in a policy table. Inside a household it is enormous.

By raising the parity exception, the 1985 decree told a woman with four children that her existing reproductive burden was insufficient to qualify her for legal termination on that ground. The state had converted family size into an eligibility credential and then changed the credential.

This exposes the arithmetic at the heart of coercive pronatalism. Individual children become both people and units toward a threshold. The law does not erase love or family meaning; it overlays them with a state count.

Household decisions, however, are not made in national averages. Another pregnancy changes food, housing, work, childcare, health risk and time. These burdens vary sharply by income and support. A uniform parity rule therefore distributes pressure unevenly.

The poorest families face the hardest trade-offs while often having the least access to safer clandestine alternatives. That inequality complicates any simple account of compliance. High-parity births can reflect desire, acceptance, lack of alternatives, failed contraception, fear of illegal abortion, or combinations of all five.

The archive rarely gives a clean motive for each birth. The ethical temptation is to fill the gap with certainty. This book will not. What the law can establish is the choice architecture the state imposed.

Five children was not a demographic abstraction. It was a legal number attached to a woman's ability to end a pregnancy.
