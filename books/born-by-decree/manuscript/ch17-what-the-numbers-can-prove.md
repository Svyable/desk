# Chapter 17 — What the Numbers Can Prove

Romania's reproductive history invites big numbers: births doubled, ten thousand women died, one hundred thousand children were institutionalized. Some are robust at one level and fragile at another.

A responsible book separates stocks, flows and estimates. Maternal deaths can be counted from vital statistics but classification may be imperfect. Illegal abortions are intrinsically undercounted. Institutional populations vary by year and by definition. A cumulative estimate of deaths across decades is not interchangeable with a single-year mortality rate.

The strongest statistical pattern is not one dramatic total. It is the divergence between abortion-related and other maternal mortality during restriction, followed by the sharp fall in abortion-related mortality after legalization. That time series carries more causal information than a memorable rounded number.

The fertility series tells a parallel story. There is a large immediate increase after 1966, then substantial decline despite continued legal restriction. This is evidence of both policy power and policy limits.

The four evidence classes protect the argument from its own strongest temptations. Class 1 includes the decree texts, threshold changes, repeal, aggregate fertility shock and well-documented mortality pattern. Class 2 includes testimony and reconstructed enforcement practices that are credible but not fully quantified. Class 3 contains popular claims whose numbers or causal chains cannot yet be secured. Class 4 contains the book's broader theory: reproductive coercion becomes an information problem because people adapt.

The purpose of uncertainty is not to make the history less severe. It is to make the severity harder to dismiss.
