# Chapter 7 — The Baby Boom

Decree 770 was published on October 1, 1966, and took effect thirty days later. The demographic response arrived almost immediately in the annual series. Romania's crude birth rate rose from 14.2 births per thousand people in 1966 to 27.0 in 1967. The total fertility rate moved from 1.90 births per woman to 3.66. In a country whose fertility had been falling for years, the line on the graph nearly doubled in a single step.

That is why 1967 mattered so much politically. The regime had changed the legal conditions of pregnancy and, within the first full calendar year, obtained a result large enough to be visible without statistical subtlety. The surge was not an artifact of one measure. The crude birth rate stayed at 26.6 in 1968, while total fertility remained at 3.63. Whatever else Decree 770 became, its immediate demographic effect was real.

The seduction is to stop there. A before-and-after comparison can show that the intervention moved behavior without showing what kind of behavior it moved, how durable the change would be, or what it cost. Total fertility rate is a period measure built from age-specific birth rates; it is not a count of the children an actual woman will eventually have. The 1967 jump therefore cannot, by itself, tell us how much consisted of births brought forward, pregnancies carried because abortion was no longer legally available, changes in marriage or conception timing, or durable increases in completed family size.

The following years make the distinction unavoidable. The crude birth rate fell to 23.3 in 1969, 21.1 in 1970, 19.6 in 1971, and 18.2 by 1973. Total fertility moved with it: 3.19, 2.89, 2.67, then 2.44. The prohibition had not vanished. Neither had the commissions or the criminal law. What changed was the population living under them.

That decline is evidence of adaptation, but it should not be turned into the opposite fairy tale, in which private behavior simply defeated the state and restored the old trajectory. Even in 1973, total fertility remained well above the 1.90 recorded in 1966. Later statistical work on the Ceaușescu period finds a significant positive effect of pronatalist policy on fertility across 1967–1989. The longer record has to hold both facts at once: coercion changed reproductive outcomes, and the spectacular first-year effect eroded while coercion remained.

The curve does not tell us exactly how that erosion happened. Families and doctors learned the new system unevenly. Legal exceptions mattered. So did scarce contraception, altered timing, sympathetic physicians, informal networks, and unsafe abortion. Each route carried different risks and was distributed differently by money, geography, connections, and medical knowledge. Aggregate fertility records the result of those choices and constraints without identifying their proportions.

The 1967 cohort consequently became more than a demographic curiosity. It was unusually large because a policy had changed the reproductive environment abruptly enough to alter the age structure of the country. That larger cohort would move through maternity wards, schools, conscription, universities, workplaces, and housing markets on a schedule set partly by a decree. But cohort size is not biography. The later label decreței can name a historical generation without making every person born into it a copy of the policy that enlarged it.

The cleanest claim the numbers permit is narrower than either triumph or failure. Decree 770 could move fertility quickly and substantially. The same series shows people and institutions changing their behavior while the law stayed in force. After 1967, the demographic problem for the regime was no longer whether prohibition could move the line. It was what would be required to keep the line where the state wanted it.
