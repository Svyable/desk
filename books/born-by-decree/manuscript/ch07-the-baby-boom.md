# Chapter 7 — The Baby Boom

The first-year numbers looked like vindication. Births rose sharply after the ban. Total fertility jumped from roughly two children per woman to well above three in the immediate aftermath. The 1967 cohort became the emblematic generation of the decree.

For policy makers, this was the seductive moment. A legal change had produced a visible population response within months. Few government interventions offer such clean-looking before-and-after graphs.

But a graph can be causally strong and politically misleading at the same time. The decree plainly changed fertility behavior. What it did not prove was that families had adopted the state's desired family size. Later fertility decline under continued restriction shows that adaptation resumed.

The system thus generated a sequence common to many prohibitions: shock, compliance, learning, circumvention, enforcement escalation. The initial response is often the easiest to measure because substitutes have not yet matured. The later response is harder because behavior fragments into legal exceptions, informal markets, illegal practices and changed timing.

The baby boom also created downstream institutional demand. Larger cohorts required maternity capacity, nurseries, schools, classrooms, teachers and eventually jobs. The state had not merely changed the number of births in 1967. It had shifted the age structure of an entire generation.

The Romanian term decreței would later attach identity to those cohorts. Yet cohort identity can become too neat. People born after 1966 had different families, classes, regions and life trajectories. The label is historically meaningful because policy changed cohort size, not because every person carried the same experience.

The boom was real. So was its decay. The distance between those two facts is where the rest of the regime lived.
