# Publication Review — The Warning Problem

## Current state

The manuscript is complete at 18 chapters plus prologue and back matter. Its subject is high consequence: nuclear warning, command and control, false alarms, automation, AI-assisted analysis, cyber risk, and escalation. The release standard should therefore be stricter than ordinary trade nonfiction.

The central thesis is deliberately narrower than “AI will control nuclear weapons.” The book argues that AI can improve sensing, fusion, retrieval, and decision support while increasing risks of false confidence, automation bias, time compression, adversarial manipulation, and opaque uncertainty. Human authorization at the point of nuclear use does not eliminate those upstream risks.

## Evidence posture

The research package includes GAO audits of the 1979–1980 false-warning incidents, declassified Able Archer material, National Security Archive collections, U.S. posture documents, statutory human-action language, NTI/FAS/SIPRI research, entanglement scholarship, and public analysis of Chinese early-warning development.

Publication review should preserve a strict hierarchy:

- **Official audits, statutes, posture documents, and declassified records** are preferred for historical and institutional claims.
- **Open-source technical and policy analysis** is useful for architecture and risk interpretation but must not be presented as proof of classified system behavior.
- **Journalistic or memoir-like accounts** of famous incidents such as Petrov or Perimeter require special caution because simplified public narratives often outrun the documentary record.
- **Current China and Russia claims** must be dated, attributed, and qualified where public evidence is incomplete.
- **AI scenarios** must remain scenarios unless a public deployment is documented.

## Chapter review map

### Chapters 1–4 — the warning problem, false alarms, command system, sensors

The 1979 and 1980 NORAD false-warning episodes should remain anchored to GAO and declassified material. Preserve the difference between the November 1979 test-data incident and the 1980 hardware-related false warnings rather than combining them into one generic “computer error” story.

The command-system chapter should distinguish warning, assessment, authentication, decision support, communication, and execution. Avoid language that makes NC3 sound like a single machine or one continuous automated chain.

Sensor chapters should make clear that satellites and radar provide observations that still require interpretation. A sensor can accurately observe an event while analysts misclassify its meaning.

### Chapters 5–10 — fusion, confidence, cyber, spoofing, automation bias, human judgment

These chapters are most vulnerable to conceptual slippage. Data fusion can reduce some uncertainty while correlating or amplifying shared errors. A confidence score is not automatically a calibrated probability. Explain what any numerical confidence language means before using it rhetorically.

Cyber and spoofing chapters should not imply that an adversary must fully penetrate an NC3 network to create risk. Ambiguity about data integrity, communications reliability, or sensor trust can itself matter. At the same time, do not invent penetration pathways or imply public knowledge of classified vulnerabilities.

“Meaningful human judgment” must be defined institutionally. A human clicking approval after machine-generated analysis is not necessarily meaningful review; nor does human involvement guarantee wisdom. The manuscript should focus on time, independence, evidence access, challenge authority, and responsibility.

### Chapters 11–14 — shrinking clock, entanglement, China, Russia

Every claim about decision time should distinguish technical flight time, warning time, decision time, and perceived time pressure.

The entanglement chapter should rely on documented dual-use or ambiguous C3I relationships rather than implying that every conventional strike creates nuclear escalation. The mechanism is uncertainty about what the target supports and what the attacker intends.

China coverage should clearly attribute public assessments of launch-on-warning or early-warning-counterstrike developments. Avoid presenting contested interpretations as settled Chinese doctrine.

Russia coverage needs the same discipline. Treat Perimeter, continuity systems, escalation concepts, and historical warning behavior cautiously; do not repeat “automatic doomsday machine” shorthand.

### Chapters 15–18 — crisis communication, testing, designing for doubt, time to think

Risk-reduction proposals should be separated from existing agreements. Hotline use, incident notification, model testing, audit trails, red teaming, and decision-process design can be argued for without claiming governments have adopted them.

The final chapter should preserve the book’s key asymmetry: machines may accelerate detection and option generation faster than they reduce uncertainty about adversary intention. “Time to think” is therefore an institutional design objective, not nostalgia for slower technology.

## High-risk factual lanes

Before release, recheck:

- all current U.S., Chinese, and Russian posture or system-development claims;
- any description of launch-on-warning policy or operational readiness;
- all claims about Perimeter or automated retaliation;
- exact chronology and mechanics of the 1979–1980 NORAD incidents;
- Able Archer claims against the declassified record;
- any assertion about AI currently integrated into nuclear warning or command systems;
- statutory language governing human action in nuclear employment decisions.

## Claims requiring explicit labels

Use four categories where useful:

1. **Historical record** — documented event or official finding.
2. **Publicly described capability or policy** — official or attributable present-tense claim.
3. **Analytic inference** — the manuscript’s interpretation of risk or mechanism.
4. **Scenario / proposal** — hypothetical stress case or recommended design.

## Publication gates

Before any Shelf snapshot:

- conduct a claim-by-claim fact check of nuclear-history chapters;
- have every quotation tied to a primary or clearly identified secondary source;
- replace weak popular accounts with documentary evidence where available;
- revalidate 2025–2026 policy claims immediately before release;
- ensure no hypothetical cyber or AI pathway is written as a known operational fact;
- run Desk integrity and length checks on the reconciled branch;
- perform a terminology pass for NC2, NC3, warning, command, control, launch authority, and decision support so the manuscript does not use them interchangeably;
- preserve counterarguments showing where automation, redundancy, or improved sensing may increase stability rather than only risk.

## Release posture

The book has enough substance for a full editorial and fact-checking pass. It should not be released until the historical warning incidents and current nuclear-system claims have been checked with unusually high rigor. The manuscript’s credibility depends on disciplined uncertainty more than on dramatic certainty.
