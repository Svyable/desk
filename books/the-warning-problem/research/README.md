# Research Brief

## Thesis under test

The Warning Problem tests whether AI-enabled warning and decision-support tools can improve detection while simultaneously increasing nuclear risk through automation bias, opaque confidence, cyber exposure, compressed deliberation, and misinterpretation of ambiguous intent.

The book does not assume autonomous launch authority and must not imply deployment where only research, modernization plans, or proposals exist.

## Core mechanisms

1. **Detection versus false alarm:** earlier warning and lower miss rates can raise sensitivity to ambiguous events.
2. **Fusion versus correlated error:** more data can improve confidence or concentrate hidden common-mode failures.
3. **Automation bias:** coherent machine recommendations can shift the burden of proof onto dissenting humans.
4. **Time compression:** faster analysis and planning can reduce perceived room for deliberation.
5. **Cyber uncertainty:** attacks on sensors or communications can create doubt without needing to fabricate an entire false picture.
6. **Entanglement:** conventional attacks on dual-use systems can be interpreted through a nuclear lens.
7. **Deliberate friction:** authentication, redundancy, independent verification, and human challenge can be safety features rather than inefficiencies.

## Falsification and counter-evidence

The book should change if AI integration measurably reduces warning error and expands deliberation without introducing offsetting vulnerabilities. Preserve evidence that machine assistance can reduce human bias and fatigue. Do not assume opacity is inherent to every AI system or that faster analysis necessarily causes faster launch decisions.

Historic false alarms require careful sourcing; later retellings often simplify what operators actually saw and what procedures followed. See [Chapter 2 historical false-alert note](ch02-historical-false-alerts.md) for primary/declassified records on the November 1979 and June 1980 NORAD incidents and the counterevidence those records provide against a simple machine-versus-hero story.

## Chapter evidence map

Chapters 1–6 cover warning, historical false alarms, command systems, sensors, fusion, and confidence. Chapters 7–12 cover cyber, spoofing, automation bias, human judgment, compressed timelines, and conventional-nuclear entanglement. Chapters 13–18 examine national cases, crisis communication, testing, deliberate doubt, and design for decision time.

## Research discipline

Prefer declassified records, official doctrine and modernization documents, congressional testimony, technical evaluations, national laboratory research, SIPRI and UNIDIR work, and careful interviews with former operators. Distinguish proposals for AI use from deployed capability.
