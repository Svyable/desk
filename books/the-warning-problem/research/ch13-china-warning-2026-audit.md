# Chapter 13 evidence audit — China warning system

Reviewed 2026-09-09.

## Revision purpose

Chapter 13 was rebuilt around a narrower proposition: **outside observers can see the physical modernization of China’s strategic warning architecture much better than they can see the political decision rule attached to it.** The chapter therefore keeps observable capability, Chinese declaratory policy, U.S. external assessment, institutional inference, and projected crisis behavior visibly separate.

This replaces the prior chapter’s long sequence of frameworks, question stacks, maturity ladders, ledgers, state machines, and repeated uncertainty morals with one continuous argument built from the 2024 open-ocean ICBM launch, current Chinese declaratory policy, and the U.S. Defense Department’s current external assessment.

## Primary / authoritative sources checked

### U.S. Department of Defense, 2025 China Military Power Report

Source: https://media.defense.gov/2025/Dec/23/2003849070/-1/-1/1/ANNUAL-REPORT-TO-CONGRESS-MILITARY-AND-SECURITY-DEVELOPMENTS-INVOLVING-THE-PEOPLES-REPUBLIC-OF-CHINA-2025.PDF

The December 23, 2025 report says China **probably** made progress in 2024 toward an early warning counterstrike capability similar to launch on warning. It assesses that China probably expanded its space-based warning architecture in 2024 and early 2025 with two additional TJS/Huoyan-1 satellites with likely infrared warning payloads; that large phased-array radars probably contribute corroborating warning; and that December 2024 ICBM launch activity was consistent with rapid launch procedures required for such an operation.

The report also records the September 2024 full-range ICBM launch into the Pacific and says China provided pre-notification to the United States and some other countries, including France, while Japan and the Philippines were not notified.

**Claim boundary:** these are U.S. Department of Defense assessments. The manuscript preserves its probability language and does not relabel the report’s judgments as Chinese doctrine or publicly verified internal procedure.

### Ministry of National Defense of the People’s Republic of China, June 16, 2026

Source: https://eng.mod.gov.cn/2025xb/P/16467751.html

In response to outside estimates of Chinese nuclear modernization, a Chinese defense spokesperson stated on June 16, 2026 that China follows a nuclear strategy of self-defense, a policy of no first use, maintains nuclear capabilities at the minimum level required for national security, and does not engage in an arms race.

**Claim boundary:** this is current Chinese declaratory policy and official self-description. It does not directly reveal internal alert status, launch thresholds, delegation, crisis procedures, or the degree of machine assistance in strategic warning.

### Ministry of Foreign Affairs of the People’s Republic of China, May 7, 2025

Source: https://www.fmprc.gov.cn/eng/wjb/zzjg_663340/jks_665232/kjfywj_665252/202505/t20250507_11616571.html

China’s delegation to the 2026 NPT Review Conference preparatory process reiterated an undertaking not to use nuclear weapons first and described that policy as unchanged since China first acquired nuclear weapons.

**Use:** corroborates the durability of current declared no-first-use policy. It is not used as evidence of hidden operational practice.

## Strongest counterargument / disconfirming evidence

The chapter deliberately rejects the easy equation “better warning = more dangerous posture.” Better missile warning can strengthen second-strike survivability, reduce vulnerability to surprise, improve confirmation, and give leaders more information. A state whose retaliatory capability is harder to eliminate may face less pressure to preempt.

The narrower concern is conditional: once pre-impact retaliation becomes more technically feasible, warning quality, command resilience, institutional confidence, and the cost of waiting become more consequential. Whether the net result is stabilizing or destabilizing cannot be inferred from sensors alone.

Likewise, there is no public evidence strong enough to justify detailed claims about how much artificial intelligence is currently integrated into Chinese nuclear warning, what authority such systems possess, or the exact decision sequence during a nuclear crisis. The manuscript therefore discusses AI in conditional design terms rather than asserting deployment.

## Narrative / anti-slop decisions

The old chapter repeatedly named analytical devices—two ledgers, observation/inference/projection labels, layered confidence, maturity ladders, sensor-to-politics chains, source-lineage ledgers, signal half-life, challenge cells, reciprocal model audits, and a warning state machine. Most expressed variations of one good point: analysts should not hide the distance between what they observe and what they infer.

The revision keeps that point but removes most of the branded scaffolding. It also consolidates the old sequence of single-line paragraphs and rhetorical questions into fuller paragraphs, gives the 2024 launch enough concrete detail to carry the opening, and makes the strongest countercase part of the main argument rather than a late falsification section.

## Remaining uncertainty

A future release audit should re-check:

- any post-2025 U.S. official assessment of Chinese early warning counterstrike development;
- any new authoritative Chinese nuclear doctrine or no-first-use statement;
- credible public evidence about actual AI integration into strategic warning or nuclear command-and-control workflows;
- the status and practice of U.S.-China missile-test notification or strategic risk-reduction channels.

Until stronger evidence appears, Chapter 13 should continue to distinguish physical capability, declaratory policy, external assessment, institutional integration, and projected crisis behavior.
