# The Cyber Door

The cyber door into nuclear warning is usually drawn as a villain stealing launch authority from a glowing vault. That picture flatters cinema and misplaces the near-term strategic risk. The more common danger is an operation that leaves leaders unsure whether empty screens mean safety or blindness, and whether crowded screens mean attack or manipulation. Confidentiality theft can expose plans. Availability attacks can deafen a center. Integrity attacks—and attacks on the perception of integrity—reshape crisis behavior without requiring a perfect synthetic war. An adversary who makes a nuclear-armed state distrust its picture of the sky has already changed the decision environment that deterrence depends on. Artificial intelligence walks through this door in both directions at once. Defensive models can watch sprawling digital estates for anomalous traffic, poisoned inputs, and distribution shifts that smell like tampering. Offensive tooling can shorten the time a capable actor needs to find seams in complex software. Assessments from the Nuclear Threat Initiative on AI in nuclear-weapon systems and from the Federation of American Scientists on risk frameworks for AI in NC3 both underline the dual edge without leaping to autonomous launch fantasies. AI may be needed to defend increasingly digital command networks. AI may also open paths if convenience outruns adversarial testing. Aging analog systems are not a refuge either; unsupported software and vanishing expertise create their own openings. The real choice is disciplined digital hardening with integrity as a first-class requirement, not a nostalgia contest between vacuum tubes and neural nets.

Air gaps remain necessary and become hazardous when treated as talismans. Removable media, vendor maintenance laptops, insider mistakes, imperfectly segmented upgrade programs, and supply-chain implants have defeated isolation fantasies across industries; nuclear warning is not exempt from ordinary human pathways. Serious architecture assumes partial compromise and asks which functions can still yield trustworthy phenomenology when a segment is marked suspect. That question should be answerable in a drill, not invented during a missile timeline. If nothing works without the advanced fusion layer, modernity has become a single point of failure. Progressive shedding is the operational pattern built for partial compromise: when integrity alarms rise, shed commercial enrichments and secondary models first, retain dual phenomenology, and require human authentication of remaining hops. The picture gets uglier and more trustworthy. Organizations that measure success by feature richness will resist shedding because feature richness is what they sold to funders. Under attack, feature richness can be the channel through which contamination travels. Write the shedding playbook in peacetime. Exercise it until crews can execute without debating philosophy while the clock runs. Precomputed integrity-doubt procedures deserve the same seriousness as attack-assessment procedures. If the only trained path assumes green cybersecurity lights, the first amber light produces improvisation, and improvisation under nuclear time pressure is how small cyber events become large strategic ones.

Integrity attacks often beat theatrical fabrication for strategic effect. Intermittent false cues can train operators to discount alerts. Gaps can push a state toward frantic reliance on whichever channel remains. Obvious fingerprints can stage attribution theater rather than seek covert success. Third-party spoilers can attack the warning systems of two nuclear peers to provoke a confrontation neither peer intended. Doctrine that cannot name spoilers, accidents, and self-generated false alarms will default to the worst bilateral narrative because that narrative is emotionally available under fear. Attribution during a missile-flight timeline will be contested and slow. Leaders may hold a physical indicator in one hand and a cyber indicator in the other with different authors. The pairing invites nightmare coherence. Structured hypothesis boards—including spoiler—belong on the same timeline as tracks. Removing them to reduce clutter is how clutter becomes destiny. Artificial intelligence can enumerate alternatives quickly; humans must keep alternatives from being deleted by a confidence sort. Time spent on integrity triage is either stolen from judgment or rightly invested in judgment. Architecture should make triage fast and bounded so that panic and infinite doubt are both harder. Infinite doubt is also a way to lose a real war. Bounded, rapid integrity triage with predefined thresholds for shedding and restoration is the design target.

Machine-learning supply chains draw doors older wiring diagrams never showed. Model files, feature stores, training pipelines, evaluation harnesses, and update channels can be poisoned or swapped. A fine-tune on corrupted logs can teach a detector to sleep through the signature an adversary plans to use. A compromised update can alter behavior without changing familiar display chrome; operators will trust the chrome, and the chrome is not the model. Nuclear-grade ML operations therefore mean pinned configurations, signed models, known-good rollback images, continuous monitoring for distribution shift, and red teams whose careers do not depend on shipping the model this quarter. Budget justifications that bury nuclear incident analytics and warning decision support inside opaque RDT&E lines make such oversight harder. Visibility is not vanity. Without it, cyber test resources will not match AI ambition. Cryptographic authentication of messages remains necessary and insufficient. A signed packet can carry garbage if the sensor was owned before the signature. Warning cybersecurity is end-to-end from aperture to eyeball. Middleboxes that excel while edges rot produce beautiful diagrams and bad decisions. End-to-end accountability crosses program offices that prefer not to share blame; make them share it anyway through dependency-class declarations for each AI component stating what happens if an input bus is untrusted.

People remain doors, which is awkward for cultures that prefer network diagrams to psychology. Phishing a warning officer, spoofing an authentication step, or manipulating a contractor laptop can bypass elegant architecture drawings. Classic information security remains strategic here, not clerical. Nuclear warning cybersecurity that forgets people is ornamental. At the same time, insider-threat programs must not crush the dissent historical false alarms required. A culture that treats every challenge to a machine as suspicion will suppress the human check. Balance is possible: challenge the assessment, authenticate the person, log both. Allied connectivity multiplies doors. A partner's compromised ground station can become your contaminated track file if sharing is naive. Cyber hygiene for shared early warning is a collective good with weak individual incentives to fund fully. Alliance managers should treat it as core infrastructure, not as an IT afterthought patched after interoperability demos succeed. Interoperability without integrity-flag preservation is how one nation's incident becomes five nations' confusion. Space-segment and ground-segment cyber meet inside the product leaders see. A lying antenna can invent health for a sick path; a clean ground cloud can faithfully process a manipulated space feed. Segment-aware integrity should be visible to assessors, not buried in a maintenance submenu. Treating orbital assets as unreachable is unserious in an era of jamming, dazzling, and rendezvous experiments.

Vendors will sell detectors for deepfakes and synthetic tracks as finished shields. Sometimes the tools help. An adversary who knows a detector's features will generate the next synthesis to evade it; the contest is continuous. Procurement should buy testing cadence, logging, and red-team process rather than a permanent detector fantasy. Interface design is cyber defense in this domain. Displays that make integrity state unmistakable, that resist cosmetic normality when backends are suspect, and that refuse to let generative tone override integrity flags are part of the lock on the door. A manipulated feed that arrives with a confident AI narrative is more dangerous than a manipulated feed that arrives looking sick. Healthy-looking corruption is the attacker's premium product; deny the healthy look when health is unverified. Success metrics should include how quickly a center can isolate a suspect channel without losing overall warning function. Counting blocked intrusions matters. Continuing to see the sky while under attack on the network matters more. Report both to overseers. What is unmeasured becomes optional when schedules slip. Historical humility blocks cyber exceptionalism: GAO reviews of NORAD false warnings show dangerous failure before today's digital density through test tapes in live paths and faulty components writing wrong quantities. Cyber adds a class; it does not repeal older classes. Defense in depth still overlaps physical, procedural, electronic, and cognitive protections.

Cognitive lies—persuading humans to trust a bad picture—are where cyber meets automation bias. Convenience arguments will keep leaning on the door because tired operators err, good tools reduce fatigue, and automation catches what exhaustion misses. Those points are true and incomplete. Sequencing still matters: integrity properties before convenience features, tests before testimonials, degraded modes before VIP demos that assume clean networks. Demos that require fairy-tale networks teach fairy-tale readiness. Congress, inspectors general, and allied auditors can ask for dependency-class declarations and shedding-drill results the way they already ask for other readiness metrics. Make cyber readiness for warning AI a first-class measured item or watch it traded away when schedules slip. Training must include cyber-ambiguous vignettes, not only kinetic attack vignettes. Crews should feel the specific nausea of a screen that might be right and might be owned. Grading should reward orderly shedding and clear upward communication of integrity state, not only fast declaration of attack. If promotions flow to the officer who always sounds sure, the culture will manufacture surety under cyber doubt. If promotions flow to the officer who keeps the state from outrunning its trust in its tools, the culture may hold. Culture is a control surface; write it into evaluation forms.

Contractors and laboratories need aligned incentives. A vendor who discloses a dependency weakness early should be protected from reflexive punishment that teaches silence. Laboratories that publish fail-safe protocols for AI in high-consequence settings—as NTI recommended—should be funded as stability work, not treated as obstacles to fielding. Openness about methods, where classification allows, helps allies and constrains fantasies of omniscience. Secrecy about limitations often protects bureaucratic embarrassment more than it denies adversaries useful detail, and embarrassment is not a strategic asset. Choose the embarrassment; fix the door. Semantic firewalls between conventional joint pictures and nuclear assessment are cyber controls as well as fusion controls. They limit which contaminated meanings may climb toward nuclear assessment. Climbing should be hard even when joint warfighters want one screen for everything. Strategic nuclear warning is not the same job as theater targeting. Hardness is not the opposite of readiness; hardness is how readiness survives an enemy who prefers your certainty to your clarity. The door will never seal forever. It can be narrowed, alarmed, watched, and sometimes simplified so its state is obvious. In nuclear warning, obvious security often beats clever security that fails closed into opacity, because opacity is where false confidence breeds.

Stand far enough back, and the cyber problem is the book's thesis in network form. Machines can improve detection and defense while making the decision environment more complex and more vulnerable to false confidence unless doubt remains visible. A defended network that produces a fluent, integrity-unmarked picture can be more dangerous than a ragged network whose raggedness is visible. Visibility of doubt is a feature, even when it annoys people who want clean common operating pictures. Watching the door is a strategic function; fund it like one. Assume spoofers study hinges while briefers celebrate locks. The measure of success is not whether intrusion attempts are blocked in a laboratory demo, but whether a warning center under contested integrity can still give leadership a picture that knows what it does not know. A picture that knows its uncertainty is still a picture. A picture that hides its uncertainty is a lobbyist for action. Cyber adversaries prefer the lobbyist. Build the other kind. Then assume they will try again tomorrow, and keep integrity day on the calendar anyway. Calendars beat slogans. With that standard stated, the craft of spoofing—supplying the wrong world on purpose for sensors built to notice—can be examined without treating it as magic.

A dependency-class declaration should travel with every AI component proposed for warning: a written statement of what happens if the update channel, feature store, or input bus is untrusted. Silent failure into confident output means the component is unready for this domain. Loud degradation into a simpler corroborated mode that crews have rehearsed may mean readiness. Independent reviewers outside the shipping chain should read the declaration without authority to rewrite it into optimism under schedule pressure. Independence in review is part of independence in sensing. The same independence belongs in after-action reviews when integrity alarms fired and when they failed to fire. Learning systems that cannot admit near misses will not improve. Nuclear warning already knows this about kinetic false alarms; it must learn it about cyber-adjacent near misses before a real crisis grades the exam. International partners can share sanitized lessons about integrity failures without sharing exploits. Shared lesson libraries are a public good for strategic stability. States that refuse to learn from others' cyber-warning accidents will pay tuition in their own bloodless minutes of panic—or worse.

There is a temptation to answer every cyber scare with more automation, as if speed alone closed doors. Automated cyber defense is valuable and can itself be subverted, poisoned, or used as a channel for false reassurance. The green dashboard of an AI defender is another surface that can lie. Humans still need a way to ask whether the defender is healthy. Nested monitoring sounds bureaucratic because it is bureaucratic; bureaucracy is how complex systems remember to distrust themselves. Nested monitoring should extend to the generative layer: if a language model summarizes integrity state, the underlying flags must remain visible beside the prose. Prose without flags is how a center talks itself into calm or into terror without evidence. Keep the flags. Keep the drills. Keep the authority to shed. Those three habits will not make warning cyber-proof. They will make warning cyber-honest, which is the only achievable standard compatible with nuclear decision-making.

The chapter ends on craft rather than purity. Networks will not become pure. Spoofers will not retire. Insiders will not become angels. What can be built is a warning enterprise that expects contamination attempts, rehearses ugly trustworthy modes, measures integrity readiness, and refuses unmarked fluency when inputs are in doubt. That enterprise can still use AI to defend itself. It simply refuses to let the defender become an usher. Spoofing, treated next, is what the usher would prefer to escort: a phenomenology that looks like the war the system was built to see, arriving on purpose from someone who wants the seeing to hurt.

Incident response in a warning center cannot copy a corporate security operations playbook without modification. Eradication matters, yet the first priority is preserving a minimum trustworthy situational picture for national leadership while the fight continues. That may require shedding advanced analytics and returning to dual phenomenology with human authentication of each hop, even while network defenders still hunt. Leaders must be told, in plain language, which parts of the picture are currently trusted, which are suspect, and which have been voluntarily discarded. Plain language here is a safety control. Euphemism about "degraded operations" without specifying what degraded means will be filled in by fear.

International law and norms discussions about cyber operations against nuclear command systems remain thinner than the risk warrants. Some scholars and former officials have argued for taboos against attacking NC3. Taboos are not self-enforcing, especially for spoilers, but declaratory restraint among major peers could still reduce the legitimacy of certain targeting choices and provide a language for crisis protest. Technical communities should support those conversations with unclassified explanations of why warning integrity is stabilizing for everyone who prefers not to die by accident. Stability arguments are easier to make when they are not classified into silence.

The last image is mundane on purpose: a checklist on a wall beside a watch desk, listing integrity alarms, shedding steps, and the phone tree for notifying leadership that the picture is partially untrusted. Checklists lack glamour. They have saved aircraft and operating rooms. They can save warning centers from improvising the wrong bravery. Tape the checklist up. Drill it. Update it when the architecture changes. Then assume someone is already probing the door the checklist guards.

With the cyber door described as craft rather than myth, spoofing can be treated as the wider art of feeding warning systems the wrong world—sometimes through networks, sometimes through physics and electromagnetic theater that never needs a stolen password.

A closing accounting helps keep the chapter honest about what it did not claim. It did not claim that cyber attackers already own strategic warning systems. It did not claim that AI defenders are useless. It did not claim that air gaps should be abandoned. It claimed that integrity risk is the strategically salient cyber problem for warning; that AI raises both defensive capacity and attack surface; that partial compromise must be rehearsed; that supply chains and people remain pathways; that metrics and culture decide whether doubt stays visible; and that unmarked fluent pictures under contested integrity are a pathway to false confidence. Those claims are enough to justify spending political capital on ugly trustworthy modes. They are also enough to motivate the next subject. Spoofing is how an adversary supplies a wrong world to sensors that are doing their jobs. Sometimes spoofing uses the cyber door. Sometimes it uses heat, metal, radio, and timing. Sometimes it uses the fusion machine's hunger for confirmation. The craft is older than neural networks. AI changes the price and the polish.

Operators who have lived through integrity scares often describe a particular fatigue afterward: not the fatigue of shift work alone, but the fatigue of having to rebuild trust in tools that remain necessary. Leadership should plan for that fatigue. Rotate crews. Debrief without humiliation. Fix what broke. Avoid the twin failures of pretending nothing happened and of punishing everyone who touched the incident. Learning organizations survive. Theatrical organizations repeat. Nuclear warning needs the first kind, especially as AI increases the number of components that can fail in correlated ways while looking independently healthy.

The National Security Archive's collections on the 1979–1980 false missile warnings, and Harold Brown's contemporaneous observation that false warnings are inevitable and that human safeguards matter, remain useful under cyber conditions. Cyber does not invent the need for disbelief. It invents new ways for disbelief to be delayed by integrity theater—green dashboards that claim health while a feed is already owned. Brown's point survives: expect false pictures; design so that people can still say no.

Section 1638 of the FY2025 National Defense Authorization Act stated a policy that AI in support of strategic deterrence should not compromise positive human action in presidential nuclear employment decisions. The Federation of American Scientists correctly noted that the harder near-term problem sits upstream of employment: early warning and decision support, where automation bias, confabulation, and information security can reshape the picture a president sees. Keeping a human larynx at the final switch is necessary and incomplete. The cyber door opens earlier, at the integrity of the path that feeds the switch.

DTRA budget justifications that fund nuclear incident analytics, warning reporting, and decision support should be read as commitments that require matching test money for adversarial integrity evaluation. A line item without a red team is a wish. Overseers can ask for dependency-class declarations and shedding-drill results the way they already ask for other readiness metrics. What is unmeasured becomes optional when schedules slip. Integrity readiness for warning AI should not be optional.
