# Automation Bias

Automation bias does not require people to worship machines.

It can emerge from workflow.

A system presents a recommendation. The human is busy. The recommendation is usually right. Challenging it takes time. Accepting it is easy.

Over thousands of routine decisions, deference becomes rational.

Then the rare case arrives.

This is why nuclear decision support cannot rely on a warning label that says “human judgment required.”

The interface and institution must make judgment real.

One danger is asymmetric burden.

If the model says an event is likely hostile, the analyst who disagrees may feel required to prove the negative. The machine’s view becomes the baseline.

This is powerful even when everybody knows the model can be wrong.

Another danger is deskilling.

If operators rely on automation for years, their independent ability to interpret the underlying data can decay. When the system behaves strangely, the human fallback exists on paper but not in practice.

A third danger is attentional capture.

Models are good at ranking. Whatever they place first receives human attention. Information they deprioritize may disappear from the practical decision space.

The system therefore shapes judgment before it issues any recommendation.

Countermeasures have to be structural.

Require independent assessment before revealing the model in selected high-stakes cases.

Present alternative hypotheses rather than one answer.

Show evidence and provenance.

Train with scenarios in which the model is wrong.

Measure whether users can detect failure, not merely whether the model performs well.

Rotate people through manual processes enough to preserve competence.

Most importantly, reward challenge.

An organization that punishes false alarms from humans but excuses confident machine mistakes will learn deference very quickly.

Automation bias is not a flaw inside the person.

It is a property of the relationship between a person, a system, and an institution.

That relationship must be designed.
