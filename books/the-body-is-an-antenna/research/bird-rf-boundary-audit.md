# Bird RF boundary audit — from disrupted compass to identified receiver

This is a bounded evidence pass for *The Body Is an Antenna*, stacked on the opening manuscript rather than editing Chapter 1 concurrently. The opening is strongest when the bird is a positive control for a genuinely strange weak-field effect. It becomes weaker if behavioral disruption is allowed to stand in for identification of the biological receiver.

## What the 2014 experiment establishes

Engels et al. (2014) reported that European robins tested on the University of Oldenburg campus failed to orient magnetically in unscreened wooden huts, regained magnetic orientation when grounded aluminium screening attenuated electromagnetic noise, lost orientation again when the grounding was removed, and also lost it when broadband noise was deliberately generated inside the screened huts. The paper describes the tests as fully double-blinded and reports that birds at a rural site oriented without screening.

Primary source: Svenja Engels et al., “Anthropogenic electromagnetic noise disrupts magnetic compass orientation in a migratory bird,” *Nature* 509, 353–356 (2014), DOI 10.1038/nature13290:
https://www.nature.com/articles/nature13290

This is unusually useful evidence because the intervention is reversible. It supports a behavioral claim about a magnetic compass under specified electromagnetic-noise conditions. It does not by itself identify cryptochrome, a radical pair, a retinal cell, or the complete transduction pathway.

## The later molecular result is a different rung

Xu et al. (2021) purified cryptochrome 4 from European robins and showed that its photochemistry is magnetically sensitive *in vitro*. Robin CRY4 was more magnetically sensitive in the reported assay than CRY4 from chicken and pigeon, and site-specific mutations helped identify a chain of flavin–tryptophan radical pairs relevant to the magnetic-field effect.

Primary source: Jingjing Xu et al., “Magnetic sensitivity of cryptochrome 4 from a migratory songbird,” *Nature* 594, 535–540 (2021), DOI 10.1038/s41586-021-03618-9:
https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-03618-9

That result is important precisely because it does **not** finish the behavioral story. It establishes that a plausible molecule has relevant physical chemistry. It does not demonstrate that CRY4 is the receptor responsible for the 2014 robin behavior inside the intact animal.

A useful contemporaneous control is Einwich et al. (2020), which found a second CRY4 isoform in the European-robin retina and explicitly described the primary sensory molecule underlying the light-dependent compass as still unidentified. The existence of multiple isoforms and unresolved retinal signaling architecture makes a simple “CRY4 is the bird’s antenna” sentence premature.

Primary source: Angelika Einwich et al., “A novel isoform of cryptochrome 4 (Cry4b) is expressed in the retina of a night-migratory songbird,” *Scientific Reports* 10, 15794 (2020):
https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-020-72579-2

## Frequency is evidence, but not a fingerprint yet

The manuscript currently says weak RF noise can disrupt avian magnetic orientation and that the radical-pair model predicts such disruption. That is defensible at the level stated, but later chapters should resist converting “RF sensitivity” into a unique molecular fingerprint.

The experimental frequency story is structured rather than universal. Schwarze et al. (2020) found no disruption of Eurasian-blackcap orientation under broadband 0.1–100 kHz fields and used that negative result, together with earlier higher-frequency disruption, to constrain candidate spin-coherence times. Later behavioral work reported disruption in a much higher 75–85 MHz band. Those positive and negative windows are potentially powerful because a real receiver should have edges.

Primary sources:
- S. Schwarze et al., “Electromagnetic 0.1–100 kHz noise does not disrupt orientation in a night-migrating songbird implying a spin coherence lifetime of less than 10 μs,” *Royal Society Open Science* 7 (2020): https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6936046/
- “Broadband 75–85 MHz radiofrequency fields disrupt magnetic compass orientation in night-migratory songbirds consistent with a flavin-based radical pair magnetoreceptor,” *Journal of Comparative Physiology A* (2022), DOI 10.1007/s00359-021-01537-8: https://doi.org/10.1007/s00359-021-01537-8

The important editorial move is to make the spectrum do argumentative work. A mechanism that predicts only “some RF affects birds” is weak. A mechanism that prospectively predicts a frequency/amplitude/orientation/light dependence and survives a blinded behavioral test has paid much more of the receiver bill.

## Strongest counterargument

The receiver-first standard can itself become unfair if it implies that an organism-level effect is not real until every molecular step is known. Sensory biology often establishes reliable behavior before the receptor and pathway are fully identified. The 2014 reversible, double-blinded behavioral result therefore deserves to stand as behavioral evidence even while the molecular mechanism remains incomplete.

The counter-counterargument is the book’s real opportunity: **behavioral reality and mechanistic identification are separate achievements**. The existence of the first cannot be used to borrow certainty for the second. Conversely, uncertainty about CRY4 should not be used to erase a reproducible behavioral effect.

## Publication holds

- Do not write that the 2014 Oldenburg experiment demonstrated that cryptochrome or CRY4 was the receiver.
- Do not describe CRY4 as the established avian magnetoreceptor without qualifying that the intact in-vivo receptor/pathway remains unresolved.
- Do not present RF disruption as broadband in the everyday sense of “all radio frequencies”; preserve tested frequency ranges, amplitudes and negative windows.
- Do not use the 2021 purified-protein result as an independent behavioral replication. It tests a different rung of the mechanism.
- Do not use failure to identify the complete receptor as evidence that the behavioral magnetic compass is unreal.

## Quantitative burden for the bird chapters

For each decisive behavioral experiment, extract species, season, light spectrum/intensity, geomagnetic field vector, RF/noise spectrum, magnetic and electric field amplitudes at the animal, cage and shielding geometry, grounding state, blinding procedure, orientation metric, preregistered or stated analysis, sample size, effect size/circular statistic, negative frequency windows, and replication laboratory.

For each molecular experiment, separately extract protein/isoform, species, construct, chromophore occupancy, illumination, field strength and direction, frequency, radical lifetime/coherence estimate, measured chemical endpoint, mutations, effect size and whether the result was in vitro, ex vivo or in vivo.

Only then join the two ledgers. The bridge from molecule to behavior is itself a claim.

## Narrative opportunity

Chapter 1 already has the right object: a bird that loses north in radio noise. The stronger later return is not “and scientists found the antenna.” It is stranger and more honest. Years after the behavioral result, researchers can hold one candidate protein outside the bird and show that its chemistry notices a magnetic field. The reader gets closer to the receiver, but not all the way there.

That partial convergence is better than a solved mystery. It lets the book demonstrate its central discipline on its own favorite positive control: do not make evidence climb a rung merely because the next rung is plausible.
