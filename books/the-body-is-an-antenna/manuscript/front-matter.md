# The Body Is an Antenna

## The rule of the book

A biological effect is not a message.

A receptor is not a radio.

A mechanism that can alter chemistry is not evidence that a distant operator can write content into a nervous system.

Those sentences will save us several hundred pages of nonsense.

This book uses four evidence classes and keeps them visible whenever the subject becomes controversial.

**1 — Verified / documented.** Peer-reviewed physical or biological results that can be stated narrowly; official program goals and records; documented historical exposures. Examples include animal geomagnetic orientation, magnetic-field sensitivity of particular radical-pair systems, DARPA’s stated RadioBio research question, and published RF manipulation of flavoprotein spin chemistry.

**2 — Credible but incomplete.** Results with serious methods or institutional provenance whose replication, generalization, mechanism or operational meaning remains incomplete.

**3 — Disputed / unconfirmed.** Claims of endogenous RF communication carrying useful information between cells, robust human magnetoreception, remote semantic neural influence, psychotronic weapons, or field effects whose proposed mechanism has not survived independent testing.

**4 — Speculative synthesis.** The book’s own conjectures: that sensory biology can be treated as an information-link-budget problem; that “mechanism laundering” explains why genuine weak-field effects repeatedly lend credibility to capabilities orders of magnitude larger; and that the cleanest way to investigate extraordinary electromagnetic claims is to build the receiver model before arguing about the transmitter.

Patents are evidence that somebody filed a patent. Program solicitations are evidence that an agency wanted a question answered. A military research program is not evidence that its premise worked. A molecular effect is not a behavioral effect. A behavioral effect is not communication. Communication is not control.

The burden rises at every rung.
