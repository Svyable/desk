# Tissue Is a Terrible Optical Fiber

The phrase *light in the brain* carries an image of clear beams crossing a dark interior. Real tissue ruins the picture.

Biological tissue scatters. It absorbs. Interfaces redirect photons. Blood is optically consequential. Water is not transparent at every wavelength. Myelin, membranes and cellular structure create heterogeneous refractive environments. A photon born in a mitochondrion does not get a straight corridor to another neuron. It enters a crowded city.

Modern neurophotonics is built around this inconvenience. Researchers trying to deliver or recover external light from brain tissue spend enormous effort on wavelength selection, fiber placement, multiphoton excitation, computational reconstruction and optical clearing. A 2025 study modeling photon transport through an entire adult human head emphasized just how difficult long source-detector separations are even in the near infrared, where tissue is relatively permissive. External neuroimaging works by engineering around loss.

That literature is a useful control for endogenous-light claims. If a theory proposes photons traveling centimeters through brain tissue, it inherits decades of measured attenuation and scattering. It cannot simply call myelin a waveguide and erase the rest of the anatomy.

But poor long-range transport does not settle short-range signaling. Cells are small. Organelles are smaller. The same scattering that destroys a centimeter-scale beam may be irrelevant across a few micrometers. An endogenous optical mechanism could be local, with photons influencing nearby molecules before being absorbed. In that case the external UPE detector sees only the tiny fraction that escaped, while the biologically relevant interactions happen inside.

This possibility changes the experimental target. Instead of asking whether the brain broadcasts a meaningful photon field, ask whether local oxidative photon production perturbs a nearby photosensitive pathway. That can be tested with genetically encoded absorbers, spectral blockers, altered chromophore concentrations and microscopy designed around cellular distances.

There is also an inverse mistake: assuming that because tissue is opaque to our eyes it is opaque to every relevant photon. Optical properties are wavelength-dependent, and multiple scattering does not equal zero transport. The right answer is a transport model tied to measured spectra and anatomy.

The field would benefit from importing the habits of biomedical optics. Report absorption and reduced-scattering coefficients. Specify source depths. Model path-length distributions. Measure detector collection efficiency. Distinguish ballistic, quasi-ballistic and diffuse photons. Validate simulations against tissue phantoms. None of this is glamorous, which is precisely why it could rescue the subject from glamour.

The brain is not a fiber-optic network just because photons exist in it. If evolution has built optical structures, those structures should be anatomically identifiable and should outperform surrounding tissue at some measurable transport task.

Myelin is the obvious candidate and therefore deserves its own chapter. Before getting there, however, it is worth keeping the default picture: warm, wet, scattering tissue full of conventional electrochemical signaling. A proposed photon channel starts as the underdog.

That is the proper handicap. Extraordinary architecture should have to beat ordinary anatomy on its own field.
