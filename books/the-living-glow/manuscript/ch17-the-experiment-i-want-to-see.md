# The Experiment I Want to See

Build two rooms inside one room.

In the first sits the sender: a biological preparation whose ultraweak photon emission can be modulated reliably by a known perturbation, perhaps a mitochondrial or oxidative challenge. In the second sits the receiver: a preparation selected because prior work suggests a physiological response to the sender. The two chambers have independent temperature control, airflow, power conditioning and mechanical support.

Between them runs an optical channel that can be switched without the operators knowing its state. Not a curtain. An engineered path.

The sender chamber contains a calibrated photon-counting system measuring spectrum and time series continuously. The optical path can operate in several modes: broad transmission; opaque block; wavelength-selective passbands; neutral-density attenuation; and fiber-routed transmission that carries photons while changing geometry. A separate synthetic source can inject a replay of the sender's measured photon stream into the receiver path at matched intensity.

Both chambers monitor temperature at multiple points, vibration, audible and ultrasonic acoustic energy, electric and magnetic fields and pressure. Headspace sampling checks volatile contamination. Equipment clocks are synchronized but trial assignments are generated remotely and cryptographically committed before data collection.

The biological protocol is equally severe. Sender perturbations include active and sham conditions. Receiver outcomes are defined before the first run. The analysis distinguishes immediate response from slower drift and corrects for autocorrelation. The primary endpoint is one variable, not a menu from which significance can be chosen later.

The trial sequence contains a trap for every favored explanation.

If receiver changes track sender stress with the optical path open but persist under opaque block, the photonic hypothesis loses. If they disappear under optical block but also disappear when a fiber carries the measured light around the barrier, some other direct coupling may have been disturbed. If artificial replay reproduces the effect, the case for photons becomes much stronger. If only live-source transmission works, the next step is to identify what measured property of the live light differs from the replay rather than declare a special life photon.

Wavelength tests should be prospective. Use the sender spectrum and candidate receiver absorption to predict a band before unblinding. A narrow spectral dependency that matches a molecular absorber would be one of the most persuasive results available.

Then replicate the entire protocol in at least three laboratories. One should be selected by proponents, one by skeptics and one by expertise in precision optical measurement rather than prior views about biophotons. Hardware designs and analysis code should be shared. Each lab should receive blind calibration sources that mimic biological signals and nulls.

The experiment should publish everything: positive trials, null trials, environmental excursions, excluded data and protocol deviations. A public dataset allows independent time-series analysis without forcing readers to trust one statistical pipeline.

If the effect survives, the project enters a second phase: molecular receiver identification. Candidate photoreceptors or chromophores are manipulated. The predicted receiver response should weaken, shift spectrally or disappear. A causal chain begins to emerge.

If the effect fails, the result is not that life emits no photons. It means the tested communication claim did not survive a design that gave photons every reasonable opportunity to carry it.

This experiment is expensive by the standards of a fringe field and cheap by the standards of modern biology. The difficulty is cultural. It requires people who disagree about the phenomenon to agree that the apparatus gets the final vote.

I want to see that apparatus because the question has been around long enough. A weak signal deserves a strong test.
