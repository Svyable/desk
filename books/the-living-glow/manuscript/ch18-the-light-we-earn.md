# The Light We Earn

There is a tendency in frontier biology to believe that wonder lives on the far side of mechanism. Explain the light as oxidative chemistry and something has supposedly been taken away.

I think the opposite happened while writing this book.

Living tissue is not perfectly dark. Reactions in cells create excited molecular states. Those states relax. Some release photons. Stress changes the flux. Injury changes it. Time of day can change it. Human bodies placed before instruments sensitive enough to count the escaping events acquire a faint, structured glow that no unaided eye can see.

That is not metaphor.

Around this measured fact has grown a larger family of claims: that the emissions are coherent in unusual ways, that cells communicate through them, that mitochondria exchange optical information, that myelin forms quantum cavities, that neural synchronization may use entangled photons. Some of those claims are grounded theoretical possibilities. Some rest on suggestive experiments. Some have accumulated more certainty in popular retelling than their evidence supports.

The answer is not to flatten the field into "real" and "pseudoscience." The categories are finer.

Ultraweak photon emission is real. Its major links to oxidative chemistry are well grounded. Its value as a research reporter is plausible and, in some applications, already practical. Evidence that biological preparations can exhibit non-chemical coupling is intriguing in particular experiments but incomplete and mechanistically unsettled. Evidence for evolved photonic communication is much thinner. Quantum-neural proposals are presently hypotheses and models, not demonstrated brain mechanisms.

Those sentences can all be true at once.

The most productive change would be to stop asking whether biophotons are "real." That question bundles the photon with every story attached to it. Ask instead which layer is real at which level of evidence.

There is precedent for this patience. Magnetoreception moved for decades through arguments about behavior, receptors, radical pairs and magnetic particles. Photosynthesis research separated measured efficiency from proposed quantum mechanisms. Neuroscience learned repeatedly that an observed correlate need not be a communication channel. Fields mature by making nouns smaller.

Biophotons may mature the same way. The near future is probably less cinematic than the claims that made the subject famous. Better cameras. Better spectral measurements. Better redox biomarkers. Single-cell photon mapping. Simultaneous electrophysiology and photon counting. Calibrated interlaboratory standards. Carefully isolated sender-receiver preparations. Direct tests of candidate absorbers. Quantum-optical measurements applied to biological sources without assuming the result.

If those experiments find no communication, the science will still have gained a strange, non-invasive view of metabolism. If they find a local causal optical pathway, biology will have acquired a signaling mechanism that was hiding in the noise of its own chemistry. If genuinely nonclassical photon states are measured and then tied to function, the claim will be stronger precisely because it crossed the smaller bridges first.

The standard should be asymmetrical in only one respect: the stranger the interpretation, the more specific the measurement.

I began in a dark room because the subject needs darkness. Not the romantic darkness of secret forces, but the engineered darkness that lets a detector distinguish one photon from the flood we normally live inside. The room has shutters, cooling lines, cables, stray reflections and a notebook full of failed baselines. That is where mystery becomes useful.

The light worth keeping is the light the apparatus earns.
