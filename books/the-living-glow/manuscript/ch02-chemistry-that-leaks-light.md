# Chemistry That Leaks Light

Light is usually taught as an input to biology. Plants absorb it. Eyes transduce it. Skin is damaged by too much ultraviolet. Photodynamic therapies deliberately put light into tissue. Ultraweak photon emission asks us to watch the arrow run the other way.

The most grounded explanation begins with electronically excited species. Oxidative metabolism is messy. Reactive oxygen species participate in normal signaling and become damaging in excess. Lipids oxidize. Proteins and nucleic acids can be attacked. Peroxides decompose. Along those pathways, molecules can enter excited electronic states. When they relax, some fraction of the energy leaves as photons. A 2014 review by Cifra and Pospíšil summarized this chemistry in ordinary terms: triplet excited carbonyls, excited pigments and singlet oxygen are among the species capable of producing the measured emission. No new physics is required.

This matters because it gives the field a strong conventional baseline. If ultraviolet exposure makes skin emit more photons and antioxidants suppress part of that increase, the first interpretation should be oxidative chemistry. In 2019, researchers imaging human skin after ultraviolet irradiation reported exactly this kind of relation: emission rose with UV dose and was reduced by antioxidants. That makes ultraweak light potentially useful as a reporter of oxidative state. It does not make it a messenger.

There is a subtle trap here. A reporter can be extraordinarily informative. A physician might someday infer local stress from a photon map. A plant pathologist can watch a defense response through light emission. A neuroscientist can ask whether a change in neural metabolism leaves a detectable optical trace. The more informative the reporter becomes, the easier it is to slide into the language of biological information transfer. But the observer may be the instrument, not the organism.

Consider exhaust gas. Its chemistry can reveal engine load, combustion efficiency and malfunction. A sensitive analyst can reconstruct a great deal from it. None of that requires a second engine to read the exhaust. Ultraweak emission could be like that: a rich external trace with no endogenous receiver.

There is, however, one reason not to stop there. Evolution is opportunistic. It can recruit by-products. A molecule produced accidentally can become a hormone if a receptor and regulatory advantage emerge. A mechanical vibration can become a courtship signal. A metabolic glow could in principle become a channel if photoreceptors or photochemical targets exist at the right scale and if the photon budget is sufficient.

That conditional is the hinge of the book. The chemistry tells us where the photons come from. It does not tell us whether they matter after they leave.

The strongest experiments therefore need two ledgers at once. The first ledger is source chemistry: what reactions generate the photons, at what wavelengths, rates and spatial distributions? The second is receiver physics: what absorbers are present, what cross-sections do they have, how many photons reach them, and does absorption alter downstream biology above stochastic background?

Without the second ledger, signaling claims float free of energy accounting. Without the first, every detected photon becomes mysterious merely because its origin was not measured in the same experiment.

The field improves immediately when those two ledgers are kept separate. Oxidative chemistry can explain emission without explaining communication. Communication, if it exists, must survive the conventional explanation rather than compete with it.
