# Plants, Wounds, and False Telepathy

Plants are where weak-light research can become beautiful and dangerous at the same time.

Wound a leaf and chemistry changes immediately. Electrical potentials propagate. Calcium waves move. Reactive oxygen species participate in local and systemic responses. Hydraulic changes occur. Volatile compounds can be released. Gene expression changes. Ultraweak photon emission changes as well. Experiments have used photon counting as a nondestructive way to follow plant stress and defense. That is class-one territory: a measurable optical correlate of physiology.

The temptation is to promote the correlate into the courier.

A wounded plant may emit more photons while an unwounded region elsewhere in the plant changes state. Both events are real. But the same wound also launches several known signaling systems. Unless the optical channel is manipulated independently, the existence of light at one end and biology at the other says almost nothing about causation.

Plant research supplies unusually good controls because the organisms tolerate interventions that would be impossible in an animal. One can insert optical barriers without severing every vascular connection, use spectral filters, alter reactive oxygen pathways, change contact geometry, place plants near one another without touching, and compare intact networks with isolated tissues. It is possible to ask whether a receiver responds when the only engineered path between it and a sender is optical.

The phrase "plant communication" also needs policing. Plants unquestionably communicate in several ordinary senses. Volatile chemicals can influence neighbors. Roots alter shared environments. Mycorrhizal networks can mediate interactions, though popular descriptions often outrun the evidence. Direct contact can transmit electrical and physiological effects. None of this licenses an unknown long-range photonic system, but it establishes the ecological context in which another channel would have to compete.

A 2022 study of aboveground plant-to-plant electrical signaling is instructive. Researchers reported that wound- or high-light-induced electrical signals could be transmitted between plants in direct physical contact, with associated changes in reactive oxygen species and photosynthesis. The pathway is extraordinary enough without telepathy. Touch was doing real work.

That should change how we read experiments in which separated biological samples show correlated behavior. Separation from bulk chemistry does not produce a vacuum of mechanisms. Surfaces touch supports. Chambers share air. Light leaks. Vibrations travel through benches. Power supplies synchronize. Temperature cycles. The more sensitive the biology, the more ways two preparations can share a world.

Some plant-UPE papers have reported surprising temporal correlations, including work relating emission rhythms from germinating seedlings to local gravimetric tides. Such findings belong in the book because they illustrate the epistemic difficulty. A correlation with a periodic geophysical variable can emerge from a direct influence, from environmental covariates, from analytical flexibility, or from chance amplified by searching. The appropriate response is prospective replication with preregistered phase relationships and environmental monitoring, not immediate mechanism.

Plants also reveal why "weak" does not mean "irrelevant." A seedling can respond to tiny hormonal concentrations, slight directional light, mechanical touch and subtle chemical gradients because evolution has built amplification systems. The question is not whether a few photons could in principle matter. The question is whether there is a receiver architecture that makes these particular photons matter.

If such architecture exists, plants may be the place to find it. Their photoreceptor biology is rich. Cryptochromes, phytochromes and phototropins already turn photons into developmental decisions. That provides plausible molecular hardware, but it also creates a confound: external environmental light is enormously stronger than endogenous UPE. Any proposed internal or interplant optical channel must explain how receivers distinguish the whisper from daylight.

The answer could involve darkness, local geometry, spectral niches, intracellular distances or temporal coding. Or there may be no answer because the photons are only exhaust.

The experiment should decide. Until then, wounded plants are not telepathic. They are luminous, electrically active, chemically articulate organisms whose overlapping channels make them an unusually difficult place to prove a new one.
