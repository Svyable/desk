# A Signal Is Not an Emission

A lighthouse emits light and communicates. A hot brick emits infrared radiation and usually does not. The distinction is not the photon. It is the relationship among source, receiver and function.

Biophoton literature becomes much easier to audit once this obvious point is made explicit. There are at least four levels of claim. First, a specimen emits photons. Second, the photon rate covaries with some biological state. Third, another biological system can detect those photons and change in response. Fourth, evolution uses that interaction as a signaling channel. Each level requires evidence not supplied by the one below it.

The second level is already scientifically rich. A stressed cell can become brighter because oxidative chemistry changes. A damaged plant can produce a spatial wave of emission correlated with wound responses. Neural tissue might alter UPE with activity because metabolism and reactive oxygen chemistry change. If we can image those changes, photons become a passive sensor output. That is analogous to reading heat from an engine with a thermal camera.

The third level is the difficult one. The receiver cannot be the scientist's photomultiplier. It has to be biology.

A candidate receiver might be a chromophore, opsin, flavin, porphyrin, cytochrome or other molecule with an absorption spectrum overlapping the emission. But spectral overlap is only the beginning. Photon flux at the receiver matters. So does geometry. Tissue scatters and absorbs. A molecule that can absorb a wavelength in vitro may see essentially no endogenous photons above background in vivo. Even if absorption occurs, one must show that the event changes a pathway at a biologically meaningful rate.

This is why photon budgets are merciless. Imagine a cell producing a certain number of photons per second. Only a fraction leaves the relevant compartment. Only a fraction travels toward the target. Only a fraction survives absorption and scattering. Only a fraction intersects the receiver molecule. Only a fraction of those absorptions produce the downstream state change. Every step reduces the available signal.

Biology can work at low copy numbers, so low flux is not automatically fatal. Rhodopsin in a dark-adapted retina approaches single-photon sensitivity. But the retina is an extreme organ built around catching photons: dense pigment, specialized geometry, amplification cascades and neural pooling. Invoking retinal sensitivity for arbitrary tissue is like pointing to a radio telescope to prove that a kitchen spoon can receive pulsars.

The fourth level, evolved communication, asks an additional question: is there evidence that sender or receiver characteristics have been shaped to use the channel? Signals usually show some adaptation to transmission. Fireflies produce light deliberately and have eyes tuned to it. Bacteria using quorum sensing synthesize and detect molecules through regulated machinery. Electrical synapses have junctions. A biophotonic communication system should eventually reveal comparable architecture, even if subtle.

The strongest possible discovery would not be a dramatic photograph. It would be a boring causal chain. Change sender state. Measure a reproducible change in photon output. Permit only the relevant wavelengths to reach a receiver while excluding chemical, mechanical, thermal and electromagnetic paths. Observe a receiver response. Block the photons and lose the response. Restore a matched photon pattern artificially and recover it. Identify the absorbing molecule. Alter that molecule genetically or pharmacologically and abolish the effect.

That is a signal.

Anything short of that may still be interesting. It just belongs on a lower rung. The ladder is not a device for dismissing unconventional ideas. It is how we prevent a real emission from being burdened with a function it has not yet earned.
