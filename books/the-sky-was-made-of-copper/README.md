# The Sky Was Made of Copper

## Project West Ford, the Artificial Ionosphere, and the Day We Put 480 Million Antennas in Orbit

By Sven Hardy Benson

## Thesis

Project West Ford is a forgotten hinge in technological history. American engineers attempted to manufacture a radio-reflecting layer by releasing hundreds of millions of hair-thin copper dipoles into orbit. The experiment worked well enough to demonstrate its radio principle, alarmed astronomers enough to trigger an international argument about who gets to modify the shared sky, and failed messily enough that clumped remnants persisted far longer than the clean engineering model expected. It is a book about the moment space stopped being scenery and became infrastructure—and about how quickly infrastructure becomes pollution when assumptions are wrong.

The book does not claim West Ford permanently damaged space or caused unrelated atmospheric phenomena. The documented story is strange enough.

## Evidence classes

1. **Verified/declassified/documented facts.** Project purpose, launches, dipole dimensions and quantities, 1963 communications demonstration, contemporary scientific opposition, and subsequent tracking of clumped remnants.
2. **Credible but incomplete reporting/testimony.** Participant recollections and later historical accounts that require checking against program records.
3. **Disputed or weak claims.** Assertions of major persistent environmental effects or links to unrelated anomalous phenomena are not accepted without independent evidence.
4. **Speculative synthesis/open conjecture.** West Ford is treated as an early prototype of a recurring governance problem: technically rational actors can alter a commons before institutions possess either a consent mechanism or a mature failure model.

## Defeat condition

The thesis weakens if primary records show that the 1963 experiment did not meaningfully demonstrate the intended communications principle, that persistent clumping is materially overstated, or that the astronomical and international controversy had no meaningful institutional afterlife.
