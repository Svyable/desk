# What Counts as Solved

A tumor specimen can now do something it could not do for most of cancer history.

It can help define the treatment that will be made from it.

The tissue still answers the old questions. What kind of cancer is this? How aggressive does it look? Which proteins does it express? Which mutations or rearrangements place it inside a known molecular category?

Neoantigen medicine adds another question.

What did this tumor change that the immune system might be taught to see?

That is the smallest defensible meaning of the word solved in this book.

A targeting problem that once looked almost philosophical has become an engineering problem.

Cancer comes from the patient. The immune system is built to tolerate the patient. For decades, cancer immunology kept running into that fact. Many tumor-associated targets were also versions of normal self. Attack them too weakly and the cancer survived. Attack them too strongly and healthy tissue could become collateral damage. The central discrimination problem was not merely how to kill a malignant cell. It was how to distinguish malignant self from healthy self with enough precision that a powerful immune response could be useful.

Somatic mutations offered an answer because they record where the malignant lineage departed from the inherited body.

A mutation can alter a protein. Cellular machinery can process that altered protein into a peptide. An HLA molecule can display the peptide. A T cell can recognize the peptide-HLA complex. The target can therefore be specific not only to a cancer type but to one tumor's evolutionary history.

That chain contains enough conditions to prevent the word answer from becoming the word cure.

The mutation may not be expressed. The altered protein may not generate the expected peptide. The patient's HLA molecules may not present it. A useful T-cell receptor may not exist or may not expand. The tumor may suppress the response. The relevant clone may represent only part of the disease. HLA presentation may be lost. Manufacturing may take too long.

Yet the chain can now be measured and acted upon.

That is new enough to matter.

The field no longer has to argue only from isolated observations that tumors sometimes contain mutation-specific T cells. It can sequence patients prospectively, rank candidate neoantigens, manufacture individualized vaccines or discover individualized receptors, treat people through repeatable platforms and compare outcomes in trials.

The positive Phase 3 melanoma topline announced by Merck and Moderna in August 2026 marks the strongest transition so far from possibility to platform. According to the companies, intismeran autogene added to pembrolizumab improved recurrence-free survival and distant metastasis-free survival compared with pembrolizumab alone after complete resection of high-risk melanoma. More than a thousand patients entered a trial in which the investigational product had to be designed and manufactured individually.

The finding remains a topline company report at this book's reporting cutoff. Full presentation, peer review, regulatory evaluation, longer follow-up and overall-survival data still matter. The product remains investigational unless and until regulators approve it.

Those caveats do not erase the milestone.

A large randomized trial has reported that a process which changes its molecular payload patient by patient can improve clinically important outcomes in a defined cancer setting.

Nine days later, BioNTech supplied the necessary counterweight.

The company terminated a Phase 2 trial of autogene cevumeran as adjuvant monotherapy in ctDNA-positive resected colorectal cancer after an independent monitoring board concluded further continuation was unlikely to change the efficacy outcome. No new safety signal was reported, but the study had already crossed a futility boundary and was stopped.

The proximity of the two announcements is almost too useful for a book writer.

It prevents triumph from becoming theology.

The same broad therapeutic idea can produce success in one setting and failure in another. Melanoma and colorectal cancer are not interchangeable immune environments. A vaccine used with checkpoint blockade is not the same regimen as a vaccine used alone. Mutation burden, residual disease, HLA, timing, clonal architecture and the surrounding immune system remain consequential.

Personalization does not abolish disease biology.

It gives medicine another way to interrogate it.

That is why the next stage of the field will be less glamorous and more informative than the first.

The question is no longer whether neoantigens exist.

It is where they matter enough to justify intervention.

Some cancers may become excellent vaccine diseases after surgery because residual burden is low and the immune system needs only to police microscopic disease. Some may require checkpoint blockade to make vaccination useful. Some may need engineered T cells because vaccination alone cannot generate enough force. Some may offer recurrent driver neoantigens that support prebuilt receptor or antigen libraries. Some may escape through HLA loss or immune exclusion so consistently that another therapeutic principle is needed.

A technology becomes medicine by learning its boundaries.

That includes learning when not to use it.

The history of oncology is full of treatments that became better after their eligible populations became smaller. A drug discovered for one broad cancer type is restricted to tumors carrying a particular mutation. An immune therapy becomes useful only in a biomarker-defined subgroup. A toxic regimen is abandoned in patients who can be spared it.

Precision is often subtraction.

Neoantigen medicine will probably mature the same way. The platform may become more powerful while the claims around each use become narrower.

That is progress, not retreat.

The title The Cancer Key risks suggesting that a lock has finally met its universal key. The biology points toward the opposite image.

The useful discovery is that the lock can be read.

Once a tumor's molecular history is legible, a key can sometimes be cut for that lock. If the tumor changes, another key may be needed. If the lock was never accessible to the immune system, a key alone is insufficient. If the patient carries an HLA type for which the platform has sparse evidence, the cut is made with greater uncertainty.

The breakthrough is a key-making capability.

Capabilities change industries more profoundly than individual products because they can migrate across products.

The same target-discovery logic can feed an mRNA vaccine, synthetic peptides, DNA, dendritic-cell approaches or engineered T-cell receptors. The same sequencing infrastructure can support therapy selection and recurrence monitoring. The same HLA databases can help both academic researchers and commercial platforms. The same manufacturing lessons can inform other individualized medicines.

This is why the ownership question cannot be reduced to who owns the successful vaccine.

The valuable object is distributed across a stack.

Public agencies funded cancer genomics, immunology, clinical networks and basic RNA science. Universities built methods and patented inventions. Open databases accumulated epitope and HLA knowledge. Patients entered experimental protocols. Private companies invested in target-selection systems, delivery, quality control, manufacturing, global trials and regulatory development. Pharmaceutical partners supplied checkpoint inhibitors and established oncology infrastructure.

The finished capability is neither a government invention handed to industry nor a private invention built from nothing.

It is an accumulation system.

That matters because accumulation determines what happens after success.

If clinical use produces proprietary datasets that let one company improve target selection faster than everyone else, data can become the strongest moat. If manufacturing remains difficult, factory capacity can become the moat. If regulators accept one validated platform first, regulatory experience can become the moat. If a vaccine works only with one companion therapy for which the evidence base is strongest, the combination can become the moat.

The bottleneck moves.

Public policy that focuses only on the original patent may arrive at the right argument too late.

The more useful question is which layer has become scarce enough to control access and learning.

If the scarce layer is HLA evidence for underrepresented populations, public research can fill it.

If it is manufacturing capacity, public procurement, nonprofit facilities or qualified contract manufacturers can widen it.

If it is clinical outcome data, reimbursement and trial agreements can require structured return without forcing raw genomes onto the open internet.

If it is comparative evidence, public trial networks can ask questions a product sponsor has little incentive to finance.

If it is geography, community oncology networks can connect local patients to centralized expertise.

The objective is not to eliminate private advantage.

Without private advantage, much of the capital required to translate uncertain biology into a regulated product will go somewhere else.

The objective is to prevent private advantage from becoming scientific dependence where dependence is unnecessary.

That distinction becomes clearer when the first product succeeds.

Before success, exclusivity can look like an incentive.

After success, the same exclusivity can look like a toll.

Both descriptions may be accurate at different moments.

The public bargain therefore needs time in it.

Early development may justify stronger protection because failure risk is enormous. Mature manufacturing may justify stronger competition because the process has become routine. A proprietary clinical dataset may deserve a period of advantage while still being expected eventually to replenish a governed research commons when public funding or reimbursement contributed materially to its creation.

Static ideology is a poor match for a learning technology.

The manufacturing system learns too.

Personalized medicine initially looks expensive partly because every patient seems to require a new production run. That does not mean every part of the cost remains unique.

The sequencing instruments are shared. The algorithms are shared. The manufacturing suites are shared. The quality system is shared. The delivery platform is shared. The electronic records are shared. The validation work is shared. Automation can reduce manual labor. Better sample handling can reduce failures. More accurate prediction can reduce wasted payload. Regional networks can shorten logistics.

The sequence changes.

The industrial system does not start from zero.

This is the difference between bespoke and artisanal.

Bespoke describes the output.

Artisanal describes the production method.

A mature personalized-medicine industry will try to keep the first while eliminating as much of the second as possible.

Whether price follows cost will be a separate question.

The first approved platform, if it arrives, may be expensive for reasons that are real: individualized manufacturing, low early volume, complex logistics, checkpoint combinations, failed batches, long clinical development and the need to finance programs that never succeed.

As those costs fall, market power may allow price to remain high.

That is where competition matters more than moral argument.

A second validated platform is a pricing policy.

A shared-neoantigen alternative is a pricing policy.

A cheaper interchangeable companion drug can be a pricing policy.

A public or nonprofit manufacturing benchmark can be a pricing policy.

A biomarker that identifies which patients do not need treatment can be a pricing policy.

The health system rarely gets one lever large enough to solve the bill alone.

The patient experiences the result as one question.

Can I get the treatment?

That question may be more consequential than the list price.

A therapy can be covered and still inaccessible if no nearby site knows how to order it. It can be approved and still inaccessible if tumor tissue was discarded or exhausted. It can be technically available and still inaccessible if the manufacturing queue is too long. It can be scientifically appropriate and still inaccessible if the patient's HLA type, tumor type or disease course falls outside the evidence base.

Access begins before reimbursement.

It begins with who gets biopsied and sequenced.

It begins with which populations entered the training data.

It begins with whether a community hospital can preserve a specimen correctly.

It begins with whether a clinical trial opened in the region.

It begins with whether the patient has enough time to wait.

The phrase patient of one can hide all of this because it makes personalization sound like independence from institutions.

The opposite is true.

The more individualized the output becomes, the more coordinated the surrounding institution has to be.

A factory for one requires standards for thousands.

That is the book's second meaning of solved.

A scientific insight is not fully solved when a laboratory can demonstrate it once. It becomes solved in an industrial sense when the system can repeat it reliably across ordinary variation: different hospitals, specimens, HLA types, shipping routes, manufacturing loads and clinical teams.

The large melanoma trial suggests that individualized manufacturing can operate at a scale far beyond a single academic laboratory.

Commercial deployment will be the harsher test.

Trials protect their own workflows.

Healthcare exposes them.

The third meaning is clinical.

A process can be scientifically and industrially solved while remaining clinically narrow. If a platform can manufacture thousands of perfect vaccines but only one disease setting shows meaningful benefit, the achievement is real and limited.

Clinical success has to remain disease-specific until evidence earns generalization.

This is where the frontier market is most vulnerable to confusion.

A direct-pay clinic can offer genuine sequencing, genuine HLA prediction and a genuinely manufactured personalized product. Every technical step can be real. The clinical inference can still be unsupported.

One company's Phase 3 result does not validate another provider's product.

One tumor type does not validate another.

One delivery technology does not validate another.

The category can become legitimate before every member of the category does.

That makes regulatory vocabulary unusually important.

Investigational should mean investigational.

Expanded access should mean access outside a trial under an investigational framework, not approval by implication.

A physician-led treatment route in another country should be described under the law that actually governs it, not as a rhetorical escape from regulation.

A certificate showing that a batch meets manufacturing specifications should not be presented as proof that the batch will improve survival.

The more sophisticated the science becomes, the less useful visual sophistication is as a test of legitimacy.

Evidence has to carry the distinction.

That leads to the fourth meaning of solved, the one no regulator can decide alone.

A public-health problem is not solved merely because a product exists.

It is solved to the extent that population outcomes change.

A treatment used by a small number of wealthy, well-connected patients can be a scientific masterpiece and a public-health footnote. A moderately expensive, reliably delivered treatment that prevents recurrence across thousands of ordinary patients may matter more than a spectacular rescue available only at a handful of centers.

This does not diminish the rescued patient.

It changes the unit of judgment.

Public health counts people whose names never become stories.

If neoantigen vaccination succeeds in the adjuvant setting, its ideal result may be invisibility. A tumor is removed. The patient receives a personalized product. Microscopic disease is eliminated or controlled. Recurrence never appears.

Nothing happens.

The absence is the outcome.

That is a difficult breakthrough to narrate and an easy one to measure if systems follow patients long enough.

Long follow-up will determine how much of the promise is prevention and how much is delay.

It will show whether immune memory continues to police disease years after vaccination. It will show whether escape mechanisms eventually erode the benefit. It will show whether overall survival separates despite effective salvage therapies after recurrence.

The industry will move faster than those answers.

It has to.

Companies cannot wait a decade between every product decision. Regulators and payers also make decisions under incomplete follow-up. The answer is not paralysis. It is a system capable of revising itself.

Approval can expand when evidence strengthens.

Claims can narrow when evidence weakens.

Manufacturing can improve under controlled change procedures.

Prices can be renegotiated as competition and cost change.

Guidelines can move therapies earlier or later in care.

Postmarket data can reveal populations missed by trials.

Public policy can respond to whichever bottleneck becomes visible next.

The technology is adaptive.

Its institutions have to be as well.

That may be the most realistic reason for optimism.

Cancer has defeated many therapies by changing. The neoantigen approach is built around measuring change itself. Mutation is not only the source of resistance. It is also the source of the target. A tumor's instability can expose it.

That does not guarantee victory. Evolution can remove targets as easily as it creates them. Presentation machinery can fail. The microenvironment can remain hostile. The immune system can tire.

But medicine is no longer asking only how to find one vulnerability that every cancer shares.

It can read what this cancer became.

Then it can decide whether anything in that history is useful.

The shift is from universal target to renewable search.

That is a smaller claim than humanity solved cancer.

It is also more durable.

A universal target can fail once and collapse an entire strategy.

A search system can learn why a target failed and look again.

The system will sometimes return no useful answer.

That is important to admit.

Some tumors will have too little visible antigen. Some patients will have no well-supported HLA-target combination. Some cancers will progress too quickly. Some immune environments will remain inaccessible. A future in which sequencing is universal does not imply a future in which every sequence yields a treatment.

Precision medicine needs the right to say there is no good target here.

Otherwise prediction becomes sales.

The mature platform will be judged partly by how well it recognizes its own limits.

A good model should know when an HLA allele lies outside its strongest evidence. A good manufacturer should report when specimen quality makes the design unreliable. A good clinician should know when a trial in another cancer does not support the proposed use. A good regulator should permit uncertainty where patients rationally accept it without allowing uncertainty to be marketed as certainty.

The common requirement is calibrated confidence.

That is an institutional achievement as much as a scientific one.

The word solved should therefore remain uncomfortable.

It is useful because it forces the standard upward.

If the field claims cancer's private mutations are a key, then the key should have to prove more than immunogenicity. It should have to alter clinically meaningful outcomes. The factory should have to deliver it reliably. The algorithm should have to work across more than the easiest HLA types. The price should have to become compatible with real access. Experimental markets should have to preserve honest uncertainty. Publicly supported learning should have to replenish the field instead of disappearing entirely into private silos.

No single product can meet all of those standards by itself.

That is the point.

The cancer key is a system achievement.

The science made the lock legible.

Industry is learning to cut the key.

Medicine has to learn which doors it opens.

Public institutions have to make sure the locksmith does not become the only person who can read the map.

And cancer will keep changing the locks.

Solved, in that world, does not mean finished.

It means we know how to make the next move.
