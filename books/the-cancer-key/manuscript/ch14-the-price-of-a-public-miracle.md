# The Price of a Public Miracle

The easiest number in medicine to criticize is the price.

It is visible.

The harder numbers sit behind it.

How much did the successful company spend on programs that failed? How much public research made the field possible? How much manufacturing cost is truly patient specific? How much value does the treatment create by preventing recurrence? How much bargaining power comes from patent protection rather than scientific superiority? How much of the price is needed to fund the next generation of trials, and how much becomes return to shareholders?

The invoice cannot answer these questions.

A personalized cancer therapy will make them harder.

There is no single factory cost because each patient triggers a molecular workflow. Tumor sequencing, normal sequencing, RNA analysis, target selection, patient-specific synthesis, release testing and logistics all create real expenses. If the product is combined with a checkpoint inhibitor, the regimen includes the cost of that drug as well. Follow-up imaging, immune monitoring and clinical visits add more.

The therapy can be expensive before anyone earns excess profit.

That fact can become a shield against asking whether the final price is justified.

Pharmaceutical pricing in the United States is not generally cost-plus pricing. Companies do not calculate raw materials, add a standard margin and stop. Price reflects clinical value, available alternatives, market size, expected duration of use, competitive position, payer bargaining and the company's need to recover research and development costs across successful and failed programs.

A personalized product will be priced inside the same system.

If intismeran eventually prevents a large fraction of melanoma recurrences when added to pembrolizumab, the economic value could be substantial. Recurrent melanoma can require additional immunotherapy, targeted therapy, radiation, surgery and hospitalization. It can reduce productivity and quality of life. It can kill.

Avoiding those costs creates value even if the vaccine itself is expensive.

The payer will ask how much value.

The manufacturer will answer with trial data and health-economic models.

The disagreement will be political because the patient cannot shop the way a consumer shops for an ordinary product.

A person at high risk of cancer recurrence is not bargaining over a television.

The threat of death changes willingness to pay.

Insurance exists precisely because individuals cannot bear that bargaining position alone.

But insurance also weakens the normal consumer price signal. The doctor recommends a treatment. The insurer negotiates or sets coverage terms. The patient may see only a copayment, or may face a devastating share of the cost depending on benefit design.

The market is distributed among parties with different incentives.

Personalization intensifies the asymmetry because substitutes can be scarce.

If one FDA-approved individualized vaccine exists for a specific melanoma setting, the payer may have no therapeutically equivalent personalized competitor. It can compare the product with the standard treatment without the vaccine, but not with another vaccine offering the same demonstrated benefit.

That is temporary market power created by evidence.

The company spent money to produce the evidence.

Society still has an interest in preventing the evidence from becoming a permanent tollbooth.

This is where public funding changes the moral argument more than the accounting.

The public did not pay Moderna's entire oncology development bill. It did fund major portions of the scientific world in which mRNA, genomics, immunology and cancer vaccines became possible. It funded universities that produced relevant inventions. It funded tumor databases and clinical research. During the pandemic, governments also bought enormous quantities of mRNA vaccines and supported manufacturing expansion.

Public contribution reduces the plausibility of a story in which the finished product is purely private creation.

Private risk reduces the plausibility of a story in which the company is merely charging rent on a government invention.

The policy has to work between those caricatures.

The United States has tried explicit reasonable-pricing conditions before.

In 1989, NIH inserted a reasonable-pricing clause into cooperative research and development agreements with private companies. The clause reflected concern that products developed through public-private collaboration should be available to the public on reasonable terms.

Industry objected that the language was vague and discouraged collaboration.

NIH removed the requirement in 1995.

The episode is a useful warning for both sides.

Price conditions can express a legitimate public claim and still be poorly designed if companies cannot predict how the condition will be applied.

Investment depends on rules that are knowable before success.

The answer is not to abandon access conditions.

It is to make them more specific.

A public funder can tie conditions to concrete forms of support rather than all upstream science. If government directly pays for a manufacturing facility, the contract can reserve surge capacity or public procurement rights. If a public program finances a pivotal trial, it can negotiate data-access and pricing provisions in advance. If a university grants an exclusive license, it can include development milestones and access terms for low-income markets.

The bargain becomes proportional to the contribution.

Another approach is public equity or royalties.

If taxpayers accept venture-like risk, they can receive venture-like upside.

Government already takes royalty-free licenses and other rights in some federally funded inventions, while universities can earn royalties from patents. A more explicit public-return model could route a share of exceptional commercial gains into research funds or patient-access programs.

The attraction is symmetry.

The public participates when the investment wins.

The drawback is governance.

Government would have to decide which projects deserve equity-like terms, how to value them and whether profit motives distort public research priorities. A royalty can also increase marginal product cost and be passed back to payers.

A public return is not automatically a public benefit.

The destination of the money matters.

Competition may be more powerful than recoupment.

If several companies can build clinically effective personalized vaccines, price pressure and manufacturing innovation may produce more public benefit than government collecting a percentage from one dominant licensee.

This argues for keeping foundational tools broadly available and avoiding unnecessarily broad exclusive licenses.

The public earns its return when no company can permanently own the field.

There is also a case for direct public capability.

Academic cancer centers already manufacture experimental cell and vaccine products. National health systems and public research hospitals have clinical laboratories. As individualized manufacturing becomes more automated, non-profit or public platforms could produce certain therapies, especially for rare cancers or populations too small to attract commercial development.

This would not require nationalizing the pharmaceutical industry.

It would create a benchmark competitor.

If a public or non-profit platform can make a personalized peptide vaccine under rigorous quality standards for a fraction of a commercial price, the private company has to justify the premium through stronger evidence, faster turnaround, better outcomes or easier delivery.

Public capacity can discipline a market even when most products remain private.

The difficulty is that manufacturing is only part of the cost.

Large randomized trials are expensive. Regulatory submissions are expensive. Pharmacovigilance is expensive. A public factory without an evidence program risks reproducing the mini-clinic problem at government scale: technically plausible products offered without enough knowledge of whether they help.

The state has to finance evidence as seriously as production.

This is why direct public drug development has historically been selective.

The system can fund basic science across thousands of projects more easily than it can choose which specific products deserve billion-dollar late-stage commitments.

Markets are good at concentrating capital around a thesis.

They are also good at concentrating ownership around the winner.

The optimal system may deliberately use both.

Let private capital compete to translate open science.

Use public policy to keep entry possible, require evidence, negotiate prices and preserve data for future competition.

The price debate also changes when the patient population is small.

A common drug can recover development cost across millions of prescriptions. A personalized neoantigen therapy may address a narrower group defined by cancer stage, surgery status, biomarker and treatment history. The manufacturing process then changes again for every individual.

The company will argue that a high price is necessary to recover fixed development costs across a limited market.

The payer may accept that argument if the benefit is large.

This is the logic that already supports expensive rare-disease therapies.

The danger is platform accounting.

If the same manufacturing and target-selection platform serves melanoma, lung cancer, kidney cancer and pancreatic cancer, how much fixed cost should be allocated to each product?

A platform company can reuse much of its infrastructure while pricing each indication as if it were individually rare.

This is where cost transparency can become useful.

Not because government needs to reimburse only at cost.

Because policymakers need to know when genuine bespoke expense has turned into scalable platform economics.

The first million-dollar genome would have been impossible to sell as a routine diagnostic.

Sequencing cost collapsed because the platform scaled.

Personalized vaccine manufacturing may follow a similar, though not identical, curve.

The molecule changes. The process can still improve.

If price does not follow any portion of that efficiency, the public will correctly ask who captured the learning curve.

The mini-clinic market already offers a crude preview.

Direct-pay individualized cancer-vaccine programs in Europe advertise packages costing tens of thousands of euros. A German clinic, IOZK, publicly lists substantial charges for testing and vaccine cycles. Other providers market individualized peptide or mRNA services at prices paid directly by patients.

These prices are not directly comparable with a future FDA-approved product.

The evidence, manufacturing systems, treatment protocols and included services differ.

But the existence of a cash market reveals demand before regulatory approval.

People will pay large sums for personalization under uncertainty.

That willingness to pay creates a pricing anchor from below.

If an experimental clinic can charge tens of thousands without randomized evidence, an approved sponsor with Phase 3 data will have little reason to price cheaply unless payers force it to.

This is why reimbursement policy matters early.

Waiting until launch gives the company a completed product and the public a patient constituency demanding immediate access. Negotiation becomes politically difficult because delay itself imposes harm.

Rules established before approval are more credible.

Value-based pricing offers one route.

A payer can link payment to clinical outcomes, paying more when the therapy prevents recurrence and less when it fails. Personalized therapies are conceptually suited to this because patients are already tracked closely.

The problem is attribution.

If a patient receives vaccine plus pembrolizumab, which therapy gets credit? If cancer recurs after three years, was the product a failure or did it buy three valuable years? If the patient moves insurers, who bears the original cost and who receives the later savings?

Healthcare systems are bad at contracts whose benefits unfold over long horizons.

National health systems have an advantage here because payer continuity is stronger.

The U.S. system fragments the time horizon among Medicare, employers, commercial insurers and patients.

A therapy that prevents costs five years later may be paid for by an insurer that will not cover the patient five years later.

The economics of prevention are therefore structurally undercoordinated.

Adjuvant cancer vaccines are prevention after cancer.

They inherit this problem.

There is a more basic form of public return that pricing debates often miss.

Time.

If a public research system helps a therapy arrive five years earlier than it otherwise would, patients receive years of life before any royalty calculation begins. Faster scientific progress is itself a benefit.

Policy that extracts too much value from successful firms can reduce that speed.

Policy that extracts too little can reduce access and public trust, which can also slow progress through political backlash.

The optimal bargain is dynamic.

The early stage may deserve strong exclusivity.

The mature platform may deserve more competition.

The rare indication may justify a premium.

The mass-market indication may not.

The publicly financed trial may justify access conditions.

A wholly privately financed manufacturing improvement may justify stronger private return.

The system needs rules capable of distinguishing these cases.

Slogans cannot.

The price of a public miracle is not zero.

The question is who pays which part of it, who owns the resulting capability and whether success leaves the public richer in more than invoices.

Price becomes more legible when the regimen is separated from the product.

A personalized vaccine may have one list price. The patient may also require a checkpoint inhibitor, administration, laboratory monitoring, imaging, pathology work and management of adverse events. Some of those costs would have occurred under standard care anyway. Others are incremental because the personalized therapy was added.

The payer's question is therefore not “What does the vaccine cost?”

It is “What does this strategy cost compared with the best alternative, and what additional outcome does it buy?”

That sounds like a technical health-economics question.

It is also a moral one because the answer depends on what value is assigned to delayed recurrence, avoided metastasis, quality of life and years of survival. Different health systems make those tradeoffs differently. The United States often tolerates higher launch prices and more fragmented bargaining. National systems can negotiate harder but may delay or restrict adoption when cost-effectiveness is uncertain.

Neither model removes scarcity.

They locate the rationing in different places.

A high U.S. price can ration through premiums, prior authorization and patient cost sharing. A national payer can ration through a coverage decision or narrower eligibility. A manufacturer with limited capacity can ration through slots even when everyone agrees to pay.

The patient experiences all of these as access.

That is why price alone should not become the book's villain.

A therapy priced at half as much but available only at six centers may remain inaccessible. A costly therapy with rapid outpatient delivery and broad insurance coverage may reach more people. A cheap experimental product can waste money if it does not work.

Affordability is the intersection of price, coverage, capacity and benefit.

The magnitude of benefit also changes the acceptable price in ways relative statistics can obscure.

Suppose a treatment reduces the relative risk of recurrence by a meaningful percentage. The absolute number of recurrences prevented depends on baseline risk. Treat a group in which recurrence is common and many events can be avoided. Treat a lower-risk group and the same relative effect prevents fewer events while exposing more people to cost and toxicity.

This makes risk stratification an economic technology.

Better selection can improve cost-effectiveness without lowering the product price at all.

If ctDNA, stage, pathology or other markers identify patients most likely to recur, the health system can concentrate personalized vaccination where the expected absolute benefit is largest. The therapy becomes more expensive per treated patient and cheaper per recurrence prevented.

That is a more useful metric.

It also creates an access hazard.

Biomarker-driven coverage can exclude people near thresholds. A negative ctDNA result may reflect assay sensitivity rather than true absence of disease. A rural patient whose sample was mishandled can become “low risk” administratively for reasons that have nothing to do with biology.

Precision reimbursement inherits precision medicine's measurement errors.

Outcome-based contracts create another apparent solution.

Pay the full amount if the patient remains recurrence free; refund part if cancer returns.

The appeal is intuitive and the implementation is messy.

A patient may recur for reasons unrelated to target selection. The therapy may delay recurrence substantially without preventing it. Follow-up can move between health systems. The definition of failure has to be set years before the event. A manufacturer cannot control whether the patient completes checkpoint therapy, whether surveillance is performed on schedule or whether later treatments affect survival.

Risk sharing quickly becomes risk argument.

Simpler contracts may work better: staged payment linked to treatment completion, rebates tied to predefined early outcomes, or price adjustments after real-world evidence matures. The exact mechanism matters less than the principle that uncertainty at launch does not have to be priced as certainty forever.

The opposite should also be true.

If long-term data reveal durable benefit larger than expected, a payment system should not make future development impossible by pretending the early uncertainty never resolved. Dynamic evidence deserves dynamic bargaining.

Manufacturing learning complicates the bargain again.

The first commercial years may carry high per-patient costs because facilities run below optimal scale, manual review is extensive and remakes are common. As volume grows, automation improves and failure rates fall. Those savings may legitimately finance expansion for a period. Eventually they become margin unless price moves.

A mature system needs some way to observe the learning curve.

Not every proprietary cost line has to be published. But purchasers can ask about turnaround, success rates, capacity utilization and major process changes. If the platform is demonstrably becoming easier to deliver, future prices can be negotiated with that reality in mind.

This is one reason long-term exclusive contracts can be dangerous in a rapidly improving technology.

They can freeze the economics at the moment of maximum uncertainty.

Shorter contracts with predictable renegotiation let both sides learn.

The public-funding debate belongs inside this dynamic model too.

A public grant made twenty years earlier does not tell us the fair launch price. A government-funded manufacturing expansion made two years earlier may be directly relevant to capacity and cost. A public trial network that reduces development expense can justify a specific access condition. A public database that every competitor uses may be better rewarded through continued openness than through tracing pennies into a single product.

Proportionality is more defensible than ownership mythology.

The public should claim strongly where it took specific risk or created specific leverage.

The company should claim strongly where it took specific risk and built specific capability.

The patient should not be asked to adjudicate the argument at the pharmacy counter.

That is ultimately why insurance and public policy exist.

The purpose of financing is to decide the bargain before one frightened person has to make it alone.

There is one additional price that almost never appears in the health-economic model: the patient's time spent making the system work.

Personalized treatment can require extra blood draws, pathology retrieval, consent visits, travel, insurance calls, scheduling around manufacturing and repeated coordination among providers. A family member may take time off work to manage logistics. A patient may remain near a specialized center because an infusion date depends on product release.

Those burdens are costs even when the hospital never bills them.

They fall unevenly.

A salaried professional with flexible work and family support can absorb a week of administrative uncertainty differently from someone paid hourly, caring for children or living far from the treatment site. Two patients can have identical insurance coverage and very different practical access.

This is why a lower treatment price does not automatically create equity.

A system can remove the copayment while leaving the travel, unpaid leave and coordination burden untouched.

Commercial platforms have an incentive to reduce some of this friction because smoother logistics improve completion rates. Health systems should measure the patient burden explicitly rather than assuming operational efficiency inside the company captures it.

The same principle applies to companion testing.

If the vaccine is covered but a necessary genomic assay is not, the treatment has been priced incompletely. If one insurer recognizes the sequencing as part of the therapeutic pathway and another treats it as an optional diagnostic, access can vary before the drug claim is ever filed.

Bundled payment can solve some fragmentation and create other problems.

A single episode price for sequencing, vaccine manufacture and administration can simplify reimbursement. It can also obscure which component failed or how much margin sits at each layer. Separate billing improves visibility and creates transaction cost.

The payment architecture should follow the clinical architecture closely enough that no necessary step becomes an orphaned expense.

That is a mundane standard.

It is the kind that determines whether a scientific breakthrough feels like medicine or a scavenger hunt.

The eventual price debate will probably focus on a headline number because headline numbers travel well.

The more important question is the total burden required to convert an eligible patient into a treated patient and the clinical benefit produced at the end.

A public miracle is affordable only when the whole path is.
