# The Regulator's Clock

Cancer runs on biological time.

Regulation runs on evidentiary time.

The two clocks rarely agree.

A patient with rapidly progressive disease can worsen in weeks. A randomized trial can require years to enroll, treat and follow enough people to detect a meaningful difference. A regulator charged with protecting the population has to demand evidence that may arrive after the individual patient asking for access is dead.

This is the ethical pressure underneath every argument about experimental cancer drugs.

It cannot be solved by pretending one clock is illegitimate.

The FDA's job is not to decide whether a desperate person is allowed to hope. It is to decide whether a sponsor has shown that a product is sufficiently safe, effective and consistently manufactured for the claims and market access being requested.

Those standards are population standards.

Personalized medicine strains them because the product can change with every patient.

A conventional tablet has a defined active ingredient. A company manufactures many units intended to be materially the same. The clinical trial evaluates that product in a population.

An individualized neoantigen vaccine can contain a different sequence for every participant.

The sameness lies in the process.

The same sequencing logic is used. The same target-selection rules are applied. The same vector or RNA architecture carries the targets. The same manufacturing controls operate. The same dosing schedule is followed.

Regulation therefore has to evaluate a platform that repeatedly produces distinct products.

This is not unprecedented.

Autologous cell therapies already vary because every starting cell population comes from a different patient. Blood and tissue products have long required chain-of-identity controls. Personalized diagnostics interpret unique molecular profiles.

Neoantigen therapy pushes variability farther into the encoded active content.

The regulator's question becomes: which aspects are allowed to change without requiring the evidentiary system to start over?

That question will determine how fast the field scales.

If every new patient-specific sequence were treated as a completely new drug, commercialization would be impossible.

If every output of a platform were accepted automatically, a flawed algorithm could manufacture thousands of weak or unsafe products under one umbrella.

The regulatory object has to sit between them.

The process is the product, but the process must include rules for variability.

This is why individualized therapeutics outside cancer matter to oncology policy.

In 2026, the FDA issued draft guidance describing a “Plausible Mechanism Framework” for some individualized therapies intended for ultra-rare genetic diseases. The framework addressed a setting in which a treatment might be designed for one or only a few people, making conventional large trials impossible. The agency discussed ways to use mechanistic evidence, manufacturing controls and carefully structured clinical information when populations are extremely small.

The guidance was not written as a rule for personalized cancer vaccines.

It was a sign of institutional pressure.

Regulators are having to learn how to evaluate medicine when the traditional unit of repetition shrinks.

Cancer offers an advantage that ultra-rare genetic disease often does not.

The platform can be tested across many people even when each payload differs.

A thousand-patient melanoma vaccine trial can evaluate the strategy of individualized target selection and manufacturing. The population supplies statistical power at the platform level.

That may make neoantigen vaccines easier to regulate than true one-person genetic therapies.

The problem returns when small clinics offer personalized products whose platforms have never undergone that validation.

They can invoke individuality as a reason large trials are impractical even when dozens of similar patients could, in principle, be studied under a common protocol.

Individuality should not become an exemption from aggregation.

If the process repeats, the evidence can repeat.

The FDA already has tools for speeding access when preliminary evidence is compelling.

Accelerated approval can allow certain drugs for serious conditions onto the market based on surrogate endpoints reasonably likely to predict clinical benefit, with confirmatory obligations. Breakthrough Therapy designation can intensify agency interaction when early clinical evidence suggests substantial improvement over available therapy. Priority review can shorten review timelines.

These programs are not lower standards in the simple sense.

They are attempts to move learning earlier while preserving obligations to confirm benefit.

The danger appears when confirmatory evidence does not arrive or contradicts the surrogate.

Cancer history contains examples of accelerated approvals later withdrawn when follow-up trials failed to confirm benefit.

That is not necessarily proof the pathway failed.

A system that allows earlier access under uncertainty should sometimes reverse itself when the uncertainty resolves badly.

The integrity of the pathway depends on the reversal being real.

Personalized vaccines may create new surrogate temptations.

T-cell expansion is measurable quickly. Circulating tumor DNA can change before scans. Minimal residual disease can be detected at molecular levels. These biomarkers may become valuable indicators of whether an immune intervention is doing what it was designed to do.

The risk is treating a mechanistic biomarker as the endpoint because the clinical endpoint takes too long.

A vaccine can induce neoantigen-specific T cells without preventing recurrence.

A ctDNA decline can be encouraging without proving longer survival.

The surrogate has to earn its relationship to the outcome.

This is another reason large randomized trials remain important even in precision medicine.

They calibrate the shortcuts.

Once a biomarker is repeatedly shown to predict meaningful benefit, future development can move faster.

The first generation pays the evidentiary cost so the next generation can use a better map.

Expanded access sits on a different part of the regulator's clock.

It does not approve a product.

It permits treatment access for individual patients or groups under defined conditions when standard options are inadequate and trial participation is not possible.

The FDA reviews the request, but the company must agree to provide the product.

That company discretion is often criticized.

Why should a dying patient need permission from a corporation?

Because the corporation owns a limited investigational supply and remains responsible for development, manufacturing and safety reporting. One patient's access can consume product needed for a trial, create logistical work or generate adverse-event information that affects the program.

The explanation is rational.

It is not emotionally satisfying.

Right to Try emerged partly from that dissatisfaction.

Federal law created a pathway for certain patients with life-threatening diseases to seek eligible investigational drugs after exhausting approved options and being unable to participate in a clinical trial. The law limits the FDA's preauthorization role.

But it cannot manufacture the drug.

The sponsor can still refuse.

This reveals a general truth about access policy.

Legal permission is not capacity.

A personalized product makes the capacity problem sharper because the company cannot pull a spare vial from inventory. It may have to run the patient's tumor through the complete manufacturing process.

Access becomes production.

That is one reason mini-clinics can claim an ethical advantage.

Their business is built around saying yes.

The mainstream sponsor is built around proving something to the regulator.

A socially useful system needs both responsiveness and evidence without making patients choose between them.

One path is platform-expanded access.

Once an individualized manufacturing platform has accumulated substantial safety data, regulators and sponsors could create broader protocols allowing patients outside pivotal trials to receive treatment while contributing standardized outcomes. The product would remain investigational, but the access system would be designed for learning rather than one-off exceptions.

Another path is decentralized trials.

Much of the neoantigen workflow is already distributed. Local oncologists can collect samples and administer injections while central laboratories perform sequencing and manufacturing. Remote monitoring can reduce travel. Academic centers can supervise complex aspects without requiring every patient to relocate.

The clinical-trial site can become a network instead of a building.

This would reduce one reason patients seek direct-pay foreign treatment: geography.

Regulators also need a strategy for algorithm updates.

A target-selection system that improves every year should not be frozen in the version used in the original trial if the newer system demonstrably performs better.

But continuous change can outrun validation.

The agency may need a predetermined change-control framework analogous to emerging approaches for adaptive medical software: define which model changes are permitted, what validation is required, how performance is monitored and when a major change triggers additional review.

This is especially important when the algorithm affects populations unevenly.

A model update could improve common HLA alleles while degrading rare ones. Aggregate performance might rise while a minority group loses accuracy.

Post-market surveillance therefore has to look below the average.

Manufacturing changes raise the same issue.

If a company automates a manual step and cuts production time from six weeks to three, patients benefit. Requiring a new Phase 3 trial for every process improvement would freeze the platform.

Allowing unrestricted changes could undermine comparability.

Regulators already manage manufacturing supplements and comparability for biologics. Individualized products will make the framework more central.

The best regulation may become less about approving a static object and more about governing an evolving system.

This shift has political consequences.

A platform company will want broad approval language so it can improve the product without returning for constant review. Competitors and public-interest groups will want enough transparency to know whether the system still resembles the tested version.

The regulator becomes the keeper of continuity.

It has to know when a changing product is still the same product in the ways that matter.

International differences complicate the task.

A patient can travel.

Tumor data can cross borders electronically. Manufacturing can occur in one country and treatment in another. A product unavailable under U.S. law may be offered under a physician-led pathway in Europe or another jurisdiction.

National regulation is confronting transnational personalization.

This creates incentives for regulatory tourism.

Patients seek countries with faster access. Companies choose jurisdictions with favorable rules. Clinics locate where individualized treatment pathways are more permissive.

Competition among regulators can be useful if it reveals better ways to manage risk.

It can become a race to the bottom if jurisdictions compete for medical tourism by weakening evidence and oversight.

The difference is data.

A permissive jurisdiction that requires transparent outcomes can teach the world.

A permissive jurisdiction that allows treatment to disappear into private files creates commercial access without public learning.

The latter wastes the risk patients are already taking.

Every experimental patient is generating evidence whether the system records it or not.

The ethical failure is letting the evidence evaporate.

The regulator's clock should therefore be judged by two speeds.

How fast can a plausible therapy reach a patient?

How fast can the system learn whether it helped?

Moving the first clock without the second creates a marketplace of permanent uncertainty.

Moving the second without the first creates perfect evidence after too many patients have died waiting.

The ambition should be synchronized learning.

Treat earlier where uncertainty is ethically acceptable.

Measure harder.

Publish failures.

Tighten or expand access as evidence changes.

Withdraw claims that do not survive.

Let manufacturing and algorithms improve under controlled rules.

Personalized medicine does not make regulation obsolete.

It makes regulation more like medicine.

The rules have to adapt to the patient without forgetting the population.

There is a third clock inside that last sentence.

The product itself changes while the evidence is still maturing.

A first-generation manufacturing process may be slow and heavily manual. A later version may automate sequence transfer, reduce release-testing time or move production closer to treatment centers. The target-selection algorithm may absorb new immunopeptidomic data. The diagnostic assay may improve sensitivity. None of these changes waits politely for overall-survival follow-up to finish.

The regulator therefore has to manage three distinct speeds.

Access asks how soon a patient can receive a plausible therapy.

Evidence asks how soon the system can know whether the therapy improves meaningful outcomes.

Change control asks how quickly the platform can improve without becoming a different, untested intervention by increments.

Treating those as one clock produces bad policy.

A fast review cannot compensate for a slow factory. A flexible manufacturing policy cannot compensate for weak efficacy evidence. A strong randomized trial cannot justify every future algorithm update automatically.

Each clock needs its own standard.

In May 2026, the FDA issued final guidance on chemistry, manufacturing and controls flexibilities for developing human cellular and gene therapy products for a biologics license application. The guidance was not a rule for individualized cancer vaccines, and it should not be read as one. Its relevance is institutional: the agency explicitly recognized that advanced biological products can need risk-based flexibility in CMC development while still requiring adequate assurance of quality.

Flexibility is often mistaken for relaxation.

The useful distinction is evidence proportionality.

A manufacturing change with little plausible effect on product quality should not demand the same burden as a change that can alter potency, identity or safety. An automation step that performs a validated operation more consistently may deserve a different evidentiary response from a new formulation that changes where the RNA travels in the body.

The same logic can govern individualized platforms.

Change only the patient-specific sequence within a validated design space and the platform should not restart development. Change the rules that select the sequence, the delivery system or a critical release attribute and more evidence may be needed.

The design space becomes regulatory infrastructure.

A company has an incentive to define it broadly because broad space means operational freedom.

A regulator has an incentive to define it carefully because hidden novelty can enter through a broad category.

The negotiation is not paperwork.

It determines whether the commercial product can learn.

Post-market surveillance then carries more weight than it does for a static medicine.

An approved personalized platform can drift through software updates, new sites, new analytical methods and a changing patient population. Each individual modification may be justified. The accumulated system after five years can still differ meaningfully from the one evaluated in the pivotal trial.

Continuous evidence is how the approval remains attached to the product.

That evidence should include ordinary safety reporting, but personalized manufacturing creates additional signals worth tracking.

How often does a patient enter the process and never receive a product?

How often does sequencing fail?

How often is a batch rejected or remade?

How long does manufacture take across sites, not only on average but in the delayed tail?

Do particular HLA groups receive fewer high-confidence targets?

Do algorithm versions change target diversity or clinical outcomes?

Does moving production to a regional facility alter release failure or turnaround?

These questions are too operational to fit the public image of drug safety.

They are exactly how a platform can become unequal or unreliable after approval.

A conventional pharmacovigilance system is good at asking whether patients developed an adverse event after receiving a drug.

A personalized platform also needs vigilance for patients harmed by non-delivery.

A six-week delay that causes a rapidly progressing patient to miss the treatment window may never appear as a drug adverse event because the person never received the drug.

The system still failed clinically.

This is why failure-to-treat belongs in the denominator after commercialization.

The regulator does not need to decide every hospital appointment. It does need enough information to know whether the approved strategy remains practicable for the population described in the label.

Capacity complicates the approval/access distinction further.

A regulator can approve a product without guaranteeing manufacturing slots. A payer can cover it without creating a factory. A physician can prescribe it without controlling turnaround. Formal access can therefore exist while effective access is rationed by production.

That problem will tempt policymakers to ask regulators to solve scarcity they do not control.

The better response is coordinated governance.

Regulators can require truthful performance reporting. Payers can avoid reimbursement rules that create unnecessary delay. Governments can support manufacturing capacity where scarcity threatens access. Companies can build redundant supply and publish service-level performance. Hospitals can standardize specimen handling so avoidable failures do not consume capacity.

No single institution owns the clock.

The patient experiences only elapsed time.

The same is true of evidence after accelerated or conditional pathways.

A confirmatory trial obligation that begins years late is not equivalent to one already enrolling at approval. A withdrawal process that takes years after benefit fails to confirm does not fully preserve the original bargain. Speed at the front end creates a reciprocal obligation to speed the back end.

Earlier access is ethically defensible when later correction is operationally credible.

That requires infrastructure before controversy.

Trial protocols can be agreed early. Data systems can be built to capture outcomes. Manufacturing versions can be linked to patients. Registry participation can be part of coverage. Withdrawal criteria can be explicit enough that a negative result is not renegotiated from zero after patients and revenue depend on the product.

The regulator's authority matters most when everyone already wants a different answer.

That is why good rules are written before the pivotal result.

Personalized medicine will also force agencies to cooperate internationally because the platform is naturally transnational.

A target-selection algorithm can be centralized in one country, a patient specimen sequenced in another, a product manufactured in a third and administered in a fourth. Duplicating every quality review nationally can slow access without producing proportional safety. Accepting every foreign judgment automatically can import weaknesses the local system cannot see.

Shared technical standards offer a middle path.

Common expectations for chain of identity, sequence provenance, batch records, adverse-event definitions and outcome reporting can make evidence portable without requiring legal systems to become identical.

Regulatory convergence is not regulatory surrender.

It is agreement about what must remain legible when the product crosses borders.

The fastest future is therefore unlikely to come from removing the regulator.

It will come from moving regulatory attention toward the places where uncertainty actually lives.

A stable step should become routine.

A changing step should remain visible.

A plausible shortcut should be calibrated against clinical outcomes.

An early-access decision should create a later evidence obligation.

A manufacturing improvement should be allowed to improve the product without hiding a new one inside the old name.

That is a more demanding regulator than a slow one.

It is also a faster one.

The goal is not to make every clock tick at the same speed.

It is to stop pretending that only one of them matters.
