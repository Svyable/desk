# The Data Commons

The first generation of neoantigen medicine was built on data that researchers could often reach.

The next generation may be built on data they cannot.

This is the quietest shift in the field and potentially the most important.

Public genomic projects made tumor mutations broadly legible. Academic laboratories published HLA-binding measurements. The Immune Epitope Database accumulated experimentally characterized epitopes. Researchers released prediction methods and benchmark datasets. Clinical papers reported which vaccine targets produced T-cell responses.

The scientific commons was imperfect, but large enough to let many groups enter the problem.

Commercial success changes the source of the most valuable information.

Once thousands of patients receive individualized therapies, every treatment produces a longitudinal record that no static public atlas contains.

The tumor sequence is known.

The HLA type is known.

The candidate neoantigens are known.

The selected targets are known.

Manufacturing quality is known.

Post-treatment T-cell responses may be measured.

Circulating tumor DNA may be tracked.

Scans reveal recurrence.

Later therapy and survival are recorded.

The dataset connects prediction to consequence.

That is the dataset the field ultimately needs.

A target-selection model should not merely predict what binds HLA. It should learn which target sets, in which disease settings, delivered through which modalities, produce immune responses that alter clinical outcomes.

Those labels arrive only after patients are treated.

The company with the largest treatment network can therefore build a learning advantage that compounds with market share.

The better model attracts more use.

More use generates more outcomes.

More outcomes improve the model.

The improved model makes the platform more attractive.

This is the familiar data-network effect of digital platforms appearing inside a drug business.

It changes competition.

A new vaccine company can buy sequencing machines. It can hire excellent immunologists. It can use public HLA-prediction software and manufacture clinical-grade RNA.

It cannot buy ten years of linked outcomes from another company's treated patients unless those data are sold or shared.

The moat becomes historical experience.

Medicine has always valued experience. A great cancer center knows things because it has treated many unusual cases. The difference is that machine-learning systems can convert distributed experience into a scalable proprietary asset.

The expertise leaves the head of the physician and enters the platform.

That shift can improve care.

A model trained on thousands of real treatments may choose better neoantigens than an academic algorithm trained primarily on binding assays. It may recognize subtle combinations of clonality, expression, HLA context and disease state that researchers did not anticipate.

Private concentration can produce genuine technical superiority.

The policy problem begins if superiority becomes impossible to challenge because the evidence required to compete is locked inside the incumbent.

This is not the ordinary patent problem.

Patents expire.

A proprietary clinical dataset can keep growing.

The first company to achieve scale can remain ahead even after core patents weaken because every new patient improves the private map.

Antitrust law is poorly designed for this kind of biomedical advantage.

A company may not have acquired competitors or fixed prices. It may simply have learned faster because it treated more people.

The resulting concentration can be efficient and still socially consequential.

The public has a special claim here because many of the outcomes are generated inside publicly financed healthcare.

Medicare may pay for the treatment. NIH may fund the cancer center. A public university may employ the physician. The patient's earlier tumor sequencing may have been supported by a research grant. Yet the most valuable derivative learning can flow into a private model under data-use agreements the patient never sees.

The public pays for the event that trains the private system.

This is not automatically exploitation.

Companies need patient-level data to improve products and satisfy regulators. Hospitals need commercial partners. Patients may benefit directly from the learning loop.

The question is whether enough of the learning returns.

A useful data-commons policy would not require publishing every raw genome.

That would be reckless.

Genomic information is identifying and familial. Tumor data can reveal germline variants. Rare cancers and uncommon HLA types can make supposedly anonymous records recognizable. Clinical timelines can be linked with outside information.

Privacy is not a technical footnote.

The commons needs governance.

One model is a controlled research environment.

Participating companies and hospitals contribute standardized de-identified or pseudonymized records to a secure repository. Approved researchers query the data without freely downloading raw patient files. Access is logged. Outputs are reviewed for privacy risk. Commercial use can be permitted under defined terms rather than prohibited categorically.

The goal is not to make private investment impossible.

It is to prevent clinically important learning from becoming permanently non-reproducible.

Another model is reciprocal access.

A company receiving public funding, trial-network support or reimbursement for an individualized therapy could retain exclusive use of detailed outcome data for a limited period, after which defined fields enter a shared research resource.

The temporary advantage rewards investment.

The delayed release preserves the commons.

This resembles the logic of patents more than the current model of indefinite data accumulation.

A third model is federated analysis.

Data remain at hospitals or companies while standardized algorithms run across participating sites and return aggregate results. This can reduce privacy risk and allow institutions to retain local control.

The approach is technically more complex and vulnerable to inconsistent data quality.

It is still better than assuming the only choices are total secrecy or public release.

Standardization may be the hardest part.

Neoantigen data are messy.

Different companies define candidate targets differently. HLA callers differ. Variant pipelines differ. Immune assays differ. Recurrence endpoints differ. Manufacturing timestamps are recorded from different starting points. A T-cell response can be measured through ELISpot, intracellular cytokine staining, multimer assays, TCR sequencing or other methods.

A common repository full of incompatible fields becomes a data swamp.

The public sector is well positioned to create standards because standards are public goods.

NCI, FDA, standards organizations, academic consortia and industry could define a minimum data model for individualized cancer immunotherapy.

The fields need not expose every proprietary feature.

They could include disease, stage, treatment setting, sample quality, mutation class, HLA alleles, number of candidate and selected targets, broad target characteristics, manufacturing success, turnaround, dosing, adverse events, immune response categories, ctDNA status, recurrence and survival.

The exact design should be made by experts, not by a book.

The principle is enough.

If the treatment is individualized, the evidence infrastructure has to remember the individual in a standardized way.

This would also improve regulation of frontier clinics.

A boutique provider could participate in the same registry as a large sponsor without pretending its treatment has equivalent evidence. The provider's results would be tagged by product type, manufacturing standard and legal pathway.

Over time, the system could see whether particular clinic-based approaches produce signals worth formal testing or safety problems requiring intervention.

The frontier would become observable.

Observation is more useful than blanket suspicion.

The data commons could also expose inequality in the algorithms.

HLA diversity varies across populations. If trial enrollment and training datasets overrepresent people of European ancestry, prediction models may perform better for common alleles in those populations. Personalized medicine could therefore reproduce population-level inequity at the target-selection stage.

Without shared performance data, the problem can remain invisible.

A regulator may see one company's aggregate trial result and find it acceptable. A public dataset could allow independent researchers to ask whether patients with rare HLA alleles received fewer high-quality targets, had more manufacturing failures or generated weaker immune responses.

Personalization should be audited for who gets well personalized.

The same principle applies to tissue quality and geography.

Patients at large academic centers may enter the pipeline with well-preserved surgical samples and expert pathology review. Patients referred from smaller hospitals may arrive with old paraffin blocks, limited biopsies or incomplete molecular records.

A manufacturing failure can look like random attrition when it is actually an infrastructure disparity.

The data can show where access breaks before the drug is ever administered.

This is one reason public return should be measured beyond drug price.

A society can negotiate a 20 percent discount and still allow its most valuable clinical data to accumulate privately.

The discount saves money now.

The lost learning can cost future competition.

Conversely, a company can charge a high price and still create public value if it returns rich data that accelerates the field and helps competitors develop better therapies.

Price and openness are different policy levers.

They can be traded.

A government procurement contract could accept a higher price in exchange for structured data return and broad manufacturing rights after a period. A university license could reduce royalties for companies that contribute outcomes to a shared repository. Public trial networks could prioritize sponsors willing to use common data standards.

The public does not need one mechanism.

It needs negotiating assets.

Data are increasingly one of them.

The companies will resist broad sharing for understandable reasons.

They will argue that data are expensive to generate, privacy obligations are heavy, competitors can free-ride and context is lost when records are stripped from the proprietary platform that created them.

Those arguments deserve weight.

A poorly designed data mandate could reduce investment or flood the field with uninterpretable records.

The answer is not to pretend the tension disappears because medicine is socially important.

It is to design reciprocity.

Companies benefit from public datasets every day.

A mature biomedical economy can ask successful platforms to replenish the resource.

The analogy is not taxation.

It is crop rotation.

A field that only extracts becomes less fertile.

The most optimistic future for neoantigen therapy is one in which clinical use generates more knowledge than any company can use alone.

A million personalized treatments would contain an extraordinary map of human tumor immunity. They could reveal recurring neoantigens, rare HLA opportunities, common escape routes, better vaccine architectures and new T-cell receptors.

If the map is fragmented across corporate silos, progress will depend on mergers and bilateral deals.

If enough of it becomes a governed commons, the field can support more competitors, more academic discovery and more treatments for populations too small to build proprietary datasets quickly.

The first public map was the cancer genome.

The next public map should be what happened after we tried to treat it.

The hardest part of building that map may be deciding what a failed treatment record means.

A patient can receive a well-manufactured product containing targets that were predicted appropriately and still recur. The failure might belong to the targets. It might belong to immune suppression, tumor heterogeneity, timing, combination therapy or biology no current assay captured. A common database that labels the entire event simply nonresponse can teach a model the wrong lesson.

Clinical labels are not ground truth merely because they are outcomes.

They need context.

This is where the old disciplines of medicine remain essential inside a data-heavy field. Pathologists have to describe the specimen. Oncologists have to record the disease setting and intervening therapies. Immunologists have to distinguish failure to generate T cells from failure of generated T cells to control cancer. Bioinformaticians have to preserve which model version selected the targets. Manufacturing teams have to record deviations and delays.

A row in a database can contain a whole causal argument disguised as fields.

Poorly designed fields freeze bad assumptions at scale.

That is why the data model should evolve with the science.

Early registries may record only whether a candidate was selected and whether an immune response was observed. Later versions may need clonality, peptide presentation, class I and class II features, spatial immune context, prior checkpoint exposure and measures of residual disease. The system should preserve old records without pretending old measurements were as rich as new ones.

Longitudinal science needs versioned data as much as software does.

The same is true of consent.

A patient who agrees to sequencing for one treatment may not have imagined that the combined record of germline DNA, tumor DNA, HLA type, immune repertoire and long-term clinical outcome could become training material for a commercial model used decades later. Broad consent can make future research possible, but broad cannot mean invisible.

Patients should be told when their treatment is also creating a reusable data asset.

That disclosure need not imply they are owed a personal royalty every time a model improves. The economics would quickly become absurd if every observation generated perpetual micropayments across a learning system. It does mean the institution should be honest about the secondary value and about who can access it.

Trust is easier to maintain before the value becomes obvious.

The history of genomics contains repeated episodes in which participants learned later that samples or data had been used in ways they did not expect. The legal details differ across cases. The recurring lesson is that permission obtained through a dense form is weaker than legitimacy sustained through a relationship.

Personalized cancer medicine has an opportunity to begin differently because the learning loop is visible from the start.

Every patient knows the treatment was designed from information specific to them.

The platform can explain that the same process becomes better by learning across many people.

That is not a sinister secret.

It is the scientific proposition.

A commons worthy of the name should also return something to participants beyond abstract social benefit.

One return is portability. Patients should be able to receive useful records of what was learned about their tumor and what was actually administered. If they recur at another hospital, the new team should not have to reconstruct the treatment from a billing code and a PDF summary.

The record should identify the sample, the relevant sequence data or accessible location, HLA type, selected targets, product composition, treatment dates, manufacturing platform and major immune or molecular follow-up results where available.

This does not require exposing proprietary model weights.

It requires treating the patient's therapeutic history as part of the patient rather than as exhaust owned by the vendor.

Another return is auditability.

If a platform consistently performs less well for certain HLA alleles or specimen types, clinicians and regulators need to know. The company may be allowed to keep its model private. It should not be allowed to keep clinically significant blind spots private indefinitely.

The August 2026 FDA draft guidance on potency assessment for active immunotherapy products is relevant to this broader direction even though it does not prescribe a neoantigen data commons. The agency is explicitly thinking about how potency assurance should work for products intended to induce or modulate immune effector cells, including peptide- and protein-based active immunotherapies. That is evidence that the measurement problem is becoming a regulatory object rather than remaining only an academic concern.

Potency is not identical to clinical outcome.

It is one more bridge that has to be documented.

For a mass-produced vaccine, a potency assay asks whether repeated lots meet a standard related to expected biological activity. For a personalized product, the assay strategy has to coexist with intentional variation in antigen sequence. The exact solution will differ by modality. The data generated through that solution can become another private learning layer unless common concepts and enough performance information are shared.

The commons therefore includes standards for measurement, not only records of patients.

Without common measurement, data volume can create false confidence.

Ten thousand immune-response records generated by incompatible assays may be less useful than one thousand carefully harmonized records. A company with internally consistent methods can learn quickly from its own data while the public field fails to compare across platforms.

This is another way private integration can beat public fragmentation without any company doing anything improper.

The public response should be to improve the shared measurement infrastructure, not to punish integration.

There is a precedent in genomics.

Reference materials, common file formats, benchmark datasets and standardized variant nomenclature allowed sequencing laboratories to disagree usefully rather than merely incompatibly. They did not erase proprietary instruments or software. They created a language in which performance could be compared.

Neoantigen medicine needs the equivalent language across the full chain from variant to peptide to immune response to outcome.

The details will be technical and tedious.

That is why they matter.

Markets are good at financing a product that can produce a dramatic result. They are less reliable at financing the grammar that lets everyone compare results.

The public sector has repeatedly been useful at grammar.

The data commons should also remember time.

A five-year melanoma outcome can overturn the interpretation of a one-year immune response. A model trained on early recurrence may undervalue a target set that creates slower but more durable immune control. An adverse effect may emerge after the period that interested the original sponsor. A company may be acquired while patients remain in follow-up.

The dataset cannot end when the press release does.

Long-term follow-up is expensive precisely because the commercial event often arrives earlier than the scientific answer.

Public registries and cooperative research networks can keep watching after a product has become routine or a sponsor's attention has moved elsewhere.

That continuity becomes more valuable when the product changes patient by patient. There is no single frozen molecule from which later researchers can infer what earlier patients received. The record is part of reproducibility.

A commons also needs a rule for correction.

Clinical data are not fixed once collected. A pathology interpretation can change. A sequencing pipeline can later identify an artifact. A death date can be reconciled across records. A recurrence initially coded as local may later be shown to have been metastatic. If a shared dataset cannot amend its history without destroying traceability, it will become simultaneously permanent and wrong.

The right model is closer to version control than to publication.

An original observation should remain attributable. A correction should be visible. Analyses should identify which data release they used. Models trained on a particular version should be reproducible against that version even after the registry improves. This is ordinary practice in serious data engineering and should become ordinary in serious personalized medicine.

Correction matters commercially too.

A company that discovers a systematic target-selection weakness should be able to improve its platform without pretending the old decisions never happened. The public record should allow later investigators to ask whether outcomes changed after the correction. A learning system that erases its mistakes cannot prove it learned.

This is the governance bargain in miniature.

The company gets to improve.

The regulator gets to audit.

The scientific community gets enough continuity to compare.

The patient gets a durable account of what happened.

None of those requires that every raw field become open on day one.

They require that the history not disappear.

The most radical claim in this chapter is therefore also the most administrative.

A personalized medicine should leave behind a standardized, durable account of what was attempted and what happened.

Not every account must be public.

Not every field must be shared immediately.

Not every company must surrender the data advantage it paid to create.

But the system should reject the idea that thousands of patient-specific experiments can vanish into mutually inaccessible silos and still count as an efficient scientific economy.

The patients already supplied the heterogeneity.

The institutions should supply the memory.
