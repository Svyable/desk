# Hope for Sale

A treatment can be experimental and still be sold with a price.

The price changes the relationship.

Research asks a patient to accept uncertainty so medicine can learn. Commercial care asks a patient to pay for a service expected to help. Experimental direct-pay care does both at once.

The provider says the treatment is uncertain.

The invoice says the treatment is a product.

That tension is not unique to neoantigen vaccines. Stem-cell clinics, exosome businesses, unapproved regenerative products and overseas cancer programs have operated in the same space for years. The scientific mechanism is often plausible enough to sound sophisticated. The quality of evidence varies enormously.

Neoantigens raise the stakes because the underlying field is now producing genuine breakthroughs.

A weak provider does not need to invent fake biology.

It can borrow real biology from someone else's trial.

In 2026, a website operating under the name PYRALIS advertised a personalized mRNA cancer vaccine for a stated price of 75,000 Swiss francs. The site described a process involving tumor sequencing, selection of up to dozens of neoantigens and multiple doses. It also stated that the treatment was experimental and not approved by any regulatory authority.

That combination is revealing.

The marketing does not need to hide the lack of approval if the customer is specifically shopping for experimental access.

“Not approved” can become part of the frontier identity.

The patient is not being promised ordinary medicine.

They are being sold earlier medicine.

Whether any particular provider can deliver what it advertises, whether its manufacturing meets appropriate standards and whether its treatment helps patients are separate factual questions that require evidence. The point is not to declare every direct-pay program fraudulent.

The point is that a market now exists in which a personalized cancer-vaccine concept can be packaged like a purchasable service before regulatory approval.

The transaction depends on a special kind of demand.

The buyer is purchasing possibility.

Most markets punish poor products through repeat behavior. If a restaurant disappoints, the customer goes elsewhere. If a phone breaks, reviews accumulate. The producer faces a population of buyers who can compare alternatives and learn over time.

Experimental cancer care does not work like that.

The individual buyer may have no second chance.

The outcome is also noisy. A patient may live longer because the therapy worked, because surgery removed all disease, because chemotherapy helped, because the cancer was biologically indolent or because the original prognosis was wrong. A patient may worsen because the therapy failed, because the cancer was too advanced, because manufacturing took too long or because no available treatment would have changed the course.

Without a control group and systematic follow-up, almost any individual outcome can be explained after the fact.

This protects bad theories from failure.

It also prevents good theories from proving themselves.

The provider can accumulate stories rather than evidence.

A dramatic responder becomes the face of the program. A patient with stable disease is described as controlled. A transient biomarker decline becomes encouraging. A strong laboratory immune response becomes proof the vaccine “worked” even when scans later worsen.

None of these statements must be deliberately deceptive.

Human beings are good at converting ambiguous events into narratives that fit what they already hoped.

The patient does it.

The physician can do it.

The company can do it.

Randomization exists partly to protect everyone from the story they want to tell.

Direct-pay experimental medicine weakens that protection.

The strongest marketing language is often technically careful.

A provider may write that neoantigen vaccines have “shown promise,” that personalized vaccines can “activate tumor-specific T cells,” or that mRNA technology has “demonstrated potential.” These claims can be supported by real studies.

The customer reads a stronger message.

This might save me.

The gap between the literal claim and the inferred claim is where hope becomes monetizable.

Scientific literacy does not necessarily close the gap.

A well-educated patient can read the papers and still misjudge the evidence because oncology studies are difficult to compare. Phase 1 trials focus heavily on safety and feasibility. Phase 2 trials may use surrogate endpoints or small cohorts. Single-arm studies cannot control for patient selection. Immune-response assays can be meaningful without proving clinical benefit.

A clinic can provide a bibliography and still overstate what the bibliography supports.

The most dangerous word is often personalized.

Personalization creates a feeling that population-level evidence is less relevant because the treatment is being designed for the individual.

That intuition is partly correct.

If every vaccine contains different targets, no trial tests the exact molecular sequence another patient will receive.

The platform must therefore be evaluated as a process.

But personalization does not make statistics optional.

The question is whether patients treated through the process do better than comparable patients receiving existing care.

The process can be individualized and the evidence can still be population based.

This is how Moderna and Merck's large trial works. Every patient's vaccine differs, but the randomized comparison evaluates the treatment strategy.

A clinic that says its products are too personalized for clinical trials is not embracing precision.

It is avoiding the level at which the product can be tested.

The economics of direct pay create another distortion.

An approved medicine is usually reimbursed only after evidence has been reviewed by regulators and payers. A direct-pay experimental service can collect revenue before clinical utility is established.

The provider therefore has a business model that does not require the trial to succeed.

This does not prove the provider lacks scientific intent.

It changes the incentives.

A commercial sponsor developing an FDA-approved product has to spend enormous sums before meaningful revenue. Failure destroys value. The regulatory system forces the sponsor to make a large bet on whether the evidence will survive.

A clinic can receive revenue from each treatment attempt.

Failure may reduce reputation but does not automatically halt sales, especially if outcomes are heterogeneous and poorly reported.

The market can finance itself through uncertainty.

This is why transparent registries matter so much.

If every direct-pay neoantigen treatment generated a standardized public outcome record, the business would become more epistemically expensive. Providers would no longer be able to preserve only the successes. Patients could see how many people were treated, how many completed manufacturing, how many experienced serious adverse events and what happened over time.

The denominator would become part of the product.

Adjacent fields show what happens without it.

The FDA has repeatedly warned consumers about unapproved stem-cell and exosome products marketed for diseases they were not authorized to treat. Regulators have cited risks including infections, contamination and serious adverse events. Warning letters have targeted businesses whose products were marketed in ways that required approval they did not have.

The lesson is not that every new biological treatment belongs in the same category.

It is that technical legitimacy at the level of cell biology does not guarantee product legitimacy.

Stem cells are real.

Exosomes are real.

Neoantigens are real.

The question is what evidence supports this product, made by this process, for this disease.

Cancer makes enforcement politically delicate.

If a regulator acts against a clinic, patients can experience the action as government taking away their last chance. The clinic can frame itself as an innovator being blocked by bureaucracy. Families may testify that a treatment helped them.

The regulator then appears to be arguing against hope.

This is a powerful narrative because delay in cancer really can kill.

The answer is not for regulators to become indifferent to speed.

It is to make speed a regulated capability.

Expanded access is one attempt. Right to Try is another. Accelerated approval and breakthrough designations are others in appropriate settings. Adaptive trials and real-world evidence can reduce some of the distance between laboratory promise and patient access.

The system should be judged by how quickly it can learn, not simply by how slowly it can say no.

Frontier clinics expose every place the mainstream system learns too slowly.

They also expose why learning matters.

A patient facing death has a different tolerance for uncertainty from a healthy person considering a preventive therapy. Ethical medicine should respect that difference.

It should not convert it into a lower standard for truthful claims.

This is the central moral distinction.

Risk tolerance belongs partly to the patient.

Evidence description does not.

A provider should not inflate the evidence because the patient is willing to accept risk.

Consent is only meaningful when the uncertainty is described accurately.

Financial consent belongs beside medical consent.

A patient considering a 50,000- or 75,000-unit direct-pay treatment should know what happens if sequencing yields no usable targets. They should know whether manufacturing failure is refundable. They should know whether the quoted price includes adjuvants, immune monitoring, hospitalization, travel and follow-up. They should know whether standard therapy will be delayed.

Experimental care can be financially toxic even when medically safe.

Oncology already uses the term financial toxicity to describe the harm caused by treatment costs: debt, depleted savings, reduced adherence, stress and tradeoffs with housing or basic needs.

Direct-pay frontier care concentrates the risk because insurance often does not absorb it.

The family becomes the payer of last resort.

Crowdfunding adds another layer.

A compelling story can raise money for an experimental treatment, which can make the therapy feel socially validated before it is medically validated. Donors are funding hope, not conducting evidence review.

A successful campaign can therefore send market signals unrelated to clinical effectiveness.

The more emotionally legible the treatment, the easier it is to finance.

“Personalized vaccine made from your tumor” is extraordinarily legible.

The phrase compresses complex uncertainty into a story of precision.

The cancer created a mutation.

The lab finds it.

The vaccine teaches the immune system.

The immune system attacks the cancer.

Every step is biologically plausible.

The chain can still fail.

Good regulation makes the failure points visible.

Good marketing would too.

A scientifically responsible clinic could publish its complete cohort outcomes, describe the legal status of the product, distinguish immune-response data from clinical benefit, disclose conflicts of interest and maintain external oversight.

It could charge for treatment and still contribute to evidence.

The distinction is not profit versus purity.

It is accountable uncertainty versus monetized ambiguity.

The same standard should apply to large companies.

Pharmaceutical marketing can also overextend early data. Investor presentations can emphasize favorable subgroup analyses. Press releases can use relative risk reductions without absolute numbers. Companies choose which programs to highlight and which failures disappear into pipeline tables.

Scale does not eliminate narrative incentives.

Regulatory filings, trial registries and securities laws create more compulsory disclosure.

That is one reason institutions matter.

Trust should not depend on the virtue of the seller.

It should depend on structures that make selective storytelling difficult.

Neoantigen medicine will test those structures because it gives every actor a powerful story.

The scientist sees the immune system recognizing cancer at molecular resolution.

The company sees a platform.

The physician sees an option for a patient running out of them.

The patient sees a key cut specifically for the lock killing them.

The metaphor is almost irresistible.

The responsible response is not to destroy the metaphor.

It is to keep the metal visible.

The key may fit.

Someone still has to test whether the door opens.
