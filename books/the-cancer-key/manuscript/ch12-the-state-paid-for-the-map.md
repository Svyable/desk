# The State Paid for the Map

A company can own a cancer drug.

It cannot own the history that made the drug thinkable.

Neoantigen medicine emerged from a public research system whose contributions are easy to miss because they rarely appear on the label.

The National Cancer Institute did not manufacture intismeran autogene. The National Human Genome Research Institute did not run Moderna's Phase 3 melanoma trial. The federal government did not write every target-selection algorithm or build every lipid nanoparticle.

The public contribution is more structural.

It paid for much of the map.

Cancer biology is a field in which knowledge accumulates through enormous shared investments. A single experiment may test one receptor or mutation, but the experiment relies on reference genomes, cell lines, sequencing methods, animal models, pathology classifications, statistical tools, clinical-trial networks and generations of trained researchers.

Public funding is unusually well suited to these foundations because their value is broad and difficult to capture in one product.

No company has a strong incentive to fund a thirty-year project whose main commercial output may be better opportunities for competing companies.

Governments can.

The National Institutes of Health became one of the world's largest engines of biomedical research by financing basic science, university laboratories, training grants, clinical research and its own intramural programs. NCI, as part of NIH, built an oncology-specific system that includes designated cancer centers, clinical-trial networks, epidemiology, genomics, screening research and the Frederick National Laboratory for Cancer Research.

Its budget is not a line item for drugs.

It is an attempt to maintain a field.

In fiscal year 2026, NCI's appropriation was about $7.35 billion. That money spans research far beyond neoantigens. The relevance is scale. A therapeutic platform can draw from discoveries generated by thousands of projects whose relationship to the eventual product was impossible to predict when the grants were awarded.

This is what basic research funding buys.

Options.

The Cancer Genome Atlas is one of the clearest examples.

NCI and NHGRI launched TCGA to characterize the molecular changes across many forms of cancer. The project eventually analyzed more than 20,000 primary tumor and matched normal samples across 33 cancer types and generated more than 2.5 petabytes of genomic information made available to researchers.

The project did not need to know which personalized vaccine would later use the knowledge.

It made the landscape legible.

Researchers learned which mutations recur, which pathways are disrupted, how tumors differ across organs and how genomic subtypes relate to biology. Methods for tumor-normal comparison improved. Computational pipelines were tested. Public datasets became training grounds for scientists who later joined biotechnology companies.

The value escaped the original grant.

That is the point.

Open science produces spillovers.

The Human Genome Project did the same at a larger scale. Reference sequences and sequencing technology became inputs into diagnostics, ancestry testing, rare-disease medicine, drug discovery and cancer genomics. The commercial value generated downstream was many times larger than any single public budget line.

Public investment can therefore look inefficient if judged like a venture-capital portfolio.

Most grants do not create blockbuster products.

The system succeeds because it does not have to know in advance which line of investigation will become foundational.

Neoantigen science depended on immunology as much as genomics.

Government grants supported research on HLA molecules, antigen processing, T-cell receptors, dendritic cells, immune tolerance and cytokines. The Immune Epitope Database, funded by NIAID, assembled experimental data and prediction resources used across infectious disease, allergy, autoimmunity and cancer.

Open HLA-prediction tools developed in academic environments became routine components of neoantigen pipelines.

A startup could begin with years of public knowledge already encoded in software.

The NCI intramural program contributed through another route: patients.

Steven Rosenberg's Surgery Branch and collaborators treated people with advanced cancer in experimental immunotherapy protocols over decades, building the conceptual and practical foundations of adoptive cell therapy. The work helped establish that T cells can recognize mutation-derived cancer antigens and that personalized receptors can be identified and used therapeutically.

The public laboratory could pursue an objective that a small company might struggle to finance: learn how antitumor immunity works, even when the first versions of treatment are too complex to commercialize.

The patients assumed risk too.

They entered experiments with uncertain benefit so future patients could know more.

Any accounting of public contribution that counts grants but not participants is incomplete.

The Cancer Moonshot added another layer.

The 21st Century Cures Act authorized $1.8 billion over seven years for the initiative launched in 2016, supporting research programs ranging from tumor atlases and immunology to prevention, data sharing and childhood cancer. Later Moonshot efforts broadened the goal toward reducing cancer mortality and improving the experience of patients and families.

The political rhetoric can be grander than the appropriations.

No program “ends cancer” by announcement.

But mission-driven funding can coordinate projects that ordinary grant mechanisms might leave fragmented.

Tumor atlases are especially relevant to neoantigens because immune targets are contextual. A mutation is only one part of a tumor ecosystem. Spatial maps of immune cells, stromal cells, gene expression and molecular states can help explain why the same target succeeds in one tumor and fails in another.

The public map keeps getting more detailed.

This creates a recurring commercial pattern.

Government funds precompetitive knowledge.

Universities and public laboratories publish discoveries.

Patents may be filed on particular inventions.

Startups license some of them.

Private capital finances product development.

A pharmaceutical company may acquire or partner with the startup.

A drug reaches the market.

The public then buys the drug through Medicare, Medicaid, the Veterans Health Administration, private insurance supported by tax preferences, or national health systems abroad.

The public can appear at both ends of the transaction.

This is what critics mean when they say taxpayers pay twice.

The phrase is rhetorically powerful and economically incomplete.

The second payment is purchasing a different thing from the first. Research funding buys knowledge and options. Drug reimbursement buys a manufactured product with clinical evidence, regulatory approval, distribution and ongoing support.

The fact that both payments are rational does not mean their terms are optimal.

The public can still ask whether it negotiated enough return when it funded the upstream risk.

One answer is that the medicine itself is the return.

Public research exists to improve health, not to make government a venture fund. If federal science enables a company to build an effective cancer therapy that would otherwise not exist, society has gained even if the company earns large profits.

That argument becomes weaker when access is narrow.

A cure no one can afford is not the same public benefit as a cure broadly available.

The right policy therefore depends on what actually limits access.

Sometimes price is the barrier.

Sometimes manufacturing capacity is.

Sometimes a lack of trained treatment centers is.

Sometimes insurance coverage is delayed.

Sometimes the therapy is useful only to a small biological subset.

Attaching a price cap to an upstream grant does little if the real bottleneck is a six-week manufacturing queue.

Conversely, celebrating the existence of the therapy does little for a patient whose insurer will not cover it.

Public-return policy has to follow the actual bottleneck.

Bayh-Dole is central to the debate because it created a durable framework for patents arising from federally funded research.

Before the Bayh-Dole Act of 1980, federally supported inventions were often owned or controlled by government agencies under inconsistent policies. Universities argued that promising discoveries languished because companies were reluctant to invest in development without secure licensing rights.

Bayh-Dole allowed universities, small businesses and other contractors to retain title to many federally funded inventions while giving the federal government defined rights, including a paid-up license and march-in authority under specified conditions.

The policy succeeded at creating a university technology-transfer industry.

Universities patented discoveries. Licensing offices negotiated with companies. Faculty founded startups. Venture investors could fund development around exclusive rights.

The line between public science and private enterprise became more permeable by design.

That permeability is one reason the United States became so good at turning university biology into venture-backed biotechnology.

It also made ownership harder for the public to see.

A discovery can be funded by an NIH grant, patented by a university, exclusively licensed to a startup, sublicensed to a pharmaceutical company and eventually sold in a product reimbursed by Medicare.

At each step, the underlying right changes hands while the original public contribution recedes from view.

The public retains legal interests in some federally funded inventions, but the government's march-in authority has rarely been a practical pricing mechanism. Agencies historically resisted using it to regulate the price of successful drugs, and the legal criteria have been contested.

By late 2025 and early 2026, federal policy around whether product price should play a role in march-in analysis remained unsettled.

This does not make Bayh-Dole irrelevant to price.

It means the law was designed primarily as a commercialization framework, not a modern drug-pricing statute.

Trying to force every affordability problem through it may produce more political symbolism than access.

There are other tools.

Government can negotiate prices directly where law allows. Public funders can include access conditions in specific contracts. Universities can use nonexclusive or socially responsible licensing. Agencies can require data sharing. Governments can fund competing manufacturers. Public health systems can use procurement volume to bargain. Patents can be licensed differently in low-income markets. Clinical-trial networks can require publication of negative results.

The public return can be designed at several layers.

Neoantigen medicine may require that broader view because no single patent owns the platform.

The product is assembled from many inputs: sequencing, prediction, RNA or peptide manufacturing, lipid delivery, immune checkpoints, clinical data and manufacturing know-how.

Even if one upstream patent were subject to public conditions, the final therapy could remain protected by other rights.

The strongest public asset may therefore be the scientific commons itself.

Open cancer data lowers barriers to entry. Open prediction tools let competitors test ideas. Publicly funded trials can compare products without depending entirely on sponsor priorities. Government cancer centers can preserve expertise outside private firms. Training grants distribute human capital across the field.

Competition is a form of public return.

A platform monopoly is easier to sustain when all high-value knowledge is private.

The state does not have to own the medicine if it keeps enough of the map open that someone else can build a competing road.

This is where data policy becomes as important as patent policy.

The first generation of neoantigen algorithms learned heavily from public datasets. The next generation may learn from proprietary treatment outcomes. If clinical data from thousands of personalized therapies remain siloed, the most important map can migrate into private hands after the public paid for the original terrain.

A sensible bargain could require more structured return of de-identified outcome data from publicly subsidized trials and reimbursement programs, with privacy protections strong enough for genomic information.

That idea sounds administrative.

It may be more valuable than a royalty.

The next startup can compete with an incumbent's patent more easily than it can compete with an invisible dataset accumulated across ten thousand patients.

Public science gave biotechnology its starting advantage.

The future question is whether society keeps replenishing the common substrate or allows each successful layer to close behind the company that commercialized it.

The state paid for the map because no one knew which road would matter.

Now that one road may lead to a valuable medicine, the map should not disappear.
