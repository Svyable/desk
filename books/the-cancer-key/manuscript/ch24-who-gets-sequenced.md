# Who Gets Sequenced

The personalized cancer factory begins with a sample.

That makes access unequal before anyone mentions the price of the drug.

A tumor has to be removed or biopsied. Enough malignant material has to survive pathology and handling. A normal sample may be collected. The material must reach a laboratory capable of producing data the therapeutic platform can use. The report has to return while the patient is still in the relevant clinical window. Someone has to know what to order, how to interpret it and what to do next.

The sequencing machine is often described as though it has already become ordinary infrastructure.

In some cancer centers, it has.

A newly diagnosed patient can move from pathology to broad molecular profiling almost by default. The oncology team may have access to next-generation sequencing panels, germline testing, RNA analysis, liquid biopsy and a molecular tumor board. Research protocols sit nearby. Tissue can be routed into biobanks. The electronic record can absorb a complex report. A specialist can distinguish an actionable driver from a passenger mutation and a validated biomarker from an interesting possibility.

That is one version of modern oncology.

It is not the only one.

Most patients do not receive cancer care at a handful of famous academic centers. They are treated in community practices, regional hospitals and systems with different pathology workflows, payer contracts, staffing and access to trials. Even when genomic testing is technically available, the path from availability to meaningful use can break at many points.

A test can be ordered too late.

The specimen can be inadequate.

Insurance can delay authorization.

The assay can be narrower than the future therapy needs.

A result can arrive as a dense report with no specialist available to interpret the uncertain findings.

The patient can be too ill by the time a trial slot appears.

A platform can be commercially available in theory and unreachable in practice.

Neoantigen medicine magnifies these frictions because the sequence is not merely diagnostic information.

It is raw material for the treatment.

When a molecular test is used only to select among existing drugs, a failed or delayed test can lead to a worse treatment decision. When the test is part of manufacturing, failure can prevent the individualized product from being made at all.

The diagnostic boundary dissolves.

This has consequences for reimbursement.

Health systems are accustomed to paying separately for tests and drugs. The laboratory submits one claim. The infusion center submits another. The pharmacy benefit or medical benefit pays for the therapeutic product under its own rules. Personalized neoantigen treatment links these activities so tightly that separate payment can create perverse gaps.

Who pays for sequencing when the main purpose is to manufacture a therapy that may never be successfully produced?

Who pays for repeat sequencing after relapse?

Who pays for RNA analysis or HLA typing if those tests are not independently necessary under an older coverage policy but are necessary for the personalized platform?

Does the therapy price include diagnostics, or does the patient enter a chain of separate coinsurance obligations before a dose exists?

These questions sound administrative until a denial arrives.

In the United States, Medicare has national coverage rules for some next-generation sequencing in cancer. The policy was a major recognition of genomic medicine, especially for advanced cancers and FDA-approved companion diagnostic contexts. It does not mean that every form of sequencing required for every future personalized therapy is automatically covered under every circumstance. Coverage depends on the test, indication, regulatory status and payer.

The distinction will matter more as neoantigen platforms demand richer inputs.

A broad tumor panel designed to find druggable driver mutations is not necessarily the same technical product as tumor-normal sequencing designed to identify private mutations for a personalized vaccine. A clinical laboratory may report dozens or hundreds of variants relevant to standard oncology while the neoantigen platform wants a different depth, a matched normal sample, HLA information, expression data or access to raw sequence files.

The data can be clinically useful and still be insufficient for manufacturing.

That creates pressure toward platform-controlled testing.

A company may prefer to receive tissue and perform or contract the sequencing itself. It controls quality, file formats, turnaround and the exact assays used by its algorithm. From an engineering perspective this is sensible. From a health-system perspective it can duplicate testing the patient already had.

One tumor can be sequenced once for diagnosis and again for therapy because the first test did not meet the second system's specifications.

The redundancy may be medically justified.

It can also be a symptom of non-interoperable markets.

The mature industry will have to decide which inputs can become portable.

If high-quality tumor-normal sequence data already exist, should a patient be able to send them to any qualified neoantigen platform? Or should every platform control its own laboratory pipeline because small differences in assay and processing affect target prediction? If a proprietary model was validated only on data from one sequencing workflow, accepting outside data can introduce uncertainty. If every company insists on proprietary sequencing, cost and tissue burden rise.

There is no universal answer, but the direction of travel matters.

Personalized medicine can become more modular or more vertically locked.

The first model treats sequencing as infrastructure. Qualified labs produce standardized data that different therapeutic platforms can use. Patients and health systems can choose among downstream services. Innovation occurs at each layer.

The second model treats the sequence as the first step inside a closed therapeutic product. The platform owns or controls the pipeline end to end. Integration improves consistency and learning. Switching becomes difficult.

Both models can work.

The public interest is not identical to either company's interest.

Interoperability can lower barriers to competition. Integration can lower error. The goal should be open seams where open seams are safe, not openness as an ideology.

That requires standards more precise than the slogan that patients should own their data.

Ownership does not make a file useful.

A patient can have a legal right to a genomic record and still receive a PDF when the next platform needs aligned sequence files, variant-call files, metadata about sample purity, details of library preparation and quality metrics. The difference between access and portability is technical.

The most valuable right may be a right to usable provenance.

What tissue was sequenced?

When was it collected?

What treatment had the patient received before collection?

What platform and chemistry were used?

What depth was achieved?

How was the normal comparator handled?

Which pipeline called variants?

Which genome build was used?

Can the original data be reanalyzed later?

Without that context, the sequence can become an expensive dead end.

This is already a challenge in precision oncology. Neoantigen manufacturing raises the stakes because reanalysis is one of the field's strengths. The tumor sequence generated today may become more useful tomorrow when prediction models improve or a new TCR library becomes available. Throwing away raw data because only the original report seemed clinically relevant wastes future option value.

Storage is cheap compared with another biopsy.

That is an argument for preserving data, not for making every genomic file universally public.

Cancer genomes are identifying. Germline information can reveal inherited risk and family relationships. Even tumor-only data can create privacy concerns. The more longitudinal and integrated the dataset becomes, the harder true anonymization can be.

The access bargain has to separate patient portability from public release.

A patient should be able to move relevant data through care without requiring that the data enter an open repository. Researchers should be able to study sufficiently protected datasets under governance that respects consent. Companies can retain proprietary improvements while still returning clinically and scientifically meaningful information to the patient and, where appropriate, the research commons.

The worst system is the one in which data are neither safely shared nor reliably portable.

Then fragmentation produces no privacy dividend and no learning dividend.

Geography is the next barrier.

A personalized product can be manufactured centrally and shipped, which sounds like a way to democratize access. In practice, the clinical site still needs capabilities. Tissue has to be collected correctly. The patient may need checkpoint therapy, specialized infusion, toxicity monitoring or immune assays. Trial protocols may require imaging and follow-up on a precise schedule. Some cell therapies require far more intensive infrastructure.

The mini-clinic frontier exists partly because entrepreneurs see an opportunity to package these steps outside major centers.

There is nothing inherently wrong with decentralization.

Community oncology itself is decentralization. Dialysis moved complex care outside hospitals. Infusion centers deliver therapies that once required more centralized settings. Home care is absorbing procedures that would have seemed impossible to move a generation ago.

The question is which complexity can safely travel.

For a personalized cancer vaccine with a relatively manageable administration profile, the answer may eventually be: quite a lot. A community oncologist could collect tissue, transmit data, receive a patient-specific product and administer it with a standard checkpoint inhibitor. The specialized intelligence lives in the platform rather than the building.

That is a powerful vision.

It can also create a franchise model for experimental medicine before evidence is mature.

If the platform is unapproved, if quality varies among manufacturers, or if clinical claims run ahead of trials, decentralization can make oversight harder. A polished portal can distribute not only medicine but certainty. The local physician may rely on a remote algorithm and a vendor's interpretation of the literature. Patients may assume personalization itself is evidence of effectiveness.

The more the center of expertise moves into software, the more important independent standards become.

This is why access and evidence cannot be treated as opposing values.

Critics of regulation sometimes frame the issue as bureaucrats versus dying patients. Defenders of regulation sometimes frame it as science versus quackery. Real patients encounter a messier system.

A treatment can be scientifically plausible, manufactured competently and still lack evidence that it improves outcomes.

A trial can be rigorous and still exclude the patient who needs an option now.

A regulator can prevent dangerous marketing and still impose delays that feel unbearable at the bedside.

A clinic can operate legally and still charge for an intervention whose benefit is deeply uncertain.

A patient can understand the uncertainty and rationally choose to proceed.

Access policy has to make room for all of those facts at once.

Sequencing is where the uncertainty can be made concrete.

Imagine two patients with the same stage of melanoma after surgery.

One is treated at an academic center participating in a personalized-vaccine trial. The tumor is routed automatically. Sequencing is covered by the study. The sponsor manufactures the product. Checkpoint therapy is protocol-supplied. Immune monitoring is performed. Follow-up is meticulous. The patient pays in time, travel and risk, but the research system absorbs much of the technical complexity.

The second patient lives hundreds of miles away. The local practice has no trial. A commercial product, if approved, may eventually make access easier. Before approval, the patient may search online and find a private clinic offering personalized vaccine services abroad or under a different legal pathway. Now the patient may pay for sequencing, consultation, manufacturing, travel and administration directly.

The molecular concept is similar.

The institutional protections are not.

The first patient is inside a system designed to produce generalizable evidence. The second may receive a treatment without contributing data to a trial that can resolve whether it works. If the second pathway expands rapidly, society can end up with many treated patients and surprisingly little usable knowledge.

That is the evidence leakage problem.

Experimental access outside trials can be humane for individuals and inefficient for collective learning.

The solution is not to force every desperate patient into randomization. It is to make nontrial use legible. Registries, standardized outcome reporting, adverse-event capture, product characterization and long-term follow-up can turn some compassionate or individual treatment attempts into useful evidence without pretending they are randomized trials.

The closer experimental treatment moves to a commercial service, the stronger that obligation becomes.

If a clinic charges tens of thousands of dollars to manufacture an individualized cancer vaccine, it should be difficult to argue that collecting structured outcomes is an unreasonable burden. The patient is already funding the experiment.

Public systems can make this easier by building shared registries rather than requiring every small clinic to invent one.

This is another form of public infrastructure that private medicine can use.

The return should be data adequate to evaluate the frontier.

Who received the treatment?

What exactly was manufactured?

What other therapies were given?

How quickly was the product delivered?

What immune responses occurred?

What happened to the cancer?

What harms occurred?

How long was follow-up?

Without those facts, the market selects testimonials.

Testimonials are a terrible database.

The patients with dramatic responses become visible. The patients who die may disappear from the clinic website and from the online communities where hopeful cases circulate. A customized product intensifies this bias because failure can always be explained as unique biology while success is presented as proof of the platform.

Systematic follow-up removes some of that narrative freedom.

The same discipline should apply after approval.

Commercial availability does not guarantee equal access. The therapy may be covered only in certain indications. Prior authorization can delay treatment. Copay structures can create large out-of-pocket costs. Sites need contracts. Rural practices may not want to manage a complicated tissue-to-manufacturing workflow. Hospitals may face financial risk if reimbursement arrives long after they have paid for the product.

A personalized medicine can be approved nationally and remain metropolitan in practice.

This is where the health system's boring capacities decide whether a breakthrough is real.

Pathology staffing.

Courier pickup.

Broadband.

Laboratory contracting.

Tumor-board expertise.

Nursing.

Insurance navigation.

Scheduling.

Data exchange.

The rhetoric of precision medicine rarely puts these on the cover.

Patients live inside them.

Public benefit therefore requires more than negotiating the final drug price.

If the policy goal is broad access to neoantigen medicine, investment has to reach upstream. Community pathology needs reliable molecular workflows. Genomic data need portable standards. Clinicians need decision support that does not require working at one elite center. Payers need coverage rules that recognize sequencing as part of a therapeutic pathway when appropriate. Trial networks need more geographically diverse sites.

This is less glamorous than funding another vaccine platform.

It may determine who gets one.

There is a useful historical parallel in diagnostic imaging.

The invention of magnetic resonance imaging did not make MRI access universal. Machines had to be manufactured, hospitals had to buy them, technicians had to be trained, radiologists had to interpret scans, insurers had to pay, and rural regions had to attract enough volume to sustain services. A breakthrough became ordinary through capital stock and workflow.

Sequencing is cheaper and more portable than an MRI scanner, but the principle holds.

Technology becomes healthcare when institutions absorb it.

The personalized cancer future could therefore develop along two very different paths.

In one, a small number of wealthy patients and academic centers receive increasingly sophisticated bespoke care. The science advances quickly, companies build powerful datasets and outcomes improve for those inside the system. The rest of oncology receives simplified versions years later.

In the other, the difficult shared layers become infrastructure. Sequencing quality is broad. Data are portable. Community sites plug into standardized manufacturing networks. Underrepresented populations are deliberately enrolled. Experimental use is tracked. Public funding supports the parts of the stack that markets undersupply.

The second future is not automatically cheaper.

It is more scalable.

That distinction matters because price and access are not identical.

A medicine can have a lower list price and still be inaccessible if no nearby site can deliver it. A medicine can be extremely expensive and broadly used if insurance absorbs the cost and infrastructure is ubiquitous. Cancer care already contains both examples.

The economic debate around neoantigen therapy will naturally focus on the number attached to the product.

The access debate has to begin earlier.

Who gets biopsied?

Who gets sequenced?

Whose sample produces data good enough for the algorithm?

Who has a clinician who knows the option exists?

Who can reach a site before the manufacturing window closes?

Who can appeal a denial?

Who can travel when the only trial is three states away?

Who can pay when the treatment is legal but investigational and insurance refuses?

A sequencing disparity is therefore not one thing.

It can be a coverage disparity, a tissue disparity, a logistics disparity, an interpretation disparity or a timing disparity. Collapsing all of them into the percentage of patients who received a genomic test can make a health system look more equal than it is.

The relevant denominator is not who got a report.

It is who obtained information usable for the next clinically justified decision.

That standard exposes a second gap: sequencing without referral.

A community practice can order an excellent assay and still lack a path into the trial, vaccine platform or specialist consultation that gives the result meaning. The report may correctly identify candidate biology while functioning as little more than a receipt for sophisticated testing. Access to measurement without access to action is an incomplete form of precision medicine.

Networks can close that gap.

Regional molecular tumor boards can review cases across hospitals. Tele-oncology can connect community physicians with academic specialists. Trial-matching systems can flag eligibility while the patient is still medically able to travel. Standard specimen protocols can let a regional pathology lab produce material accepted by several therapeutic platforms. Public or nonprofit navigators can help patients distinguish a genuine research option from a direct-pay offer using adjacent science.

These are relatively small interventions compared with inventing the therapy.

They increase the value of the therapy by widening the population capable of reaching it.

Measurement should follow that whole funnel.

A health system evaluating personalized cancer access should know how many eligible patients had adequate tissue, how many were successfully sequenced, how many results arrived within the intended window, how many were reviewed by someone capable of acting on them, how many patients were referred, how many began manufacturing and how many actually received treatment.

Every drop between those stages has a cause.

Some causes are unavoidable biology.

Others are administrative choices masquerading as biology.

A biopsy may truly contain too little tumor. A pathology block may also be unavailable because records transfer took three weeks. A patient may truly lack useful neoantigens. The patient may also never reach HLA typing because an insurer denied the upstream assay. A manufacturing slot may truly be scarce. It may also be reserved contractually for a larger center.

Equity becomes actionable when the loss is assigned to the right stage.

The final paradox is that personalization can make universal infrastructure more valuable.

The more unique the treatment becomes, the more standardized the pathways into it have to be. Patients should not need unique luck to obtain the biopsy, data transfer, specialist review and authorization that precede a medicine already designed to account for their biological differences.

A patient of one still depends on a system of many.

The key can be personalized perfectly.

It does not matter if the patient never reaches the locksmith.
