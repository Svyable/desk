# The mRNA Detour

The most famous mRNA vaccines were built for a virus.

The companies that made them had spent years thinking about cancer.

That reversal is one of the stranger turns in the neoantigen story. When COVID-19 arrived, messenger RNA suddenly became a household term, associated with standardized vaccines manufactured by the hundreds of millions. The public saw a technology optimized for speed against an infectious agent.

Much of the commercial infrastructure behind it had been built around harder problems.

BioNTech was founded in Mainz in 2008 by Uğur Şahin, Özlem Türeci and Christoph Huber with cancer immunotherapy at the center of its work. Moderna, founded two years later in Cambridge, pursued mRNA as a programmable therapeutic platform across multiple diseases, including oncology. CureVac, another German mRNA pioneer, also grew from attempts to turn messenger RNA into medicine.

The common proposition was conceptually simple.

DNA stores instructions. Cells transcribe selected instructions into messenger RNA. Ribosomes read mRNA and make proteins. If synthetic mRNA could be delivered into cells safely and efficiently, the molecule could become a temporary instruction set rather than the final drug product.

For vaccines, that meant asking the patient's own cells to manufacture an antigen long enough for the immune system to learn it.

For personalized cancer vaccines, mRNA offered another advantage.

The sequence could change without rebuilding the entire production philosophy.

One patient might receive RNA encoding a set of melanoma neoantigens. The next patient's construct could encode a different set. The manufacturing process could remain broadly standardized while the informational payload changed.

This is personalization in the language of a platform.

The idea looks obvious after the fact because software has trained us to separate hardware from instructions. Biological manufacturing did not begin with that luxury.

Proteins are difficult physical objects. Manufacturing a different recombinant protein for every patient would require repeated purification and characterization. Long synthetic peptides can be personalized more flexibly, but they still involve chemical synthesis of each selected sequence. RNA offers a way to encode many antigens in one manufactured nucleic-acid product.

The molecule itself created problems for decades.

Unmodified RNA is fragile. Enzymes that destroy RNA are everywhere. The molecule can provoke innate immune sensing that limits protein production. Getting RNA into the appropriate cells is difficult because naked RNA does not naturally cross cellular membranes efficiently.

A therapeutic platform therefore had to solve chemistry, delivery and manufacturing before the programmable metaphor became useful.

Academic science supplied crucial pieces.

Work by Katalin Karikó and Drew Weissman showed that modifying nucleosides in synthetic mRNA could reduce unwanted innate immune activation while improving the ability of cells to translate the message. Other researchers advanced RNA design, purification, cap structures and delivery. Lipid nanoparticles emerged as a way to protect nucleic acids and carry them into cells.

The history is frequently compressed into a heroic invention story.

The reality is a network.

Decades of public and university research in molecular biology, immunology, RNA chemistry, lipid science and delivery were combined with private investments in manufacturing, formulation, quality control and clinical development. Universities licensed intellectual property. Startups raised venture capital. Pharmaceutical companies partnered with or ignored them. Public agencies funded research that had no obvious near-term mass market.

Cancer supplied one of the reasons to persist.

A viral vaccine can target an antigen shared by everyone exposed to the virus. A personalized cancer vaccine can ask mRNA to carry a different antigen set for every patient. The latter is technically elegant and commercially difficult.

Then the pandemic arrived.

SARS-CoV-2 offered the mRNA platform a target that was almost the opposite of a neoantigen program.

The antigen was shared. The sequence was known. The global market was enormous. Governments were willing to spend at wartime speed. Regulators created rapid review pathways without waiving the core trial requirements. Manufacturers could run large standardized batches instead of producing individualized sequences.

The result changed the platform's industrial base.

Facilities expanded. Raw-material supply chains scaled. Lipid nanoparticle manufacturing matured. Regulators accumulated experience reviewing mRNA chemistry, manufacturing and controls. Billions of people became familiar with the idea that a vaccine could deliver genetic instructions without altering the recipient's genome.

Cancer did not suddenly become easier.

The pandemic made parts of the machine less exotic.

This distinction matters when people say COVID proved mRNA cancer vaccines.

It did not.

COVID proved that mRNA could be manufactured at extraordinary scale, distributed under demanding conditions and used successfully to induce protective immunity against a shared viral antigen. It supplied human safety experience for particular formulations and enormous operational learning.

A tumor is a different target.

Cancer antigens may be weakly immunogenic. Tumors suppress immunity. The target set can be unique to the patient. Treatment may occur in people whose immune systems have been altered by surgery, chemotherapy or prior immunotherapy. The endpoint is not preventing infection. It is controlling an evolving population of malignant cells.

The pandemic validated a delivery technology, not the oncology claim.

For the cancer companies, however, that was still a gift.

BioNTech and Moderna emerged from the pandemic with far more capital, manufacturing capacity, regulatory experience and public visibility than they had possessed before 2020. Their oncology pipelines could draw on capabilities paid for partly through pandemic-era revenue and government contracts.

The financial loop ran in both directions.

Cancer research helped sustain the companies long enough for an infectious-disease emergency to reveal the platform's value. The infectious-disease windfall then financed more cancer development.

This is a useful counterexample to the idea that public and private research can be assigned to separate boxes.

A public grant in basic RNA biology may eventually contribute to a private vaccine company. A private company's cancer platform may become emergency national infrastructure. Government procurement can expand a factory that later manufactures commercial oncology products. Profits from a public-health crisis can fund a privately owned Phase 3 cancer program.

The technologies do not respect accounting categories.

The politics does.

During the pandemic, debates over public investment and private profit intensified because governments had funded research, supported development, guaranteed purchases and in some cases helped absorb manufacturing risk. Critics argued that the public paid more than once: first to build the science, then to accelerate the product, then to purchase the doses.

Companies replied, with justification, that translating decades of science into a reliable product required vast private investment and risk before the pandemic, and that successful manufacturing at global scale was itself a major achievement.

Both statements can be true.

The interesting question is what follows from them.

If society repeatedly socializes important parts of scientific risk and later privatizes most of the commercial upside, is that a bug or the intended function of the system?

Bayh-Dole policy in the United States was explicitly designed in part to improve commercialization of federally supported inventions by allowing universities and other contractors to retain patent rights under defined conditions. The theory was not that government should donate value to companies. It was that inventions sitting unused in public files create less social benefit than inventions licensed into products.

Commercialization was a public-benefit strategy.

The argument becomes contentious when commercialization succeeds beyond expectation.

A medicine can be widely used and still be unaffordable to many people. A company can earn extraordinary returns and still have taken real risks. A public patent license can stimulate development and later create concentrated bargaining power. The same contract can look socially efficient before success and overly generous afterward.

mRNA cancer vaccines will bring this argument into personalized medicine.

The manufacturing economics are different from a mass vaccine, which makes simple price comparisons misleading.

A personalized product requires sequencing, target selection and patient-specific production. Some costs are truly variable. The company cannot spread every manufacturing step across millions of identical doses.

Other costs are platform costs.

The software, factory, regulatory systems, lipid formulation, quality systems and clinical evidence can serve many patients. As volume rises, portions of the cost should fall. Automation can reduce labor. Better prediction can reduce failed manufacturing. Standardized release testing can shorten timelines.

Personalization does not imply that the price must remain permanently artisanal.

This will become one of the central economic battles if individualized vaccines are approved.

Companies will price not only against manufacturing cost but against clinical value, competing therapies, duration of benefit and willingness of insurers or governments to pay. If a treatment materially reduces cancer recurrence after surgery, the value can be high because recurrence often leads to years of additional treatment, disability and death.

A payer will ask whether the incremental benefit justifies the incremental price.

The company will ask what price supports return on development and the next generation of trials.

The patient will ask a less abstract question.

Can I get it?

The answer will depend on much more than FDA approval.

Hospitals need workflows. Pathology teams need tissue. Insurers need coverage policies. A manufacturer needs capacity. The patient's cancer has to fit the label. The product has to arrive on time. Clinicians have to understand where it belongs relative to checkpoint inhibitors, chemotherapy, radiation and surveillance.

The technology may be programmable while the healthcare system remains difficult to program.

mRNA also changes the meaning of intellectual property.

The personalized sequence is derived from the patient's tumor. The platform for designing and manufacturing it can be patented. The selection algorithm can be proprietary. Lipid nanoparticle technologies can be licensed through complex patent landscapes. Manufacturing know-how can be protected as trade secret. Clinical data can support regulatory exclusivity and commercial advantage.

The final product is therefore surrounded by multiple layers of exclusion even though its informational content begins with the patient's own cancer.

This creates a morally intuitive but legally misleading reaction: if the mutation came from my tumor, why does someone else own the medicine?

The company does not own the mutation merely because it manufactures a therapy based on it.

It may own or license methods for selecting, encoding, formulating or delivering the relevant sequence. It may hold regulatory rights to a product platform. It may possess trade secrets about manufacturing.

The value is in the transformation.

The political question is how much exclusivity that transformation deserves when its inputs include publicly funded knowledge and the patient's own biological information.

There is another reason mRNA became attractive for personalized vaccines: speed.

A bespoke therapy has to be made before the treatment window closes. Nucleic-acid manufacturing can be highly digital in the early stages. Once a sequence is selected, the same general machinery can produce a different RNA payload without inventing a new chemical synthesis route from scratch.

Companies increasingly describe turnaround times measured in weeks rather than months.

Those claims need to be read carefully.

The clock can start at different points: biopsy, sample receipt, successful sequencing, final target selection or manufacturing order. “Manufacturing time” may exclude pathology and shipping. A median turnaround can hide failed or delayed cases. Trial patients may benefit from dedicated logistics unavailable after commercial launch.

Still, the direction is clear.

Personalized manufacturing is moving from bespoke laboratory craft toward an industrial service.

That movement is what makes the frontier clinic possible.

A physician does not need to own an RNA factory if an outside manufacturer can accept a sequence file and return a clinical-grade product under an appropriate legal pathway. The clinic can become an orchestrator.

This is also where safety can break.

Industrial platforms earn trust through validated processes, batch records, release testing, pharmacovigilance and regulatory inspection. A decentralized market can imitate the surface of personalization without reproducing the quality system underneath it.

A website can say “mRNA,” “neoantigen” and “AI” more cheaply than a company can run a randomized trial.

The words have acquired credibility because the underlying technology is real.

That makes disciplined evidence more important, not less.

The mRNA detour through a global pandemic did not settle the cancer question.

It changed what the answer could cost to build.

Before COVID, personalized mRNA cancer vaccines were a frontier biotechnology proposition supported by early trials and substantial scientific uncertainty.

After COVID, they were still uncertain.

But the world had built the factories.
