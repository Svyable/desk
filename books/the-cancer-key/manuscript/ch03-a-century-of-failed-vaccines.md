# A Century of Failed Vaccines

Cancer vaccines have been promising for longer than most of the companies now building them have existed.

That is useful to remember whenever a new platform is described as if the idea arrived with the latest delivery system.

The central intuition is old. If infection can train the immune system to remember an enemy, perhaps cancer can be made to look enough like an enemy for immunity to do the same. The difficulty is that a tumor is not a virus wearing a foreign coat. It is a population of the patient's own cells, altered by mutation and evolution but still surrounded by the molecular language of self.

For decades, researchers repeatedly discovered things the immune system could recognize in cancer and then learned that recognition was not the same thing as control.

One of the earliest chapters in the history of cancer immunotherapy begins with William Coley, a New York surgeon working in the late nineteenth century. Coley observed that some tumors occasionally regressed after severe bacterial infections. He began treating some patients with mixtures of killed bacteria intended to provoke an immune response. The treatment became known as Coley's toxins.

It would be a mistake to turn Coley into the inventor of the modern cancer vaccine. His preparations were variable, the trials lacked modern controls, the mechanisms were not understood, and the historical record is difficult to interpret by current standards. But the work established a recurring idea: the immune system could alter the course of cancer.

The next century spent much of its time trying to make that idea precise.

Cancer immunology advanced unevenly because the immune system itself was still being mapped. Scientists had to discover T cells, B cells, antigen presentation, major histocompatibility molecules, cytokines, dendritic cells, immune checkpoints and the mechanisms of tolerance before a rational cancer vaccine could be more than a general attempt to provoke inflammation.

As the parts came into view, researchers began looking for antigens associated with tumors.

Some targets were proteins that healthy adult tissues expressed at low levels while tumors expressed them heavily. Others were proteins normally active during fetal development and later reactivated in cancer. Some were differentiation antigens marking a particular cell lineage. A few cancers offered viral proteins because the malignancy had been driven by infection.

The attraction was obvious.

If many patients shared a target, one vaccine could be manufactured in advance and used broadly. The economics resembled ordinary pharmaceuticals. A stable product could be tested, approved, stocked and distributed.

The immunology was less cooperative.

The body is designed not to launch powerful attacks against its own common proteins. T cells capable of strongly recognizing self-antigens are often deleted, restrained or rendered functionally quiet. A tumor-associated antigen can therefore be visible yet immunologically uninteresting. When a response is induced, the target may also be present on healthy cells, creating a narrow safety margin.

Then there is the tumor itself.

A growing cancer is not a passive target waiting for an immune response. It modifies its environment. It recruits suppressive cells. It produces signals that dampen activation. It can reduce antigen presentation. It can exhaust T cells through chronic stimulation. It can create physical barriers that keep lymphocytes away. It can evolve under selective pressure.

A vaccine may succeed at the task measured in a laboratory assay and fail at the task that matters to the patient.

That distinction became one of the most important lessons in the field.

Researchers could take blood after vaccination and detect T cells that recognized the intended antigen. The immune response was real. The tumor could still grow.

The result was not necessarily evidence that vaccination was useless. It showed that the immune response was operating inside a larger system.

Cancer had more than one brake.

The first generation of therapeutic vaccines often treated immunity as a switch. Find an antigen, deliver it with an adjuvant, observe a response, hope the response clears the tumor. The real system looked more like a circuit with multiple checkpoints, suppressors, feedback loops and missing components.

The arrival of immune checkpoint inhibitors changed the field because they attacked one of those restraints directly.

T cells carry inhibitory receptors that help prevent immune responses from damaging healthy tissue. Tumors can exploit those control systems. Drugs blocking CTLA-4, PD-1 or PD-L1 release some of the restraint and can produce durable tumor regressions in subsets of patients.

The clinical impact was large enough to alter what scientists believed was possible.

Metastatic melanoma, once a disease in which durable remissions were rare, became one of the clearest demonstrations that the immune system could control advanced solid tumors for years in some patients. Checkpoint blockade later spread across lung cancer, kidney cancer, bladder cancer, head-and-neck cancer and many other malignancies.

The drugs did not work for everyone.

That failure became information.

Checkpoint inhibitors do not manufacture an immune response from nothing. They work best when a relevant antitumor response exists or can emerge. Tumors with many mutations sometimes responded more frequently. Cancers with defects in DNA mismatch repair could accumulate large numbers of mutations and become unusually susceptible to PD-1 blockade. The relationship was imperfect, but it helped connect mutation burden with immune visibility.

The field began to ask a more specific question.

What exactly were the successful T cells seeing?

Experiments in melanoma and other cancers showed that some tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes recognized peptides created by somatic mutations. These were not merely overexpressed self-proteins. They were altered sequences unique to the cancer.

The old vaccine problem had contained its own escape route.

The strongest tumor-specific targets might not be the antigens cancers share.

They might be the mistakes cancers make individually.

This insight grew alongside another line of work at the National Cancer Institute.

Steven Rosenberg and colleagues had spent decades developing adoptive cell therapy, including the use of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes. Surgeons removed tumor tissue. Laboratories isolated lymphocytes that had entered the tumor. Those cells could be expanded in large numbers and returned to the patient after preparative treatment.

Some patients with advanced melanoma experienced remarkable regressions.

The therapy was complicated, toxic and difficult to manufacture. But it offered something intellectually valuable: a way to study the immune cells that had already reached the tumor and ask what they recognized.

The answers increasingly pointed toward mutation-derived targets.

Cancer immunology was converging with cancer genomics.

That convergence took time because the two fields had developed with different instruments and assumptions. Immunologists studied receptors, cells and cytokines. Cancer geneticists studied mutations, oncogenes and tumor suppressors. Sequencing brought the disciplines into the same room.

Once a tumor could be read deeply enough, researchers could list its mutations. Once HLA typing and peptide-binding prediction improved, they could estimate which altered fragments might appear on the tumor surface. Once T-cell assays improved, they could test whether the patient's immune system recognized them.

The neoantigen became a bridge between genotype and immunity.

The first modern personalized neoantigen vaccine studies were therefore not simply better versions of old cancer vaccines. They represented a different product logic.

Instead of searching for one antigen shared by a disease category, researchers accepted that the useful antigen set might change patient by patient. Instead of manufacturing first and selecting patients later, they would characterize the patient first and manufacture afterward.

The cost of this reversal was obvious.

Every patient became a new discovery project.

The benefit was specificity.

A mutation-derived peptide could be absent from healthy tissue because the mutation itself was absent. The immune system might not have been trained to tolerate it. If the mutation occurred in every malignant cell, the target could be both tumor-specific and broadly present inside that person's cancer.

This did not solve the problem of immune suppression, trafficking or escape. It did not guarantee that a predicted peptide would be presented. It did not turn an exhausted T cell into an effective one.

But it improved the address.

The history of cancer vaccines is easiest to misunderstand when told as a parade of failures followed by a breakthrough. That flatters the present.

Many unsuccessful approaches supplied the methods that individualized vaccines now depend on. Researchers learned how to measure T-cell responses, choose adjuvants, culture dendritic cells, identify antigens, monitor immune escape and design trials with meaningful endpoints. The field learned the difference between immunogenicity and efficacy because it had seen them separate so often.

A failed trial can eliminate a target, reveal a disease setting, expose a manufacturing problem or show that a biological response is too weak to matter.

The difficulty is financial.

Science can treat a failed experiment as information. Capital markets treat it as lost value.

A biotechnology company may have one major platform, several related programs and a cash runway. A trial failure can wipe out most of its market capitalization in a day, trigger layoffs and make the next experiment impossible. The public research system can absorb failure differently because its time horizon and portfolio are larger.

That difference helps explain the institutional ecology of neoantigen medicine.

Government laboratories and universities are unusually good places to explore mechanisms that may take years to become products. They can study rare immune responses, develop assays, publish negative results and maintain datasets whose value accrues across many projects. Venture-backed and public biotechnology companies are unusually good at concentrating resources around a manufacturable platform, hiring specialized teams and moving a candidate through the regulatory sequence required for commercialization.

Neither system is naturally complete.

Academic research can prove that a personalized vaccine generates T cells without building a global manufacturing network. A company can build the network but may have little incentive to keep its highest-value clinical data open. Public institutions can sustain uncertainty but move slowly. Private institutions can move quickly but can disappear when financing closes.

Cancer vaccines traveled through both systems.

One milestone often cited in the history is sipuleucel-T, approved by the FDA in 2010 for certain patients with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer. It is an autologous cellular immunotherapy rather than a personalized neoantigen vaccine. A patient's immune cells are collected, exposed ex vivo to a prostate antigen fused to an immune-stimulating protein, and reinfused.

Its significance was partly regulatory.

Sipuleucel-T showed that a patient-specific manufacturing process could become an approved commercial product.

It also exposed the economics that would return later.

Autologous therapies require scheduling, collection, chain-of-identity controls, manufacturing coordination and timely return to the patient. The factory cannot simply make a million identical doses and place them in a warehouse. Every patient generates operational work.

The commercial challenge was therefore not separate from the medical one.

A therapy can work and still be difficult to deliver at scale.

CAR-T therapy reinforced that lesson. Products such as tisagenlecleucel and axicabtagene ciloleucel transformed treatment for some blood cancers by engineering a patient's T cells to recognize a shared surface antigen. These therapies are not neoantigen-specific, but they normalized an important concept: medicine could manufacture a different living product for each patient while standardizing the process enough for regulation and commerce.

They also showed the limits.

Collection slots, manufacturing capacity, treatment-center certification, toxicity management, long hospital stays and high prices became part of the therapeutic reality. Personalization did not eliminate industrial organization. It made industrial organization more demanding.

Neoantigen vaccines arrived after these lessons had accumulated.

By the 2010s, sequencing was fast enough, bioinformatics mature enough and synthetic manufacturing flexible enough to attempt individualized vaccines within clinically relevant timeframes. Early studies in melanoma showed that vaccines containing multiple patient-specific neoantigens could induce both pre-existing and new T-cell responses. Some patients remained disease-free after treatment; others with recurrence later responded to checkpoint blockade. The studies were small, but they demonstrated feasibility.

The deeper significance was not that a vaccine had finally won.

It was that the old category had split.

There were preventive vaccines against cancer-causing viruses, such as HPV and hepatitis B vaccines, which reduce cancer risk by preventing infection. There were therapeutic vaccines against shared tumor-associated antigens. There were cellular immunotherapies. There were checkpoint inhibitors. There were personalized vaccines encoding mutation-derived targets. There were TCR therapies using neoantigen-specific receptors.

Cancer vaccination was no longer one idea.

That matters when reading claims about success and failure.

A failed peptide vaccine against a shared self-antigen does not predict the fate of a personalized mRNA neoantigen vaccine. A successful checkpoint inhibitor does not prove that adding a vaccine will improve survival. A CAR-T result in leukemia does not mean a TCR therapy will work in a solid tumor. A strong neoantigen response after surgery does not show that the same approach can rescue widespread metastatic disease.

The field advances by narrowing the claim.

The Moderna and Merck melanoma program is important because it tested a personalized neoantigen product in a large randomized setting beside a proven checkpoint inhibitor. The BioNTech colorectal program is important because its failure shows that the general logic does not erase disease biology. The pancreatic studies are important because they suggest immune priming may be possible in a cancer long considered difficult for immunotherapy, while still leaving the clinical-benefit question open.

This is what a mature breakthrough looks like.

Not a door suddenly opening.

A corridor full of doors, some unlocked, some painted to look like doors, and some leading nowhere.

The century of failed vaccines did not waste a century.

It taught the field which doors were real.

It also taught the field when to knock.

Disease burden changes the problem a vaccine is being asked to solve. Teaching an immune system to recognize several tumor-specific targets after surgery, when only microscopic disease may remain, is a different task from asking the same immune response to eliminate kilograms of metastatic tumor that have already evolved under multiple treatments. The antigen can be identical while the strategic situation is not.

Large tumors contain more cells, and more cells mean more chances for resistant subclones. They can contain regions with poor blood flow, abnormal vessels and dense stroma. They may have accumulated defects in interferon signaling or antigen presentation. Chronic exposure can leave tumor-reactive T cells exhausted. Prior therapies can reshape both the cancer and the immune compartment.

Minimal residual disease removes some of those obstacles without making the problem easy.

After surgery, the immune system may be hunting scattered malignant cells rather than attacking an established organ-sized ecosystem. A vaccine may have time to expand specific T-cell populations before those cells are needed at scale. Checkpoint blockade may help keep the response functional. Repeated dosing may reinforce memory while the number of malignant targets is still small.

This helps explain why so much of the current individualized-vaccine effort has moved into adjuvant settings.

It is not merely a convenient clinical-trial choice. It is a biological wager that immunity is better at preventing a malignant population from re-establishing itself than at dismantling a mature hostile environment.

The wager carries a statistical price.

When visible disease is gone, investigators cannot measure tumor shrinkage. They have to wait for recurrence, distant metastasis or survival events. Some patients in both trial arms may already have been cured by surgery and standard therapy. The absolute benefit can be meaningful while taking years to become visible. A therapy given to prevent an event will also treat some people who would never have experienced that event.

This changes the tolerance for toxicity, cost and uncertainty.

A patient with rapidly progressing metastatic cancer may rationally accept a dangerous treatment for a small chance of rescue. A patient who feels well after surgery faces a different decision. The treatment is being offered against a probability. Side effects occur in the present; the recurrence being prevented is hypothetical at the moment of dosing.

Successful prevention therefore sets a higher operational bar than rescue medicine often admits.

Manufacturing delays matter because the intervention has to fit between surgery and other treatments. False-positive target selection matters because the patient may receive a complex product without visible disease to prove whether any component was necessary. Financial toxicity matters because the person may live for decades after therapy. Long-term immune effects matter because the intended benefit is long-term.

The history of preventive cancer vaccines already shows how powerful timing can be, though through a different mechanism.

HPV and hepatitis B vaccines do not train the immune system against an existing person's tumor mutations. They prevent infections that can later cause cancer. Their success does not prove that therapeutic cancer vaccination will work. It proves something narrower and more important: immunity can prevent some cancers far more easily than medicine can treat the same disease after malignant evolution has occurred.

Therapeutic neoantigen vaccines borrow the temporal logic without borrowing the biology.

They arrive after cancer has already formed, but the most promising use may still be before recurrent disease has rebuilt its defenses.

That is one reason a century of disappointment should make the field more ambitious about trial design, not less.

A weak historical reading says cancer vaccines failed, then technology improved.

A better reading says different generations kept discovering that the target, the immune context, the disease burden, the combination partner and the endpoint are inseparable. Each failure removed another excuse for treating vaccination as a single-variable intervention.

The modern platform is more complicated because the old experiments earned the complication.

The test of progress is whether that added complexity buys the patient something measurable.

Failure also changes the scientific vocabulary available to the next trial.

An old cancer-vaccine study might have been remembered simply as negative. A modern program can ask more precise questions after the primary endpoint fails. Did the vaccine induce the intended T cells? Did the selected peptides appear to be presented? Was the immune response weaker in particular HLA contexts? Did patients with lower disease burden behave differently? Did recurrence occur through clones that lacked the targeted mutations? Did the product reach patients quickly enough for the intended treatment window?

These analyses cannot rescue a failed primary endpoint after the fact.

They can prevent the next trial from asking the same vague question.

That difference is one measure of a field becoming cumulative.

It also explains why negative trials are a public asset even when the product is private. Participants accepted risk and contributed tissue, immune assays and follow-up. The sponsor paid for manufacturing and operations. If the only public conclusion is that the trial missed, much of the learning remains trapped in the development program.

The best failure leaves a map.

That map should include the parts that threaten the platform's own story. A company has understandable incentives to emphasize the subgroup that looked encouraging or the biomarker that moved in the expected direction. Researchers have similar incentives because positive mechanisms publish more easily than null ones. A mature field has to preserve the denominator and the unfavorable correlations too.

The century of failed vaccines is valuable because the failures were not erased completely.

They left enough evidence to show that antigen recognition alone was insufficient, that self-tolerance mattered, that tumor context mattered, and that immune brakes could be therapeutically important. The modern neoantigen field inherits those lessons whether or not a current product succeeds.

The standard for the next century should be higher.

When a personalized trial fails, the field should learn not only that a product did not work, but where the chain from mutation to outcome most likely broke.

That is how failure stops being a graveyard and becomes infrastructure.
