# Cancer Learns the Address

A target is useful only while the tumor continues to display it.

That sounds obvious. It is one of the deepest problems in cancer medicine.

The image behind neoantigen therapy is unusually satisfying: the cancer mutates, the mutation creates a foreign-looking protein fragment, the immune system learns that fragment, and malignant cells carrying it become visible. The tumor has committed an error and medicine has turned the error into an address.

But cancer is not a static building.

It is an evolving population under selection.

The moment a therapy begins killing cells that carry one vulnerability, the population changes. Sensitive clones disappear. Resistant clones survive. A mutation that once made a tumor easier to see can become costly to the tumor if the immune system attacks every cell displaying it. Any malignant cell that can grow while hiding, losing or bypassing that target acquires an advantage it did not have before treatment.

Therapy does not simply encounter cancer evolution.

Therapy becomes part of it.

This is familiar from targeted drugs. A kinase inhibitor can produce a dramatic response and then lose control when a resistant mutation appears or a bypass pathway takes over. Antibiotics select resistant bacteria. Insecticides select resistant insects. The principle is broader than oncology: when a population contains heritable variation, pressure changes which variants reproduce.

Neoantigen medicine adds a particularly intricate set of escape routes because the target must survive an entire chain between DNA and immune killing.

The mutated gene has to remain in the tumor. It has to be expressed. The protein has to be processed. The peptide has to be loaded onto HLA. The HLA complex has to reach the surface. T cells have to recognize it. Those T cells have to enter the tumor, stay functional and kill. The cancer can interfere with almost any link.

It can lose the mutation if the mutation was present only in a subclone.

It can reduce expression of the altered gene.

It can change antigen-processing machinery.

It can lose an HLA allele.

It can disrupt beta-2 microglobulin or other components needed for class I presentation.

It can create an immunosuppressive microenvironment.

It can exclude T cells physically.

It can upregulate inhibitory signals.

It can recruit cells that suppress immune activity.

It can survive by changing enough of the neighborhood that a T cell which recognizes the address still cannot complete the arrest.

The difference between recognition and control is the difference between a target and a therapy.

This is one reason early neoantigen studies could produce magnificent immune data without settling the clinical question. Researchers could show that vaccination expanded T cells that recognized predicted neoantigens. They could show that those T cells were real, specific and persistent. That was an achievement. It did not guarantee that the relevant cancer cells were still displaying the same targets in enough places, or that the T cells could reach and eliminate them.

The immune system can win the assay and lose the tumor.

Tumor heterogeneity makes this harder.

We speak of a patient's cancer as though it were a single thing because a diagnosis needs a noun. In reality, one tumor can contain multiple related populations. A primary lesion and a metastasis can differ. Two metastases can differ from each other. A mutation found in every malignant cell is different from a mutation found in one branch of the tumor's family tree.

This distinction gives neoantigen ranking another important variable: clonality.

A clonal neoantigen is generated by a mutation present broadly across the tumor population. If immunity can be directed against it, the address appears on more of the enemy. A subclonal neoantigen may identify only part of the disease. Killing that part can still matter, but it can leave behind malignant relatives that never carried the target.

The therapeutic dream is therefore not merely to find neoantigens.

It is to find neoantigens that are hard for the tumor to abandon.

Driver mutations are attractive partly for this reason. A driver contributes to malignant growth. If the tumor depends on it, losing the mutation may carry a fitness cost. The ideal target is tumor-specific, well presented, strongly immunogenic, clonal and biologically difficult to discard.

Biology rarely gives all five at once.

That scarcity changes the case for multi-target personalized vaccines.

A traditional drug often has one principal molecular target. A personalized neoantigen vaccine can encode many candidate antigens in one product. At first glance this looks inefficient. Why manufacture twenty targets if only a subset proves immunogenic?

Because diversity can be insurance.

If the tumor escapes one immune response by losing a target or one clone, responses against other neoantigens may remain. A vaccine can attempt to make the evolutionary escape problem harder by presenting the cancer with several simultaneous threats. The concept resembles combination therapy, except the combination is partly a set of immune addresses rather than a set of separately approved molecules.

This creates a design problem that is computational, biological and economic at the same time.

How many targets are enough?

Every additional candidate uses manufacturing capacity and payload space. It may add useful breadth, or it may add noise. Some candidate peptides compete for presentation. Some may generate weak responses. Some are redundant. Some may be excellent on paper and invisible in the patient. The platform has to choose a portfolio under uncertainty.

A good neoantigen product is therefore closer to an investment portfolio than a single bet.

The analogy should not be pushed too far, but one feature is useful: expected value depends not only on the quality of each asset but on correlation among failures. Twenty targets that can all be lost through one defect in antigen presentation do not create twenty independent escape barriers. If the tumor loses a key component of HLA class I presentation, many class I neoantigen responses can fail together.

The unit of resistance is often the shared failure mode.

This is why immune escape belongs in the industrial design of the platform, not only in academic discussion after relapse.

A company deciding which neoantigens to include needs to think about clonality and presentation. A trial sponsor needs tissue and blood sampling capable of showing what changed when patients recur. A cell-therapy program needs to know whether the selected TCR target persists. A post-treatment monitoring strategy needs to distinguish a tumor that is shrinking from a tumor that is editing itself under immune pressure.

The relapse sample can be more valuable than the pretreatment sample.

It tells us what the therapy taught the cancer to become.

This creates an uncomfortable incentive around biopsy.

Patients experience biopsies as procedures, not data points. They can be painful, risky and logistically difficult. Some lesions cannot be sampled safely. A research program may desperately want tissue at progression precisely when a patient is exhausted and moving to another treatment. The scientific value of the sample can be enormous while the personal benefit to the patient is uncertain.

Precision medicine depends on tissue, and tissue is not free merely because the pathology slide already exists.

The ethical solution is not to stop learning from relapse. It is to design studies in which the burden is justified, consent is clear, less invasive measurements are used where possible and the resulting knowledge is shared enough to matter.

Liquid biopsy may help.

Fragments of tumor DNA circulating in blood can reveal whether disease is returning, whether particular mutations are disappearing and whether new alterations are emerging. Circulating tumor DNA is already changing how some cancers are monitored. For neoantigen medicine, it offers a potential way to watch the evolutionary population without repeatedly cutting into it.

But a blood signal does not tell the whole immune story.

It can show that a mutation is present or changing in abundance. It does not necessarily show whether the altered peptide is being presented, whether a T cell recognizes it, or whether the microenvironment is suppressing that T cell. Genomics can observe the script while missing the performance.

This is why the next generation of platforms will likely combine data types.

DNA sequencing says what changed.

RNA says what is being expressed.

Proteomic and immunopeptidomic methods can provide evidence about what peptides are actually present or displayed.

T-cell assays show whether immune recognition exists.

Spatial measurements can show where immune cells are relative to tumor cells.

Blood can track clones over time.

Clinical outcomes determine whether any of the molecular beauty mattered.

The more of these layers a platform connects, the more defensible its prediction engine may become.

It also becomes harder to reproduce outside the largest systems.

A small clinic can buy tumor sequencing. It can outsource peptide manufacture. It can send blood for immune assays. What is harder to buy is an integrated longitudinal dataset across thousands of patients in which every layer has been measured consistently and linked to outcomes. That is where scale can turn into learning.

The competitive moat may be relapse.

A company that treats enough patients learns which targets disappeared, which immune responses persisted, which HLA contexts failed, which manufacturing choices mattered and which combinations prevented escape. Each failed patient can, with consent and careful study, improve the platform for the next.

That sentence contains the ethical tension of data-driven medicine.

Patients do not exist to train products.

Yet refusing to learn from failure wastes something they have already paid for with their bodies.

Public policy should therefore care not only about access to successful treatment but about access to the knowledge produced by unsuccessful treatment. Negative results are especially vulnerable to private capture. A sponsor has every reason to publish a decisive trial failure, particularly if securities rules require disclosure. It has less reason to publish every candidate that failed to generate a T-cell response, every allele on which prediction was poor, every batch that arrived too late or every relapse whose tumor lost presentation machinery.

Those small failures are exactly what a field needs to improve.

The neoantigen commons should include escape maps.

This does not require companies to upload patient-level proprietary datasets indiscriminately. Privacy, consent and legitimate commercial interests matter. It does suggest that regulators, public funders and trial networks can design standards for sharing de-identified aggregate failure modes: rates of antigen loss, presentation defects, clonal versus subclonal performance, turnaround failures and other lessons that otherwise remain buried inside separate organizations.

We already accept something similar in safety.

Adverse-event reporting exists because no company should own exclusive knowledge that a product may be harming patients. Resistance and escape are not identical to safety signals, but they share a public-health logic. If a common therapeutic mechanism creates a common failure mode, fragmented secrecy slows everyone.

The challenge is deciding what must be shared and what can remain a competitive advantage.

Companies need room to learn better than competitors. That is part of the reward for running trials and building platforms. Society needs enough shared learning that every firm does not rediscover the same dangerous or futile pattern in a new group of patients.

The line will move as the field matures.

Early industries tolerate more proprietary experimentation because almost everything is uncertain. Mature industries standardize the parts that no longer create useful differentiation. Aviation firms compete fiercely while sharing safety lessons. Semiconductor companies protect process secrets while operating inside common standards. Drug companies protect molecules while regulators publish class warnings.

Neoantigen medicine will develop its own boundary.

Escape biology will force the issue because the same tumor strategy can defeat products from different companies.

If loss of a particular presentation pathway repeatedly predicts failure, that is not merely a disappointing result for one sponsor. It is information about where the therapeutic class may not work. If certain cancers evolve around single-target TCR therapies rapidly, platform designers need to know. If multi-target vaccination reduces a particular pattern of relapse, the field needs independent confirmation.

The scientific conversation should not end at approval.

Approval is when natural selection gets a larger laboratory.

Thousands of patients treated outside tightly controlled trials will reveal combinations of tumor biology, prior therapies, ancestry, comorbidity and immune history that development programs could not capture. Some responses will be better than expected. Some failures will be stranger. Rare escape pathways will become visible only at scale.

This is the traditional promise of postmarket surveillance, but personalized therapy adds a twist.

The product itself varies.

When a patient relapses after a conventional drug, researchers can say the patient received the same molecule as thousands of others. When a patient relapses after an individualized vaccine, the payload was unique. Analysis has to move up one level. The object of surveillance is not merely the lot or molecule. It is the platform's decision process and the biological patterns across different outputs.

That is a harder statistical problem.

It is also one that software-heavy companies may be unusually equipped to solve.

The platform can log every input, every ranked candidate, every manufactured sequence and every model version. If clinical systems feed back outcomes, the sponsor can search for patterns across apparent uniqueness. Perhaps products with more clonal targets perform better. Perhaps class II responses matter more in a subgroup. Perhaps turnaround time interacts with disease burden. Perhaps a model update improves predicted binding but worsens real immunogenicity. Perhaps a recurrent escape mechanism appears across cancers.

The bespoke products become a structured experiment repeated thousands of times.

This is where the phrase learning health system stops being a slogan.

The factory can learn from its own output.

But learning changes the regulated product.

If a neoantigen-selection algorithm improves every quarter, when does an improvement require regulatory review? A conventional manufacturer cannot silently alter the active ingredient because internal analysis suggests a better molecule. A software company updates models constantly. An individualized therapy company lives between those cultures.

The regulator needs to distinguish changes that preserve the validated process from changes that materially alter therapeutic selection.

Too little oversight freezes trust.

Too much oversight freezes learning.

The answer will probably involve predetermined change protocols, validation sets, audit trails and thresholds for when a model modification becomes significant. The regulatory vocabulary may come from medical software, manufacturing comparability and adaptive platform trials at once.

The point is not to predict the exact rule.

It is to see that immune escape turns continuous improvement from a business preference into a medical necessity.

Cancer does not keep using the version of itself on which the model was trained.

Tumors are exposed to new drugs. Patients live longer and accumulate new treatment histories. Screening changes stage at diagnosis. Combination therapies change selective pressure. The population of relapses in 2030 will not be identical to the population sequenced in 2020.

The target landscape moves because medicine moves it.

That is another reason not to speak of neoantigens as a solved key.

A key implies a lock waiting passively to be opened.

Cancer is closer to a lock that notices the burglary.

The first successful therapy can make the next tumor harder. The immune system can edit malignant populations, removing the most visible cells and leaving variants that are less visible. This phenomenon, often described within the broader idea of cancer immunoediting, is not evidence that immunotherapy failed conceptually. It is evidence that the immune system exerted enough pressure to shape the disease.

Success can manufacture the next resistance problem.

Oncology has lived through this cycle repeatedly. Chemotherapy created resistant relapses. Targeted therapy produced spectacular responses and new resistance mutations. Checkpoint blockade produced durable remissions for some patients and new questions about why others never respond or later escape. CAR-T therapy transformed selected blood cancers and then encountered antigen loss, T-cell exhaustion and difficult solid-tumor biology.

Neoantigen medicine will not be exempt.

Its advantage is that the target space is enormous and patient-specific. Its weakness is that the tumor evolves inside that same space.

This suggests a more mature picture of the future than one universal cancer vaccine.

The neoantigen platform may become an adaptive layer of oncology.

A patient is sequenced after surgery. A vaccine teaches the immune system several clonal tumor-specific targets. Circulating DNA monitors residual disease. If one clone returns, sequencing identifies what changed. A TCR therapy or updated vaccine addresses a surviving target. Checkpoint blockade or another immunomodulator changes the environment. The treatment plan evolves as the cancer evolves.

That vision is technically plausible in pieces.

It is also financially terrifying.

Repeated sequencing, repeated manufacturing, cell therapy, checkpoint drugs, immune monitoring and specialist care can turn one patient's disease into a custom research program. The medical system was not designed to price an arms race whose weapons are rebuilt after every move.

The economics of escape therefore matter as much as the biology.

A therapy that produces durable cure with one manufactured course can justify high upfront cost differently from a platform that requires serial redesign. A payer may welcome individualized medicine if it prevents years of recurrence and expensive salvage therapy. The same payer may resist if personalization becomes a permanent subscription layered on top of other premium oncology drugs.

The clinical endpoint and the business model will meet at durability.

That makes long follow-up essential.

A short-term improvement in recurrence-free survival is important. A durable flattening of the relapse curve is more important. The field has to learn whether neoantigen-driven immunity creates memory that continues to police residual disease or whether evolutionary escape eventually erodes the benefit.

The most valuable patients in the dataset may therefore be the ones observed longest.

Biotechnology markets are not famous for long attention spans. Companies report quarterly. Trial readouts create sharp valuation events. A positive hazard ratio can add billions of dollars in a day. Durable immune control unfolds on a slower clock.

Public institutions are useful partly because they can keep watching after the market has moved on.

Cancer registries, cooperative groups, long-term follow-up studies and academic cohorts can preserve questions that are no longer the sponsor's most urgent commercial priority. If public funding helped discover the key, public infrastructure may be equally important in learning how the lock changes after it is used.

The central problem is not that cancer is clever.

Cancer has no intention.

It does not plan its escape. It does not understand the vaccine. It does not decide to lose an HLA allele. Variation exists. Treatment changes which variants survive. The result can look strategic because natural selection is good at producing populations adapted to pressure without requiring a strategist.

That distinction matters because it keeps us from romanticizing the enemy and from underestimating the mechanism.

The tumor does not need to be smart.

It needs to be diverse enough that one cell can live where another dies.

Neoantigen medicine answers diversity with diversity: multiple targets, multiple immune cell populations, combination treatments, repeated measurement and adaptive design.

The cancer writes addresses.

Medicine learns to read them.

Then cancer changes which addresses remain.

The breakthrough is not finding one perfect key.

It is building a system that can keep cutting keys while the locks are changing.
