# The Companies Around the Key

There is no neoantigen industry.

There are several industries stacked on top of one another and learning to share a patient.

The obvious companies are the vaccine developers. Moderna and BioNTech sit at the center because mRNA gives both of them a programmable way to encode individualized antigen sets. Merck and Roche matter because checkpoint inhibitors and global oncology operations can turn a technically interesting vaccine into a combination regimen tested against standard care.

But the product begins earlier.

Sequencing companies read the tumor. Bioinformatics systems decide which mutations matter. Laboratories type HLA alleles. Specialized manufacturers synthesize peptides or nucleic acids. Couriers move tissue and temperature-sensitive products. Hospitals obtain biopsies and administer treatment. Diagnostic companies detect residual disease. Cell-therapy companies turn neoantigens into receptors rather than vaccines.

The same patient can therefore create revenue for a chain of firms before the vaccine reaches the clinic.

This is why the business story cannot be told as a race between Moderna and BioNTech.

They are two large platforms inside a much wider value stack.

Moderna's oncology strategy is easiest to understand through intismeran autogene.

The company brings mRNA design, manufacturing and lipid-nanoparticle experience. Merck brings pembrolizumab, development scale and oncology infrastructure. Their collaboration aligns a personalized product with a blockbuster checkpoint inhibitor already embedded in clinical practice.

The partnership is structurally powerful because the vaccine is not required to replace Keytruda.

It can make Keytruda more valuable by making more useful T cells available for the checkpoint inhibitor to release.

If the combination becomes standard in high-risk melanoma, value can accrue to both sides rather than moving from an old drug to a new one.

BioNTech built a related but broader immunotherapy portfolio.

The company began with cancer and later became globally associated with the Pfizer-BioNTech COVID vaccine. Its oncology work includes individualized mRNA cancer vaccines, off-the-shelf cancer vaccines, antibodies, cell therapies and other immune platforms.

Its partnership with Genentech connects personalized vaccines to Roche's oncology franchise and diagnostic ecosystem.

BioNTech also acquired Neon Therapeutics in 2020, adding neoantigen-specific T-cell capabilities and intellectual property. The acquisition was small compared with later biotechnology deals, but strategically revealing.

The company was not treating vaccines as the only way to exploit tumor-specific mutations.

It was collecting modalities around the target class.

Roche occupies another position in the stack.

Its ownership of Genentech gives it deep oncology development capacity. Its ownership of Foundation Medicine gives it a major genomic-testing platform. A company can therefore participate in identifying tumor alterations, developing therapies and gathering clinical data across the treatment pathway.

Integration does not guarantee dominance.

It does create learning opportunities.

The more layers a company touches, the more chances it has to link diagnostic information with treatment response.

Personalis sits closer to the information layer.

The company's ImmunoID NeXT platform was developed to characterize tumors deeply, including neoantigens and features of the immune microenvironment. Personalis has worked with Moderna on personalized cancer vaccine programs.

This makes the company a useful example of an infrastructure business whose value rises if individualized therapy expands.

It does not need to own the approved vaccine.

It needs more therapies to require the kind of data it produces.

The same economic pattern appears in semiconductor booms, cloud computing and oil exploration.

The company selling picks and shovels can benefit from several competing prospectors.

Precision oncology creates its own picks and shovels: sequencing, HLA typing, variant interpretation, ctDNA monitoring, manufacturing software and clinical logistics.

Foundation Medicine occupies one version of that role through comprehensive genomic profiling. Guardant Health and Natera represent adjacent liquid-biopsy and molecular-residual-disease markets. Their products are not neoantigen vaccines, but the ability to detect residual disease can help define when an adjuvant immune intervention is most valuable and whether molecular disease is responding.

A successful vaccine can pull diagnostics into the treatment algorithm.

That creates a commercial flywheel.

The test identifies the patient.

The treatment generates a reason to repeat the test.

The repeated test generates longitudinal data.

The data improve patient selection.

The selection increases treatment efficiency.

The treatment creates more demand for the test.

This is why the boundary between pharmaceutical and diagnostic value is becoming unstable.

Nykode Therapeutics offers a different delivery technology.

Its VB10.NEO program uses a DNA-based vaccine platform designed to encode individualized neoantigens. The company has described a workflow intended to move from biopsy to product release in a matter of weeks.

The significance is not whether Nykode ultimately wins.

It is that mRNA does not own personalization.

DNA, synthetic peptides, viral vectors, dendritic cells and engineered T cells can all compete for the same target layer.

Geneos Therapeutics illustrates another variation with personalized DNA immunotherapy. Its GNOS-PV02 program has been studied with interleukin-12 and pembrolizumab in hepatocellular carcinoma.

Again, the combination matters.

Few companies are betting that a personalized vaccine will live alone.

The commercial unit is increasingly the regimen.

Regimens change bargaining power.

If a vaccine only works with a checkpoint inhibitor, the checkpoint company becomes a natural partner. If several checkpoint inhibitors work equally well, the vaccine company gains leverage. If the vaccine improves outcomes enough to reduce later therapies, payers may accept a high upfront price. If the benefit is modest, every additional drug makes the combination harder to justify economically.

The science determines part of the value stack.

Clinical substitutability determines the rest.

Small firms face a different problem from Merck or Roche.

Personalized medicine consumes cash.

Sequencing, manufacturing, trial operations and regulatory work all occur before commercial revenue. A biotech company can have excellent Phase 1 data and still fail financially if capital markets close before the confirmatory trial.

This makes company survival an independent variable in scientific progress.

A program can die because the biology failed.

It can also die because the balance sheet did.

The distinction matters when reading the history of the field. Failed companies may leave useful patents, datasets, manufacturing teams and clinical lessons that larger firms later acquire. The corporate vehicle can disappear while the technology survives.

Neon Therapeutics is one example of this recycling through acquisition. Other neoantigen-focused startups have merged, restructured or narrowed their pipelines as investors learned how expensive personalization would be.

The public sees a discontinuity.

One logo disappears.

The industry sees assets moving.

This is one reason intellectual property becomes so important during downturns.

A company with little cash can still own patents, datasets and know-how another firm wants. The private value of publicly rooted science can therefore change hands repeatedly without returning to the public domain.

The patent becomes more durable than the startup.

There is also a layer of companies that rarely appear in cancer-vaccine headlines because their products are generic enough to serve multiple sectors.

Illumina and other sequencing-platform providers sell the machines and reagents that make tumor sequencing routine. Cloud providers supply computational infrastructure. Contract development and manufacturing organizations can handle portions of personalized production. Thermo Fisher Scientific and other life-science suppliers sell instruments, enzymes, reagents and laboratory systems used across the workflow.

The eventual commercial winners may include companies whose names never appear on the treatment consent form.

This is a recurring pattern in technological revolutions.

The application captures attention.

Infrastructure captures durable demand.

Neoantigen medicine could produce the same migration of value.

If target-selection algorithms converge and mRNA manufacturing becomes standardized, sequencing and clinical networks may become commoditized or highly competitive. If one company develops a dramatically superior ranking system, software and data may capture more of the margin. If regulators approve platform-level manufacturing changes, factories may scale efficiently. If each product requires repeated bespoke review, regulatory expertise may remain a major moat.

The shape of the industry is not fixed because the scarce layer is still moving.

At first, sequencing was scarce.

Then sequencing became cheap enough that interpretation mattered more.

At first, mRNA manufacturing was exotic.

The pandemic expanded it.

At first, proving that personalized vaccines could induce T cells was a major scientific achievement.

Now the field needs randomized clinical benefit.

Every solved bottleneck exposes the next one.

This is the correct way to think about company advantage.

Not “Who owns neoantigens?”

Nobody does.

Who owns the current bottleneck?

The answer can change.

Merck owns a critical companion drug and oncology relationships. Moderna owns a validated individualized mRNA platform with a positive Phase 3 topline result. BioNTech owns a broad mRNA and immunotherapy platform with deep cancer origins and Roche partnership, while also carrying the knowledge from unsuccessful trials. Roche owns diagnostics and oncology assets. Personalis specializes in deep tumor and immune profiling. Smaller firms explore alternative vaccine formats and niches.

Academic medical centers own something different.

Patients and trust.

The best cancer centers have pathology departments, sequencing programs, tumor boards, trial infrastructure and clinicians who see difficult cases. They can run investigator-initiated studies and generate the evidence companies later use to define markets.

The relationship between academic centers and industry is not simply buyer and seller.

They are co-producers of the field.

A company needs patients for trials. The center needs investigational products and funding. Scientists move between them. Patents are licensed. Data are shared under agreements. Publications establish credibility.

The commercial boundary passes through the hospital.

That complicates the public-private story because many leading academic centers are themselves hybrid institutions.

They receive federal grants, philanthropy, insurance payments, patent royalties and industry-sponsored trial money. A publicly funded discovery may be patented by a university and exclusively licensed to a startup whose founders are university faculty. A hospital can simultaneously be a charitable institution, a patent owner, a clinical-trial site and a buyer of the eventual drug.

The public/private dichotomy is therefore institutional shorthand.

The real question is how rights and obligations move through the chain.

Who funded the discovery?

Who owns the patent?

Who paid for the clinical trial?

Who controls the dataset?

Who can manufacture the product?

Who negotiates the price?

Who bears the risk if it fails?

Who pays if it succeeds?

The answer can be a different organization at every step.

Mini-clinics enter at the far end of this value stack.

They do not need to recreate Moderna.

They can purchase fragments of the stack and assemble a service.

A boutique clinic can order sequencing from a commercial lab, use an external bioinformatics provider, contract peptide synthesis and administer a personalized vaccine under whatever legal framework applies in its jurisdiction.

Its competitive advantage may be neither science nor manufacturing.

It may be willingness.

The clinic will treat a patient whom a large trial excludes.

That can be humane.

It can also be commercially dangerous because willingness is hard to distinguish from weak standards when the patient is desperate.

The large pharmaceutical company and the small clinic therefore occupy opposite ends of the same technological trajectory.

The large company spends years making an individualized process standardized enough for regulators.

The small clinic exploits the fact that the components have become available before the evidence is standardized.

Both are enabled by the falling cost of the stack.

Only one may have randomized data.

The companies around the key are not merely competing to sell a medicine.

They are competing to decide which parts of personalization remain proprietary after the science becomes ordinary.

The deepest commercial prize may not be the vaccine sequence.

It may be control of the loop that turns each treated patient into a better decision for the next one.

That loop changes how conventional competition should be measured.

Two companies can sell different products while depending on the same sequencing instruments, the same hospital systems and the same companion checkpoint drug. They may compete at one layer and cooperate at three others. A diagnostic company can supply rival vaccine developers. A pharmaceutical partner can collaborate with one personalized platform while keeping the option to test another. Contract manufacturers can learn from several customers without owning any therapy.

The industry is less a row of firms than a graph of dependencies.

That matters during consolidation.

An acquisition can look small if measured by the revenue of the acquired company and large if it closes a missing edge in the graph. A pharmaceutical company that already owns drugs and clinical relationships may value a target-discovery dataset more than another molecule. A vaccine developer may value a cell-therapy startup because receptor data improve the same underlying neoantigen models. A diagnostic platform may be valuable because it controls the point where patients enter the therapeutic funnel.

The prize is often optionality.

Own enough adjacent capabilities and a company can redirect when one modality fails.

BioNTech's colorectal setback illustrates the value of a broad platform. A failed vaccine trial hurts the program without necessarily invalidating antibodies, other vaccine designs, cell therapies or the data generated along the way. A single-asset startup facing the same result may have no second act.

Corporate diversification can therefore preserve scientific learning that capital markets would otherwise erase.

It can also reduce competitive pressure if the surviving firms become the only organizations capable of financing Phase 3 development.

The economics of evidence create a moat before patents do.

Running a large global randomized trial is expensive, slow and organizationally difficult. A small company may possess a plausible vaccine, a capable algorithm and a workable manufacturing process yet still need a large partner because the proof required for routine oncology exceeds its balance sheet.

That gives incumbent pharmaceutical companies bargaining power over which scientific ideas reach definitive testing.

The market can contain dozens of technical platforms and only a few evidentiary platforms.

This distinction matters for public policy because competition at the laboratory level does not guarantee competition at approval.

If several startups ultimately depend on the same two or three large oncology partners, strategic priorities inside those partners can determine which approaches survive. A program that threatens an existing franchise, requires an unattractive combination or serves a small market may struggle even if the science is strong.

Public and nonprofit trial networks can partly counter that concentration by testing questions no single sponsor has an incentive to own.

Comparative trials are especially important.

Companies are naturally motivated to show that their product adds value to standard care. They are less naturally motivated to fund a trial asking whether a cheaper competitor, a shorter regimen or a shared-antigen library performs almost as well as the proprietary personalized product.

The most socially valuable question can be commercially orphaned.

That is one reason an ecosystem needs institutions with different objectives.

Companies optimize products and portfolios.

Academic groups can interrogate mechanisms.

Government funders can support comparisons and shared infrastructure.

Payers can demand evidence about value.

Patients and clinicians can reveal which burdens matter outside the endpoint table.

No actor sees the whole stack unless the system forces the views to meet.

Data ownership is where those views increasingly collide.

A sequencing provider may hold raw molecular data. The vaccine company holds target rankings and manufacturing records. The hospital holds the medical record. A ctDNA company holds longitudinal molecular measurements. The checkpoint-drug sponsor may hold trial data. The patient generated all of it biologically and may have limited practical ability to recombine it.

The commercial loop can therefore be fragmented even while each company tries to make its own portion closed.

Interoperability becomes a competitive issue.

A platform that makes it easy to move data in can recruit hospitals and partners. A platform that makes it hard to move data out can retain them. Proprietary file formats, contractual limits and incompatible identifiers can create friction that looks technical but functions economically.

This is familiar from software markets.

The difference is that switching costs in cancer medicine can arrive in the middle of a patient's treatment.

A useful regulatory and policy objective is therefore portability of the evidence needed for care, even when proprietary models remain protected. A patient should not lose the ability to understand what targets were selected, what product was manufactured and what happened clinically because the companies involved changed partners or disappeared.

Corporate mortality is certain.

Clinical records need a longer life than startups.

The companies around the key are temporary custodians of pieces of a therapeutic system that patients may need to understand for decades.

That obligation is easy to overlook while markets are growing.

It becomes obvious after the first bankruptcy, merger or discontinued platform strands longitudinal data across several owners.

The most durable company advantage may indeed be the learning loop.

The most durable public requirement should be that the loop leaves enough evidence behind for medicine to remember what happened.

There is another form of concentration that becomes visible only after a category succeeds: control of the clinical interface.

A therapy can have several technically capable manufacturers and still depend on a small number of hospital networks experienced enough to order, coordinate and administer it. Those centers become gateways for patients and attractive partners for companies. Trial relationships turn into commercial relationships. Referral patterns harden around expertise built during development.

That can be efficient.

Experience reduces error. High-volume centers learn how to preserve tissue, handle logistics and manage complex combinations. The same concentration can make adoption outside those centers slower than the biology requires.

Companies then face a distribution choice.

They can keep the network narrow and reliable, accepting slower geographic reach. Or they can train a much wider set of community sites, investing in support systems that reduce the expertise required locally. The second strategy expands the market and also risks exposing weak points hidden by academic-center trial conditions.

The winner may be the company that productizes the workflow, not only the molecule.

Ordering portals, specimen kits, reimbursement support, courier integration, manufacturing-status tracking, treatment scheduling and clinician education can become parts of the competitive product. None is a neoantigen. Together they determine whether the neoantigen reaches the patient.

This is why software companies may appear around the edges of the industry without ever ranking a peptide. A logistics platform that prevents failed handoffs can create more clinical value than a marginal improvement in a molecular predictor if the predictor was already good enough.

Bottlenecks migrate toward the ordinary.

The same migration affects bargaining power. Once sequencing is inexpensive and target prediction converges, a hospital may care most about turnaround guarantees and reimbursement support. Once manufacturing becomes reliable, a payer may care most about comparative benefit and total regimen cost. Once several products work, patients may care about travel and treatment burden.

Competitive advantage changes because the customer's problem changes.

The company that wins the scientific race does not automatically win every later race.

That is good news for competition if interfaces remain open enough for new firms to enter at the newly scarce layer.

It is bad news if the first successful platform uses contracts, data control or proprietary integration to make every adjacent layer depend on it.

The question for policymakers is therefore not whether integration is good or bad.

It is whether integration creates efficiency that can be challenged.

A vertically integrated system can reduce handoff errors and accelerate treatment. It can also make substitution difficult. The public interest is served when a competitor can enter by being better, not when it has to recreate an entire hospital-manufacturing-data network merely to test a different therapeutic idea.

Standards are what separate those futures.

Portable sequence data, interoperable treatment records, clear manufacturing specifications, accepted outcome definitions and fair access to clinical networks do not guarantee competition.

They reduce the cost of trying.

The companies around the key will spend the next decade learning where the money settles.

The public should spend the same decade making sure the settlement does not become geology.
