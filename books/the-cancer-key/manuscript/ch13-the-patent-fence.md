# The Patent Fence

A scientific discovery and a commercial product do not need the same kind of openness.

Confusing them produces bad arguments in both directions.

The strongest version of the public-funding critique says taxpayers funded the science, so companies should not be allowed to own the resulting medicine.

The strongest industry reply says a discovery without patents and private capital would remain a paper, so strong exclusivity is the price of translation.

Both positions skip the middle.

Neoantigen therapy is built from many discoveries, many rights and many forms of know-how. The legal object is not one patent fence around one molecule. It is a landscape of overlapping claims.

A university may patent a method for identifying tumor-specific mutations. A company may patent a vaccine architecture. Another may license lipid-delivery technology. A target-selection system may be protected partly by patents and partly as trade secret. A diagnostic assay may carry its own intellectual property. The checkpoint inhibitor used in combination has a separate patent estate. Manufacturing methods may be proprietary even after composition patents expire.

The final product can therefore remain commercially defended even if one patent disappears.

This matters for the public-benefit debate because it limits the power of any single legal lever.

Bayh-Dole march-in rights are often discussed as if the government could simply seize a drug patent whenever a publicly supported medicine becomes expensive.

The reality is narrower.

Bayh-Dole applies to subject inventions arising from federal funding under defined circumstances. Contractors can retain title, but the government receives rights and can require licensing under statutory criteria when, for example, practical application has not been achieved or health or safety needs are not reasonably satisfied.

Whether high price alone should justify march-in has been disputed for years.

Agencies have historically declined petitions seeking march-in primarily as a drug-pricing tool. Policy proposals have tried to clarify the role of price, but the mechanism was not designed as a general substitute for pharmaceutical price regulation.

The narrower point is more important.

Public funding creates legal interests, but those interests do not automatically translate into a simple public claim on the entire downstream product.

The product may contain privately invented layers that were never federally funded.

This is not an excuse for weak bargaining.

It is a reason to bargain earlier and more intelligently.

Patent terms are set before anyone knows the final commercial value.

Universities face a genuine dilemma.

Suppose an academic laboratory identifies an important neoantigen technology. The university can publish it openly and allow anyone to use it. That maximizes immediate diffusion but may make it difficult for a company to justify an expensive development program if competitors can copy the final product without sharing the trial cost.

The university can patent the invention and license it nonexclusively to several firms. Competition is preserved, but no single licensee may believe it has enough protection to invest in a risky Phase 3 program.

Or the university can grant an exclusive license to one company, which can raise capital around a defensible asset but can later become the sole gatekeeper.

There is no universal answer because inventions differ.

A research tool may benefit from broad nonexclusive licensing. A therapeutic candidate requiring billions in development may need more exclusivity. A platform patent affecting an entire field can create different public consequences from a narrow composition claim.

The problem is that technology-transfer offices often have to make these judgments with limited information and institutional incentives to maximize licensing revenue.

The public interest is larger than the royalty stream.

A university can earn more from one exclusive license while society earns more from five competitors.

Those returns do not appear on the same spreadsheet.

This is the patent fence problem.

A fence can create a farm.

It can also block a road.

Biotechnology needs both exclusion and passage.

The challenge is deciding which knowledge should remain precompetitive infrastructure and which asset needs temporary private control to justify development.

Neoantigen target prediction is a good example.

Basic HLA-binding models and public epitope databases created a common foundation. If every early prediction method had been locked behind restrictive licenses, the field would have advanced more slowly because researchers could not easily compare models, generate targets and build new approaches.

Once companies began linking predictions to proprietary clinical outcomes, stronger private incentives became plausible because the company itself was paying to generate the new evidence.

The dividing line is not public versus private discovery.

It is public versus private marginal contribution.

What did the company add after receiving the common substrate?

A better model trained on thousands of outcomes is a real addition.

A contractual arrangement that merely closes access to data generated in a publicly funded trial is harder to defend.

The same principle applies to manufacturing.

A general mRNA method may descend from academic science. A company can still develop proprietary improvements in sequence design, purification, formulation, process control and release testing. Those improvements can be essential to safety and scalability.

Calling the whole product “publicly invented” erases them.

Calling the whole product “privately invented” erases the substrate.

The legal system tends to avoid this philosophical accounting by defining specific claims.

Politics cannot avoid it because prices are paid for the integrated product.

One possible public-return mechanism is retained licensing.

Government and universities can preserve rights for research use, humanitarian use or specific markets. A license can grant exclusivity in wealthy countries while permitting lower-cost use elsewhere. Milestones can require active development so a company cannot warehouse an invention. Rights can revert if the licensee abandons a program.

These are ordinary contract tools.

They often matter more than dramatic ex post intervention.

Another mechanism is data return.

If public money supports a clinical trial, de-identified results and relevant negative findings can be required to enter shared repositories. This is particularly important for individualized therapies because each patient's product differs.

A conventional drug trial already produces a relatively standardized exposure. A personalized-vaccine trial produces thousands of micro-experiments inside the larger trial: different mutations, different HLA alleles, different target combinations, different immune responses.

The aggregate endpoint tells whether the product works.

The patient-level molecular data can tell the field why.

Keeping those data permanently private can slow everyone else.

Requiring indiscriminate release can also create privacy risk and undermine legitimate competitive investment.

Genomic data are not ordinary clinical data. A germline genome can be identifying. A tumor genome can contain inherited information. Family members share portions of the same sequence. Rare HLA genotypes and disease combinations can make re-identification easier.

The public commons therefore needs controlled access, not naive openness.

Well-governed data trusts, secure research environments and standardized de-identification can create broader scientific use without publishing raw genomes to the open web.

This is a solvable institutional problem if society considers the data valuable enough.

Trade secrets are harder.

A company can patent an invention and disclose how it works in exchange for time-limited exclusivity. A trade secret can remain secret indefinitely if competitors cannot lawfully discover it.

Target-selection pipelines increasingly contain both.

The published papers describe the architecture. The production model includes internal thresholds, training data, assay calibrations and operational rules that outsiders cannot reproduce.

This can create a durable advantage after patents expire.

The classic social bargain of patent law is disclosure for exclusivity.

Data-driven medicine can migrate value outside that bargain.

The moat becomes accumulated experience.

That is not inherently wrong. A hospital surgeon with twenty years of experience also knows things a textbook cannot reproduce.

The policy issue is scale.

When a private platform's hidden learning determines access to a national standard of care, society may want stronger auditing and portability than it expects from an individual expert.

Regulators already receive confidential information from companies. The FDA can evaluate proprietary manufacturing and clinical data without releasing all of it publicly.

The future may require more structured algorithmic oversight inside that model.

A regulator could require evidence that target-selection performance is not systematically worse for rare HLA alleles. It could require version control when models change. It could require post-market data on manufacturing failures and target-selection outcomes.

The goal would not be to open-source the model.

It would be to ensure that exclusivity does not make performance unknowable.

Patents can also affect frontier clinics in an unexpected way.

A small clinic may offer a personalized product outside a company's regulatory program using different manufacturing methods. If the clinic avoids patented claims or operates in a jurisdiction where the relevant rights are unavailable, it can compete technically without competing evidentially.

The large company bears the cost of the randomized trial.

The small provider can sell the concept.

This creates a free-rider problem that patent law only partly addresses.

The clinic may not copy the exact product. It may synthesize a different set of peptides based on the same general neoantigen idea.

The commercial sponsor cannot own the scientific category.

That is good for innovation.

It also means product-level regulation has to preserve the difference between a validated therapy and an unvalidated implementation.

The stronger the scientific breakthrough, the more valuable that distinction becomes.

There is a parallel risk on the other side.

A company can use a broad patent portfolio to suggest that only its implementation is legitimate, even when academic or competing methods are scientifically reasonable.

Patent ownership is not scientific truth.

It is a legal right to exclude defined activities for a limited period.

The best policy keeps these categories separate.

Clinical evidence determines whether a treatment works.

Regulatory review determines whether a product meets legal standards for a particular market and indication.

Patent law determines whether others may practice particular claimed inventions without permission.

None substitutes for the others.

The patient experiences them as one gate.

If the only approved product is patented, regulatory and patent exclusivity reinforce each other. If insurers reimburse only the approved product, payment becomes a third layer. If manufacturing requires proprietary software, a fourth layer appears.

The result can be a very strong fence even when no individual piece was designed to create monopoly power by itself.

This is how modern healthcare concentration often emerges.

Not from one villainous contract.

From several reasonable protections stacked until substitution becomes difficult.

The solution is not to abolish the protections.

Drug development really is risky. Personalized products really do require manufacturing know-how. Investors really can move their money elsewhere. A policy that makes successful commercialization impossible will reduce the number of therapies that reach patients.

But the opposite error is just as serious.

Treating every private claim as the natural reward for innovation can allow a publicly built field to become privately gated at the exact moment it becomes clinically useful.

The right question is whether the fence is doing work.

Is it financing a trial that would not otherwise occur?

Is it protecting a manufacturing improvement that required costly development?

Is it encouraging a company to serve a small patient population?

Or is it merely allowing the owner to tax a scientific standard after the investment has been recovered and alternatives have been blocked?

Those questions change over time.

An exclusive license that is socially useful in year one can become socially expensive in year fifteen.

Patent law already reflects this by limiting term, but other forms of exclusivity can outlast the patent.

Data, manufacturing complexity, regulatory precedent and clinical network effects can become the new fence.

Neoantigen medicine will make those invisible barriers more important because the product is a process.

The sequence can be unique and still depend on one company's infrastructure.

The mutation came from the patient.

The ability to turn it into a regulated medicine may belong to someone else.

That is not automatically injustice.

It is a bargain.

The bargain should be visible enough to renegotiate when the public side stops receiving what it paid to create.
