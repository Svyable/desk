# The Public Option for Personalization

The phrase public option usually arrives carrying an entire political argument.

That is not how I mean it here.

The narrower question is what the public should own, operate or guarantee when a therapeutic platform depends on infrastructure that markets have weak incentives to provide broadly.

Neoantigen medicine makes that question unusually concrete.

Governments do not need to manufacture every personalized vaccine to have an industrial role. They already participate in the system through research grants, cancer centers, sequencing programs, public hospitals, regulators, reimbursement and intellectual-property law. The real choice is not intervention or no intervention.

It is where public capacity belongs.

The first candidate is data.

Private companies can create better neoantigen-ranking systems by linking mutations, HLA types, peptide presentation, T-cell responses and clinical outcomes. That learning can be a legitimate source of competitive advantage. But the field also benefits from shared datasets that no single company has a strong reason to build: rare HLA alleles, negative binding results, uncommon cancers, treatment failures, long-term escape patterns and standardized validation sets.

These are classic public-good problems.

The value of one additional observation may be small to the organization paying to collect it and large when combined across the field. Negative data are especially awkward. A company can often extract more commercial value from a successful target than from publishing a thousand peptides that did not work. Yet those failures can save every other group from repeating the same experiment.

A public data program can buy the boring knowledge.

That is not a small role.

Machine learning systems improve not only when they see more successes but when the boundary between success and failure becomes better mapped. A predictor trained mostly on positive examples can become overconfident. A field that publishes spectacular immune responses and leaves weak ones in lab notebooks teaches itself a distorted biology.

Public funding can reward completeness rather than spectacle.

The second candidate is standards.

A fragmented personalized-medicine market can become a collection of proprietary islands. One platform requires its own sequencing workflow. Another uses a different HLA-typing format. A third stores treatment designs in a way no outside hospital can interpret. A clinic contracts with a manufacturer whose batch records do not map cleanly into the local electronic record.

Standards are how society avoids turning every seam into a tollbooth.

The government does not have to design all of them. Professional societies, standards organizations, hospitals, companies and regulators can do much of the technical work. Public authority can make interoperability valuable by recognizing standards in reimbursement, procurement and regulatory pathways.

That is often more effective than commanding openness directly.

If Medicare, a national health service or a major public hospital network requires portable chain-of-identity records for reimbursement, vendors have a reason to implement them. If regulators accept validated common formats for portions of a manufacturing submission, companies save money by converging. If publicly funded datasets require standardized metadata, researchers learn to produce compatible records.

Infrastructure is frequently built by rules that make coordination cheaper.

The third candidate is manufacturing capacity.

This is more controversial because factories look like the territory of companies.

There are good reasons for that. Manufacturing biologics requires specialized management, quality culture, constant investment and responsiveness to technology changes. Government facilities can become bureaucratic, underutilized or technologically stale. A public factory protected from competition can cost more and improve more slowly than private alternatives.

That is the strongest argument against treating state ownership as the default.

It is not an argument against public capacity under any circumstances.

The market can undersupply manufacturing for therapies with small patient populations, uncertain commercial returns or urgent public need. Academic centers already fill some of this gap by making early cell therapies, vaccines and other experimental products that no large company is ready to commercialize. The National Cancer Institute has historically operated clinical and manufacturing capabilities for research that industry would not necessarily initiate.

A modern public option could look less like a government pharmaceutical company and more like shared advanced-manufacturing infrastructure.

Qualified facilities could produce clinical-grade personalized products for publicly funded trials, rare targets and investigators without commercial partners. They could validate interoperable processes. They could provide surge or bridge capacity when a private manufacturer exits. They could train the workforce that later moves across the industry.

The analogy is not a post office competing with FedEx.

It is closer to a research reactor, a national laboratory or a shared semiconductor facility: expensive capability maintained partly because the ecosystem needs somewhere to do work that no single early project can justify building alone.

The risk is obvious.

A government facility can become a subsidy for private firms that use public equipment to create proprietary products and then charge the public monopoly prices.

That would reproduce the very capture problem the facility was meant to address.

The bargain therefore matters.

Public manufacturing could come with conditions tied to the public contribution. A trial using heavily subsidized capacity might be required to publish specified process and outcome data. A commercial product developed through the facility might owe a royalty, a domestic supply commitment, a reasonable-access plan or a license back for defined public-health uses. The exact mechanism would need to be narrow enough not to make the facility unattractive and strong enough that public investment does not disappear at the moment of commercial success.

This is harder than saying taxpayers should get a return.

Return can mean several things.

Lower prices.

Royalty revenue.

Domestic manufacturing.

Open data.

A nonexclusive license.

Priority access during shortage.

Workforce development.

The existence of a medicine that otherwise would not have been developed.

Policy arguments become confused when one side means cash and the other means capability.

A good public bargain specifies the desired return before the asset becomes valuable.

That lesson applies to patents.

Bayh-Dole allowed universities and other recipients of federal research funding to retain rights in inventions developed with federal support under a framework designed to promote practical use. The law helped create a technology-transfer system in which academic discoveries can be licensed to companies with the capital and expertise to develop them.

It also created a recurring political dispute: if taxpayers funded the underlying invention, why can the resulting medicine be expensive?

The intuitive answer is that public money already paid.

The legal and economic answer is more complicated.

The publicly funded patent may be only one component of the final product. Development can require hundreds of millions or billions of private dollars, many additional patents, manufacturing know-how and failed trials. In many drugs, the patents relevant to federal march-in rights do not cover the entire commercial exclusivity stack. Research examining Orange Book drugs has found that Bayh-Dole patents are present in only a minority and are rarely the sole listed patent barrier.

This does not make public leverage meaningless.

It makes patent leverage narrower than the political slogan.

Neoantigen medicine widens the problem beyond patents anyway.

Suppose the core peptide-binding predictor was developed with federal grants and made public. The company's value may come from proprietary training data accumulated in trials, manufacturing systems, model tuning, regulatory know-how and integration with a checkpoint drug. Marching in on one academic patent would not recreate the platform.

The asset is a system.

Public policy has to become systems-literate.

That means funding the shared layers before they are privatized rather than trying to reconstruct them afterward.

A public HLA and immunopeptidomics program can reduce dependence on one company's database.

Common clinical outcome standards can make cross-platform evidence easier to compare.

Public biobanks can preserve samples for future research under appropriate consent.

Shared manufacturing centers can keep rare investigator-initiated work possible.

Coverage rules can require structured outcome reporting for expensive early products.

Procurement contracts can preserve continuity if a vendor exits.

None of these measures confiscates a successful drug.

They shape the terrain on which drugs compete.

The fourth candidate for a public option is clinical trials.

Cancer cooperative groups and publicly supported trial networks have long allowed questions to be studied that no single commercial sponsor owns. Personalized medicine may need them even more.

Commercial trials naturally follow products. A public network can follow questions.

Does a personalized vaccine add value after surgery in a cancer too small for a major commercial program?

Which patients can safely omit checkpoint therapy?

Can a lower-cost peptide platform produce similar immune control to a proprietary mRNA product in a selected setting?

Does updating a vaccine after molecular relapse improve outcomes?

How should treatments be sequenced?

Which biomarkers identify patients who do not need the expensive personalized step?

Some of the most socially valuable questions reduce the market for a product if answered successfully.

Industry has weaker incentives to fund them.

This is not because executives are villains.

A public company is supposed to allocate capital toward projects likely to create value for shareholders. A trial designed to prove that half of eligible patients can skip the company's therapy is difficult to prioritize against a trial expanding use into a new tumor type.

Public comparative-effectiveness research exists precisely for these gaps.

The logic becomes stronger as oncology stacks multiple expensive treatments.

A neoantigen vaccine may be combined with a checkpoint inhibitor. A patient may also receive surgery, radiation, targeted therapy or chemotherapy. If the new personalized layer gains approval only as part of a combination, the health system needs to know which parts remain necessary over time. Otherwise the initial successful regimen can become a permanently priced bundle even after science suggests simplification.

Public trials can unbundle success.

The fifth candidate is access infrastructure.

If the treatment requires high-quality tumor-normal sequencing, public insurance can define coverage pathways that recognize the diagnostic as part of manufacturing rather than as an optional extra. If community sites need training to handle tissue and administer the therapy, public cancer networks can support it. If rare HLA groups are underrepresented, grants can deliberately recruit and study them.

This is not the glamorous frontier of immunology.

It is the frontier between a breakthrough and a health service.

The strongest objection to all of these ideas is that government can make innovation slower.

That objection deserves more respect than it often receives.

Biotechnology is unusually sensitive to time. A company with eighteen months of cash cannot wait three years for a grant contract to be negotiated. A manufacturing platform cannot freeze while a standards committee deliberates. Academic procurement can turn a simple equipment purchase into a season. Public salary structures can make it difficult to retain specialized staff.

A badly designed public option becomes a reliability problem of its own.

The answer is not to assume that public institutions can match private firms at every operational task.

They should choose the tasks where their different incentives are an advantage.

Long time horizons.

Rare populations.

Open standards.

Negative data.

Comparative trials.

Continuity.

Training.

Baseline capacity.

Private firms should dominate where speed, integration, risk capital and focused execution matter most.

Product development.

Manufacturing optimization.

Commercial scaling.

Rapid software improvement.

Global trial execution.

The boundary will not stay fixed.

A public capability can seed a market and later shrink. A private market can mature enough that government no longer needs to operate capacity directly. A strategic bottleneck can become ordinary commodity infrastructure. Good industrial policy knows when to leave.

This is why I prefer public option to public solution.

An option preserves bargaining power.

If every personalized therapy depends on one or two private manufacturers, the health system has little leverage when capacity is scarce. If a public or nonprofit facility can produce clinical-grade alternatives for selected use, the threat of exit disciplines the market even when the public facility handles little commercial volume.

The same is true of data.

A public benchmark does not need to be better than every proprietary model. It needs to be good enough that the field is not epistemically dependent on one company's claims. An open HLA dataset does not eliminate private databases. It establishes a floor from which everyone can start.

Options are valuable even when unused.

This principle is familiar in infrastructure. A backup generator is valuable because the primary grid may fail. A strategic reserve matters because ordinary suppliers may not deliver during crisis. A public hospital matters partly because it treats people private systems find unprofitable.

The personalized-medicine equivalent is the ability to keep knowledge, manufacturing and access alive when the commercial path becomes narrow.

That matters because oncology markets are selective.

A platform can be scientifically compelling and commercially unattractive in a rare cancer. A target can benefit only a few HLA-defined patients. A company can abandon a program after a strategic merger even though investigators still believe a subgroup is worth studying. The private decision can be rational and the public loss real.

A resilient research system keeps some doors open after the market walks away.

There is a final reason to build public capacity: price discovery.

When the only entities capable of making a therapy are the entities selling it, society has little independent basis for understanding the cost structure. Personalized products will legitimately cost more to manufacture than ordinary pills, but the size of that premium is not ordained by biology. It depends on volume, automation, failure rates, facilities, intellectual property, required assays, turnaround guarantees and margin.

A public or nonprofit manufacturing program can reveal what competent production costs at a different required return on capital.

That information can improve reimbursement negotiation without requiring that government become the dominant manufacturer.

Benchmark capacity creates benchmark economics.

Companies will object that public facilities are subsidized and therefore not comparable.

They are right.

The comparison still has value if the subsidy is explicit. A policymaker can ask whether the private premium reflects superior speed and reliability, the cost of capital, risk taken during development, proprietary technology, or simple market power. The answer may justify a high price. At least the conversation becomes empirical.

The same transparency should run the other direction.

Public facilities should publish their true cost, including capital, failed batches, staffing, compliance and overhead. Government can hide inefficiency through budgets as easily as companies can hide margin through transfer pricing.

A public option worth trusting must be auditable.

The deeper theme is that public benefit should be designed rather than assumed.

Taxpayer funding does not automatically make a future product affordable.

Private development does not automatically mean public exploitation.

Open science does not automatically create equitable access.

Patents do not automatically create innovation.

Government manufacturing does not automatically lower cost.

Markets do not automatically supply neglected infrastructure.

Every institution has strengths that become weaknesses when pushed too far.

Neoantigen medicine arrived through their combination.

The Human Genome Project and public sequencing investments expanded the map. Universities and public laboratories built immunology. Companies industrialized RNA, checkpoints, diagnostics and manufacturing. Patients entered trials. Regulators created pathways. Investors financed failure. Hospitals turned protocols into care.

The next bargain should be equally plural.

Let companies compete to build the best key-making machines.

Let public institutions keep the measurements, standards and long-tail capacity broad enough that no one machine becomes the only definition of a key.

Let intellectual property reward the pieces that required private risk without converting public datasets into permanently private toll roads.

Let reimbursement pay for outcomes and reliable delivery without assuming every price is simply the cost of innovation.

Let publicly funded trials ask which expensive pieces patients can safely avoid.

Let experimental access produce usable evidence instead of disappearing into testimonials.

None of that is a revolution in ownership.

It is maintenance of a mixed system.

The American biomedical model already mixes public discovery with private development. The mistake is not that the sectors overlap. The mistake is pretending the handoff happens only once.

In personalized medicine the handoff happens at every layer.

A public dataset enters a private model. A private therapy enters a public hospital. A government payer buys a patented product. A company-generated failure teaches an academic lab. A university invention becomes a startup. A commercial platform trains the clinicians who later build a competing public protocol.

Value moves in both directions.

The policy task is to keep it moving.

Private capture becomes a problem when the flow turns into a one-way valve: public resources enter, monopoly rents leave, and neither data, capacity nor access returns to the system that made the platform possible.

Public capture would be the mirror-image mistake: appropriating so much of the private return that capital stops funding the difficult transition from scientific possibility to reliable medicine.

The durable bargain sits between them.

Society should be generous before success because research is uncertain.

It should be less forgetful after success because infrastructure has value.

The public option can also be contractual rather than physical.

A government may not need to own a factory if it can buy an enforceable right to capacity. A procurement agreement can reserve manufacturing slots during shortage, require geographic service levels, or trigger technology-transfer provisions if the sole supplier exits an indication. A public payer can purchase continuity rather than pills.

That distinction matters because ownership is expensive.

Maintaining a facility that must stay current across rapidly changing RNA, peptide and cell technologies could become a poor use of public capital. Contractual capacity can preserve leverage while leaving routine optimization with firms that specialize in it. The public role becomes specifying the failure it cannot tolerate and paying explicitly to prevent it.

This is similar to other critical infrastructure markets.

Airlines remain private while governments define safety and, in some contexts, subsidize service to places the market would undersupply. Electricity generators can be private while system operators pay for reserve capacity. Pharmaceutical production can remain private while public purchasers treat resilience, interoperability and minimum access as purchased attributes rather than charitable extras.

Personalized medicine will need the same vocabulary.

Price for the dose.

Price for readiness.

Price for data return.

Price for geographic reach.

Price for a second source.

Once those values are separated, the public bargain becomes easier to audit. A company can charge for providing a capability society genuinely wants. A government can stop pretending that the cheapest invoice necessarily buys the most resilient system.

The point of a public option is not to prove that public institutions can outperform private ones at every layer.

It is to make abandonment, opacity and single-supplier dependence choices rather than inevitabilities.

The cancer key was never made by one hand.

There is no reason its benefits should depend on one either.
