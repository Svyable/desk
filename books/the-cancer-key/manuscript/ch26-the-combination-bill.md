# The Combination Bill

The personalized vaccine may not be the most expensive drug in the regimen.

That is where the pricing argument becomes harder.

Cancer medicine rarely advances by replacing everything that came before. It stacks. Surgery remains. Radiation remains. Cytotoxic chemotherapy remains. Targeted drugs remain. Checkpoint inhibitors remain. A new therapy enters beside them and, at least at first, is usually tested in the setting most likely to produce a detectable benefit.

For individualized neoantigen vaccines, that often means immunological partnership.

A vaccine can teach T cells what to recognize. A checkpoint inhibitor can remove inhibitory signals that keep those T cells from acting effectively. The combination has a biological logic. The positive melanoma program developed by Moderna and Merck pairs the individualized vaccine with pembrolizumab rather than asking the vaccine to carry the entire treatment effect by itself.

Scientifically, the combination can make the platform more powerful.

Economically, it creates a bundle.

The patient does not buy a neoantigen vaccine in isolation. The health system may pay for surgery, pathology, sequencing, vaccine design, manufacturing, checkpoint therapy, administration, imaging, laboratory monitoring and management of immune-related adverse effects. If recurrence still occurs, the patient may then move into another sequence of expensive treatment.

The price of the key is not the price of the door.

This matters because public arguments about drug cost often fixate on one list price.

List prices are visible. Regimens are messy.

A therapy can appear expensive and still reduce total spending if it prevents metastatic recurrence that would require years of treatment. Another can appear modest relative to existing oncology prices and still increase total spending if it is layered onto therapy patients would have received anyway without allowing anything else to be removed.

The economically relevant question is incremental cost for incremental health.

That sounds like the language of health economics because it is.

In the United States, where explicit cost-effectiveness thresholds play a less direct formal role in coverage than in some national health systems, the concept still exists whether anyone says the words. Payers estimate budgets. Hospitals compare protocols. Employers feel premium growth. Patients confront coinsurance. Policymakers ask whether a benefit is worth the expenditure.

Personalized medicine does not repeal scarcity.

It can make scarcity harder to see because the treatment feels morally singular.

A patient-specific product is literally made for this person. Once manufacturing begins, denying the final therapy can feel different from declining to stock a mass-produced drug. The sunk cost becomes emotionally and operationally powerful. The sequence has already been read. The targets have already been selected. The batch may already exist with one patient's name on it.

Personalization turns a coverage decision into a story.

That can improve humane care.

It can also weaken price discipline.

A manufacturer negotiating with a payer knows that the therapy cannot be redirected to another customer. A payer knows the patient and oncologist may have waited weeks for it. A hospital may have invested staff time coordinating the process. Everyone becomes committed before the final claim is adjudicated.

The system will need authorization earlier in the pathway.

One likely model is a single bundled approval for the personalized process. Before sequencing intended for manufacture begins, the payer confirms that the patient meets clinical criteria and that the therapeutic pathway is covered. This reduces the risk that a product is manufactured and then denied.

But bundling creates its own question: what exactly is inside the price?

If the manufacturer controls sequencing, algorithm, vaccine manufacture and logistics, a single platform price is administratively simple. It can hide margins across the stack. If every component is billed separately, prices are more visible but coordination becomes more fragile.

The choice between bundle and itemization is not merely accounting.

It shapes competition.

A bundled platform can cross-subsidize expensive patients with easier ones. It can guarantee turnaround and absorb failed batches. It can offer the health system one accountable counterparty. It also makes it difficult for a cheaper sequencing lab or alternative manufacturer to compete for one layer.

Unbundling makes the modules contestable.

It can also make nobody responsible for the whole.

Healthcare has lived through this problem elsewhere. Bundled payments can reward coordination but create pressure to avoid high-risk patients. Fee-for-service can preserve access to complex inputs but reward volume. Capitation can encourage prevention but create incentives to underprovide expensive care.

There is no payment model without a failure mode.

Neoantigen medicine will find new versions of old incentives.

Consider manufacturing failure.

If the payer reimburses only successful delivered products, the manufacturer absorbs the cost of failed sequencing, synthesis and release. That creates a strong incentive to improve reliability. It may also encourage the company to exclude technically difficult patients before starting.

If the payer reimburses every manufacturing attempt, access can be broader. The payer assumes process risk and may end up financing inefficient operations.

A hybrid contract could pay a smaller initiation fee and the majority on successful delivery.

That sounds reasonable until the definition of successful delivery becomes contested.

Is a product successful because it passed release testing?

Because it was infused?

Because it generated a T-cell response?

Because the patient remained recurrence-free for a defined period?

Because the patient lived longer?

Every step moves financial risk from one institution to another.

Outcome-based payment is especially seductive in oncology.

If the therapy works, pay the high price. If it fails, refund part of it.

The idea aligns payment with value. The implementation is difficult.

Cancer outcomes are influenced by multiple treatments. A patient receiving vaccine plus checkpoint therapy who remains recurrence-free may have benefited from either, both or surgery alone. A patient who recurs may still have had recurrence delayed. Follow-up can last years. Patients change insurers. Definitions of failure matter. Companies need predictable revenue to finance manufacturing capacity.

A beautiful contract can become an expensive data system that settles surprisingly little.

Still, personalized medicine may make outcome contracting more practical in one respect: its records are unusually rich.

The platform already knows the patient's tumor sequence, manufacturing date and treatment composition. If connected to clinical follow-up and circulating tumor DNA, it may detect recurrence precisely. The same digital infrastructure used to build the drug can support the contract.

That creates a new commercial temptation.

The manufacturer can become the longitudinal data intermediary.

It does not merely sell a vaccine. It monitors molecular residual disease, triggers updated sequencing, recommends another product and reports outcomes to the payer.

The company becomes difficult to replace because it holds the history.

This can improve care through continuity.

It can also turn cancer treatment into an ecosystem business.

Technology investors know the model: sell the initial product, accumulate data, raise switching costs, expand into adjacent services and let each layer strengthen the others. In software this can be efficient and annoying. In medicine it can determine which therapy a patient receives next.

Antitrust policy is not the first thing an oncologist thinks about after surgery.

It may eventually belong in the background.

A dominant neoantigen platform could integrate sequencing, immune monitoring, manufacturing and proprietary decision support. If its checkpoint partner also controls a standard component of the combination, the alliance can create a powerful commercial stack.

There is nothing inherently anticompetitive about integration. The combination may be exactly what made the therapy work. Joint development can reduce transaction costs and align incentives to run expensive trials.

The concern appears when successful combination evidence becomes commercial lock-in.

Suppose the personalized vaccine is approved only with one checkpoint inhibitor because that is the regimen studied. A competing checkpoint drug may be pharmacologically similar, cheaper or better in another setting, but substituting it can fall outside the evidence base or label. The clinical bundle becomes a proprietary bundle even if the biological mechanism does not require brand exclusivity.

This is a familiar problem in combination oncology.

The remedy is evidence.

Independent and publicly funded trials can test whether components are interchangeable or removable. Biosimilars can reduce the price of mature biologics. Head-to-head studies can establish whether a cheaper checkpoint backbone works. De-escalation trials can ask whether some patients need the checkpoint drug for as long as originally studied.

The most valuable cost-saving trial may happen after the breakthrough.

That trial is often commercially unattractive.

A sponsor earns revenue when the product is used, not when researchers prove half the doses can be omitted. This is another place where public research has a different comparative advantage.

Combination pricing also reveals the importance of timing.

The same therapy can have different economic value in metastatic disease and after curative-intent surgery.

In metastatic cancer, the health system may compare a personalized regimen with other ongoing treatments. Benefits may be measured in response, progression-free survival and overall survival. Manufacturing delay can be especially dangerous because disease is active.

In the adjuvant setting, the tumor may have been removed. The therapy is intended to eliminate microscopic residual disease and prevent recurrence. Many treated patients might never have recurred even without the new therapy.

That is the paradox of preventive oncology.

You treat people who feel well to prevent an event you cannot know would have happened to each of them.

The better the baseline prognosis, the more patients must be treated to prevent one recurrence unless biomarkers can identify who is truly at risk.

This is where circulating tumor DNA may reshape the economics.

If molecular residual disease can identify patients with a much higher likelihood of recurrence, personalized vaccines could be concentrated where expected benefit is greatest. That can improve cost-effectiveness and spare low-risk patients unnecessary treatment.

But using a biomarker to reduce treatment volume changes the manufacturer's market.

Again, the most socially valuable precision may reduce sales.

A rational health system should reward the therapy more generously when precision produces greater value, not simply punish the company for treating fewer patients. Value-based pricing is one theoretical answer: a smaller high-benefit population can support a high per-patient price while total spending remains manageable.

That logic already underlies many rare-disease therapies.

It becomes politically difficult when the same platform was built on public science.

The headline writes itself: taxpayer-funded research leads to a bespoke cancer vaccine priced at hundreds of thousands of dollars.

The counter-headline is equally simple: private companies risked billions to turn public science into an approved product for a small, complex population.

Both can be true.

Price cannot settle the argument about credit.

It can settle whether patients get treated.

The public-private bargain therefore needs a vocabulary more useful than cheap and expensive.

What did development cost?

How many failed programs did the successful product have to finance?

What does one commercial batch cost at current volume?

How quickly do manufacturing costs fall with automation?

What is the value of recurrence prevented?

How much of the regimen's cost comes from the personalized product and how much from the checkpoint partner?

How much public funding supported directly relevant inventions versus broad foundational science?

What return on capital is needed to keep competitors entering the field?

Which portion of price reflects exclusivity rather than cost or risk?

These questions are harder to print on a protest sign.

They are where policy lives.

The manufacturing curve may be especially important.

Personalized products have high variable costs because every patient requires a design and batch. But software, sequencing and automation can become cheaper quickly. The first commercial price may reflect an immature factory. Five years later, the process could be dramatically more efficient.

Will price fall with cost?

Markets do not guarantee it.

If the product remains protected by patents, biologic exclusivity, data, manufacturing know-how and regulatory complexity, cost reductions can become margin rather than savings.

That is not automatically wrong. High margins can finance next-generation platforms and failed research. It does mean payers should not assume the launch economics are permanent facts of nature.

Reimbursement can include productivity sharing.

A payer could negotiate price resets as manufacturing volume rises or turnaround improves. A public program could exchange guaranteed volume for lower unit price. A manufacturer could offer separate prices for product and premium turnaround. Countries could pool purchasing for rare HLA-defined subgroups.

All of these mechanisms treat the factory as an economic system rather than a black box.

The company may prefer the black box.

So would most industries.

Trade secrets are legitimate. Cost accounting can reveal competitive information. Payers also guard negotiated rebates. The oncology price is often opaque from both directions.

Opacity makes political mythology easier.

One side imagines the drug costs pennies to make because the raw RNA or peptide is cheap.

The other imagines every dollar of price is necessary to fund innovation.

Neither story survives a serious look at the stack.

Material is only part of manufacturing. Facilities, quality systems, failed batches, logistics, software, assays, staff and regulatory compliance are real. So are monopoly rents, marketing, corporate overhead and returns to capital.

The honest answer is not that personalized medicine is inherently unaffordable.

It is that affordability will be designed.

Scale can lower cost.

Automation can lower cost.

Competition can lower price.

Biosimilars and interchangeable combination partners can lower the bundle.

Biomarkers can concentrate treatment on patients most likely to benefit.

Public capacity can benchmark manufacturing economics.

Coverage can spread catastrophic expense across insured populations.

Outcome contracts can shift some failure risk.

Public research can identify which pieces of the regimen are unnecessary.

Each mechanism solves a different part of the bill.

The final part is the patient's share.

A therapy can be cost-effective to the health system and financially devastating to the patient.

Coinsurance tied to a percentage of a high biologic price can create bills no ordinary household can absorb. Travel, lodging, unpaid work and caregiving add costs that do not appear in the drug budget. An individualized process requiring repeated visits can make these indirect expenses larger.

Financial toxicity is a medical side effect administered by the payment system.

It affects adherence, stress and sometimes treatment choice.

A serious public-benefit argument has to include it.

If the neoantigen revolution produces therapies that insurers nominally cover but families cannot practically reach, the science will have solved a narrower problem than the title of this book suggests.

The same is true globally.

A regimen built around a personalized product plus a premium checkpoint inhibitor may be commercially viable in the United States, Switzerland, Germany and other wealthy systems while remaining impossible for most of the world. Manufacturing automation may eventually help. Shared neoantigens may help more. Regional production may reduce logistics. Competition among checkpoints and vaccine platforms may lower prices.

But the initial frontier is likely to be unequal.

Breakthroughs usually are.

The relevant question is whether inequality is a temporary diffusion pattern or a permanent business model.

The answer will depend partly on how quickly the platform becomes simpler.

The first genome cost billions. Sequencing later became a routine clinical input.

The first CAR-T therapies required extraordinary infrastructure. The field is now trying to make cellular therapy faster, more standardized and more distributed.

The first personalized cancer vaccines may look artisanal.

The industrial goal is not to preserve artisanal scarcity.

It is to make specificity cheap.

If that happens, price debates will move again. The valuable layer may shift from manufacturing toward data, algorithms, combinations or clinical networks. A company that once earned its advantage from an RNA factory may later defend itself through proprietary outcome data. The cost of the molecule can fall while the cost of the ecosystem stays high.

That is why following only the vial is a mistake.

The combination bill reveals where value is actually captured.

The personalized product is one line.

The checkpoint drug is another.

The diagnostic is another.

The hospital is another.

The platform fee may be hidden inside one of them.

The public contribution sits upstream of all of them.

A society deciding whether the breakthrough produced public benefit has to add the whole page.

Then it has to ask what disappeared from the page because the treatment worked.

A recurrence prevented has no invoice.

That absence may be the most valuable line of all.

The bundle also complicates evidence attribution after launch.

A positive combination trial establishes that the tested strategy outperformed its comparator. It does not automatically tell the payer how much of the incremental benefit should be credited economically to each component. The checkpoint inhibitor may already have an established standalone effect. The vaccine may supply the increment. The interaction between them may be the actual therapeutic object.

Pricing systems prefer separable products.

Biology may prefer complements.

That tension creates a bargaining problem when the two products belong to different companies. Each firm can point to the other as necessary while negotiating for its own share of the value. A payer may have leverage over the mature checkpoint drug but none over the novel personalized product, or the reverse in another market. The final regimen price can reflect corporate boundaries as much as scientific ones.

One response is indication-specific contracting around the regimen rather than the molecule.

The payer can ask what the complete treatment strategy costs for the defined population and compare that with the expected downstream care avoided. Manufacturers can still settle revenue between themselves under private agreements. The health system buys the result it is actually trying to produce.

That model becomes more plausible when combination benefit is large and the patient population is clearly defined.

It becomes more dangerous when the bundle includes components that later prove unnecessary.

So every successful bundle should carry a de-bundling agenda.

Can the checkpoint duration be shortened?

Can lower-risk patients omit the vaccine?

Can a biosimilar backbone substitute?

Can ctDNA identify patients who need intensification rather than treating everyone?

Can immune monitoring show that some patients require fewer doses?

These are not attacks on the breakthrough.

They are how a breakthrough becomes affordable infrastructure instead of a permanently maximal regimen.

The first trial proves what works together.

The next generation should learn what no longer has to stay together.
