# Chapter Evidence and Falsification Notes

## Chapter 1 — The Patient of One

**Evidence:** Merck/Moderna Phase 3 INTerpath-001 announcement, August 19, 2026; BioNTech BNT122-01 colorectal termination, August 28, 2026; NCI/NHGRI TCGA scale; FDA individualized-therapy guidance as a regulatory analogy; FDA expanded-access materials.

**What would make the chapter's framing false:** evidence that the Phase 3 topline result does not survive full analysis or regulatory review; evidence that neoantigen specificity contributes little beyond pembrolizumab; evidence that individualized treatment cannot be manufactured reliably at commercial scale.

## Chapter 2 — The Mark That Shouldn't Be There

**Evidence:** 2026 Nature Biotechnology review by Patrick Ott; IEDB and HLA-prediction literature; neoantigen vaccine and T-cell studies showing mutation-derived antigen recognition.

**What would make this false:** systematic evidence that predicted mutation-derived targets rarely appear on tumors or do not materially improve therapeutic specificity; evidence that HLA prediction cannot generalize across clinically relevant populations.

## Chapter 3 — A Century of Failed Vaccines

**Evidence:** historical cancer-immunotherapy literature; NCI adoptive-cell-therapy program; sipuleucel-T and CAR-T regulatory history; early personalized neoantigen vaccine studies.

**What would make this false:** evidence that modern personalized vaccines are mechanistically continuous with earlier shared-antigen vaccines in ways that make the target-class distinction clinically unimportant.

## Chapter 4 — The Sequencing Machine Changed the Question

**Evidence:** NHGRI TCGA history and scale; clinical tumor-normal sequencing practice; public cancer-genomics infrastructure; Foundation Medicine and Personalis platform descriptions.

**What would make this false:** evidence that sequencing and molecular interpretation remain too slow, inaccurate or tissue-limited to function as routine therapeutic inputs for individualized manufacturing.

## Chapter 5 — The Algorithm Between Mutation and Medicine

**Evidence:** peer-reviewed HLA-binding and presentation prediction literature; IEDB resources; open tools including NetMHCpan and MHCflurry; published personalized vaccine workflows.

**What would make this false:** evidence that target ranking contributes little to outcome because manufacturing many targets makes selection quality largely irrelevant; evidence that direct experimental target validation is consistently superior and fast enough to replace prediction.

## Chapter 6 — The mRNA Detour

**Evidence:** mRNA cancer-vaccine history; nucleoside-modification and delivery literature; company histories of BioNTech and Moderna; public mRNA investment analyses; pandemic-era manufacturing scale.

**What would make this false:** evidence that COVID-era mRNA scaling did not materially reduce oncology manufacturing, regulatory or supply-chain constraints; evidence that another delivery format proves substantially superior for individualized cancer vaccination.

## Chapter 7 — The Melanoma Result

**Evidence:** Merck/Moderna INTerpath-001 Phase 3 topline announcement; five-year KEYNOTE-942 Phase 2b update; trial registry; published pembrolizumab adjuvant melanoma evidence.

**What would make this false:** full Phase 3 data showing the reported endpoints are not clinically meaningful, material safety concerns, regulatory rejection, or later evidence that the effect is not durable.

## Chapter 8 — The Pancreas and the Warning

**Evidence:** 2023 and 2025 Nature reports of autogene cevumeran in resected pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma; BioNTech's August 28, 2026 colorectal statement.

**What would make this false:** randomized pancreatic data failing to confirm meaningful benefit would weaken the optimistic interpretation; a later explanation showing the colorectal termination was unrelated to the vaccine strategy would narrow the warning.

## Chapter 9 — Cells That Learn a Name

**Evidence:** NCI 2024 personalized TCR-engineered lymphocyte study in metastatic colorectal cancer; NCI/Rosenberg neoantigen and TIL literature; FDA history of autologous cell therapy.

**What would make this false:** larger studies showing personalized neoantigen TCR approaches cannot produce durable solid-tumor benefit or that toxicity prevents practical use.

## Chapter 10 — The Factory for One

**Evidence:** company platform descriptions; Reuters August 2026 reporting on individualized manufacturing barriers; FDA individualized-therapy quality guidance; established autologous cell-therapy manufacturing practice.

**What would make this false:** evidence that individualized manufacturing cannot achieve reliable commercial turnaround or that variable payloads create unacceptable batch or regulatory failure rates.

## Chapter 11 — The Companies Around the Key

**Evidence:** public company materials from Moderna, Merck, BioNTech, Roche/Genentech, Foundation Medicine, Personalis, Nykode and Geneos; BioNTech acquisition of Neon Therapeutics.

**What would make this false:** consolidation, program termination or technological commoditization that moves value away from the described bottlenecks. Company status must be refreshed in later editions.

## Chapter 12 — The State Paid for the Map

**Evidence:** NCI appropriation; TCGA; Cancer Moonshot authorization and projects; NCI clinical-trial and intramural infrastructure; NIH-funded foundational immunology and genomics.

**What would make this false:** product-specific accounting showing the public contribution was negligible relative to private invention and development would weaken the book's public-return argument for that product, though not the broader infrastructure claim.

## Chapter 13 — The Patent Fence

**Evidence:** NIH Bayh-Dole regulations; NIH grants policy; GAO discussion of march-in policy; scholarship on pharmaceutical patent portfolios and federal funding.

**What would make this false:** evidence that exclusivity plays little role in financing late-stage development, or that current public rights already provide an effective and routinely usable affordability mechanism.

## Chapter 14 — The Price of a Public Miracle

**Evidence:** NIH historical reasonable-pricing-clause materials; public/private mRNA investment studies; direct-pay clinic pricing pages; health-economics logic of adjuvant therapy and personalized manufacturing.

**What would make this false:** transparent commercial cost data showing personalized production remains structurally so expensive that price reductions are impossible even with scale; evidence that access is dominated by non-price bottlenecks.

## Chapter 15 — The Clinic at the Frontier

**Evidence:** FDA expanded access and Right to Try materials; Swiss PVAX Phase 1/2 registry entry; Swiss Medical Network clinical-trial announcement; German clinic and company descriptions of individual-treatment attempts.

**What would make this false:** legal authorities showing described physician-led pathways were characterized incorrectly; evidence that modular individualized treatment remains too technically difficult to support small-provider models.

## Chapter 16 — Hope for Sale

**Evidence:** PYRALIS public order and disclaimer pages; IOZK public pricing; FDA warning materials in adjacent unapproved regenerative-product markets; general trial-design literature on uncontrolled outcomes.

**What would make this false:** independent, audited clinical evidence demonstrating that a named direct-pay platform achieves outcomes comparable to regulated trial programs would require rewriting its characterization. Provider claims are never used as independent proof of efficacy.

## Chapter 17 — The Regulator's Clock

**Evidence:** FDA expanded access guidance, Right to Try, investigational-drug materials and 2026 Plausible Mechanism Framework draft guidance for individualized ultra-rare therapies.

**What would make this false:** regulator policies specifically tailored to individualized cancer vaccines that supersede the analogies used here; major evidence that current access pathways materially impede rather than protect clinical development.

## Chapter 18 — The Key Is Not a Cure

**Evidence:** synthesis of the clinical, biological and institutional evidence in prior chapters.

**What would make this false:** broad randomized success across multiple hard-to-treat cancers would make the chapter too cautious; repeated failure outside melanoma would make it too optimistic.

## Chapter 19 — The Data Commons

**Evidence:** TCGA as an example of open cancer-data infrastructure; public epitope resources; emerging proprietary clinical-learning loops in precision medicine; HLA diversity literature.

**What would make this false:** evidence that proprietary outcome data produce little durable competitive advantage, or that mandatory data sharing reliably harms patient privacy or therapeutic investment more than it accelerates research.

## Chapter 20 — After the Breakthrough

**Evidence:** CAR-T commercialization as a logistics analogy; current individualized-vaccine manufacturing constraints; payer and regulator structures; evidence from first-wave precision-oncology adoption.

**What would make this false:** the first approval could arrive with unexpectedly rapid manufacturing, broad payer coverage and distributed access, reducing the predicted implementation gap. Conversely, no approval would require reframing the chapter around a stalled platform rather than commercialization.
