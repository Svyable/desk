## Evidence audit

The manuscript's fast-moving empirical anchors are primarily grounded in first-party or public institutional sources:

- International Renewable Energy Agency for global renewable generation costs, utility-scale PV balance-of-system shares, and battery-storage cost trends.
- International Energy Agency for energy investment, renewable integration, negative-price/curtailment trends, transmission supply chains, interconnection pressure, critical-mineral outlooks, development finance, and electricity-system forecasts.
- IEA Photovoltaic Power Systems Programme for global PV deployment and market/manufacturing context.
- U.S. Department of Energy for photovoltaic history, technology, and end-of-life management.
- World Bank / ESMAP for off-grid solar electricity-access evidence.

The book avoids claiming that queue capacity is built capacity, installed capacity is annual generation, LCOE is delivered-system cost, or one storage duration solves seasonal reliability.

## Falsification discipline

The manuscript preserves the strongest counterclaims:

- Cheap modules can be offset by expensive grids, storage, financing, land, labor, permitting, and reliability requirements.
- Firm low-carbon generation can remain economically important or dominant in some systems.
- Batteries do not solve every duration and can create mineral, safety, degradation, and replacement costs.
- High solar penetration can depress solar capture prices and increase curtailment.
- Transmission, mining, and utility-scale development impose real land and community impacts.
- Manufacturing concentration can create security risk even when it lowers global hardware costs.
- Emerging markets can have excellent solar resources while high capital costs erase much of the advantage.
- Distributed generation can deepen inequality if ownership and network-cost recovery are badly designed.
- A dark-week reliability requirement can make the last portions of a highly renewable system materially more expensive than early deployment.
- Solar need not supply every electron for the title to hold, but it must materially reorganize complementary infrastructure; otherwise the book's central claim is too strong.

## Editorial boundaries

This is infrastructure and energy-system nonfiction, not individualized investment, engineering, legal, tariff, procurement, or project-development advice. Market structures, incentives, grid codes, connection rules, costs, forecasts, and technology performance differ by jurisdiction and change over time.

“Global South” is not used as a single empirical market category. The chapter titled “The Solar South” explicitly rejects that flattening and focuses on recurring financing and infrastructure constraints while preserving regional differences.

China's manufacturing role is treated as a central industrial fact inside a cumulative international history rather than as sole invention, sole cause, or normative geopolitical verdict.

## Publication thesis

The durable claim is:

> **Cheap generation does not eliminate scarcity. It relocates scarcity.**

The solar transition is therefore not adequately described by a module cost curve. Fossil systems move stored fuel through space. High-solar systems increasingly must coordinate electricity through both space and time, making networks, storage, flexible demand, power electronics, materials, capital, and institutions part of the generator's effective infrastructure.
