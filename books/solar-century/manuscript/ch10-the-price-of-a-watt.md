# Chapter 10 — The Price of a Watt

Solar's most famous graph goes down.

The price of modules falls.

The installed cost falls.

The levelized cost of electricity falls.

The line is so persuasive that it can become a substitute for understanding.

But a watt has many prices.

There is the price of the module.

The price of the installed system.

The price of financing it.

The cost of connecting it.

The value of the electricity when it is produced.

The system cost of balancing it.

The retail value behind a meter.

The wholesale value on a congested grid.

The social value of reduced fuel imports or emissions.

The price of a watt is not one number.

It is a stack of numbers describing different questions.

## Cheap hardware, expensive project

IRENA reported that by 2024 the global weighted-average installed cost of utility-scale solar PV had fallen to roughly $691 per kilowatt, while crystalline-silicon module costs had declined about ninety-seven percent from January 2010 to December 2024.

The module's spectacular decline creates a new arithmetic.

Once the module becomes a smaller share of total project cost, cutting the module price again produces a smaller reduction in the final cost of electricity.

The rest of the project starts to dominate.

IRENA estimated that balance-of-system costs excluding modules and inverters represented around sixty-five percent of total installed utility-scale PV costs in 2024.

The cheapest solar module in history can therefore enter a project made expensive by land, labor, permitting, grid upgrades, financing, engineering, taxes, supply constraints, or delays.

This is not failure of the module cost curve.

It is victory.

The hardware got cheap enough to expose everything else.

## LCOE is a useful lie

Levelized cost of electricity is one of the most useful metrics in energy and one of the easiest to abuse.

It asks a sensible question: over the life of a project, what constant price per unit of electricity would recover its costs, given assumptions about investment, operations, financing, output, and lifetime?

That makes technologies comparable in a common unit.

It does not make the electricity identical.

A solar megawatt-hour at noon is not automatically worth the same as a gas-generated megawatt-hour during an evening scarcity event.

A wind farm in a constrained region is not equivalent to a generator next to load.

A nuclear plant with high fixed costs and steady output is not simply comparable to a solar project with a lower LCOE and a different production profile.

LCOE compresses time and location into an average.

Power systems operate in time and location.

The metric is useful as long as we remember the information it throws away.

The danger begins when a project metric becomes a system prophecy.

## The capture price

As solar becomes common, the price solar actually earns can fall relative to the market's average electricity price.

This is the cannibalization effect.

Solar plants produce at similar times.

Their collective output pushes down prices during those hours.

The average price of electricity across the year may remain healthy while the average price captured by solar generators declines.

That creates a strange result.

The technology can continue getting cheaper while the revenue available to an unpaired solar project weakens.

The cost curve and value curve move in opposite directions.

Storage, geographic diversity, better orientation, flexible demand, transmission, and redesigned contracts can repair the gap.

But the gap itself is structural.

Abundance lowers the value of additional abundance delivered at the same time.

Every commodity market understands this.

Solar simply makes the timing unusually synchronized.

## Zero marginal cost is not zero price

People sometimes say solar electricity is free once the project is built.

This is wrong in one way and illuminating in another.

The project still has operating and maintenance costs. Equipment degrades. Land leases continue. Inverters may need replacement. Insurance and administration continue. Financing obligations may remain.

But the marginal fuel cost of sunlight is zero.

That means a solar plant often has a very low short-run cost of producing one additional available kilowatt-hour.

In competitive markets, low marginal-cost resources tend to bid low.

When many such resources are available simultaneously, wholesale prices can fall dramatically.

The market begins to resemble a software market in one narrow respect: the fixed cost matters enormously, while the incremental cost of another unit can be tiny.

This does not mean electricity becomes economically free.

It means price formation moves toward scarcity elsewhere.

Capacity.

Flexibility.

Transmission.

Storage.

Location.

Firmness.

The system pays for what remains scarce after energy becomes abundant in some hours.

## Financing is a fuel bill paid upfront

A solar project has no recurring fuel purchase, which makes capital cost disproportionately important.

The project pays for much of its lifetime energy before it produces the first unit.

This makes the interest rate feel like a fuel price.

A country with excellent solar resource but expensive capital can produce more costly solar electricity than a country with weaker sunlight and cheap financing.

That is one of the cruelest inequalities in the energy transition.

The places with the greatest need for new infrastructure can face the highest cost of borrowing to build it.

The Sun is democratic.

Capital is not.

This is why headline hardware prices do not automatically translate into globally uniform electricity prices.

The same panel can live inside radically different financial systems.

## The price of delay

Time has a cost before a solar plant is built too.

A project waiting three years for interconnection carries development expense without revenue.

A delayed permit can expire a purchase agreement.

A transformer shortage can expose the project to changing interest rates or equipment prices.

A transmission upgrade can make an initially attractive site uneconomic.

The cost of delay is often invisible in simple technology comparisons because it is not a physical component.

Yet infrastructure is a race between capital and calendars.

A slightly more expensive project that can connect quickly may be worth more than a theoretically cheaper project trapped in a queue.

Speed becomes part of cost.

## Retail solar and the avoided bill

Rooftop economics complicate the price of a watt further.

The customer values electricity according to the bill avoided, not the wholesale market price alone.

If a household offsets a high retail tariff, rooftop solar can be attractive even if utility-scale solar produces cheaper bulk electricity.

The rooftop system may also provide resilience when paired with storage, hedge future retail prices, or create nonfinancial value for the owner.

That does not mean the retail tariff is the correct compensation for exports.

An exported rooftop kilowatt-hour may impose or avoid different system costs depending on location and time.

The point is that value depends on perspective.

The consumer, utility, grid operator, taxpayer, and society can rationally assign different values to the same unit of generation because they incur different costs.

Policy is the process of reconciling those ledgers.

## The system price is the only price that matters in the end

A civilization does not consume LCOE.

It consumes reliable energy services.

The relevant question is therefore not which generator wins the cheapest-number contest.

It is which portfolio of generation, networks, storage, demand flexibility, reserves, and institutions delivers the desired reliability and other public goals at acceptable total cost.

Solar's extraordinary cost decline gives it a powerful position inside that portfolio.

It does not exempt it from system economics.

A cheap panel connected to the wrong grid at the wrong time can be worth little.

A moderately priced panel paired with the right battery, tariff, and connection can be worth much more.

The solar century begins when we stop asking, “How cheap is the panel?”

And start asking, “What is the cheapest way to make this electricity useful?”

That is the real price of a watt.
