## The Solar Century Test

When someone claims a region, company, or country is building a solar-centered energy system, ask twelve questions.

1. **Generation:** Is solar actually producing useful electricity at scale, or is the claim mainly installed-capacity theater?
2. **Connection:** Can new generation obtain a timely, financeable path onto the grid?
3. **Time:** What moves abundant midday electricity into valuable hours—storage, flexible demand, transmission, complementary generation, or deliberate curtailment?
4. **Network:** Are transmission, distribution, substations, transformers, cables, and protection systems expanding with generation?
5. **Stability:** Can inverter-based resources provide the voltage, frequency, protection, and restoration behavior the changing grid requires?
6. **Demand:** Are vehicles, buildings, industry, and other loads rewarded for consuming when electricity is abundant without sacrificing the service people actually want?
7. **Reliability:** What carries the system through the dark week, winter peak, equipment failure, drought, storm, or other correlated scarcity event?
8. **Capital:** Does the cost of financing preserve the hardware advantage, including in emerging and developing economies?
9. **Materials:** Are copper, transformers, batteries, modules, power electronics, and other industrial inputs available without creating catastrophic single points of failure?
10. **Land and consent:** Can projects and transmission be sited with credible environmental review, local participation, host benefits, and decommissioning obligations?
11. **Circularity:** Are repair, repowering, reuse, recycling, and material recovery designed before the retirement wave arrives?
12. **Distribution:** Who owns the abundance, who pays for the infrastructure, and who remains exposed to high bills or weak service?

A system that cannot answer these questions may have many solar panels without yet having solar infrastructure.

## Notes on terminology

**Bottleneck migration** — The movement of cost, delay, or strategic importance from a technology that has become cheap into the complementary infrastructure required to use it.

**Cheap noon** — A shorthand for hours in which solar-rich systems can experience very low electricity prices or curtailment because generation exceeds immediately useful local demand and flexibility.

**Temporal wire** — The book's analogy for storage: transmission moves electricity across space; storage moves usable electricity across time.

**Manufactured generator** — A power-conversion device whose essential generating function is produced repetitively in a factory before the final power project is assembled.

**Industrial sovereignty** — Resilience based on the ability to build, finance, maintain, replace, diversify, and recycle energy equipment rather than merely control a domestic fuel resource.

**Dark week** — A deliberately general term for a sustained low-renewable-output period combined with meaningful demand, used to force reliability analysis beyond ordinary daily solar cycling.

## Source note

This book draws primarily on public analysis and data from the International Energy Agency, International Renewable Energy Agency, IEA Photovoltaic Power Systems Programme, U.S. Department of Energy, World Bank, and related public research institutions. Fast-moving numerical claims are dated and bounded in the research ledger rather than presented as timeless constants.

Forecasts are treated as forecasts. Installed capacity is not equated with annual generation or firm capacity. Levelized cost is not treated as complete delivered-system cost. Interconnection queues are not treated as projects certain to be built. Negative prices are treated as market and flexibility signals rather than proof that electricity has no value.
