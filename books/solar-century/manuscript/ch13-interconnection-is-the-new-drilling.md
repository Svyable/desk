# Chapter 13 — Interconnection Is the New Drilling

The old energy company asked a geological question.

Where is the resource?

The solar developer asks an electrical one.

Where is the connection?

Sunlight is widely distributed. Good solar resource helps, cheap land helps, tax treatment helps, permitting helps, and proximity to demand helps. But a magnificent site without a viable path into the grid can be worth less than a mediocre site beside spare network capacity.

The scarce asset is not always the Sun.

It is permission to touch the wire.

That is why interconnection is the new drilling.

## A queue full of invisible power plants

The interconnection queue is one of the strangest infrastructures of the solar century.

It contains power plants that do not exist yet.

Developers submit projects for study because they need to know whether the network can accommodate them and what upgrades will be required. The grid operator models power flows, faults, stability, equipment loading, and interactions with projects already ahead in line.

The process is essential.

Connecting a large generator is not like plugging in an appliance.

But queues designed for a slower era became overwhelmed when renewable projects and storage proposals began arriving in enormous numbers.

The IEA reported that it tracked roughly 1,650 gigawatts of advanced solar and wind projects waiting for grid connections in 2024.

That number is not a promise of future capacity.

Queues contain projects that will never be built. Some are speculative. Some will fail economically. Some will lose land rights, financing, customers, equipment, or permits. Some will discover that required grid upgrades destroy the business case.

But even after discounting attrition, the queue tells a story.

The world learned to propose generation faster than it learned to connect it.

## The connection has option value

A developer entering a queue is buying information and preserving possibility.

That creates incentives to submit more projects than will ultimately be built.

If a grid region has limited future capacity, an early queue position can become valuable even before project economics are fully known.

This is not necessarily bad behavior.

Infrastructure development happens under uncertainty. Developers need a route to discover costs.

The problem begins when the process cannot distinguish serious projects from low-cost options quickly enough.

Then everyone waits behind everyone else.

A queue becomes a tragedy of optionality.

Reforms increasingly try to require stronger site control, deposits, readiness milestones, or commercial commitments before a project can hold a place.

The purpose is not to make development harder.

It is to make the queue describe reality.

## The cost arrives late

One reason interconnection is so dangerous to project economics is that a developer can spend years advancing a project before learning the full cost of connection.

Suppose the line near the site looks adequate.

The detailed study may reveal otherwise.

Perhaps another project ahead in the queue uses the available capacity.

Perhaps a substation needs expansion.

Perhaps a transformer must be replaced.

Perhaps transmission reinforcement is required hundreds of kilometers away because the new generation changes regional power flows.

The solar farm can create costs far beyond its fence.

Who pays?

That question determines which projects survive.

If the last project that triggers an upgrade pays the entire cost, the result can be arbitrary.

If costs are socialized too broadly, customers may fund speculative infrastructure.

If network planners build ahead of confirmed projects, they risk stranded assets.

If they wait for certainty, generation queues grow.

Interconnection is where electricity engineering becomes public finance.

## Connect and manage

The traditional approach often assumes a generator should connect only after the network can accommodate its maximum potential output under defined contingencies.

That is safe and understandable.

It can also be expensive.

An alternative is to connect resources with operating limits that reflect real network conditions.

A solar plant might curtail output during rare constrained hours rather than waiting years for an upgrade needed only at the absolute peak of its production.

A battery might charge when local capacity is available and stop when it is not.

A flexible customer might accept a non-firm connection in exchange for lower cost or faster service.

This is sometimes called flexible interconnection or connect-and-manage.

The idea is simple: use software and contracts to make better use of existing hardware.

It converts network capacity from a static entitlement into a dynamic resource.

That is very solar-century logic.

When physical infrastructure is slow, intelligence can create headroom.

Not infinite headroom.

But enough to change project economics.

## The grid map becomes a treasure map

Developers once hunted for the best solar irradiance and land.

Increasingly they hunt for substations.

A map showing spare transmission and distribution capacity can be more valuable than a map showing sunlight because solar resource varies gradually while electrical capacity can change abruptly across a network boundary.

One parcel may face manageable connection costs.

Another parcel a few kilometers away may require a major upgrade.

This creates a new geography of energy value.

Brownfields, retired power-plant sites, industrial areas, and locations with existing interconnections can become attractive even if their solar resource is not exceptional.

A coal plant may close and leave behind something more valuable than the building.

The wire.

That connection can become the bridge between energy eras.

## Repowering the node

Existing energy sites can have infrastructure advantages that greenfield projects lack.

Transmission.

Substations.

Roads.

Water in some cases.

A skilled workforce.

Permits or land-use history.

Community familiarity with industrial energy facilities.

Solar, storage, synchronous condensers, new generation, or combinations of technologies can reuse parts of that inherited system.

The energy transition is therefore not always a story of abandoning old infrastructure.

Sometimes the most valuable feature of a retired plant is the hole it leaves in the grid.

A new project can occupy that electrical space.

This is infrastructural succession.

## Interconnection reform is generation policy

Governments often announce generation targets as though projects appear once policy requests them.

But a gigawatt target without a gigawatt connection process is a press release.

Interconnection staffing, study methods, data transparency, queue rules, cost allocation, transmission planning, dispute resolution, and equipment procurement can determine the actual pace of deployment more than the headline subsidy.

The bureaucracy is not adjacent to energy policy.

It is energy policy.

A faster study can have the same practical effect as a cheaper component if it moves a project from possibility to operation sooner.

## The drilling metaphor has limits

Oil drilling searches for a scarce underground resource.

Interconnection searches for capacity in a network that can itself be expanded.

That difference is hopeful.

A depleted oil field cannot be regulated into containing more oil.

A constrained grid can be reinforced, reconfigured, operated differently, digitized, expanded, or paired with storage and flexible demand.

The resource is partially manufactured.

Grid capacity can be created.

But creating it takes capital, permits, equipment, labor, and political consent.

That is why the metaphor works.

The connection is scarce enough to govern project value even though the scarcity is ultimately solvable.

## The energy company becomes a queue company

Project development in the solar century can look absurd from the outside.

The panels are available.

The land is leased.

The customer wants power.

The capital is interested.

The project still waits.

This is the moment when people realize the generator was never the whole power plant.

The plant includes the institutional path through which electricity earns the right to exist on the network.

The twentieth century rewarded companies that could find and extract fuel.

The solar century increasingly rewards companies that can find and secure electrical access.

The drill rig moved.

It became a study request.
