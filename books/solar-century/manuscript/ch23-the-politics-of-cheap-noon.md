# Chapter 23 — The Politics of Cheap Noon

Technology changes faster than political bargains.

That is why cheap solar can produce expensive conflict.

The twentieth-century electricity system developed institutions around predictable roles.

Utilities built networks and recovered costs through tariffs.

Generators sold energy and capacity.

Customers consumed.

Fuel suppliers sold commodities.

Workers organized around established industries.

Governments taxed flows they could see.

Communities hosted infrastructure whose economic role was familiar.

Then noon electricity started getting very cheap.

Every old bargain had to be renegotiated.

## Cheap energy creates losers too

A falling price sounds universally good.

It is not.

Customers may benefit.

Existing generators can lose revenue.

Fuel suppliers can lose demand.

Workers can lose jobs.

Utilities can lose volumetric sales.

Tax bases can shrink in fossil-producing regions.

Landowners can gain lease income while neighbors dislike new projects.

Manufacturers can be crushed by global price competition.

Battery owners can profit from volatility that consumers experience as instability.

The political economy of solar is therefore not a simple fight between clean and dirty energy.

It is a redistribution of rents across time, geography, technologies, and ownership classes.

Cheap noon is political because somebody used to earn money during noon.

## The incumbent asset does not disappear when it loses the market

Power plants are built to last decades.

Pipelines, mines, rail infrastructure, refineries, and workforces are built around similar expectations.

When a new technology changes economics faster than those assets depreciate, society inherits stranded value.

The physical equipment may still function.

The financial model no longer does.

That creates resistance that is often framed as ideological but can be understood as balance-sheet defense.

A utility protecting a legacy plant, a community protecting tax revenue, or a worker protecting a specialized career is not necessarily confused about solar economics.

They may understand it perfectly.

The transition threatens something they own.

Good policy addresses that reality rather than assuming better cost curves automatically produce political consent.

## The rooftop rate war

Few solar debates are as intense as the question of what rooftop exports should be worth.

Pay the full retail rate and solar customers may avoid contributing enough to shared network costs.

Pay only a low wholesale rate and distributed solar may be undervalued where it reduces local peaks, losses, generation needs, or future network investment.

Use a flat fixed charge and customers may lose incentives to conserve.

Use a highly complex dynamic tariff and ordinary households may be unable to understand it.

There is no universal correct number because the value of distributed energy changes by time, location, network conditions, and system design.

The political challenge is building a tariff that is both economically credible and socially legible.

A mathematically elegant tariff nobody trusts is not durable infrastructure.

## Who owns abundance?

Solar can be centralized or distributed.

That means ownership is open for contest.

Utilities can own large projects.

Independent power producers can own them.

Households can own rooftops.

Communities can own cooperative systems.

Landowners can lease sites.

Corporations can sign long-term contracts.

Public entities can build generation.

Financial investors can aggregate thousands of assets.

The technology does not dictate the ownership model.

Politics does.

This matters because the economic story people experience depends on who receives the cash flow.

A solar farm owned by an outside fund can look very different to a rural community from a project sharing tax revenue, lease payments, local ownership, or community benefits.

The electrons may be identical.

The legitimacy is not.

## Abundance challenges conservation politics

Environmental politics grew partly around scarcity.

Use less.

Waste less.

Conserve.

Those remain sensible principles because infrastructure, land, and materials are never free.

But a high-solar system creates hours when electricity is genuinely abundant and its marginal value is extremely low.

In those hours, consuming more can improve the system if the consumption substitutes for later fossil use or captures energy that would otherwise be curtailed.

Charge the car now.

Heat the water now.

Run the process now.

The moral language changes from “use less energy” to “use energy at the right time and in the right form.”

That is a subtle political shift.

Abundance requires coordination rather than guilt.

## Permitting becomes democratic capacity

Every major energy pathway requires physical construction.

Solar fields.

Transmission lines.

Batteries.

Mines.

Factories.

Substations.

Charging networks.

If a society cannot permit any of them, its climate or energy targets are not plans.

They are preferences.

Permitting reform is politically difficult because the existing process often protects legitimate values: environmental review, local voice, property rights, cultural resources, indigenous interests, and procedural fairness.

Speed cannot simply mean removing everyone from the decision.

The challenge is reducing redundant delay while improving early participation and clarity.

A fast illegitimate process creates backlash.

A legitimate process with no end date creates paralysis.

The solar century needs institutional velocity with consent.

## The politics of the bill

The energy transition is experienced through monthly cash flow.

A homeowner with financed panels may see savings.

A renter may see none.

A driver with a new electric vehicle may reduce fuel spending.

Someone unable to afford the vehicle remains exposed to gasoline prices.

A region funding grid expansion may see tariffs rise before customers see long-term benefits.

A factory receiving a special electricity contract may be accused of shifting costs to households.

Distribution matters because people vote on bills, not levelized-cost charts.

The transition can be economically beneficial in aggregate and politically unstable in distribution.

## Cheap noon needs expensive institutions

The paradox of solar politics is that falling hardware costs increase the importance of governance.

When panels were expensive, the central policy question was how to stimulate deployment.

When panels become cheap, the harder questions emerge.

Who connects first?

Who pays for the grid?

Who bears curtailment?

Who receives land payments?

Who owns storage?

Who gets a dynamic tariff?

Who finances low-income electrification?

Who supports workers in declining fuel industries?

Who is allowed to build transmission through whose land?

Cheap technology does not depoliticize infrastructure.

It exposes the political choices hardware prices used to hide.

## The durable bargain

A Solar Century that survives elections, recessions, trade disputes, local opposition, and technological change needs a bargain broader than climate concern.

Reliable power.

Lower exposure to fuel shocks.

Industrial opportunity.

Cleaner air.

Local revenue.

Consumer savings where they are real.

Fair treatment of communities and workers carrying transition costs.

Resilient supply chains.

A grid that works better than the one it replaces.

People do not have to love solar panels.

They have to prefer the system built around them.

That is the politics of cheap noon.

The technology wins the century only when the bargain wins too.
