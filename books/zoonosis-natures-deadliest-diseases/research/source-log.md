# Research Trail

## Evidence classes

**Class 1 — Established / high-confidence:** authoritative public-health guidance, replicated epidemiology, established reservoir or transmission relationships, primary statutes/data where applicable, and mature scientific consensus.

**Class 2 — Credible but incomplete:** strong peer-reviewed inference, field observations, serology, phylogenetic reconstruction, credible testimony, and mechanisms with unresolved causal weight.

**Class 3 — Disputed / unresolved:** contested origin pathways, disputed historical transmission mechanisms, overgeneralized reservoir claims, and causal stories whose elegance exceeds their evidence.

**Class 4 — Author synthesis / speculation:** the book’s “interface design” thesis; the claim that economic incentive design is itself a surveillance technology; and comparisons across diseases that are interpretive rather than findings of a single source.

## Language expansion required

The full research pass should deliberately retrieve scholarship and public-health records in Chinese on SARS and wildlife supply chains; Arabic on MERS and camel husbandry; Bengali on Nipah/date-palm sap; French and relevant West African languages around Ebola response; Japanese and Korean sources on zoonotic surveillance; Spanish-language Cuban histories of Carlos Finlay; and local/Indigenous ecological-health literature written by knowledge holders rather than only outside anthropologists.

## Falsification notes

The central thesis weakens if comparative evidence shows that anthropogenic interface changes contribute little to spillover frequency after correcting for surveillance intensity, population growth, and reporting bias. Disease-specific causal claims must not be inferred from the general thesis. A land-use association does not prove land use caused an outbreak. A reservoir association does not identify the immediate source. A phylogenetic ancestor does not identify a transmission route.
