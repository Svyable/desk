# Research Trail

## Evidence classes

**Class 1 — Established / high-confidence:** authoritative public-health guidance, replicated epidemiology, established reservoir or transmission relationships, primary statutes/data where applicable, and mature scientific consensus.

**Class 2 — Credible but incomplete:** strong peer-reviewed inference, field observations, serology, phylogenetic reconstruction, credible testimony, and mechanisms with unresolved causal weight.

**Class 3 — Disputed / unresolved:** contested origin pathways, disputed historical transmission mechanisms, overgeneralized reservoir claims, and causal stories whose elegance exceeds their evidence.

**Class 4 — Author synthesis / speculation:** the book’s “interface design” thesis; the claim that economic incentive design is itself a surveillance technology; and comparisons across diseases that are interpretive rather than findings of a single source.

## Bengali-language Nipah record

These sources are used because the Bangladeshi public-health system is not merely a setting in the Nipah story; it generated much of the operational knowledge that makes repeated spillover legible.

11. Institute of Epidemiology, Disease Control and Research (IEDCR), Bangladesh, “মারাত্মক রোগ নিপাহঃ জনস্বাস্থ্যের জন্য হুমকিস্বরূপ” [Deadly Nipah disease: a threat to public health]. Bengali public-health guidance. Records that recognized Bangladeshi outbreaks recur during the date-palm sap season, identifies contaminated raw date-palm sap as a principal exposure route, notes person-to-person transmission, and advises against raw sap while stating that boiled sap/molasses is safe. https://iedcr.gov.bd/pages/static-pages/6922e0e8933eb65569e291de
12. IEDCR, “বাংলাদেশে নিপাহ এনকেফালাইটিস নজরদারি কার্যক্রমের বর্তমান অবস্থা” [Current status of Nipah encephalitis surveillance in Bangladesh]. Bengali surveillance history. Records first recognition in Meherpur in 2001, identification of raw date-palm sap contaminated by bat saliva as a transmission route in 2004, and establishment of hospital-based surveillance in 2006, later complemented by hotline, media, and event-based surveillance. https://iedcr.gov.bd/pages/static-pages/6922dc20933eb65569e0e89c
13. IEDCR, “নিপাহ ভাইরাস ট্রান্সমিশন সংক্ষিপ্ত বিবরণ” [Overview of Nipah virus transmission], updated 8 February 2026. Bengali/English institutional summary naming Pteropus fruit bats as the natural reservoir and raw or fermented date-palm sap contaminated by bat saliva/excreta as a principal Bangladesh transmission route, with documented human-to-human spread. https://iedcr.gov.bd/pages/static-pages/6922e04d933eb65569e26686

### Nipah evidence posture

Class 1: Pteropus reservoir relationship; Malaysian pig amplification; repeated Bangladesh association with raw date-palm sap; documented person-to-person transmission in Bangladesh; high clinical severity; surveillance chronology above.

Class 2: relative importance of individual ecological drivers, farm geometry, agricultural intensification, bat movement, and seasonal/environmental conditions in producing the 1998–99 Malaysian outbreak.

Class 3: the strongest simplified “deforestation caused the Malaysian outbreak” narrative when stated as a single demonstrated causal chain. The book should preserve the ecological hypothesis but refuse to upgrade an elegant multistep story into settled proof.

Class 4: framing the collection pot, household bedside, surveillance hotline, and hospital ward as successive interfaces in the same transmission system. This is the book’s synthesis, not a claim made by one source.

## Arabic-language MERS record

Arabic Ministry of Health material is used to recover how Saudi authorities translated an evolving scientific picture into local operational advice rather than telling the MERS story only through later English-language global summaries.

14. Saudi Ministry of Health, “(الصحة) تحذر من التعامل مع الإبل دون وقاية لتسببها في نقل فيروس كورونا إلى الإنسان” [Health Ministry warns against handling camels without protection because they can transmit coronavirus to humans], 13 September 2014. Warns about direct exposure to camel secretions; recommends masks, gloves and protective clothing in camel enclosures and slaughterhouses; describes Saudi research identifying virus in camel mucus and saliva. https://www.moh.gov.sa/ministry/mediacenter/news/pages/news-2014-09-13-001.aspx
15. Saudi Ministry of Health, “وزارة الصحة: ما زال فيروس (كورونا) يشكل تهديدًا على الصحة العامة” [Ministry of Health: coronavirus still poses a public-health threat], 28 October 2014. Describes a cluster beginning with camel contact followed by transmission to other patients inside healthcare facilities; states that available evidence indicated camel-to-human transmission followed by human-to-human spread through respiratory exposure. https://www.moh.gov.sa/ministry/mediacenter/news/pages/news-2014-10-28-004.aspx
16. Saudi Ministry of Health, “فيروس كورونا MERS (المسبب لمتلازمة الشرق الأوسط التنفسية)” [MERS coronavirus, cause of Middle East respiratory syndrome], current Arabic health-awareness page, updated September 2026. Advises precautions in farms, markets and barns, hand hygiene, avoiding sick animals, and avoiding raw camel milk, camel urine and undercooked camel meat. https://www.moh.gov.sa/healthawareness/educationalcontent/corona/pages/015.aspx
17. Saudi Ministry of Health, “باحثون من وزارة الصحة ومراكز دولية يكتشفون أنماط انتقال معقدة لـ(كورونا)” [Ministry researchers and international centers discover complex transmission patterns of coronavirus], 21 September 2013. Summarizes genomic work indicating that early MERS transmission was more complex than a single chain and could involve additional human or animal sources. https://www.moh.gov.sa/ministry/mediacenter/news/pages/news-2013-09-21-001.aspx
18. WHO EMRO, “Middle East respiratory syndrome (MERS).” Notes strong evidence for dromedaries as a major reservoir/source while preserving uncertainty over exact transmission routes, and records major healthcare-associated outbreaks including Saudi Arabia, UAE, and South Korea. https://www.emro.who.int/health-topics/mers-cov/mers-cov.html

### MERS evidence posture

Class 1: dromedaries as the principal zoonotic reservoir/source for human MERS; direct or indirect camel-associated infection; documented human-to-human transmission; healthcare amplification; concentration of recognized cases in Saudi Arabia; absence of sustained community transmission comparable to COVID-19; current WHO qualification that confirmed-case fatality overstates infection-fatality risk when mild cases are missed.

Class 2: exact contribution of specific camel exposures, husbandry practices, markets, raw products, seasonality, animal age, and regional trade to the probability of human infection; reasons for large geographic differences between camel seropositivity and recognized human disease.

Class 3: collapsing MERS into either a simple “bat virus” story or a simple “camel disease” story. Bat-related evolutionary ancestry does not establish the contemporary route into humans; camel reservoir status does not explain downstream hospital amplification.

Class 4: “the camel archive” as a synthesis joining stored sera, veterinary records, viral genomes, trade routes, husbandry knowledge and hospital epidemiology into one readable history of interfaces.

## Falsification notes

The central thesis weakens if comparative evidence shows that anthropogenic interface changes contribute little to spillover frequency after correcting for surveillance intensity, population growth, and reporting bias. Disease-specific causal claims must not be inferred from the general thesis. A land-use association does not prove land use caused an outbreak. A reservoir association does not identify the immediate source. A phylogenetic ancestor does not identify a transmission route.

Nipah-specific falsifier: if stronger outbreak reconstruction shows the Malaysian pig amplification pathway or Bangladesh raw-sap pathway has been materially misstated, the interface argument must be revised rather than preserved by changing terminology.

MERS-specific falsifier: if future evidence overturns dromedary-to-human transmission as a major route, the camel-centered chapter architecture must change. If regional case discrepancies are explained primarily by true biological differences rather than surveillance or exposure structure, the text must not use surveillance bias as the default explanation.
