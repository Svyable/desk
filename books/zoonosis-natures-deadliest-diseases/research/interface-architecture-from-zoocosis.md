# Spillover Interface Architecture

## Why this note exists

An early *Zoocosis* concept accidentally occupied the same biological territory as *Zoonosis*: wildlife reservoirs, bridge hosts, farms, markets, land-use change, mobility, surveillance, and pre-spillover prevention. That material belongs here.

*Zoonosis: Nature's Deadliest Diseases* is the literal pathogen book. *Zoocosis: The Next Pandemic* is now a separate cultural-human-condition book about humans living inside environments that may evoke maladaptive or self-defeating behavior.

This note preserves the strongest pathogen-facing architecture from the abandoned version and maps it onto the existing 18-chapter manuscript.

## Central addition to the Zoonosis thesis

The existing thesis should keep its disease-by-disease strength while making one systems claim more explicit:

**Pandemic risk is often manufactured at interfaces.** A pathogen may persist harmlessly or obscurely in one ecological network for years. Human systems alter the number, frequency, density, and geography of cross-species experiments through land conversion, livestock density, wildlife trade, housing, occupational exposure, transport, food systems, health infrastructure, and surveillance. Most cross-species encounters still fail. A tiny fraction do not. The important policy question is therefore not merely where a pathogen came from, but what interface allowed it to acquire repeated opportunities to cross, amplify, adapt, and move.

This is not a universal causal theory. Pathogen-specific biology and chance remain decisive. The interface frame earns its place only when it improves explanation or prevention for a specific disease.

## Concepts to weave into the existing 18 chapters

### Before Patient Zero

Outbreak narratives typically begin after detection. Add a prehistory of opportunity: ecological contact, occupational exposure, animal amplification, undetected human cases, transport, and surveillance gaps. The most important intervention may occur before anyone qualifies as Patient Zero.

### A Door Between Species

Treat spillover as a chain of filters rather than a magical jump: exposure, cellular compatibility, replication, immune escape, tissue tropism, shedding, transmission, and onward human spread. Most doors do not open all the way.

### Reservoir Is Not Villain

Keep the distinction among reservoir, intermediate host, amplifier, vector, incidental host, and dead-end host. Blaming the conspicuous animal can produce bad control policy and ecological harm.

### Forest Edge

Land-use change should appear as a contact-surface problem, not as a slogan that deforestation automatically causes pandemics. Ask which species move, which humans arrive, what livestock enters, how behavior changes, and whether exposure measurably increases.

### Bridge Animal

Domestic animals can become biological bridges because humans engineer repeated, high-frequency contact with them. Nipah pigs, Hendra horses, MERS camels, and influenza systems should be compared without pretending they share one mechanism.

### Barn Air

Add host density, ventilation, immune uniformity, age structure, turnover, worker exposure, and movement among facilities. Industrial scale may alter amplification opportunity, but claims should be pathogen-specific and quantified where possible.

### Market Geometry

A market is not inherently a virological category. Risk depends on species composition, sourcing, turnover, slaughter practices, ventilation, sanitation, duration, crowding, transport, and occupational contact. Avoid turning one market type into a universal origin story.

### Adaptation Has No Plan

Mutation, recombination, reassortment, and selection are not goal-directed. Language implying a pathogen is "trying" to become pandemic should be stripped out unless clearly metaphorical.

### Amplifier

Separate spillover from amplification. A rare animal-to-human infection can remain epidemiologically trivial unless a host, workplace, hospital, household, farm, social network, or other setting creates enough replication and transmission opportunities for the event to matter.

### One Flight Away

Mobility changes timing more reliably than it changes origin probability. Air travel, road networks, animal transport, cold chains, labor migration, and trade can turn a local biological event into a compressed international response window.

### Cities Without Species Boundaries

Urban and peri-urban environments create new interfaces among people, pets, livestock, wildlife, vectors, waste, water, and food. Avoid the false binary that zoonotic risk is either "wild nature" or "city."

### Hotter Maps

Climate should be handled through mechanisms: vector range, seasonality, host distribution, rainfall, heat, land use, and human adaptation. Do not make climate a universal master variable for every zoonosis.

### Medicine for Animals / AMR

Antimicrobial resistance is a second One Health problem with different causal structure from viral spillover. Keep animal use, human use, environmental contamination, husbandry, infection prevention, and access to appropriate drugs separate enough to avoid category error.

### Watching Everything

Pathogen discovery is not forecasting. Genomic surveillance, sentinel animals, wastewater, serology, syndromic surveillance, and field ecology each see different slices of risk. More sequences do not automatically yield a calendar for the next pandemic.

### Prediction Problem

Add a denominator discipline: how many apparently dangerous interfaces never produce sustained emergence? Without failed-spillover and no-outbreak comparison cases, the literature can become a catalogue of winners selected after the fact.

### Prevention Before Preparedness

Frame source reduction and downstream preparedness as complements, then test where each dominates. Some risks may be cheaper to prevent at the animal-human interface; others may be too diffuse or stochastic for source control, making vaccines, ventilation, therapeutics, diagnostics, and resilient health systems more valuable.

### Price of the Interface / Spillover Economy

Ask who captures the economic upside of risky interfaces and who pays for surveillance, culling, outbreak response, lost livelihoods, healthcare, and ecological damage. Economic incentives are part of prevention architecture.

## Candidate "spillover interface index" — research question only

Do not present this as an established metric. It may be useful as a comparative reporting device if the evidence supports measurable components such as:

- frequency and intimacy of wildlife-human contact;
- livestock or intermediate-host density;
- mixing among species and facilities;
- occupational exposure intensity;
- host movement and transport connectivity;
- biosecurity and ventilation;
- surveillance delay;
- health-system detection capacity;
- land-use change and edge formation;
- vector suitability where relevant;
- economic incentives that reward concealment or rapid animal movement.

The index fails if it cannot outperform simpler disease-specific baselines or if the components cannot be measured consistently.

## Case spine to strengthen

The existing chapters already supply the right disease diversity. Use the interface lens differently in each case rather than forcing uniformity:

- **Rabies:** dog vaccination shows upstream control when the main interface is well understood.
- **Plague:** reservoir-vector-human networks demonstrate why one iconic animal is not the whole ecology.
- **Yellow fever:** vector discovery changed the actionable interface.
- **Influenza:** bird/swine/human ecologies make reassortment and amplification central.
- **Nipah:** land use, fruit bats, pigs, farms, and workers provide a classic multi-interface reconstruction.
- **Ebola:** preserve uncertainty about reservoir and index-event reconstruction; distinguish ecology from retrospective storytelling.
- **Lassa:** household and rodent ecology make housing and food storage part of the interface.
- **Hendra:** horses function as bridge hosts in a very specific ecology.
- **MERS:** camel-human contact and healthcare amplification need separate treatment.
- **SARS:** wildlife supply chains and human transmission networks require different evidentiary layers.
- **COVID-19:** origin uncertainty must remain explicit; the chapter should teach how to reason when the chain is incomplete.
- **Bird flu:** global avian ecology, poultry production, mammalian spillovers, occupational exposure, and surveillance illustrate why "next pandemic" rhetoric often runs ahead of transmission evidence.

## Evidence posture

**One-Health-forward / pathogen-specific / interface-mechanistic / denominator-aware / origin-claim-cautious / source-prevention-open / preparedness-complementary.**

Keep the four evidence classes already used in this book. A general association between land use and emergence cannot prove a particular outbreak pathway. A phylogenetic ancestor cannot identify the immediate transmission route. A reservoir association cannot establish the index animal. A market cluster cannot by itself settle origin. An animal infection cannot by itself establish pandemic adaptation.
