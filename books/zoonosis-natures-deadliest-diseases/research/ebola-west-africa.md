# Ebola: West Africa Research Trail

This note supports Chapter 8 and keeps the book from collapsing the 2013–2016 West African epidemic into a single “bat spillover” or “culture” story.

## Sources

1. World Health Organization, “Origins of the 2014 Ebola epidemic.” Retrospective account of the first recognized chain in Meliandou, Guinea; identifies an 18-month-old boy who became ill on 26 December 2013 and died two days later; states that the exact source of his infection was not identified. https://www.who.int/news-room/spotlight/one-year-into-the-ebola-epidemic/origins-of-the-2014-ebola-epidemic

2. WHO, “Ebola virus disease, West Africa – update,” 1 July 2014. Identifies three major patterns then sustaining transmission: rural-community transmission, transmission in densely populated peri-urban areas including Conakry and Monrovia, and cross-border transmission along socially and commercially active border areas. https://www.who.int/emergencies/disease-outbreak-news/item/2014_07_01_ebola-en

3. WHO, “Le virus Ebola continue de défier les pays d’Afrique de l’Ouest alors que l’OMS intensifie sa riposte,” French-language media note, 26 June 2014. Records the rapidly expanding Guinea–Liberia–Sierra Leone epidemic and explicitly notes transmission in communities, health facilities, cities, rural areas, and border zones. https://www.who.int/fr/news/item/26-06-2014-ebola-challenges-west-african-countries-as-who-ramps-up-response

4. WHO, “Maladie d’Ebola,” French fact sheet, current version consulted September 2026. States that fruit bats of the Pteropodidae family are thought to be natural hosts of orthoebolaviruses, while preserving the language of inference rather than presenting reservoir ecology as fully solved; summarizes wildlife-to-human and human-to-human exposure routes and stresses community engagement and safe, dignified burials. https://www.who.int/fr/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/ebola-disease

5. WHO, “Ebola virus disease in Guinea – update,” 27 March 2014. Records laboratory-confirmed disease in Conakry and early case investigation, travel-history reconstruction, contact tracing, and community sensitization. https://www.who.int/emergencies/disease-outbreak-news/item/2014_03_27_ebola-en

6. WHO, “Ebola virus disease, West Africa – update,” 27 July 2014. Notes continued community and health-facility transmission and explicitly warns that renewed Guinea case counts demonstrated undetected chains of transmission in the community. https://www.who.int/emergencies/disease-outbreak-news/item/2014_07_27_ebola-en

7. WHO, “Fin de la transmission de la maladie à virus Ebola en Guinée,” French-language release, 29 December 2015. Records the declaration ending the original transmission chain after 42 days since the last confirmed case's second negative test and transition to intensified surveillance. https://www.who.int/fr/news-room/detail/29-12-2015-end-of-ebola-transmission-in-guinea

## Four evidence classes

**Class 1 — Established / high-confidence**

- The recognized West African epidemic chain began in southeastern Guinea in late 2013 and was confirmed as Ebola in March 2014.
- Human-to-human transmission through direct contact with blood/body fluids became the decisive propagation mechanism once the epidemic was underway.
- Healthcare-associated infection, funeral/body contact, urban spread, cross-border mobility, and delayed recognition materially shaped the epidemic.
- Community engagement, contact tracing, infection prevention, laboratory support, case management, and safe/dignified burials became central control measures.

**Class 2 — Credible but incomplete**

- Fruit bats are likely natural hosts for some orthoebolaviruses, but exact reservoir ecology and the immediate animal exposure that infected the first recognized Meliandou case remain unresolved.
- Forest loss and altered human-wildlife contact are plausible contributors to spillover risk in the region, but should not be written as a proven single-cause explanation for the 2013 event.
- The quantitative causal contribution of mistrust, conflict history, specific burial practices, urbanization, and health-system weakness differs across locations and periods.

**Class 3 — Disputed / unresolved**

- Any claim identifying a specific bat species or single wildlife encounter as the proven source of the first human infection without direct evidence.
- Any account that treats “African culture” as a sufficient explanation for epidemic growth.
- Any account that treats international response delay, state capacity, or community behavior as the single dominant cause across all three main countries.

**Class 4 — Author synthesis / speculation**

- “The epidemic grew through both infection and subtraction” is Sven's synthesis describing how health-worker infection reduced future response capacity.
- “Trust altered contact rates” is an analytical bridge: specific behaviors influenced by trust clearly affect transmission, but trust itself is not a single measured epidemiological parameter.
- Treating treatment-center fences, burial teams, and press conferences as political “interfaces” extends the book's interface thesis beyond interspecies contact.
