# Indigenous Knowledge, Disease Surveillance, and Governance

This note supports Chapter 16. Its purpose is to prevent two opposite errors: treating Indigenous knowledge as folklore outside evidence, or romanticizing it as automatically correct because it is Indigenous. The chapter should distinguish empirical observation, cultural practice, governance rights, laboratory evidence, and author synthesis.

## Four evidence classes

**Class 1 — Established / high-confidence**

- Trichinella infection can be acquired from viable larvae in raw or inadequately cooked infected meat.
- Walrus has been associated with human trichinellosis outbreaks in Arctic Canada.
- Nunavik has an Inuit-owned research institution with a walrus testing program; Polar Knowledge Canada credits that program with eliminating outbreaks once common in participating Nunavik communities.
- WHO's 2026 framework explicitly calls for FPIC, Indigenous data sovereignty, reciprocity, shared governance, safeguards, and accountability in work involving Indigenous knowledge and biodiversity/health.

**Class 2 — Credible but incomplete**

- Indigenous/local ecological observations may improve early warning for wildlife-health change when paired with formal surveillance. The value is context-specific and should be demonstrated rather than assumed.
- Community ownership and governance may improve trust, participation, and durability of surveillance, but effect sizes will vary and should not be universalized from Nunavik.

**Class 3 — Disputed / unresolved**

- Any claim that traditional rules were historically designed to prevent a particular pathogen without direct historical evidence.
- Any generalized assertion that Indigenous knowledge is inherently more ecological, accurate, sustainable, or disease-protective than biomedical knowledge.
- Any claim that a single testing program alone explains the disappearance of all trichinellosis in a large region without checking program coverage, case definitions, and time series.

**Class 4 — Author synthesis / speculation**

- “A surveillance system can extract just as efficiently as a mine.” This is Sven's governance analogy, not an empirical finding.
- Framing laboratory testing as “information inserted at the decision point” is synthesis derived from the Nunavik case.
- The claim that suppressed or overlooked knowledge can sit in languages and institutions powerful systems are not structured to hear is a book-level thesis requiring examples across chapters.

## Publication-gate work

Before release, retrieve Makivik/Nunavik Research Centre's own description of the walrus testing program rather than relying principally on the federal award profile. Seek Inuit-authored or Inuit-governed accounts of how sampling, communication, consent, and food decisions actually work. Verify the outbreak time series behind the phrase “eliminated the trichinellosis outbreaks.” Add Inuktitut material where it changes the account rather than simply reproducing translated health advice. Keep governance and empirical efficacy separate: community ownership may be ethically required even when a specific intervention's epidemiological effect remains uncertain.
