# Reservoirs

The animal that makes us sick is not always the animal that keeps a pathogen alive.

That distinction—between reservoir, intermediate host, amplifier, vector, and dead-end host—is the grammar of zoonosis. Confuse the nouns and the story collapses.

A reservoir is not a villain. It is an ecological population in which a pathogen persists. Bats are famous reservoirs for several viral families, rodents for others, birds for influenza A lineages. But “bats carry viruses” is about as explanatory as “humans carry viruses.” There are more than fourteen hundred bat species occupying radically different ecologies. Their immune systems, roosting behavior, migration, diets, and contact with humans differ. Treating them as one epidemiological object is bad biology.

The same is true of rodents. Mastomys rodents matter enormously for Lassa fever in West Africa. Other rodents matter for hantaviruses, plague ecology, leptospirosis, and different arenaviruses. Control that ignores species and landscape can fail while appearing energetic.

Intermediate hosts complicate the chain. Dromedary camels can transmit MERS-CoV to humans even though the deeper evolutionary ancestry of related viruses points toward bats. Horses amplified Hendra virus after infection from flying foxes. Domestic animals can become bridges because humans have deliberately built intense, repeated contact with them.

Then there are vectors: mosquitoes, ticks, fleas, sand flies. They are living syringes with ecology. Temperature changes development rates. Rain changes breeding habitat. Host abundance changes meals. Human housing changes access.

The public wants the animal. Science needs the network.

That network also explains why killing a reservoir can backfire. Ecological disturbance can alter movement, reproduction, stress, and contact patterns. Vaccinating dogs against rabies is often more effective than waiting to vaccinate bitten humans because it interrupts transmission in the population maintaining the threat. In other systems the intervention point may be livestock biosecurity, food handling, housing, vector control, or occupational protection.

A reservoir is where a pathogen persists. Prevention begins by discovering where risk is produced.
