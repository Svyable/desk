# The Spillover Economy

Every spillover interface has an income statement.

Forests become farms because land has value. Wildlife moves through trade because someone earns money. Poultry are raised at high density because protein can be produced cheaply. Migrant workers accept occupational exposure because wages matter. Smallholders mix species because diversified households survive shocks. Markets concentrate animals because cities need food.

Public-health writing often names these activities “drivers” and stops there. A driver without an incentive is only a label.

Prevention becomes serious when it asks who pays. A farmer ordered to report sick animals may face destruction of the flock. If compensation is slow or absent, concealment can be economically rational. A trader facing a ban may move commerce underground. A country that reports an outbreak rapidly may suffer trade and tourism losses while neighboring states benefit from its honesty.

We have designed systems in which the first person to tell the truth can be punished for it.

That is a surveillance defect.

Pandemic prevention therefore belongs partly to economics: credible compensation, insurance, labor protection, veterinary services, safer market infrastructure, land-use incentives, and international rules that reward transparency rather than merely demanding it.

A virus does not respond to incentives. Everyone standing between the virus and the next host does.
