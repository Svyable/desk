# Ebola: Forest Knowledge and Global Panic

Ebola is usually introduced to Western audiences at the moment of horror: hemorrhage, isolation suits, frightened cities. That is late in the story.

Ebolaviruses have caused repeated outbreaks in parts of Africa. Evidence points toward bats as likely reservoir hosts for some ebolaviruses, while primates and other wildlife can become infected and serve as sources of human exposure. Exact reservoir ecology remains incompletely resolved. Certainty should not be manufactured from a plausible consensus.

The 2013–2016 West African epidemic exposed another misconception. Spillover starts outbreaks; institutions determine trajectories. Once sustained human-to-human transmission was established, the decisive variables included delayed recognition, fragile health systems, burial practices involving contact with bodies, mobility across borders, mistrust, treatment capacity, infection control, and the willingness of communities to cooperate with responders.

“Culture” became an easy explanation. It was often a lazy one. Burial is not an exotic behavior; every society has obligations to its dead. The operational problem was specific contact with highly infectious bodies. Effective response improved when public-health teams worked with communities to design safer burials that preserved dignity rather than treating local people as obstacles to epidemiology.

African clinicians and researchers had decades of Ebola experience before the world discovered the disease as a global security concern. That asymmetry recurs throughout zoonotic history: local experts live with the pathogen; international attention arrives when the pathogen threatens to travel.

Ebola’s deepest lesson is that trust is not public-relations decoration. In an outbreak, trust is transmission control.
