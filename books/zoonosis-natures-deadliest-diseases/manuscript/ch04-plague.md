# Plague: Rats Were Not the Whole Story

The rat has carried the reputation for centuries. The bacterium deserves more precision.

Plague is caused by Yersinia pestis, maintained in complex rodent-flea systems. The familiar urban story—infected rats die, hungry fleas seek human blood, people develop bubonic plague—is real. It is not the only ecology, and historical pandemics cannot be reduced to a cartoon of black rats marching across Europe.

Ancient DNA transformed the argument. Researchers recovered Y. pestis DNA from human remains, tying the bacterium directly to historical mortality episodes once debated from symptoms and chronicles. Genetics did not make the history simple. It made simplistic certainty harder to defend.

The second pandemic, including the Black Death, unfolded through trade routes, ports, settlements, climates, rodent ecologies, human ectoparasites debated by researchers, and human-to-human transmission in pneumonic cases. Different places may have supported different pathways. A mechanism capable of explaining one outbreak is not automatically the mechanism for a continent.

Plague also survives modernity. Natural foci persist in parts of Africa, Asia, and the Americas. Madagascar has experienced recurrent outbreaks. The American West contains enzootic plague in wild rodents. The medieval costume is gone; the ecology remains.

That continuity matters. Eradication is impossible when a pathogen persists across wildlife reservoirs spread over large landscapes. The objective becomes surveillance, rapid diagnosis, vector management, safer contact with animal carcasses, and treatment.

Plague teaches a recurring lesson: civilization remembers the epidemic peak and forgets the reservoir. Pathogens do not care which century we associate them with.
