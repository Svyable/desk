# Lassa: The House Mouse Problem

Lassa fever lives in the intimacy of the household.

Its principal reservoir is the multimammate rodent Mastomys natalensis, widely distributed in sub-Saharan Africa and comfortable around human settlements. Infection can occur when food, surfaces, or household environments are contaminated with rodent urine or feces. Person-to-person transmission, particularly in healthcare settings without adequate infection control, can add another route.

The dramatic hemorrhagic-fever label obscures how uneven disease can be. Many infections are mild or unrecognized; severe disease can be devastating, especially in pregnancy. That spectrum makes surveillance difficult. The cases a hospital sees are not necessarily the infections a community experiences.

Control advice often sounds deceptively simple: keep food in rodent-proof containers, dispose of waste, block holes, maintain clean homes. But a recommendation that assumes durable housing, sealed storage, reliable waste collection, and money is not merely medical advice. It is an infrastructure specification.

That is why neglected zoonoses reveal more about political economy than headline outbreaks do. The reservoir is biological. Exposure can be architectural.

West African researchers and clinicians have built much of the practical knowledge around Lassa fever under conditions far removed from the laboratories that later cite their samples and sequences. A global account should reverse the usual direction of prestige and begin where the disease is actually known.

The mouse behind the wall is not an exotic threat. It is a reminder that housing is part of the immune system of a society.
