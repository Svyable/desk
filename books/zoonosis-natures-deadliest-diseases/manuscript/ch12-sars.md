# SARS: A Supply Chain for Viruses

The first SARS epidemic was a warning written in logistics.

SARS-CoV emerged in southern China in 2002 and spread internationally in 2003. Horseshoe bats were later identified as hosts of closely related SARS-like coronaviruses, while civets and other mammals in wildlife-market systems were implicated as intermediate or amplifying hosts in the outbreak ecology.

The phrase “wet market” became globally familiar and globally sloppy. Markets differ radically. A produce market is not a live-wildlife market. A market selling slaughtered domestic animals is not epidemiologically identical to one holding multiple live mammal species in close proximity. Precision matters because the risky feature is not foreignness. It is the contact architecture.

SARS exploited another architecture after spillover: hospitals and hotels. Superspreading events showed that transmission is heterogeneous. A small fraction of infected people or settings can generate a large fraction of onward infections. Ventilation, aerosol-generating care, delayed isolation, building airflow, and chance can matter enormously.

The epidemic was contained. That success may have taught the wrong emotional lesson. Humanity proved that a coronavirus outbreak could be stopped, then allowed the structural conditions for future emergence to remain partly intact.

A warning is not prevention merely because we survived it.
