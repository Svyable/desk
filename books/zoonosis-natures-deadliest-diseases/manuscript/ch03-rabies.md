# Rabies: The Old Contract

Rabies is what zoonosis looks like when the mystery is largely gone and the failure remains.

Humans have feared rabid dogs for millennia. Modern medicine knows the agent, the transmission route, the reservoir systems, and the extraordinary fact that timely post-exposure prophylaxis can prevent disease after exposure. Once clinical symptoms appear, rabies is almost invariably fatal. Before that threshold, it is one of medicine’s most preventable catastrophes.

The geography of death therefore tells a political story. In wealthy settings, canine rabies has been pushed back through vaccination, surveillance, animal control, and access to post-exposure care. Elsewhere, people—disproportionately children—still die after dog bites because vaccine, immunoglobulin, transportation, information, or money arrives too late.

Louis Pasteur’s celebrated 1885 treatment of Joseph Meister often occupies the Western history. A more useful global history follows the dog. Mass canine vaccination changes the equation upstream. Instead of asking every exposed person to outrun a virus moving through peripheral nerves toward the central nervous system, it reduces the probability that the bite contains rabies in the first place.

This is One Health before the term: veterinary intervention preventing human death.

Rabies also exposes the moral distortion created by neglected zoonoses. A pathogen does not need pandemic potential to produce an enormous burden. Diseases that kill poor rural people one by one rarely receive the cultural attention given to novel pathogens threatening airports and financial centers. Novelty is newsworthy. Repetition becomes scenery.

That should offend us. When the biological problem is difficult, failure can be understandable. When the tools exist and deaths continue for decades, the remaining pathogen is partly administrative.

Rabies is nature’s ancient weapon. Its modern persistence is our choice.
