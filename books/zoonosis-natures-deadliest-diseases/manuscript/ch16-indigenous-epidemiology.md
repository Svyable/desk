# Indigenous Epidemiology

A walrus can feed a community. It can also carry Trichinella.

Those two facts are not opposites. That is what makes the Arctic such a useful place to think about zoonosis.

In northern Canada, country food is not a quaint survival of an earlier economy. Hunting, sharing, preparation, and eating remain bound to nutrition, culture, identity, family, skill, and the practical economics of places where supermarket food can be expensive and poor substitutes travel very long distances. A public-health message that begins and ends with “do not eat wild meat” can therefore be technically safe and practically absurd.

Trichinella larvae can live encysted in the muscle of Arctic mammals. People become infected by eating raw or insufficiently cooked meat containing viable larvae. Walrus has been associated with human outbreaks in northern communities. Freezing and fermentation do not reliably eliminate the risk. Thorough cooking does, but cooking every piece of walrus changes a food practice with cultural meaning.

There is another option: know which animal is infected.

In Nunavik, the Inuit-owned Nunavik Research Centre developed a walrus-meat testing program as part of a much broader community-based scientific institution. Polar Knowledge Canada credits the program with eliminating trichinellosis outbreaks that were once common in Nunavik communities. The Centre was created and is owned through Makivik Corporation, the Inuit organization established after the James Bay and Northern Quebec Agreement. Its work combines laboratory methods with local participation and Indigenous knowledge rather than requiring Inuit communities to choose between outside science and their own way of life.

That is not a footnote to this book. It is one of the clearest examples of what disease prevention looks like when the community at risk also owns part of the knowledge system.

The mechanics are straightforward. A hunter can provide tissue from a harvested animal for testing. If Trichinella is detected, the meat can be handled accordingly; if it is not, families gain information that lets them preserve customary food practices without treating every walrus as equally dangerous. The laboratory does not abolish the hunt. It changes uncertainty.

Compare that with a more familiar public-health model. Experts arrive. Samples leave. Papers appear. Guidance returns in a language and institutional style chosen elsewhere. The community supplied the bodies, animals, histories, and observations from which the scientific value was extracted, but ownership of the resulting data and interpretation traveled south.

For generations, this pattern was so normal that researchers called it fieldwork.

The problem is not that outside scientists have nothing to contribute. They have a great deal to contribute: diagnostics, microscopy, sequencing, toxicology, statistical methods, laboratory quality systems, comparative datasets. The problem begins when those capacities are mistaken for a monopoly on knowledge.

People who hunt, herd, fish, butcher, preserve, and live among animals observe things epidemiologists desperately need. They notice changes in body condition, migration, fat, lesions, parasites, unusual behavior, carcass quality, ice conditions, water, forage, insects, timing, and abundance. A hunter may not name a virus. That does not make his observation of an animal acting strangely irrelevant. A pastoralist may not produce a phylogenetic tree. That does not make her memory of when livestock miscarriages appear in a season unscientific by definition.

Observation comes before vocabulary.

Modern disease surveillance sometimes forgets this because its most prestigious signals arrive in standardized formats. A PCR result has a number. A genomic sequence can enter a global database. A hospital case has a form. Local ecological knowledge may arrive as a story about animals behaving differently on a river bend thirty kilometers from town. One type of information moves easily through bureaucracies; the other often depends on whether someone in the bureaucracy knows how to listen.

This chapter could easily go wrong by swinging too far in the opposite direction. Indigenous knowledge is not magic. It is not a single system. There is no universal Indigenous epidemiology. Thousands of peoples have developed distinct knowledge traditions under different ecologies, political histories, languages, and pressures. Some inherited practices reduce disease risk. Some may increase it. Some claims will withstand laboratory testing; others will not. Some knowledge is sacred or restricted and was never intended to become a public-health dataset.

Age does not prove a claim. Neither does indigeneity.

But modernity does not prove one either.

The useful question is how different systems learn, test, retain, and govern observations. Scientific institutions have mechanisms for replication, measurement, criticism, and revision, at least when they work properly. Indigenous knowledge systems may preserve longitudinal ecological observation across generations, often at spatial scales so local that remote instruments miss their meaning. These capacities are not interchangeable. That is precisely why collaboration can create something neither system contains alone.

The word collaboration, however, has been abused almost as thoroughly as the word partnership.

WHO’s 2026 framework for respectful engagement with Indigenous Peoples on biodiversity, health, and traditional knowledge is notable because it moves beyond asking researchers to be polite. It places self-determination, Free, Prior and Informed Consent, Indigenous data sovereignty, reciprocity, shared governance, safeguards, and accountability near the center of the relationship. Those terms matter because knowledge has consequences. A pathogen sample can become a paper, a patent, a commercial product, a conservation restriction, a border measure, or a justification for outsiders to enter territory. Information about medicinal plants, wildlife locations, migration routes, or disease can acquire value far beyond the context in which it was shared.

A surveillance system can extract just as efficiently as a mine.

That is an uncomfortable sentence for global health because surveillance is usually described as an unqualified good. More surveillance means earlier detection; earlier detection means fewer deaths. Often that is true. But who is watching whom, under what authority, with what consent, and who controls the resulting data are not secondary ethical details. They determine whether people participate, whether knowledge is withheld, and whether a program deserves trust.

The COVID-19 era made “data sharing” sound frictionless. Genomes should move instantly. Case numbers should move instantly. Animal detections should move instantly. The epidemiological argument is powerful: delay can cost lives. Yet communities and countries have memories of providing biological material or knowledge and receiving little in return. A system that wants rapid sharing without equitable benefit sharing is eventually surprised by reluctance it helped create.

Indigenous data sovereignty forces a harder formulation. The goal is not to make information immobile. The goal is to establish legitimate rules for its movement.

Nunavik’s research model matters here because the institution itself changes the geometry. The Nunavik Research Centre is not merely a southern university laboratory stationed in the north. It is Inuit-owned. Its projects have included contaminant monitoring in fish, marine mammals, waterfowl, and caribou as well as disease testing. Local citizens participate. The research addresses questions that matter to the communities whose food systems and environment are being measured.

This sounds obvious. It is not the default architecture of research.

In much of twentieth-century science, northern and Indigenous territories were treated as natural laboratories. Scientists arrived because the remoteness, wildlife, climate, or population offered unusual research opportunities. A place could be scientifically central while its residents remained institutionally peripheral. Samples were valuable. Local decision power was negotiable.

The resulting suspicion is sometimes described by outside responders as a “trust deficit,” a phrase with a convenient passive voice. Deficit in whom? Trust is not a mineral deposit communities fail to possess. It is a judgment about whether another party has behaved in ways that make reliance sensible.

That distinction becomes epidemiological during outbreaks.

Consider what a zoonotic investigation may ask of a community. Report dead animals. Allow strangers to sample livestock or wildlife. Tell them where you hunt. Describe food preparation. Identify who attended a meal. Let health workers enter homes. Submit blood. Change burial or caregiving practices. Accept restrictions on movement or animal trade. Believe that an invisible pathogen, rather than poisoning, politics, sorcery, outsiders, or some other explanation, accounts for sudden deaths. Each request may be scientifically justified. None is socially trivial.

The strongest surveillance system is not the one that can compel the most disclosure. It is the one people have reason to use before compulsion becomes necessary.

Indigenous-led institutions can improve that equation, but leadership should not be reduced to a tactic for extracting compliance. The ethical point comes first. Indigenous Peoples are rights-holders, not a difficult stakeholder category. WHO’s recent framework explicitly makes that distinction through consent and self-determination. Public health does not acquire an exemption from political rights because a pathogen is interesting.

There is also a more subtle scientific gain. When governance changes, questions change.

A distant researcher may ask which pathogens are present in walrus. A community may ask which harvested animal is safe to share this week. The first question produces prevalence knowledge. The second produces a testing system. Both are scientific. Only one is immediately organized around a decision families have to make.

That difference appears throughout zoonotic disease control. Pastoralists worried about an animal disease may care first about which animals to move, isolate, sell, vaccinate, or avoid slaughtering. Wildlife managers may care about population-level conservation. Physicians may care about human case detection. Virologists may care about viral diversity. A surveillance program designed entirely around the virologist’s question can generate excellent papers while remaining mediocre prevention.

The Arctic example also protects us from a common mistake in writing about traditional food. Safety advice is frequently presented as if people are choosing between science and culture. In reality, communities have always modified food practices in response to risk, scarcity, environment, technology, and observation. Laboratory testing can become part of culture rather than its enemy when the institution providing it is trusted and the intervention preserves what people value.

This is interface design in its most literal form. The dangerous interface is parasite and meat and eater. The intervention is not exterminating walrus, abandoning hunting, or pretending the parasite does not exist. It is information inserted at the decision point.

There are limits. Testing programs require equipment, trained personnel, logistics, quality assurance, and communication. Not every zoonosis can be managed by screening individual animals. Negative tests have sensitivity limits. Sampling can miss heterogeneous infection. Conditions change. Any program that becomes a reassuring ritual rather than an accurate measurement can create risk of its own.

Those cautions make the Nunavik example stronger, not weaker. It is not a fable about ancient wisdom defeating modern disease. It is a hybrid institution doing practical work.

The phrase “traditional knowledge” itself can mislead because it sounds static. Knowledge that deserves the name changes. Hunters encounter new species distributions. Climate alters ice, insects, migration, and access. Imported foods change diets. New diagnostic tools appear. Diseases move. Communities integrate some information and reject other information. A knowledge system that cannot revise is not a system of learning.

Climate change makes this especially urgent in the north. Warming alters habitats and host ranges, thaw and water conditions, vector ecology, migration timing, and human access to animals. The ecological baselines embedded in generational knowledge are therefore being stressed at the same moment outside institutions need those baselines most. A hunter saying, “we did not used to see this here,” is not a substitute for a temperature series or pathogen test. It can be the reason to start one.

That is what reciprocal surveillance should look like: not treating local observation as proof, and not treating it as noise.

The chapter title, Indigenous Epidemiology, is therefore deliberately provocative and needs a boundary. Epidemiology is a formal discipline with methods that should not be casually relabeled. I am not claiming that every hunting rule or food taboo is epidemiology in disguise. I am claiming something narrower: populations living in sustained relationship with animals and landscapes generate observations relevant to disease ecology, and systems that dismiss those observations because they arrive through another language, institution, or epistemology discard information before testing it.

The same warning runs in reverse. Outsiders eager to celebrate Indigenous knowledge can romanticize it into another form of extraction. A beautiful quotation about interconnectedness becomes a decorative opening to a report whose budget, data, authorship, and decisions remain elsewhere. Respectful language can coexist with unchanged power.

Ownership is harder to fake.

Who decides the research question? Who controls the sample? Who can say no? Who sees the results first? Who can publish? Who benefits if there is commercial value? Who determines whether sensitive ecological knowledge becomes public? Who keeps the laboratory running after the grant ends? Those questions reveal the actual architecture beneath the rhetoric of partnership.

Zoonotic disease makes them impossible to avoid because the best early-warning information often lies close to people whose political power has historically been weak relative to the institutions seeking their data.

The free world has a bad habit of imagining that suppressed knowledge exists only behind authoritarian archives or in classified files. Some of it sits in plain sight inside languages powerful institutions never learned, in communities researchers visited without ceding authority, and in observations that did not resemble the forms bureaucracies were prepared to receive.

Bringing that knowledge to light does not mean taking it.

Sometimes the most important scientific advance is building an institution in which the people who already know something are able to decide how the rest of us learn it.
