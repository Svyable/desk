# Indigenous Epidemiology

Long before “One Health,” people living close to animals were building rules about animals.

Some were spiritual, some practical, many inseparably both. Communities developed hunting seasons, food taboos, carcass-handling practices, landscape knowledge, animal-behavior observations, and rules about where or when particular species should be taken. It would be a mistake to romanticize these systems as hidden germ theory. It would be equally foolish to assume that knowledge expressed outside modern biomedical vocabulary contains no epidemiological information.

The research challenge is translation without theft.

English-language global health often treats local communities as sources of “beliefs” and outside scientists as sources of “knowledge.” That asymmetry can erase observation accumulated over generations. Hunters may notice unusual animal mortality before surveillance officers arrive. Pastoralists may know seasonal livestock disease patterns with extraordinary resolution. Indigenous ecological knowledge can identify changes in migration, vector abundance, water, flowering, fruiting, and animal condition that remote sensing sees differently or later.

Not every traditional claim is correct. Neither is every institutional assumption. Evidence does not become true because it is old, Indigenous, scientific, or modern. Claims should survive contact with observation.

But surveillance that cannot hear the people nearest the animals is intentionally deaf.

A serious zoonosis system needs reciprocal translation: molecular data moving outward, ecological knowledge moving inward, and communities retaining agency over how their knowledge is used. The frontier is not merely finding new viruses. It is finding the information our institutions have trained themselves not to recognize.
