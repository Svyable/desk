# HARBOR FOR NOBODY — tracer denominator audit

Checked 2026-09-19. This is a bounded evidence pass for Chapter 1 and the later `Dirty Dirt` / cleanup chapters. It does not revise manuscript prose yet.

## What the current DOE record actually lets us say

DOE Legacy Management's current Chariot fact sheet supplies the quantitative denominator that the opening chapter presently withholds. In August 1962, USGS ran radioactive-tracer plots along and near Snowbank Creek and its confluence with Ogotoruk Creek to study movement of fission products through saturated soils, sediment and surface water under simulated rain/runoff conditions.

DOE reports that the tracer soil contained:

- 6 mCi cesium-137;
- 5 mCi iodine-131;
- 5 mCi strontium-85;
- 10 mCi of various other isotopes;
- mixed with 15 pounds of clean native soil.

The individual plots ranged from roughly 2 × 2 feet to 5 × 7 feet. After the tests, tracer-contaminated soil was removed from the plots, placed in drums, moved to a nearby area, mixed with clean native soil, combined with boards/polyethylene used for the plots, and buried beneath about four feet of clean native soil in a mound occupying roughly 400 square feet. DOE says this material remained there until the 1993 removal.

- DOE Office of Legacy Management, **Tribal Collaboration** (Chariot section): https://www.energy.gov/lm/tribal-collaboration

## The cleanup chronology is more interesting than a simple contamination story

The same fact sheet records an important complication that should survive into the manuscript. In September 1992, Alaska DEC and DOE re-examined the tracer-soil mound. DOE says no surface radioactivity above background was identified. Risk reviews by Oak Ridge Institute for Science and Education and the Alaska Department of Health and Social Services concluded that neither the original 1962 levels nor the levels remaining in 1992 posed a risk to human health or the environment. The initial decision was therefore to leave the material in place.

DOE's current tribal-collaboration account says that, after consultation with area residents and Alaska DEC, DOE decided in 1993 to remove all remaining radiologically contaminated material as the most effective way to assure residents that the site presented no risk. Alaska subsequently issued clean-closure status for the radiological site.

This means the later cleanup should not be narrated as if a newly discovered radiological hazard forced removal. On the current authoritative record, the stronger and stranger story is that a material judged by the cited risk reviews not to pose a health/environmental risk was nevertheless removed in a context of local concern and damaged trust. Remediation can therefore be technically unnecessary under one risk model and institutionally necessary under another.

## Strongest counterargument / boundary

DOE's modern fact sheet is an authoritative summary, not the complete 1962 tracer report or the full 1992–93 decision file. Its isotope list groups 10 mCi as `various other isotopes`; it does not, by itself, supply the source Nevada event, isotope-by-isotope activity, decay-corrected inventory, measured transport from each plot, recovery fraction, exposure reconstruction, or the complete consultation record. Those remain publication burdens.

Likewise, `no surface radioactivity above background` is not equivalent to `no tracer radionuclides remained anywhere in the mound`, and a retrospective risk conclusion does not establish that residents' earlier uncertainty was irrational. Conversely, community concern and a later removal decision do not establish a Chariot-caused disease burden. Keep measurement, risk assessment, trust and health causation as separate evidentiary questions.

## Manuscript implications

Chapter 1's line that the `cleanup was real` remains defensible, but the later cleanup chapter should resist implying that cleanup proves a dangerous exposure. The more revealing sequence is:

1. a nuclear excavation is cancelled;
2. a small, documented tracer experiment imports radioactive material anyway;
3. the experiment's waste remains buried on site for three decades;
4. later official assessment says it does not pose a health/environmental risk;
5. the government removes it after consultation in a community that has reasons not to accept reassurance as a substitute for control.

That sequence is better for the book because it makes the central subject governance rather than radiological melodrama. It also sharpens `study residue`: residue can matter historically and politically even when its measured risk is low.

## Publication holds added by this audit

Do not publish without underlying primary reports:

- the Nevada test/event from which the tracer material originated;
- the identity/activity of the `various other isotopes`;
- decay-corrected 1992/1993 inventory and measured radionuclide distribution;
- plot-by-plot migration/recovery results;
- exact ORISE / Alaska health risk assumptions and dose estimates;
- the consultation/decision record showing why removal replaced the initial leave-in-place decision.

Do not use the 1993 removal itself as evidence that the material posed a demonstrated health risk. Do not use the risk reviews as evidence that community distrust or concern was scientifically meaningless.
