# The Water That Had to Be There

Every intervention theory has a hidden inventory. STORMFURY needed supercooled liquid water.

That requirement sounds almost pedestrian compared with the scale of the ambition. The project did not need a mysterious atmospheric state. It needed droplets that remained liquid below freezing in the target clouds, enough of them that added ice nuclei could materially increase freezing, release latent heat and alter convection. If the water was not there in sufficient quantity, silver iodide had little to work on.

In early hurricane microphysics, the inventory was uncertain. Measurements inside violent tropical convection were sparse, instruments were imperfect, and the structure of the eyewall and surrounding rainbands was still being mapped. The hypothesis therefore rested partly on a quantity the program itself was trying to measure better.

This is not unusual in frontier applied science. A proposed mechanism can be physically sound in principle while depending on an environmental parameter that turns out to be badly estimated. The failure then arrives without a dramatic theoretical collapse. The reaction works. The reagent exists. The system simply does not present enough reagent at the relevant place and time.

Later airborne observations became increasingly damaging to STORMFURY because they indicated that hurricane clouds contained abundant natural ice and relatively little of the supercooled liquid water the seeding concept required. Tropical hurricane updrafts, compared with strong continental convective storms, generally did not preserve the large supercooled-water reservoirs the project had hoped to exploit. Nature was already performing much of the freezing.

That finding attacks the mechanism upstream of the outcome statistics. Even if winds fell after seeding, and even if an outer eyewall appeared, the absence of enough supercooled liquid water weakens the proposed causal chain connecting the released silver iodide to the storm-scale response.

This is a different kind of disconfirmation from discovering that the outcome occurs naturally. The natural-eyewall problem says the observed signature was not specific to treatment. The liquid-water problem says the treatment may not have had enough physical leverage to produce the signature in the first place. Together they are much stronger than either alone.

The distinction is worth preserving because retrospective accounts often jump directly from "they seeded" to "the storm changed" to "scientists later decided it did not work." The interesting work lies in the middle. What concentration of liquid water was available? At what temperatures? In which cells? How much silver iodide reached them? How much additional freezing was plausible? How large a buoyancy change followed? Could that perturbation organize a ring tens of kilometers across?

STORMFURY became vulnerable when those intermediate questions got better answers.
