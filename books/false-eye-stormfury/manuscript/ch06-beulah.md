# Beulah

Hurricane Beulah in August 1963 gave STORMFURY the kind of result a young experimental program wants badly enough to distrust.

The project mounted a large operation far southeast of Bermuda. NOAA's historical account describes eleven aircraft participating while Navy A3-D jets delivered hundreds of pounds of silver iodide to the storm. The first day did not produce a clean triumph. On the second day, changes were interpreted as more encouraging, and later summaries often grouped Beulah among the experiments that appeared consistent with the modification hypothesis.

The phrase "consistent with" has to work hard here. A hurricane's wind field is not a single dial, and aircraft do not measure every point simultaneously. Researchers infer maximum winds and structural evolution from repeated traverses through a moving storm. Even a large coordinated experiment gives a sampled reconstruction, not an omniscient view.

Beulah also exposed how quickly an experimental narrative can harden. If a new wind maximum appears farther out and inner winds fall, the sequence resembles the hypothesized response. Once the experiment has been designed to produce that pattern, human attention is naturally drawn to it. That is not fraud or stupidity. It is why controls exist.

But what is a control for a hurricane? An unseeded storm of similar intensity in a similar environment is still not the same storm. A before-and-after comparison confounds treatment with time. Alternating seeded and unseeded periods inside one cyclone risks carryover because the atmosphere remembers its previous state. Randomization is constrained by safety, logistics and the rarity of qualifying storms. The problem is not solved merely by saying that scientists should have run a better experiment.

At the time, Beulah therefore added plausibility without closure. The project could point to a physical mechanism, an intentional intervention and a response that looked directionally right. That combination is powerful. It is also exactly the combination later natural-eyewall observations would destabilize.

The correct retrospective reading is neither that Beulah was a proven success nor that nothing happened. Something happened in the storm. Measurements changed. The unresolved historical question is whether the intervention materially contributed to those changes.

That difference between event and attribution will recur until it becomes the book's central object. Weather modification is unusually good at producing events. Weather produces them all day.
