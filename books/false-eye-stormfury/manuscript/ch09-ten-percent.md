# Ten Percent

The attraction of hurricane modification was never that scientists expected to erase storms. A modest reduction in maximum winds could still have enormous practical value.

Wind damage does not increase linearly with wind speed, and storm impacts depend on far more than one number: storm surge, rainfall, size, translation speed, construction quality, exposure and local geography all matter. Still, reducing the strongest winds of a major hurricane by even around ten percent looked consequential enough to justify serious investigation. STORMFURY's ambition was therefore both grand and oddly conservative. It did not need a tamed hurricane. It needed a measurable shift.

That threshold created an experimental problem. A ten-percent change is large enough to matter to society but small enough to be confounded by natural hurricane variability over hours. The storm can intensify or weaken by comparable amounts as its inner core reorganizes. An intervention designed to create a useful effect may therefore produce an effect that is difficult to distinguish from ordinary evolution.

This is one reason operational significance and statistical significance are not the same thing. A change can be valuable if caused by treatment and unpersuasive as evidence that treatment caused it. The more naturally noisy the system, the more replication is needed to separate those questions.

STORMFURY never accumulated the hundreds of controlled cases that a robust empirical estimate might have required. Suitable hurricanes were scarce, experiments expensive, aircraft risk real, and ethical constraints correctly limited which storms could be used. The program's famous percentages therefore cannot be read like results from a mature randomized trial.

There is a harder problem. If researchers select storms because they are intense and well organized, some will naturally regress toward less extreme states. If the experiment begins near a local intensity peak, later weakening can look like treatment response. In a system with internal cycles, selection criteria themselves can bias interpretation.

None of this proves that every observed decrease was unrelated to seeding. It explains why the decreases, even when operationally exciting, were epistemically fragile.

A useful intervention in a hurricane did not need to be enormous. The evidence required to prove a modest intervention, unfortunately, was.
