# Cirrus

Before STORMFURY there was Project Cirrus, and before the technical arguments there was a public-relations disaster that would cling to hurricane modification for decades.

In 1947, General Electric researchers working with the U.S. military seeded a hurricane with dry ice. The storm had been offshore. Afterward it changed direction and eventually struck the southeastern United States. The sequence was vivid enough to supply its own causal story: scientists tampered with hurricane, hurricane turned, people suffered. The fact that a temporal sequence can be narrated in one sentence does not make the causal inference sound.

Later analyses did not establish that the seeding caused the track reversal. Historical records also showed earlier storms following broadly similar paths. Yet the episode mattered because public experiments do not occur only inside scientific inference. They happen inside law, politics, memory, and blame. A weather modification program has to live with consequences attributed to it even when attribution is scientifically weak.

That creates a strange asymmetry. If a seeded storm weakens, researchers must work hard to show that the intervention rather than natural variability caused the change. If the same storm turns toward a city, the public may not wait for a counterfactual analysis. The burden of proof changes with the direction of harm.

Cirrus therefore became part technical prehistory and part cautionary tale. It demonstrated that intervention in a hurricane could not be treated like intervention in an isolated cloud. The object had a track, landfall probabilities, insurance losses, political jurisdictions and frightened people beneath it. Experimental design would henceforth need geographic safety criteria alongside physical ones.

This matters because later retellings often make Cirrus either proof that cloud seeding steered a hurricane or proof that scientists were reckless fools. The surviving evidence supports neither simple version. The seeding is documented. The track reversal is documented. The causal bridge between them is not.

That gap is the first recurring shape in this book: an intervention, a dramatic atmospheric response, and an argument about whether the response belongs to the intervention or to the atmosphere.

STORMFURY inherited the scientific ambition of Cirrus and some of its political scar tissue. It would try to make the experiment more selective, more instrumented and farther from land. What it could not do was make a hurricane stop being a historical event while it was also being used as an experiment.
