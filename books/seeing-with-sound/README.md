# Seeing With Sound: Human Echolocation and the Brain That Refuses Its Assigned Senses

**Author:** Sven Hardy Benson
**Status:** Drafting
**Chapters:** 18

## Thesis

Human echolocation is not a party trick and not a metaphor. Some blind people use self-generated clicks and returning echoes to estimate the location, distance, size, shape, and motion of silent objects. Neuroimaging and interference experiments suggest that this information can recruit cortical territory conventionally described as visual. The deeper subject is therefore not blindness. It is the possibility that the adult human brain is less committed to fixed sensory categories than our language, textbooks, and institutions imply.

The book follows a narrow empirical core and then widens carefully. It asks what human echolocation reveals about perception, neuroplasticity, rehabilitation, mobility, sensory substitution, spatial cognition, and the boundary between a sense and a skill. It does **not** claim that hearing literally becomes vision, that every blind person should echolocate, or that sensory cortex is infinitely interchangeable.

## Reader promise

A book about people who can walk into a room, click their tongues, and tell where the wall is — and about what that forces us to reconsider about the architecture of perception.

The strange part is not that sound can bounce off a wall. The strange part is that the returning sound can become spatial experience.
