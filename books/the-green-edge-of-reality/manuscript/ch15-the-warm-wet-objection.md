# The Warm, Wet Objection

Quantum mechanics does not stop at room temperature.

Quantum control does get harder.

Those two sentences resolve more confusion than most arguments about quantum biology.

A living cell is warm compared with the cryogenic laboratories used for many quantum devices. It is wet, crowded, chemically active, mechanically soft, and full of fluctuating electric fields. Molecules collide and vibrate. Water reorganizes around charges. Proteins change conformation. Ions move. Thermal energy populates many states.

All of this tends to destroy delicate phase relationships.

So the skeptical intuition is reasonable: if a biological mechanism requires a fragile coherent quantum state to persist for a long time, a cell seems like a terrible place to put it.

The mistake is leaving *long* undefined.

A femtosecond is long enough for some electronic motion.

A picosecond is long enough for excitation transfer among pigments.

A microsecond can be long enough for radical-pair spin chemistry.

A millisecond is long enough for protein conformational changes.

A second is long enough for physiology.

The mechanism only needs its quantum feature to survive relative to the reaction it influences.

No plant needs an excitonic coherence to last until sunset.

It needs it, if it matters at all, to last long enough to change where excitation or charge goes next.

This is the first answer to the warm, wet objection.

The second is that not all quantum variables decohere at the same rate.

Electronic superpositions involving spatially distinct states can be highly sensitive to environmental fluctuations. Nuclear spins can remain coherent much longer under some conditions. Electron spins in radicals have different environmental sensitivities from electronic excitations in pigments. Tunneling is not a state that has to be preserved like a qubit; it is a contribution to a transition rate.

“Quantum effect” is not one physical object.

The environment attacks different effects differently.

The third answer is stranger: the environment may participate in the effect.

We have already seen this in photosynthetic energy transfer. Protein vibrations and solvent-like reorganization can create dephasing, but they can also bridge energy gaps, drive relaxation, stabilize charge separation, and generate vibronic mixing. A model that treats the environment as featureless noise can miss dynamics produced by the environment’s structure.

Warm and wet can be a mechanism, not just an obstacle.

This was one reason the 2025 FMO simulation work attracted attention. Earlier skeptical treatments had argued that long-lived inter-exciton coherence was absent or irrelevant. The newer microscopic simulations included a much richer representation of the vibrational environment and reported picosecond excitonic coherences even at room temperature.

The result is not a verdict on higher-plant photosynthesis.

It is a warning about approximation.

If the bath has memory and resonant structure, averaging it into a simple dephasing constant can erase effects that depend on those details.

The reverse warning is equally important.

Adding microscopic detail can create model flexibility. A detailed simulation must still reproduce independent experimental observables and make new predictions. Complexity does not deserve confidence merely because it is expensive.

The honest position is uncomfortable and productive.

Some early claims of long-lived functional electronic coherence were overinterpreted.

Some later dismissals may have depended on oversimplified environmental models.

The field is still learning how much structured quantum dynamics survives in realistic open biological systems.

This is not a failure to decide.

It is the content of the frontier.

The warm, wet objection also forces a distinction between *coherence* and *quantumness*.

A process can be irreducibly quantum without maintaining a long-lived coherent superposition.

Electron tunneling is quantum.

A molecule can tunnel during a reaction and then immediately become entangled with the environment. The transition still happened through a quantum pathway.

Spin-selective radical-pair reactions are quantum.

The spin correlation can influence product yields before environmental interactions erase it.

Molecular absorption is quantum.

The excited state can dephase rapidly after the photon is absorbed.

If we define quantum biology only as long-lived coherence, we exclude much of the most secure quantum chemistry in biology.

If we define it as anything involving molecules, we include everything.

The useful region lies between.

Nontrivial quantum features must change an explanation or prediction at a biologically relevant step.

The relevant word is *step*, not *organism*.

This is where macroscopic analogies become dangerous.

A plant is warm and wet, so people imagine the whole plant as one decohering quantum object. It is not. The candidate quantum phenomena are localized in molecular complexes and reactions with their own correlation lengths and timescales.

A leaf can be classically definite in position while an exciton is delocalized over several pigments inside one chloroplast.

A root can grow downward while a proton tunnels inside an enzyme.

A plant can be entirely ordinary at human scale while microscopic quantum dynamics alter rates within it.

There is no contradiction.

Quantum theory has always produced classical-looking macroscopic objects through decoherence and statistical averaging.

Biology adds active maintenance.

Cells repair damage, replace proteins, maintain ion gradients, and regulate concentrations. This can create stable function even though the microscopic components fluctuate continuously.

Robustness emerges above the quantum details.

That may be more important than coherence itself.

Suppose a photosynthetic complex has an excitation-transfer pathway that depends on one coherent resonance. Temperature shifts the pigment energies and the resonance disappears. The transfer fails.

That is a poor biological design.

Now suppose the network has several coupled pathways, environmental motion brings different states into resonance transiently, and relaxation moves excitation downhill. Performance remains adequate across temperature and disorder.

The second system may contain less spectacular coherence and be a better organism.

Evolution rewards robustness to the environment it actually encounters.

This suggests a hypothesis about why photosynthetic networks look the way they do: not finely tuned to preserve one quantum state, but broadly tuned so that useful transport survives molecular variation.

The distinction could be tested.

Measure transfer efficiency and coherent signatures across temperature, mutations, pigment substitutions, and controlled disorder. If coherence varies dramatically while function remains stable, coherence may be incidental or redundant. If function tracks a specific coherent feature even after other variables are controlled, the causal case strengthens.

The warm, wet world provides perturbations naturally.

Temperature is not just a nuisance.

It is an experimental axis.

The same applies to solvent and hydration. Protein hydration changes dynamics and electrostatics. Membrane composition changes local flexibility. Isotopic substitution changes vibrational frequencies. Pressure changes conformational ensembles. Each can probe which parts of the mechanism depend on the environment.

The difficulty is specificity.

Heat changes almost everything.

A slower reaction at low temperature does not automatically prove tunneling. A coherence disappearing at high temperature can reflect spectral broadening, structural changes, or faster dephasing. A magnetic response changing with temperature can reflect altered radical lifetime, cryptochrome kinetics, or ordinary development.

The experiment needs a model strong enough to predict the pattern, not just the direction.

This is where quantum biology begins to resemble modern climate science or systems biology more than a tabletop quantum demonstration.

The phenomenon is embedded in many interacting variables. Inference comes from ensembles of measurements and models rather than one decisive switch.

That can frustrate physicists accustomed to cleaner systems.

It can also make physicists useful to biology because the tools of open-system dynamics were built precisely for systems where isolation fails.

Quantum information science spent enormous effort learning how environments destroy quantum states. Quantum biology can use the same mathematics in reverse: not to protect information indefinitely, but to understand what survives long enough to affect chemistry.

The question becomes one of functional coherence time.

Not “how long can the system remain quantum?”

It remains quantum forever in the fundamental sense.

Instead: how long does a specific phase relationship, spin correlation, or delocalized state retain predictive importance for the reaction outcome?

That is a measurable quantity.

The answer may be very short.

Short can be enough.

There is a useful analogy with sparks.

A spark exists for a moment. It can ignite a fire that lasts hours. We do not require the spark to remain visible throughout combustion in order to call it causal.

Quantum effects can be sparks.

The danger of the analogy is that it invites us to assume every fire had a quantum spark. The causal bridge still has to be shown.

In photosynthesis, the spark might change charge-separation probability.

In cryptochrome, spin dynamics might change chemical yield.

In PCET, tunneling might change a reaction rate.

The later biology amplifies the difference.

A warm environment can destroy the original quantum state and preserve its chemical consequence at the same time.

This is the essence of boundary conversion.

Decoherence is not always the failure of the biological mechanism.

It can be the moment the mechanism commits.

An excitation state loses phase information while population relaxes into a charge-transfer state.

A radical pair reacts and leaves a stable product ratio.

A proton tunnels and the enzyme product is formed.

The system has traded a reversible quantum possibility for an irreversible chemical result.

The environment helps write the record.

This perspective suggests that asking whether life “protects” quantum states may be the wrong default question.

Perhaps life more often **uses and disposes** of them.

A plant needs a coherent state only until the next useful chemical event.

Then keeping coherence would be pointless or even harmful if it prevented relaxation.

This is radically different from quantum computing, where the goal is to postpone environmental recording until after the computation.

Biological chemistry often wants the environment to record the result quickly.

That distinction explains why comparisons between chloroplasts and quantum computers so often mislead.

The chloroplast does not need fault-tolerant quantum memory.

It needs fast, robust transduction.

The design objective is not coherence lifetime.

It is useful yield under noise.

This also changes the way we should think about entanglement.

Entanglement is a precise quantum correlation. In a realistic pigment network, transient entanglement may arise naturally among coupled electronic or vibronic degrees of freedom. Detecting it would be fascinating.

But unless the entanglement changes an observable functional outcome, its biological importance remains open.

Entanglement can be present because the Hamiltonian produces it, not because evolution uses it.

A theoretical model showing that pulse-driven vibronic entanglement spreads excitation efficiently demonstrates a possible physical resource. The next biological question is whether real illumination and real LHCII prepare the necessary state, and whether removing the entanglement while preserving relevant couplings changes transport.

That is a high bar.

It should be.

The word *entanglement* carries too much cultural weight to be allowed a lower one.

The warm, wet objection is therefore most useful when it becomes quantitative.

How fast is dephasing?

How fast is transfer?

How fast is trapping?

How long does the radical pair live?

What are the hyperfine couplings?

What is the field magnitude?

How much does tunneling contribute to the total rate?

What vibrational modes are populated at physiological temperature?

What is the environmental spectral density?

Once numbers enter, the philosophical objection becomes a physical model.

That is where it belongs.

Some candidate effects will fail the timescale test.

Good.

Some will survive only at low temperature.

Good to know.

Some will appear under coherent laser excitation and vanish under sunlight-like preparation.

Important.

Some will persist in purified complexes and disappear in membranes.

Also important.

Some may become stronger in vivo because the structured environment supplies missing couplings.

That would be fascinating.

The goal is not to preserve the category of quantum biology.

It is to discover the boundary conditions.

The same discipline applies to the largest scaling question in the book.

Even if a quantum effect survives long enough to alter a molecular reaction, does it survive biologically far enough to matter to a leaf, a plant, a crop, or an ecosystem?

Warm and wet is only the first gauntlet.

The rest of biology is larger.

A one-percent change in excitation transfer enters a chloroplast containing feedback.

The chloroplast enters a cell with metabolic control.

The cell enters a leaf with gas exchange and hydraulics.

The leaf enters a canopy with light competition.

The canopy enters a season with weather.

Each level can amplify or erase the original effect.

The next chapter follows that climb.

The hard problem is not getting quantum mechanics into a plant.

It was there before plants existed.

The hard problem is getting a quantum effect all the way to a forest without losing the causal trail.
