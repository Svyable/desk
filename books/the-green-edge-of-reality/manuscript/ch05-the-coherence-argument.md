# The Coherence Argument

Scientific arguments are easiest to remember after they have been flattened.

One side says photosynthesis uses quantum coherence.

The other says it does not.

That is not the argument the field actually had.

The real dispute involved different systems, temperatures, pulse sequences, definitions of coherence, theoretical approximations, and meanings of the word *function*. Researchers sometimes disagreed about the interpretation of the same oscillatory feature and sometimes studied different features entirely. A result in FMO became a claim about photosynthesis in general. A result under cryogenic conditions became evidence for physiological function. A theoretical transport advantage became an evolutionary story.

By the time the argument reached the public, several rungs of inference had disappeared.

It is worth putting them back.

The first rung was experimental existence.

Ultrafast spectroscopy showed oscillatory signals in photosynthetic complexes. Those signals were real. Repeated studies found coherent features of various kinds. In oxygenic photosynthetic systems, including the photosystem II reaction center, experiments and models identified electronic, vibrational, and mixed vibronic dynamics.

The second rung was assignment.

What physical coherence generated each signal?

That question proved harder. A two-dimensional electronic spectrum can contain contributions from excited-state electronic coherences, ground-state vibrational coherences, mixed vibronic states, population dynamics, and other effects. Signals overlap. Finite pulse duration matters. Inhomogeneous broadening matters. The model used to fit spectra matters.

An oscillation with a long lifetime is not automatically a long-lived electronic superposition.

This became one of the decisive corrections to the early story.

The third rung was natural preparation.

Even if an electronic coherence can be created by an ultrafast laser pulse, would ordinary sunlight create a comparable state? A coherent femtosecond pulse drives many molecules in phase in a way that thermal sunlight does not. The system’s own electronic couplings may create transient coherence after absorption under incoherent illumination, but the initial state and amplitude can differ dramatically.

A property exposed by spectroscopy need not be a property exploited by sunlight.

The fourth rung was functional consequence.

Does the coherence alter how much excitation reaches a reaction center, how quickly charge separates, how often recombination occurs, or how robustly the system works under disorder?

This is harder than detecting the coherence because function is comparative. One must ask what would happen without it while preserving enough of the rest of the molecular system to make the comparison meaningful.

The fifth rung was evolutionary significance.

Did natural selection tune a molecular structure because the coherence improved organismal performance?

That is harder still.

The coherence debate became combustible because evidence at one rung was repeatedly used to imply success at the next.

A 2020 review in *Science Advances*, “Quantum biology revisited,” pushed back hard against the resulting mythology. Its authors argued that the long-lived oscillatory signals in the canonical FMO system were predominantly vibrational rather than evidence of long-lived inter-exciton electronic coherence, and that such electronic coherence decayed too rapidly to have the functional significance often claimed for energy transfer.

The review’s deeper argument was more important than the verdict.

Nature did not need to protect photosynthetic excitations from dissipation.

Dissipation was part of the transport.

This sounds almost disappointing if one came for the quantum magic. It becomes profound if one thinks about what a photosynthetic complex actually has to do.

An excitation begins high in energy after absorption. It must move through a heterogeneous network toward states from which charge separation can occur. If energy were perfectly conserved inside a perfectly isolated set of coupled pigments, population could slosh among states and revisit where it started. Relaxation into the environment gives the system direction. Vibrational and protein degrees of freedom absorb energy. Population settles. Reversal becomes less likely.

The environment creates an arrow.

This is ordinary thermodynamics operating inside quantum dynamics.

The 2020 review reframed photosynthetic efficiency around this structured interaction with the bath. The important quantum problem was not how biology maintained a protected superposition for as long as possible. It was how electronic states and environmental motion were coupled so that energy flowed toward useful states rather than getting trapped or dissipated prematurely.

The distinction sounds subtle.

It changes the engineering lesson completely.

If the secret of photosynthesis were long-lived coherence, a designer might try to isolate excitonic states and prevent dephasing.

If the secret is appropriate exciton-bath coupling, isolation could make the device worse.

The environment is no longer the enemy of the quantum system.

It is part of its wiring.

That conclusion fit a growing body of work that separated electronic and vibrational coherence. The long-lived beatings that had looked like electronic superpositions could often be assigned to vibrational modes. In some cases vibronic mixing allowed electronic and nuclear motions to interact in ways that still affected transfer. The binary question “is it coherent?” gave way to a more specific one: which coherences, in which subspaces, for how long, under which preparation, and with what effect?

Then the theoretical ground shifted again.

Detailed simulation of open quantum systems is difficult because the environment is enormous. A pigment does not couple to one simple bath mode. It sits in a protein with a structured vibrational spectrum. Low-frequency collective motion and higher-frequency intramolecular vibrations can both matter. The environment may retain memory, making simple Markovian approximations inaccurate. Exact treatment scales badly.

The usual solution is approximation.

Approximation is not a failure. Physics would be unusable without it.

The danger appears when a conclusion about what cannot happen is stronger than the approximation’s ability to represent the mechanism.

In 2025, Nicola Lorenzoni and colleagues published non-perturbative microscopic simulations of FMO that challenged some of the earlier negative conclusions. By treating a far more detailed vibrational environment, they reported excitonic coherences persisting on picosecond timescales at both 77 kelvin and room temperature, comparable to excitation-transfer times.

The result did not prove that a leaf uses long-lived coherence.

FMO is still bacterial.

The work was computational.

The connection to natural state preparation and organismal function remained to be established.

But the paper exposed a methodological vulnerability in the debate. If coarse-graining the environment removes the structured interactions that sustain or regenerate coherence, a model can conclude that the phenomenon is absent because the ingredients capable of producing it were averaged away.

The strange possibility is that the noise was too simplified.

That is a very quantum-biology problem.

A warm protein environment contains staggering complexity. Researchers simplify it to understand decoherence. Then they discover that specific environmental structure may be responsible for the dynamics that survive decoherence.

The bath has details.

This does not mean every detailed simulation should be believed because it finds a surprising result. A sufficiently complicated model can hide assumptions as easily as a simple one. Parameters come from experiments or calculations with uncertainty. Spectral densities are inferred. Molecular structures fluctuate. Validation against independent observables remains essential.

The right conclusion is not that coherence won.

It is that the argument moved.

The field is no longer asking only whether oscillations can be seen. It is asking whether realistic microscopic models and experiments can agree on the nature, lifetime, and functional consequences of coherent dynamics under relevant conditions.

That is progress.

Scientific progress often looks less like replacing “no” with “yes” than replacing a bad question with a more expensive one.

The plant case makes the expense worthwhile because photosystem II brings coherence close to chemistry.

The reaction center contains chlorophylls and pheophytins arranged so that excitation can couple to charge-transfer states. In the 2014 work by Romero and colleagues, electronic coherence was reported not only among excitonic states but between excitonic and charge-transfer character. That is important because the reaction center’s job is not simply to move excitation. It is to turn excitation into separated charge.

A coherence that affects that branching could matter even if it lasts briefly.

The relevant comparison is therefore not the total distance an excitation travels. It is the probability of reaching a productive charge-separated state before competing decay and recombination.

This is one reason coherence can matter without serving as a long-range quantum bus.

Suppose a small set of strongly coupled states near the reaction center includes both excited and charge-transfer character. Vibronic coupling can mix them. Coherent dynamics during the first tens or hundreds of femtoseconds can redistribute amplitude among states. Environmental relaxation then stabilizes population in a charge-separated configuration.

The coherence does not need to persist until chemistry is finished.

It only needs to change the branching ratios early enough.

This is a more plausible functional claim than the image of an excitation coherently exploring the entire antenna.

It is also harder to prove because the time window is short and the relevant states overlap spectroscopically.

The argument therefore depends on theory and experiment leaning against each other.

Spectroscopy constrains energies, couplings, lifetimes, and oscillatory features.

Theory proposes assignments and predicts dynamics.

Structural biology constrains geometry.

Mutations or modified complexes perturb specific interactions.

Temperature and isotope effects test sensitivity to motion.

Physiological measurements ask whether any of it matters outside the purified complex.

No single method owns the answer.

That is a useful lesson for the rest of the book.

Quantum claims often survive by living in gaps between disciplines.

A physicist may show that a mechanism is possible but not know whether the relevant protein exists in the required state in vivo. A spectroscopist may detect a signal but not know whether it changes growth. A plant biologist may measure a phenotype under a magnetic field but not establish the microscopic spin chemistry. A theorist may fit an elegant model to a noisy dataset with parameters that a structural biologist considers implausible.

The strongest science closes gaps.

The weakest science uses one discipline’s vocabulary to disguise another discipline’s missing evidence.

Coherence also forces us to confront the seduction of efficiency.

Photosynthesis is efficient in some microscopic senses, but not every efficient process requires coherence. A classical stochastic network can transfer energy very efficiently if its rates and energy landscape are favorable. Quantum delocalization can sometimes increase robustness or open pathways, but high efficiency alone does not identify the mechanism.

This matters because the argument was sometimes made backward.

Photosynthesis is highly efficient.

Quantum coherence can improve transport in some models.

Therefore photosynthesis is highly efficient because of quantum coherence.

The conclusion does not follow.

There are many mechanisms by which a pigment network can be efficient: dense coupling, downhill energy gradients, multiple pathways, fast trapping, favorable spectral overlap, environmental relaxation, redundancy. A good experiment must distinguish among them.

The same warning applies to optimization.

A model may show a peak in transfer efficiency at an intermediate dephasing rate. A real complex may happen to operate near that region. It is tempting to say evolution tuned the system to the quantum optimum.

But broad performance plateaus are common. Multiple parameters covary. Protein stability, synthesis cost, photoprotection, repair, and spectral coverage impose other constraints. A system can sit near an apparent mathematical optimum because many viable structures do, not because selection climbed that particular landscape.

The cleanest evidence for evolutionary tuning would require comparative biology.

Do related organisms living under different light environments show systematic changes in the relevant couplings or environmental spectral density?

Do those changes alter coherent dynamics in the predicted way?

Does that alteration improve performance specifically under the ecological condition that differs?

Can the effect be separated from changes in absorption, protein abundance, or ordinary kinetics?

That is a research program, not a rhetorical flourish.

It would be a remarkable one.

This is where the coherence argument remains exciting even after the hype is removed. The hypothesis is vulnerable.

A useful scientific idea tells you how it could lose.

Long-lived electronic coherence could prove absent under natural illumination.

It could exist but contribute negligibly to transfer.

It could affect transfer but be a by-product rather than an adaptation.

It could matter only inside a tiny subset of states near the reaction center.

It could be important in some photosynthetic organisms and irrelevant in others.

Or future methods could show that detailed vibronic environments sustain functionally significant coherent dynamics more broadly than current skeptical accounts allow.

All of those outcomes teach us something about how biological molecules work.

The least interesting outcome is the one in which *quantum* becomes impossible to falsify.

If any efficient transfer counts as quantum because molecules are quantum, the field has no boundary.

If any oscillation counts as functional coherence, the field has no boundary.

If any model showing a possible advantage counts as evidence of evolutionary use, the field has no boundary.

A science without boundaries becomes a brand.

Plants deserve better.

They already offer a genuine surprise.

Their light-harvesting complexes operate in a regime where electronic coupling, molecular vibration, environmental dephasing, and irreversible chemistry occur on overlapping timescales. None can be understood completely in isolation. The system is neither a classical resistor network nor a protected quantum circuit.

It is an open quantum machine that becomes biochemical as it runs.

The coherence argument matters because it forced researchers to stop treating openness as a nuisance.

The environment is not only what destroys the wave.

It is what gives the wave somewhere to go.

This reframing helps make sense of a result that otherwise sounds paradoxical: adding noise can improve transport.

In the next chapter, noise gets promoted from villain to machinery.

Not all noise.

Not always.

That distinction is the whole point.
