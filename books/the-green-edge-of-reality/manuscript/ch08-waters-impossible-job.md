# Water’s Impossible Job

Every breath of oxygen carries the aftermath of a chemical theft.

Plants, algae, and cyanobacteria take electrons from water.

The sentence is ordinary because the process is everywhere. Chemically, it is severe. Water is stable. Oxidizing it to molecular oxygen requires removing four electrons and four protons in a coordinated sequence while forming an O–O bond. Do it badly and reactive intermediates attack the machinery that created them.

Photosystem II does it constantly.

The catalytic site is the oxygen-evolving complex, a cluster built from four manganese atoms, one calcium, and bridging oxygens, embedded in a protein environment threaded with water molecules and hydrogen bonds. Structural biology resolved the cluster with an arrangement often described as a distorted chair. That shape is only the static beginning.

The catalyst changes as it works.

The classic framework is the Kok cycle. The oxygen-evolving complex advances through a series of oxidation states labeled S0, S1, S2, S3, and the transient S4 state. Each successful photochemical turnover of photosystem II removes another electron from the catalytic machinery, allowing oxidizing power to accumulate one step at a time. After the fourth advance, oxygen is formed and released, and the cluster returns to a more reduced state.

This is how a leaf adds four separate photon-driven events into one chemical transformation.

It is also where the timescales of the book begin stacking on top of one another.

A photon creates an electronic excitation in femtoseconds.

Primary charge separation occurs on ultrafast timescales.

Electron transfer through the reaction center follows.

The redox-active tyrosine YZ transfers an electron toward the oxidized P680 side and couples this event to proton movements around the catalytic site.

The manganese cluster changes oxidation state.

Water and protein groups reorganize across nanoseconds, microseconds, and milliseconds.

Only after multiple photochemical cycles does O2 leave.

A process that begins faster than any mechanical motion we can see ends in a gas bubble.

The plant has built a temporal gearbox.

That metaphor is imperfect because gears are rigid and this machine depends on flexibility. Recent time-resolved structural work has revealed the oxygen-evolving complex and surrounding channels changing as the S states advance. Water molecules appear, disappear, reorder, and participate in pathways connecting the cluster to the broader protein environment. Amino-acid side chains move. Proton-release networks rearrange. Electron transfer and proton transfer are coupled to structure rather than occurring inside a fixed scaffold.

The catalyst is not a lock.

It is a choreography.

In 2023, work on what researchers called the electron–proton bottleneck of oxygen evolution used time-resolved infrared spectroscopy and computation to probe the elusive S4 stage. The results supported a sequence in which a proton vacancy is created, an oxygen radical forms in a coupled single-electron, multi-proton event, and O–O bond formation follows rapidly.

The language is worth lingering over.

Single electron.

Multiple protons.

One catalytic transition.

The machinery does not separate electron transfer and proton transfer into independent plumbing.

Charge movement changes acidity. Protonation changes redox energetics. Hydrogen bonds reorganize. The catalytic path depends on coupled rearrangements.

This is the beginning of proton-coupled electron transfer, the subject of the next chapter, but water oxidation shows why the coupling is necessary.

Removing an electron without managing the associated positive charge would make successive oxidation prohibitively difficult. Moving protons changes the electrostatic landscape and helps keep later redox steps energetically accessible. The protein creates routes for proton release while maintaining water delivery and catalytic geometry.

The system is balancing charge as it accumulates oxidizing power.

The manganese atoms make that balance possible because transition metals can occupy multiple oxidation states. They act as a redox reservoir, distributing oxidizing equivalents across the cluster rather than forcing one fragile organic molecule to hold all the chemistry.

Calcium, though not itself carrying the same redox burden, helps organize the cluster and water network.

The surrounding protein controls ligation, hydrogen bonding, access, and proton pathways.

No single atom splits water.

The architecture does.

This is another place where the phrase *quantum phenomenon* can become either precise or silly.

Of course the electronic structure of the manganese cluster is quantum mechanical. Oxidation states, exchange coupling, orbital occupancy, bond formation, and electron transfer cannot be derived from classical mechanics. Modern computational chemistry uses quantum methods to model possible intermediates and reaction pathways.

That does not mean water oxidation is mysterious because it is quantum.

The scientific challenge is identifying which quantum features must be retained to predict the mechanism and which can be summarized in effective chemical variables.

Spin states matter in a multinuclear manganese cluster.

Electronic coupling matters.

Proton-coupled electron transfer can involve tunneling and vibronic effects.

Bond formation is quantum chemistry.

But a popular statement like “plants use quantum tunneling to split water” would outrun the evidence unless a particular tunneling pathway had been established as functionally decisive.

The more honest story is already astonishing.

A protein-metal complex takes a chemically demanding four-electron oxidation and decomposes it into a sequence of light-driven one-electron advances with coordinated proton management.

That is not less impressive because we can name the intermediates.

Naming is how the wonder becomes portable.

Time-resolved crystallography has made the sequence increasingly literal. In a 2024 *Nature* study, pump-probe serial femtosecond crystallography followed PSII after flashes across delays from tens of nanoseconds to milliseconds. Structural changes appeared around the quinone acceptors, the redox-active tyrosine, the oxygen-evolving complex, and water/proton channels. In the S2-to-S3 transition, a water molecule appeared near calcium on a sub-microsecond timescale and later disappeared as density associated with an additional oxygen in the cluster increased, consistent with a route for substrate water incorporation.

A static crystal structure could never tell that story.

The old temptation in structural biology was to treat a high-resolution image as the machine itself. Time-resolved methods reveal that function lives in transitions between structures.

Quantum biology has a parallel temptation.

A coherent state, a radical pair, or a tunneling pathway can be treated as the mechanism merely because it is exotic and measurable.

But function lives in the transitions and their consequences.

For water oxidation, the causal chain is unusually clear because the final product is obvious. Oxygen appears. Electrons enter the photosynthetic electron-transport chain. Protons contribute to the electrochemical gradient and chemistry. If the cluster fails, oxygenic photosynthesis stops.

That clarity makes PSII a useful standard for more speculative quantum claims.

We know what functional evidence looks like when the mechanism is mature.

Structure.

Kinetics.

Spectroscopy.

Mutational effects.

Isotope effects.

Computational chemistry constrained by experiment.

Intermediate states.

A macroscopic product.

No single experiment created the modern picture of water oxidation. The mechanism emerged by convergence.

This matters because quantum biology sometimes seeks a dramatic signature: one experiment showing coherence, one field effect, one isotope dependence.

A mature mechanism usually looks less cinematic.

It becomes overdetermined.

Several independent methods point toward the same causal sequence.

Water oxidation also exposes how badly the word *efficiency* can mislead.

The plant does not take the minimum imaginable number of steps simply because it wants fast oxygen. It advances through controlled intermediates because the chemistry must be survivable. Accumulating oxidizing power gradually allows the catalytic site to prepare substrate water, manage protons, and avoid releasing destructive intermediates into the protein environment.

The slowness is part of the safety.

This principle reappears across biology: a multistep pathway can be more controllable than a direct energetic leap.

The leaf receives high-energy photons but does not dump their energy into water in one violent reaction.

It converts photon energy into separated charge, transfers oxidizing equivalents, accumulates them on a metal cluster, adjusts protonation, and only then creates O2.

The plant domesticated a dangerous reaction by staging it.

That staging has planetary consequences.

Oxygenic photosynthesis transformed Earth’s atmosphere over geological time. The oxygen we treat as background is the waste product of water oxidation coupled to the carbon and energy needs of photosynthetic organisms. The machinery in modern plants is descended from ancient cyanobacterial innovations that became embedded in chloroplasts through endosymbiosis.

This historical fact complicates any story about plant optimization.

Plants did not design photosystem II from scratch for terrestrial life.

They inherited a core molecular machine with deep evolutionary history and built regulation around it. Antenna systems changed. Protective mechanisms diversified. Chloroplast organization changed. Land plants adapted to higher and more variable light, desiccation, temperature variation, and new ecological relationships.

The oxygen-evolving core remained remarkably conserved.

That conservation suggests strong functional constraint.

It does not prove every detail is globally optimal.

Evolution often preserves systems that are too integrated to replace easily.

Photosystem II is a monument to path dependence as much as to efficiency.

This is useful when thinking about artificial photosynthesis. Engineers do not need to copy the Mn4CaO5 cluster exactly in order to learn from it. The principle is to accumulate oxidative equivalents, couple proton and electron management, control intermediate states, use abundant materials where possible, and separate light absorption from catalytic steps in a coordinated architecture.

Artificial catalysts can use different metals, supports, and mechanisms because they are not constrained by protein biosynthesis or three billion years of inherited machinery.

A leaf is evidence that water oxidation can be done under mild conditions.

It is not necessarily a blueprint for the cheapest solar-fuel device.

The same caution applies when we try to engineer plants themselves.

If water oxidation is a bottleneck under some condition, speeding one S-state transition might sound beneficial. But oxygen evolution is coupled to electron acceptor availability and downstream metabolism. Faster water oxidation cannot help if reaction centers are closed or carbon fixation cannot consume the resulting energy. It may increase damaging redox pressure.

Again, the rest of the plant gets a vote.

One of the deepest lessons of photosystem II is that energy accumulation creates responsibility.

Every oxidizing equivalent stored on the cluster must be managed.

Every electron sent forward must eventually be accepted somewhere.

Every proton contributes to gradients and buffering demands.

A highly reactive system is useful only when the network around it can absorb the consequences.

The quantum event at the beginning is therefore constrained by the chemical capacity at the end.

There is a line connecting a femtosecond photon absorption to the concentration of carbon dioxide inside a leaf.

Close the stomata during drought and less CO2 enters.

Carbon fixation slows.

NADPH use changes.

Electron carriers become more reduced.

Reaction-center pressure rises.

Protective dissipation increases.

Photodamage risk changes.

The value of the next absorbed photon has been altered by soil water.

This is why a leaf cannot be understood from the reaction center outward only.

Causality also runs backward through regulation.

The molecular quantum machinery is embedded in a physiological economy.

Water is both electron source and ecological constraint.

A molecule of H2O at the catalytic site donates electrons that sustain photosynthesis. Vastly more water is lost through transpiration when stomata open to acquire carbon dioxide. The photosynthetic use of water at PSII is tiny compared with the plant’s hydraulic relationship with water.

That contrast is easy to miss.

The most dramatic water chemistry occurs at the manganese cluster.

The largest water problem occurs through pores on the leaf surface.

A plant in drought does not run out of substrate water molecules for PSII first. It closes stomata to conserve hydraulic integrity, restricting CO2 and indirectly changing photosynthetic energy balance.

Scale changes the meaning of water.

At the catalytic site: electron donor.

At the cell: solvent and turgor.

At the xylem: hydraulic column.

At the leaf: evaporative coolant and cost of carbon acquisition.

At the ecosystem: limiting resource.

No one description replaces the others.

The boundary-machine view lets them stack.

Quantum chemistry explains why the O–O bond can form along one pathway rather than another.

Protein dynamics explain how the catalytic environment prepares that pathway.

Bioenergetics explains how electron and proton movements contribute to stored energy.

Physiology explains when the resulting electron flow is useful.

Ecology explains why water availability determines whether the entire machine can run at full capacity.

This is what “from femtoseconds to forests” will eventually mean.

Before we get there, we need to stay near the catalytic site for one more chapter.

The manganese cluster cannot do its work by moving electrons alone.

Charge is coupled to protons.

A redox-active tyrosine changes protonation as it changes oxidation. Hydrogen-bond networks rearrange. The creation of oxygen depends on proton release and water delivery timed with electron removal.

Quantum biology often speaks of electron tunneling because electrons are light and their wave nature is impossible to ignore.

Protons are heavier.

They tunnel too, under the right conditions.

But the most important story is not that a proton can pass through a forbidden barrier.

It is that biology has learned to make electron and proton motion one problem.
