# Advertising to an Algorithm

The most valuable sentence in advertising may become the sentence the consumer never sees.

A person tells an agent, “Find a carry-on that fits the strictest major-airline limits, has replaceable wheels, and can arrive by Wednesday.” The agent searches. Somewhere inside the process, sellers compete for consideration. One offers ten percent off. Another offers free expedited shipping. A third has stronger repair evidence. A fourth has paid for a sponsored opportunity to be considered when travelers with this profile are ready to buy.

The human receives three options and a short explanation.

Where was the advertisement?

Digital advertising spent thirty years becoming more measurable because the internet exposed the steps between impression and transaction. A banner could be clicked. A search ad could be tied to a keyword. A social campaign could follow a user through a conversion pixel. Retail media could connect exposure inside a marketplace to the purchase recorded by the same marketplace. The industry did not become perfectly transparent, but it built an elaborate system around observable attention.

Agentic commerce hides attention inside computation.

The consumer may not see twenty sponsored messages. The agent can ingest offers, catalog data, reviews, promotions, and merchant claims, then present a compressed answer. The ad market does not disappear. It moves upstream into the machinery of recommendation.

This is why the advertising question in agentic commerce is not whether agents will accept ads.

Commercial pressure makes some form of paid influence nearly inevitable wherever large pools of high-intent consumers gather.

The question is how influence is separated from representation.

Search engines offered a relatively legible model. Organic results and paid results appeared on the same page, and regulations and platform norms pushed toward labels distinguishing the two. The boundaries were imperfect and changed over time, but the user could at least see that some placements were advertisements.

A conversational answer creates fewer natural boundaries.

If an agent says, “I recommend the Atlas carry-on because it fits your size constraint, has replaceable wheels, and the merchant can deliver by Tuesday,” the recommendation sounds like analysis. If Atlas paid the platform for the chance to be included, the commercial relationship is materially relevant even if every factual statement is true.

The ad is embedded in selection rather than presentation.

This creates a disclosure problem that cannot be solved with a tiny “sponsored” label alone.

The consumer needs to know what the sponsorship changed.

Did the merchant buy guaranteed inclusion in the candidate set? Did it buy a highlighted offer shown alongside the agent’s independent ranking? Did it pay a commission only if the transaction completed? Did the agent rank the product organically and merely receive payment for checkout? Did the platform exclude nonparticipating merchants from some features? Did the brand provide richer data that makes it easier for the agent to evaluate?

Each arrangement creates a different conflict.

The simplest consumer-protective design is separation. The agent forms its own recommendation using declared criteria, then presents commercial offers as a distinct layer. “My top choice is X. Y is offering you fifteen percent off if you buy today.” This preserves advertising as information without letting payment silently determine the recommendation.

Merchants may dislike the arrangement because the agent has already chosen a favorite.

Platforms may like it because high-intent offers can be extremely valuable.

Google’s early “Direct Offers” experiments pointed toward this market. A merchant could present a tailored promotion to a shopper judged ready to buy. The offer did not need to be a conventional ad with an image and slogan. It could be an economic response: a discount, bundle, loyalty benefit, or other inducement designed to close the transaction.

This is advertising becoming negotiation.

The distinction matters because the ad industry was built around persuasion under uncertainty. A company bought attention from people who might be interested. An agent can reveal much stronger intent.

The consumer has already said what she wants, what constraints matter, how much she can spend, and perhaps when she needs the product. A merchant does not need to create desire from scratch. It needs to improve its offer enough to satisfy the agent’s decision rule.

The value of one such opportunity can exceed the value of many ordinary impressions.

Advertising becomes less about shouting and more about bidding into a procurement event.

This resembles search advertising, where declared intent made a query valuable, but the agent knows more. A search for “running shoes” says little. An agent request can contain size, gait, injury history, preferred brands, budget, replacement schedule, weather, and the fact that the user has a race in six weeks.

The targeting precision is extraordinary.

So is the privacy risk.

The commercial ecosystem will want access to the context because context improves conversion. The user will want the benefits of competition without turning personal circumstances into a broadcast channel.

An architecture can preserve some separation. The agent can hold private preferences locally or inside a trusted service, send merchants only the minimum request needed to generate an offer, and compare responses without revealing the full consumer profile. A merchant does not need to know that the traveler is attending a funeral to quote a refundable hotel room near an address.

It needs dates, location, constraints, and perhaps a willingness-to-pay range.

Data minimization becomes a bargaining strategy as well as a privacy principle.

The buyer should not reveal more willingness to pay than necessary.

Human consumers routinely leak this information. Urgency appears in a search query. Location reveals alternatives. Browsing history suggests interest. Device type once became a notorious object of speculation in pricing discussions because companies constantly look for proxies for ability to pay. Loyalty status, purchase history, income estimates, and household data can all inform targeting.

The Federal Trade Commission’s investigation into surveillance pricing made the underlying market visible. Its staff reported that intermediaries could use detailed behavioral and personal data—including location, browsing, shopping behavior, and even interactions on a page—to help tailor prices or promotions.

Agentic commerce creates a counterintuitive privacy possibility.

A buyer agent can know more about the person while revealing less to each merchant.

Today privacy advocates often face a trade-off between personalization and data minimization. A merchant needs information to personalize the offer, so information flows outward. A trusted agent can perform personalization on the buyer’s side. It can compare products against private preferences without giving the seller the entire profile.

This is a powerful model.

It is also incompatible with some advertising business models.

If the platform operating the agent earns more when it knows and monetizes consumer context, the incentive to preserve local privacy weakens. A company can promise not to sell raw data while still using the data to decide which commercial opportunities are shown. The consumer’s private life does not need to leave the platform to be monetized.

The targeting happens where the data sits.

This was already the basic logic of large advertising platforms. They did not need to hand every advertiser a dossier. The advertiser selected an audience, the platform performed the match, and the user saw the ad. Agentic platforms can extend this model from attention to action.

An advertiser might specify, in effect: show this offer to consumers whose agents are actively trying to replace a washing machine this week, live within our delivery radius, care about energy efficiency, and have not rejected our brand.

The platform can answer without exposing identities in advance.

Commercially, this is magnificent.

From the consumer’s perspective, it can feel like a helpful market or a highly instrumented ambush depending on disclosure and control.

The agent itself can become an ad blocker of unusual power.

Traditional ad blockers remove visual advertisements from pages. Consumer agents can remove the underlying persuasion tax. They can ignore countdown timers, skip sponsored articles, compare the actual terms, dismiss irrelevant upgrades, and notice when a “sale” price is normal. They can refuse to let a human interface exploit fatigue because they do not experience fatigue the same way.

This threatens businesses that depend on behavioral friction.

It does not threaten every advertisement.

A good offer is information. If a merchant genuinely has a product the consumer wants at a better price, the agent should care. Advertising becomes economically useful when it reduces the cost of matching supply to demand. The problem is not commercial speech itself. It is whether the agent can distinguish information that benefits the buyer from influence that benefits the seller at the buyer’s expense.

This distinction may revive an old idea from marketing: the buying agent as a representative of the consumer rather than a channel owned by the seller.

In the 1990s, some thinkers imagined “reverse marketing” in which consumers would publish needs and companies would compete to fulfill them. The internet mostly developed in the opposite direction. Companies collected data about consumers and targeted offers toward them.

Agentic commerce makes reverse markets technically easier.

The consumer can issue a request: I need this outcome under these constraints. Qualified merchants respond. The buyer agent evaluates the bids.

This is closer to a request for proposal than a shopping mall.

Large organizations already buy this way because procurement teams can justify the process. Software lowers the overhead enough that households can do it for smaller purchases.

Imagine home broadband.

Instead of visiting provider sites, entering an address repeatedly, decoding promotional terms, and negotiating retention after the bill rises, the household agent publishes a constrained request: at least this speed, no long contract, total monthly cost under this threshold, installation before this date. Providers return machine-readable offers. The agent checks taxes, equipment, price-reset dates, and service history, then asks the household to choose if two offers are close.

Advertising in this market looks less like a billboard and more like a bid.

The seller has to improve the deal.

This shifts some marketing expenditure toward consumer surplus.

Instead of paying a platform ten dollars for a click, the merchant can put some of that ten dollars into the offer itself. Search advertising has always contained this tension. A company can spend money becoming visible or spend money lowering the price. When discovery becomes automated, visibility may be easier to commoditize, increasing pressure to compete on the offer.

Platforms will resist being disintermediated from the ad margin.

They can charge merchants for access to the request stream, take commissions on successful transactions, auction opportunities, or sell analytic tools. The consumer may save money while the platform still earns a toll.

The distribution of surplus depends on market structure.

If several agent platforms compete and merchants can reach them through open protocols, fees may remain constrained. If one assistant controls a large share of high-intent consumer demand, it can charge for access much as dominant marketplaces and app stores have charged for distribution.

Advertising becomes a tax on reaching delegated desire.

This is why openness at the protocol layer is not enough. A merchant can technically interoperate with every agent and still face a concentrated discovery market.

The parallel with search is instructive. The web was open. Any site could exist. Search still became a powerful gatekeeper because users did not visit the web uniformly. They began with a small number of discovery tools.

Agentic commerce can produce the same pattern at a more intimate layer.

The user says what she wants to one assistant.

The assistant decides which parts of the market deserve to answer.

The advertising auction migrates to that boundary.

Media companies face another consequence. Much of online publishing is funded because articles, reviews, and recommendations attract humans who can be monetized through ads, subscriptions, affiliate links, or commerce. If agents summarize those pages without sending visits, the economic loop weakens.

Product-review sites are especially exposed.

A human once searched “best cordless drill,” opened several review pages, saw display ads, clicked an affiliate link, and purchased. An agent can read or retrieve the underlying review information, compare it with other sources, and present the conclusion directly. The publisher contributes evidence but may lose the audience and commission.

This creates pressure for machine licensing, paid access, structured review feeds, or restrictions on automated use.

The agent needs an information ecosystem.

If it destroys the economics of producing that information, recommendation quality eventually suffers.

Commerce platforms may respond by funding data providers directly. Brands may sponsor independent testing, raising familiar conflict questions. Publishers may sell verified product knowledge to agents as a business-to-business service. Some will build their own agents to retain customer relationships.

Advertising does not disappear; it changes which institutions it subsidizes.

The same transition can affect creators. A fashion creator’s economic value may move from the click on a product link toward the preference she creates in the human. The agent executes afterward. Attribution becomes harder because the creator’s influence may be remembered by the user rather than encoded in the transaction.

Brands will develop ways to preserve attribution: saved collections, creator-specific product identifiers, preference imports, direct community memberships, and agent-readable recommendation lists. The commercial web will invent new breadcrumbs.

The most valuable advertising may become the advertising that changes the consumer’s standing instruction.

“Prefer this brand.”

“Try this category.”

“Never compromise on this feature.”

That influence happens at the human layer because the agent is supposed to protect the instruction once formed.

The ad industry therefore splits.

One branch markets to people, creating taste, memory, identity, and desire.

Another markets to agents, supplying offers, evidence, compatibility, availability, and price.

The first remains cultural.

The second becomes computational.

Companies that sell emotional goods will still want beautiful films, sponsorships, stores, creators, events, and stories. Companies selling comparable goods will increasingly want structured credibility and a competitive bid.

The ambiguous middle will spend money on both.

Regulators will have to decide what counts as an advertisement when the commercial influence is algorithmic. If a platform accepts payment that affects which merchants enter the candidate set, disclosure should not depend on whether a visible ad unit exists. If a commission changes the recommendation, that relationship is material. If the agent earns the same fee regardless of choice and ranking is genuinely independent, the conflict is smaller.

The law will chase architecture.

Consumers will mostly chase convenience.

That is why default norms matter early. People will not inspect the economics of every recommendation system. They will learn a simpler rule: this agent seems to work for me, or this agent seems to be selling to me.

Trust can become a competitive advantage.

A paid agent that explicitly refuses merchant influence may attract consumers with enough money to subscribe. A free agent can earn trust through clear separation and strong controls. A retailer-owned agent can admit that it searches only the retailer’s ecosystem. A comparison service can publish how commissions affect results.

The dangerous model is the one that claims neutral representation while quietly selling the route to the consumer.

That business has existed before.

The difference in the 2030s is that the route may begin with the consumer describing a private need to a system that knows the rest of her life.

Advertising has never stood closer to intention.

The consumer agent can turn that proximity into bargaining power.

Or it can rent the proximity back to the market.
