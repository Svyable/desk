# Before the Agent

The first shopping agent did not understand desire.

It understood compact discs.

In 1995, researchers at Andersen Consulting built BargainFinder, an early software agent that could visit online music stores, collect prices for the same CD, and return a comparison to the shopper. The idea was simple enough to sound inevitable: the internet contained many stores, computers were good at repetitive searching, so let a computer do the searching.

Some retailers blocked it.

That reaction is more revealing than the software itself.

The retailers understood that a customer who could compare them cheaply was a different customer. The web was supposed to give merchants new reach, but a comparison agent threatened to convert their carefully constructed stores into rows in a table. The shop’s design, promotion, and navigation could be bypassed. The merchant lost some ability to decide what the customer saw before the price.

Three decades later, the same argument is returning with vastly more capable machines.

To understand why the agentic consumer economy may be consequential, it helps to remember how many times digital commerce already promised to liberate the buyer from friction. Each wave delivered something real. Each also created a new place where power could accumulate.

The early commercial web reduced geographic search. A person in a small town could find a specialist seller across the country. A collector could browse inventories that would never fit in a local shop. A buyer looking for an obscure book did not have to hope the nearest store stocked it. Product variety exploded because shelf space became less binding.

Then search engines reduced navigational search. You no longer needed to know which sites existed before looking for a product. Type what you wanted, scan results, and follow links. Search became the map of the commercial web.

Price-comparison services reduced price search. They assembled offers from many sellers into a single interface. Researchers studying internet retail around the turn of the millennium found that online prices could be lower than conventional retail prices and that increased shopbot use could put pressure on prices and dispersion. The basic economic intuition held: lower search costs can make competition more intense.

But the “frictionless” market never arrived.

In one influential study of internet book and CD retailing, Erik Brynjolfsson and Michael Smith found substantial online price dispersion even for homogeneous products. Online retailers differed in price, and heavily branded sellers still commanded an advantage. In another study of consumers using a shopbot, Michael Smith and Brynjolfsson found that brand remained a meaningful predictor of which seller people chose. A shopper could see that another retailer charged less and still prefer the familiar name.

That did not make the comparison technology a failure. It exposed the things price comparison could not measure cleanly.

A book may be identical across sellers. The transaction is not. Will it arrive? Will the merchant misuse the card? What happens if the parcel is damaged? How annoying is customer service? Does the return process work? Is the listed item actually in stock? How much attention does the shopper want to spend checking these questions?

Brand compressed uncertainty.

The lesson should have restrained the grandest predictions about internet competition. Lowering one transaction cost reveals the value of the others.

Instead, the next twenty years produced a sequence of systems that each attacked a different friction.

Marketplaces reduced trust and logistics friction. A customer could buy from unfamiliar sellers because the marketplace standardized payment, reviews, dispute resolution, and fulfillment. The marketplace became a kind of institutional wrapper around strangers. The consumer did not need to establish trust with every merchant separately because the platform lent part of its own reputation to the transaction.

Recommendation systems reduced discovery friction. The problem of digital abundance was no longer finding any product; it was deciding which product among millions deserved attention. Amazon’s recommendations, Netflix’s personalization, music discovery systems, and later social feeds learned that ranking itself was a scarce service. The catalog grew larger, so the ability to order the catalog became more valuable.

Saved payment credentials reduced checkout friction. One-click purchasing, digital wallets, stored addresses, and standardized payment buttons turned a transaction that once required form filling into a thumbprint or glance. The merchant that already held a person’s credentials gained an advantage that looked trivial until shoppers abandoned competitors rather than re-enter twenty fields.

Mobile phones reduced temporal friction. Shopping moved into idle moments. A product seen on the train could be purchased on the train. A price could be checked while standing in a physical store. Location, camera, notifications, and saved identity turned the phone into a commercial sensor carried everywhere.

Social media reduced the distance between entertainment and demand. A person no longer needed to begin with a shopping intention. The product appeared inside the feed. Influence moved from the store shelf and search page into creators, communities, and algorithmic recommendations. The journey from “I did not know this existed” to “buy” shortened again.

Retail media then turned marketplaces themselves into advertising platforms. A seller that once paid to appear in Google search results increasingly paid to appear inside the marketplace where the transaction happened. The platform sat at the high-intent end of the funnel and could sell access to that moment.

At each stage, a consumer friction fell while a new intermediary became more important.

This pattern is easy to miss because digital markets often narrate themselves as disintermediation. The travel website removes the travel agent. The marketplace removes the distributor. The direct-to-consumer brand removes the retailer. The streaming service removes the video store. Yet the function of intermediation rarely disappears. It moves into software and often becomes more concentrated.

The human travel agent knew suppliers, routes, fares, visa requirements, and the preferences of regular clients. Online travel sites automated parts of that knowledge, then accumulated their own market power. Search engines removed the need for directories, then became directories with auctions attached. Social platforms let creators bypass publishers, then became the infrastructure through which audiences were discovered. App stores let software reach billions of users, then became tollgates for distribution.

The agentic consumer arrives at the end of this history with a dangerous inheritance: every earlier layer still exists.

It can search the open web. It can query structured product catalogs. It can use marketplace reputation. It can draw on social signals, professional reviews, customer ratings, price histories, shipping estimates, and the user’s own memory. It can hold payment credentials. It can call merchant APIs. It can potentially act across several platforms.

This breadth is what makes the new agent different from the old shopbot.

BargainFinder knew that one seller charged less for the same CD.

A modern agent can ask whether the cheaper seller is worth trusting, whether the product is compatible with something the household already owns, whether a used version is sensible, whether the warranty overlaps with card coverage, whether the delivery date matters, whether the brand’s return policy has changed, whether the user has unused loyalty credit, and whether buying at all is preferable to repairing, renting, borrowing, or waiting.

The comparison target is no longer a price.

It is an outcome.

That turns the agent from a search tool into a decision intermediary.

The distinction sounds philosophical until money moves. Search engines traditionally monetized by selling attention around a list of links. A decision intermediary can monetize the recommendation itself, the transaction, the payment, the merchant relationship, the data, the premium subscription, or some combination. The closer the intermediary moves to the final choice, the more valuable its position becomes.

This creates a problem that early comparison sites only hinted at. A price-comparison engine could claim to rank sellers by price and make that criterion visible. A general agent has to make judgments across dimensions that are not naturally commensurable.

How many dollars is a day of faster delivery worth?

How much should a poor return policy reduce a product’s ranking?

Should a local merchant receive preference over a distant one?

What probability of failure makes a cheaper appliance a worse deal?

How should the system treat environmental claims that are hard to verify?

Does the user’s historical preference for a brand represent genuine satisfaction or merely the fact that the brand has dominated her advertising exposure for fifteen years?

The agent will answer these questions even when nobody explicitly programs the answer. Ranking requires trade-offs. A system that says “best” has embedded a theory of best.

The web taught consumers to confuse ranking with reality.

Search results appeared in an order, and the order acquired authority because people did not inspect the entire web. Recommendation feeds intensified the effect. The user experienced a tiny sample selected by a machine and often forgot the size of the excluded universe. Agentic interfaces compress the sample further. A conversational assistant may present three products and an explanation rather than ten blue links or forty tiles.

Compression creates convenience and power at the same time.

The consumer sees less because the agent has seen more.

That can be a wonderful trade. Nobody wants to read five hundred listings for USB cables. But once delegated filtering becomes normal, the important economic question shifts from “Can my product be found?” to “Under what conditions will the agent choose to mention it?”

Search-engine optimization was built around a similar question. Merchants learned to make pages legible to crawlers and attractive to ranking systems. Advertising developed a parallel route for buying visibility. Social platforms created creator strategies and feed optimization. Marketplaces generated their own disciplines of titles, reviews, fulfillment scores, ad bids, conversion rates, and seller metrics.

Agentic commerce inherits all of these incentives and adds a new one: machine persuasion.

The phrase sounds like science fiction, but its first form is mundane product data.

A machine needs structured attributes. A human can look at a photograph and infer that a table is probably too wide for a small apartment. An agent works better when dimensions, materials, inventory, delivery constraints, compatibility, certifications, care instructions, and return terms are explicit and current. Commerce platforms in the mid-2020s began investing heavily in catalogs designed for AI systems because scraped web pages are an unreliable substrate for transactions.

A merchant that cannot describe itself to the agent may become commercially invisible even while its website looks beautiful to people.

This is the same transition the web produced when businesses discovered that a storefront hidden from search engines might as well be on a side street with no sign. But the agentic version reaches deeper. Search engines mainly needed enough information to rank a page. A purchasing agent may need enough information to complete the sale, calculate constraints, interpret the offer, and support post-purchase actions.

The merchant becomes partly a data service.

The consumer becomes partly a policy file.

Between them sits an agent trying to match one structured representation to another.

That sentence describes an economy that could become extremely efficient and extremely strange.

Consider grocery shopping. The twentieth-century supermarket was designed around human perception. End caps, shelf height, package color, aisle placement, coupons, music, lighting, and the smell of the bakery all shaped behavior. Loyalty cards added memory. Online grocery preserved many of the same merchandising assumptions in a grid of images and sponsored results.

An agent buying routine groceries can ignore most of this theater.

It might work from household inventory, meal plans, dietary constraints, preferred brands, price thresholds, and delivery fees. If the family always buys the same oats unless the price difference exceeds twenty percent, the agent can encode that rule. If the family cares strongly about one brand of coffee but not paper towels, the software can behave accordingly. The supermarket’s challenge becomes persuading the machine that a substitution is worthwhile or persuading the human to change the rule.

Some categories become less emotional when delegated.

Others become more emotional because the person reserves attention for them.

This is a recurring effect of automation: when routine activity disappears, the remaining human activity is not an average sample. It is what people chose not to automate. The supermarket of the future may sell commodity staples through agents while treating human browsing as a premium experience built around discovery, fresh food, prepared meals, social activity, and surprise.

Retail has seen this split before. E-commerce did not eliminate physical stores. It changed what stores had to be good at.

The agent will do the same to e-commerce itself.

The ordinary product page was designed for a person who needed persuasion and information in one place. It combined image, copy, price, reviews, availability, specifications, cross-sells, reassurance, and a path to payment. Once machines can gather those facts from structured sources, the page becomes less central for delegated transactions.

That does not mean the brand website vanishes. It becomes one surface among several: a human theater, a service portal, a trust anchor, a source of rich content, a community, and a machine endpoint. The transaction may begin elsewhere.

This migration is already visible whenever a person starts a purchase on a social platform, marketplace, search result, payment wallet, or AI chat rather than the merchant’s homepage. Agentic commerce extends the trend until the notion of “traffic” itself becomes ambiguous. If an agent queries a catalog, evaluates a product, and buys it without loading the merchant’s site, was there a visit?

Traditional digital commerce metrics begin to wobble.

Page views decline while transactions persist. Click-through rates lose meaning when there is no click. Attribution becomes a negotiation among systems. The merchant wants to know which agent generated the sale. The agent platform wants credit for discovery. The payment provider sees conversion. The commerce platform supplied the product data. An influencer or article may have shaped the user’s underlying preference days earlier. The human may simply say, “Get the one we liked last time.”

Marketing built around observable journeys struggles when the journey moves inside a model.

That is why the history before the agent matters. Every earlier digital transition created metrics that appeared natural until the next transition exposed their contingency. “Traffic” mattered when people visited websites. “Followers” mattered when feeds organized attention around accounts. “App installs” mattered when mobile distribution centered on installed software. “Search position” mattered when the search page was the front door.

The 2030s may care about representation inside the agent.

Not mindshare exactly. Not shelf space. Not search rank.

Preference eligibility.

Can the product satisfy the constraints the agent recognizes? Is the merchant trusted by the systems the agent consults? Are its attributes current? Is the total cost competitive? Does it offer machine-readable guarantees? Does it support the payment and identity rails the agent is allowed to use? Can it respond to a negotiated offer? Can it provide evidence strong enough for the agent to recommend it?

This moves competitive strategy from capturing attention toward satisfying delegated criteria.

That sounds like a cleaner market. It may be.

It also rewards whoever defines the criteria.

The early web’s shopbots were narrow enough that their politics were visible. A price table told you what it was doing. The modern agent aspires to something much harder: to know what the consumer would want if the consumer had the time, memory, expertise, and patience to investigate properly.

The better it becomes, the more authority it earns.

The more authority it earns, the more valuable it becomes to every seller trying to reach the person behind it.

This is the historical hinge.

The first digital consumer tools helped people navigate markets.

The agent begins to participate in the market on their behalf.
