# When Sellers Have Agents Too

The consumer agent sounds powerful until the merchant gets one.

Imagine a household agent asking three broadband providers for their best offer. It knows the current contract ends in twelve days. It knows the household uses roughly 700 gigabytes a month, that nobody needs the premium television bundle, that the family has tolerated two outages this year, and that one adult works from home often enough for reliability to matter more than a small discount. It asks each provider for a twelve-month price, all fees included, with no surprise increase after month six.

In the old market, this would already be an unusually prepared customer.

In the agentic market, the providers do not answer with static price sheets. Their own software reads the request, estimates the household's value, checks network utilization in the neighborhood, measures the probability of churn, considers competitor promotions, forecasts support costs, prices the risk of a future cancellation, and decides how far it can move. It may offer a discount if the household accepts autopay. It may trade a lower monthly charge for a longer commitment. It may bundle a mobile line the household did not ask for because the bundle changes the economics. It may decline to compete at all if the expected margin is too thin.

The buyer agent responds.

The seller agent responds again.

What looks like shopping has become a negotiation between two pieces of software representing opposed interests under human-defined constraints.

This is where the simple story of agentic consumer empowerment begins to break.

A great deal of early enthusiasm around consumer agents assumes that sellers remain approximately as they are today. The buyer becomes tireless while the merchant remains a product page. The buyer reads every term while the merchant publishes one price. The buyer searches every rival while the merchant waits patiently to be selected. Under those conditions, the consumer plainly gains leverage.

Markets rarely leave a profitable asymmetry undefended.

A retailer facing machine-mediated demand has strong incentives to become machine-mediated too. It will not want a general-purpose language model improvising every commercial decision. It will build or rent systems with explicit authority over price, promotion, inventory, credit, shipping, retention, merchandising, and customer service. These systems will have access to internal data a consumer agent does not possess. They will know the seller's real constraints. They will know which inventory is aging, which warehouse is congested, which customer segments are costly, which promotion budget is nearly exhausted, which products carry unusually high return rates, and which supplier agreement makes one substitute much more profitable than another.

The consumer arrives with memory.

The merchant arrives with the ledger.

This changes the meaning of negotiation. Human bargaining has always been limited by time, discomfort, etiquette, and attention. Most people will not send fifty rounds of offers over a washing machine. A salesperson cannot economically spend three hours negotiating a twelve-dollar household purchase. Software removes much of that limitation. Once both sides can bargain cheaply, negotiation can spread into markets where posted prices previously won because bargaining costs were larger than the possible gains.

That does not mean every purchase becomes a digital bazaar.

Posted prices have virtues that survive automation. They are easy to understand, fast to administer, and less likely to provoke resentment among customers who discover that someone else paid less. Retailers also use price as a public signal to coordinate inventory, promotion, and brand position. Many jurisdictions regulate discriminatory practices, and many merchants will decide that the reputational and operational cost of individualized bargaining exceeds the revenue.

But software permits something more subtle than haggling over the sticker price.

The negotiated object can become the whole transaction.

Delivery date. Return period. Warranty length. loyalty credit. financing terms. installation. service level. cancellation rights. bundle composition. data-sharing permission. subscription duration. preferred payment rail. replacement policy. carbon preference. the right to resell. the right to transfer. A seller can keep the visible price stable while allowing its agent to vary the rest.

That is a larger design space than most human consumers have ever had the energy to explore.

It is also a larger design space for discrimination.

The seller's advantage is not merely that it knows its own costs. It may know a great deal about the buyer. Digital commerce already supports behavioral targeting, dynamic promotion, customer scoring, and differentiated treatment. The Federal Trade Commission's 2025 surveillance-pricing study described intermediaries capable of using location, demographics, browsing behavior, shopping history, and even fine-grained interactions to tailor prices, promotions, or the products shown to particular consumers. The study did not establish that every imaginable practice was occurring everywhere, and its examples should not be inflated into a claim that individualized pricing already dominates retail. What it established was enough: the technical and commercial machinery for granular differentiation is real.

A seller agent can make that machinery conversational.

The buyer says, in effect, this is what I want.

The seller's system asks a different question: what is the most profitable agreement this particular buyer is likely to accept?

Those objectives are not the same.

A consumer agent can defend against some forms of price discrimination by widening the comparison set. If one hotel quotes an implausibly high rate, the agent can ask twenty others. If one insurer quietly raises a renewal premium, the agent can obtain competing quotes before the customer notices the letter. If one subscription service offers a retention discount only after a cancellation attempt, the agent can learn the path.

But comparison works only where alternatives are meaningful.

A tenant cannot search her way out of a local housing shortage. A rural household cannot compare twenty broadband providers if only one physical network reaches the address. A patient cannot bargain freely during an emergency. A traveler cannot choose among ten nonstop flights when only one airline operates the route. A software user cannot credibly threaten to leave a platform if years of data, relationships, or workflow are trapped inside it.

Agentic bargaining therefore magnifies the underlying structure of the market.

Where competition is healthy, an agent can make competition more usable.

Where competition is weak, the seller's agent may simply become a better extractor.

This is easy to miss because the same interface can appear in both cases. The consumer types a request. A polite assistant returns an offer. The economic difference sits underneath the conversation, in the number of credible outside options and the cost of taking them.

There is another complication. Seller agents may not behave independently even when their firms do.

Modern markets already contain shared pricing software, common cloud infrastructure, recommendation systems, data brokers, advertising exchanges, and third-party revenue-management tools. Two nominal competitors can buy optimization from the same vendor, train on similar market data, or respond to the same signals. The difficult antitrust question is not limited to a cartoon in which two bots explicitly agree to raise prices. Coordination can emerge through simpler mechanisms: common objectives, repeated interaction, rapid observation, synchronized responses, or shared tooling.

Economists and legal scholars have worried about algorithmic pricing for years because software can change the speed and observability of competitive reactions. The debate is not settled by saying algorithms are capable of collusion, nor by saying human firms have always watched competitors. The important question is institutional: which combinations of information, incentives, and repeated interaction make competitive undercutting less attractive?

Agentic commerce adds a new channel.

Seller agents may meet buyer agents millions of times a day. Each interaction produces information. Which consumers are price-sensitive? Which bundles work? How often do rival offers win? How much will a household pay for immediate delivery? What concession causes a buyer agent to switch? Markets learn from transactions; software markets can learn at unusual speed.

This could improve competition.

A merchant can discover that customers value a longer warranty more than a small discount and compete on warranty. A small seller can respond to demand in real time instead of guessing months in advance. Excess inventory can clear efficiently. Shipping capacity can be allocated to buyers who care most about speed while flexible buyers receive a discount. A local producer can quote directly to distant customers without maintaining a large sales department.

The same machinery can also make markets exhausting in a new way.

Today a consumer may distrust a price because she knows it can change tomorrow. In a fully negotiated market she may distrust the entire deal because she knows every dimension could have been different. Was the free shipping paid for through a worse return policy? Did the agent reveal too much urgency? Would asking from another identity have produced a different offer? Did the merchant infer willingness to pay from the very constraints supplied to help the consumer agent shop well?

A rational agent can investigate those questions.

A human may not want to live inside them.

The irony of automated negotiation is that its purpose is to remove bargaining from human attention while simultaneously creating more bargaining than humans would tolerate.

This is one reason protocols matter. If commerce becomes machine-to-machine, the structure of the message becomes part of market design. What does a buyer need to reveal? Can the agent ask for a quote without disclosing identity? Can a merchant condition a price on personal data? Is a quote binding? For how long? Can an agent prove that a user authorized a purchase without exposing unrelated information? Can the seller distinguish a legitimate delegated agent from a scraper, a fraud bot, or a competitor's intelligence operation?

Payment networks have already begun building for these questions. Mastercard's Agent Pay work, Google's Agent Payments Protocol, and related industry efforts focus on concepts such as registered agents, verifiable intent, tokenized credentials, and auditable authority. The immediate motivation is security. A merchant needs to know that software spending money is permitted to spend it.

But security architecture becomes economic architecture when it determines who may transact.

If only a handful of certified agent providers can present acceptable credentials, certification becomes a gate. If a payment network can identify the consumer agent, the agent can become a new object of risk scoring. If merchants can block classes of agents, they can potentially block the ones most effective at comparison or negotiation. If consumer agents can conceal identity until late in the transaction, sellers lose some ability to personalize but may increase prices to account for uncertainty.

Every privacy choice has a market consequence.

Consider a consumer agent that wants a hotel room.

In privacy-maximizing mode it might ask anonymously: queen room, downtown, Tuesday night, total price including mandatory fees, cancellation until 6 p.m. day of arrival.

In personalization mode it might reveal that the traveler is a frequent guest, holds a particular credit card, prefers a high floor, arrives at 9 p.m., usually buys breakfast, and has an early meeting nearby.

The second request can produce a better experience. It can also produce a more accurate estimate of willingness to pay.

The agent must decide whether context is bargaining power or bargaining exposure.

That is not a technical problem with one answer. It is a principal-agent problem. The consumer's software has to know when information helps the principal and when it helps the counterparty more.

A trustworthy consumer agent might therefore behave less like a cheerful shopping assistant and more like a competent negotiator. It would compartmentalize information. It would reveal preferences selectively. It would seek quotes before disclosing urgency where possible. It would distinguish hard constraints from soft preferences. It would remember past treatment by sellers. It would know when a loyalty identity creates value and when anonymity creates leverage.

This kind of behavior has a precedent in corporate procurement.

Companies do not normally give every supplier their full internal budget, alternative bids, production schedule, and maximum willingness to pay before requesting a quote. They disclose enough to make the transaction possible while preserving negotiating room. Households have rarely practiced this discipline because the overhead is absurd relative to ordinary purchases.

Again the consumer acquires a corporate capability.

Again the capability creates a counter-response.

Sellers can design mechanisms specifically for agents. A merchant might publish a guaranteed best-price API available only to authenticated consumer agents, avoiding negotiation costs in exchange for verified purchase intent. Another might run sealed-bid auctions for perishable capacity. A manufacturer might offer lower prices to agents that accept slower fulfillment, reducing logistics peaks. A subscription service might expose a machine-readable cancellation and retention interface rather than forcing an agent to imitate a human clicking through a website.

The best version of this future is not endless combat.

It is lower transaction cost.

Two agents can discover that the buyer values flexibility and the seller values predictability, then construct a deal that gives each side more of what it wants. Economists have always understood that gains from trade can come from differences in preferences, timing, risk tolerance, information, and constraints. Human negotiation captures only some of those gains because negotiation itself is expensive.

Software can search the contract space.

Suppose a family is buying an appliance. The family cares strongly about installation on Saturday, moderately about price, little about brand, and is willing to accept a cosmetic blemish on a side that will face a wall. The retailer has a returned unit with damaged packaging, an underused installation crew on Saturday morning, and excess inventory of one model. A static storefront may never connect these facts. Agentic negotiation can.

The transaction becomes cheaper for the family and more profitable for the retailer than the obvious alternative.

This is not consumer victory over producer.

It is coordination.

The distinction matters because much public discussion of digital markets assumes that every dollar of producer margin is evidence of consumer harm. Sometimes margin finances service, innovation, resilience, or simply the existence of the supplier. A world in which buyer agents relentlessly reduce every seller to commodity economics could lower prices in the short run while reducing variety or investment later. A world in which seller agents perfectly extract every buyer's willingness to pay could increase output in some markets while transferring too much surplus away from households.

The desirable outcome is neither side possessing perfect power.

It is contestability.

A buyer should be able to seek alternatives without unreasonable friction. A seller should be able to reach buyers without paying a permanent toll to one gatekeeper. Agents should be able to interoperate without handing one platform the entire commercial memory of the user. Deals should remain understandable enough that legal responsibility survives automation. Markets should preserve room for entry, surprise, and switching.

Those conditions sound abstract until agents begin acting at machine speed.

Then tiny design decisions compound.

A default that lets agents request three quotes instead of one changes competitive pressure. A rule that requires the buyer to reveal identity before seeing a price changes information asymmetry. A protocol that makes returns machine-readable changes the effective price of quality. A payment standard that supports spending limits changes how much autonomy users can safely delegate. A merchant policy that blocks automated comparison changes the practical meaning of a public price.

The agentic market is made of these small rules.

The seller's agent will be better funded than the buyer's in many cases. A large retailer can employ teams of economists, engineers, fraud specialists, and marketers. It can train on billions of interactions. The ordinary household may use a general-purpose assistant designed for millions of unrelated tasks.

But the household agent has one structural advantage.

It can work for the same principal across markets.

The supermarket knows grocery behavior. The airline knows flights. The bank knows finances. The streaming company knows viewing. The general consumer agent can, with permission, understand how all of these choices compete inside one life. It can know that the family should not accept a cheap annual subscription because cash is tight this month, that a premium flight is worthwhile because it avoids a missed workday, that a cheaper refrigerator is false economy because repair history is poor, and that no amount of points justifies staying with a card whose annual fee no longer earns its keep.

The seller knows the transaction.

The consumer agent can know the budget.

That may become the deepest bargaining advantage on the demand side.

It also explains why companies will fight to become the consumer's primary agent. The system that holds the cross-market context can negotiate from a richer model of value than any single merchant. It can decide which purchase is not merely cheapest but fits the household's broader objectives.

And so the contest between buyer agents and seller agents points upward toward another contest.

Who controls the agent representing the buyer?

A merchant can optimize against a customer. A marketplace can optimize across merchants. A consumer agent can optimize across marketplaces. The layer with the broadest view sits closest to the person's intention.

By the 2030s, the most important commercial negotiation may not be the one between a buyer bot and a seller bot over five dollars.

It may be the negotiation over which bot gets to speak for the buyer in the first place.
