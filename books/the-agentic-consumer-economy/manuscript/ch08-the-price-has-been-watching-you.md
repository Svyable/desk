# The Price Has Been Watching You

For most of retail history, the price watched the market.

The grocer knew what competitors charged. The airline watched demand. The hotel raised rates during a convention. The merchant discounted winter coats in spring and marked up scarce goods when supply tightened. Price was never static, but its movements were usually explained by something outside the individual shopper.

Digital commerce made the individual observable.

A retailer can know where a shopper is, which page brought her in, what device she uses, what she bought before, which products she considered, how often she returns, what sits in her cart, whether she responds to coupons, and how quickly she tends to buy. The company may not use every signal to set the final price, and legal constraints vary by market, but the technical capacity to tailor offers around individual behavior is no longer speculative.

The Federal Trade Commission gave the practice a blunt name: surveillance pricing.

In 2024 it ordered information from several companies involved in pricing and personalization. Its initial staff findings, released in early 2025, described a market in which intermediaries could use detailed consumer data—from location and demographics to browsing patterns and shopping behavior—to help retailers target prices or promotions. Even interactions such as leaving a product in a cart could become inputs.

The phrase matters because “personalization” sounds like a service.

Sometimes it is.

A student discount is personalized pricing. A loyalty coupon is personalized pricing. An insurer charging less for lower risk is, in a broad sense, personalized pricing. A merchant offering a discount to a customer likely to switch can create a deal both sides prefer. A hotel lowering the price for a flexible traveler rather than leaving a room empty can increase welfare. Price discrimination is not automatically exploitation; economics has long recognized circumstances in which it expands access or helps firms cover fixed costs.

The discomfort arrives when the seller knows the buyer’s willingness to pay better than the buyer knows the market.

A person searches for a last-minute flight to a funeral. An urgent medical need appears in browsing behavior. A household repeatedly visits a product page after payday. A consumer has few local alternatives. The data can reveal not merely preference but vulnerability.

If the merchant can estimate the maximum each person will tolerate, the old public price becomes less important.

The market begins to ask a private question: how much can we get from you?

Agentic consumers enter this world as both defense and temptation.

The defense is straightforward. A buyer agent can remember public prices, compare merchants, calculate total cost, monitor changes, hide some urgency, and reject offers inconsistent with the user’s rules. It can approach a transaction with the same analytical patience the seller already possesses.

The temptation is that the agent itself contains a better estimate of willingness to pay than almost any retailer has ever seen.

It knows the budget, preferences, calendar, prior purchases, alternatives considered, and perhaps the emotional reason behind the purchase. If that context leaks into the commercial negotiation, personalized pricing becomes extraordinarily precise.

The agentic consumer economy therefore creates a new principle of bargaining: useful private context should not automatically become seller context.

A human buyer understands this instinctively in some settings. When negotiating for a house, she does not begin by telling the seller the absolute maximum she can afford and that her children have already fallen in love with the backyard. When buying a used car, she may conceal urgency. Bargaining involves strategic information.

Online commerce trained consumers to reveal information casually because interfaces did not feel like negotiations.

The agent can restore strategic silence.

Suppose a household wants a washing machine. The agent may know that the current machine has failed, laundry is accumulating, the family can spend up to nine hundred dollars, and delivery within forty-eight hours is highly valuable. The merchant does not need all of this. It needs the requested features, delivery destination, and perhaps an invitation to make an offer under a stated ceiling.

A well-designed agent can disclose constraints without disclosing desperation.

This is the buyer-side equivalent of data minimization.

It also changes the economics of posted prices.

Retailers have historically used public prices for many reasons beyond telling customers what something costs. A posted price reduces negotiation overhead. It signals fairness. It coordinates employees. It lets buyers compare. It protects the merchant from having to bargain over every transaction. Mass retail was built partly on the social efficiency of the fixed price.

Agentic commerce lowers negotiation overhead again.

If software can handle millions of individualized offers at negligible administrative cost, merchants can return to bargaining without hiring millions of salespeople.

The posted price becomes an opening state.

This could produce a renaissance of coupons, bids, retention offers, bundles, and conditional discounts. The system may ask merchants to compete for a specific buyer rather than forcing the buyer to search public shelves.

A consumer agent could send a request to five broadband providers. Each returns a twelve-month total cost, equipment fee, installation date, and renewal rule. One provider offers a lower price if the customer accepts self-installation. Another offers a loyalty credit if the user brings a mobile line too. The agent compares everything on the same basis.

This is personalized pricing that can benefit the consumer because sellers compete for the individual.

The danger appears when only one side possesses the market view.

If a dominant platform runs the buyer agent and also controls access to sellers, it can know every bid, every consumer threshold, and every conversion outcome. That information is commercially explosive. The platform can optimize commissions, design auctions, steer offers, or become a seller itself.

The institution meant to represent the buyer can become the market maker.

Market makers can create enormous efficiency.

They can also extract enormous rents.

The history of travel booking, online marketplaces, app stores, and ad exchanges offers enough examples to avoid assuming neutrality.

Price agents will need rules about conflicts of interest.

A buyer-side system should not secretly raise the acceptable price because a merchant pays a higher commission. It should not reveal a user’s maximum budget when a lower market price is available. It should distinguish a discount from a financing arrangement that costs more over time. It should account for taxes, shipping, fees, renewals, consumables, and cancellation penalties.

The total price becomes the natural unit of comparison.

This sounds obvious, yet digital commerce repeatedly profits from fragmenting price. Airlines separate seat selection, bags, priority boarding, and flexibility. Hotels have used resort fees. Delivery services add service charges and tips. Ticketing systems have historically added fees late in checkout. Subscriptions advertise monthly equivalents while billing annually. Mobile plans bundle device credits with service commitments. “Free” products monetize data or future lock-in.

The difference between a hidden fee and an individualized price is important. They are often discussed together because both make comparison harder, but they are different problems. A hidden mandatory fee means the consumer was not shown the actual price clearly enough. An individualized price means the consumer may be shown the actual price, but a different buyer could see a different one. A coordinated price means ostensibly competing sellers may not be making sufficiently independent pricing decisions. An agent has to understand which problem it is confronting before it can help.

The United States began drawing a firmer line around the first problem in 2025. The Federal Trade Commission’s Rule on Unfair or Deceptive Fees took effect on May 12 of that year for live-event tickets and short-term lodging. In those covered markets, a business displaying a price generally has to show the total price including mandatory fees it knows and can calculate upfront, with limited exclusions such as government charges, shipping, and optional add-ons. The rule does not ban fees and does not ban dynamic pricing. It changes what must be visible when the price is presented.

That distinction is almost designed for machine comparison.

A ticket agent does not need the government to decide whether a front-row seat should cost four hundred dollars. It benefits enormously from the government requiring the four-hundred-dollar offer to mean approximately the same thing across sellers before taxes and genuinely optional extras. A market can remain dynamic while becoming more legible.

In April 2026 the FTC announced a $10 million consumer-refund settlement with StubHub over allegations that the company had continued to advertise ticket prices without including mandatory fees after the rule took effect. The significance for agentic commerce is not the size of the settlement. It is that “total price” is becoming an enforceable data concept in a market where software already does much of the comparison.

Regulation and agents can reinforce each other. Regulation makes the fields cleaner. Agents make noncompliance easier to detect because they compare the fields at scale.

A rule that helps a human avoid surprise at checkout also helps a machine reject an offer whose apparent price cannot be reconciled with its mandatory charges.

This is one reason machine-readable consumer law may become more important. A disclosure that satisfies a lawyer because it appears somewhere in a page of text can still be difficult for an agent to incorporate reliably. A structured field stating the mandatory total, renewal date, cancellation rule, financing cost, or return condition is both a technical convenience and a form of consumer protection.

The best regulation will not tell the agent what to choose. It will make the choice space harder to falsify.

Human consumers are bad at integrating scattered costs under time pressure.

Agents are built for it.

The regulatory push toward upfront total-price disclosure and the technological push toward agentic comparison therefore point in the same direction. A machine-readable market works better when fees are explicit.

Sellers that depend on drip pricing face a hostile counterparty.

That does not mean they stop trying.

Commercial complexity can be designed faster than regulation can simplify it. A merchant can change bundle structures, loyalty benefits, financing terms, delivery subscriptions, memberships, warranties, and conditional rebates. If agents become good at comparing one structure, sellers can differentiate on another.

This is an old dynamic in financial products. Regulation standardizes disclosure; firms innovate around the disclosure; consumers face a new decision surface. Agentic systems can accelerate both sides.

The buyer agent becomes a permanent financial analyst of ordinary commerce.

It can annualize the price. It can estimate the probability that a rebate will actually be redeemed. It can model the cost of a subscription if cancellation is forgotten—then remember to cancel. It can value points at a realistic redemption rate rather than the number printed in an email. It can notice that a “free” phone requires a more expensive plan for three years. It can compare cash price with financing without being seduced by a monthly payment.

These capabilities are especially valuable because price complexity is regressive in attention. The people who can least afford a bad fee are often the people with the least time to study every contract.

An agent can narrow that gap if access is broad.

If high-quality agents are expensive, the opposite occurs.

Wealthy households gain tireless fee avoidance, tax optimization, price monitoring, and negotiation. Lower-income households continue to face ordinary interfaces and targeted pricing with fewer defenses. The market becomes more efficient for people who can afford representation.

This is the consumer equivalent of professional tax planning.

Societies accept some differences in service quality. They may be less comfortable when the difference determines whether a person consistently pays more for the same necessities.

Public-interest consumer agents could emerge for this reason. Banks, credit unions, employers, unions, governments, libraries, or nonprofit organizations could provide tools that monitor recurring expenses, compare essential services, and detect predatory terms. The software does not need to shop for luxury goods to create welfare. It can focus on utilities, communications, insurance, transportation, banking, food, and benefits.

The highest social return may come from boring categories again.

Price history becomes another form of consumer memory.

Sellers have long studied elasticity: how demand responds when price changes. The consumer rarely runs the inverse analysis: how often does this seller discount, what is the ordinary price, and is today’s promotion actually unusual?

An agent can.

The result can weaken fake urgency. A “thirty percent off” banner means less if the system knows the product has sold at that price every weekend for two months. A countdown timer becomes irrelevant if the agent can wait for the next cycle. A loyalty deal can be compared against the price offered to new customers.

Retailers will adapt by making promotions more individualized and less predictable.

The agent will respond by learning the patterns.

This is where consumer commerce starts to resemble financial market microstructure. Both sides run software. Both sides observe behavior. Offers can change quickly. Strategy depends on what the other side knows.

The analogy has limits. People do not buy refrigerators at microsecond speed, and most consumer goods are not perfectly fungible securities. But the strategic direction is similar: once machines act on both sides, information advantages get competed away faster.

Sellers may stop exposing discounts broadly if agents instantly arbitrage them. Consumers may stop revealing exact thresholds if merchants price against them. Platforms may design auctions to manage the interaction.

Prices become messages between algorithms.

This raises a competition concern beyond individualized pricing: coordination among sellers.

The RealPage litigation in the United States brought public attention to algorithmic pricing in rental housing. The Justice Department’s case alleged that RealPage and participating landlords used competitors’ competitively sensitive, nonpublic information in revenue-management systems and adopted practices that aligned pricing. The details matter because the antitrust concern was not simply that landlords used software or adjusted rents dynamically. The concern was whether competitors were making independent pricing decisions when the system was trained on and informed by shared competitive data.

That case kept moving after the initial complaint. In November 2025 the Justice Department filed a proposed settlement with RealPage that would restrict the use of competitors’ sensitive information and practices designed to align pricing. Settlements with landlord defendants followed, including a final judgment with Greystar in March 2026 and a proposed settlement with Willow Bridge in July 2026. Those agreements were resolutions of specific legal claims, not a general declaration that common pricing software is unlawful.

The narrower lesson is more useful.

Algorithmic pricing has at least two different competition stories. One is a firm using its own information to react more intelligently to demand. The other is a group of competitors feeding or receiving information through a common system in ways that can weaken independent rivalry. The first can be ordinary competition. The second can become an antitrust problem depending on facts that no slogan about “AI pricing” resolves.

Consumer agents will need the same precision.

If a household sees identical high offers from five sellers, the agent should not assume collusion. The products may share a wholesale cost. Capacity may be scarce. A public event may have shifted demand. But the agent can preserve the observation, compare it with history, and make concentrated or suspiciously synchronized markets easier to study.

An economy full of buyer agents produces a new source of competition evidence: millions of systematic records of what was offered, when, under which constraints, and by whom. That could help consumers. It could also become commercially sensitive data of its own. The aggregator that sees the whole market acquires power again.

The information advantage keeps moving upward.

Imagine thousands of merchants using a small number of commercial agents that continuously watch competitors, adjust offers, and decide when to discount. Even without explicit collusion, the market can become more synchronized. If systems share data or optimize toward similar objectives, regulators will have to distinguish legitimate automation from anticompetitive coordination.

The consumer agent is both beneficiary and participant.

It may discover lower prices in a competitive market. It cannot create competition where supply is concentrated.

If three airlines control the relevant route and all are full, the agent’s tireless search does not invent a fourth seat. If one broadband provider serves the address, comparison is theater. If a patented medicine has no substitute, better shopping cannot solve monopoly.

This is a useful limit on the mythology of empowered consumers.

Search power is not market power when there is nowhere else to go.

Agentic commerce can make this fact more visible. A system can tell the user, “I checked every available provider; there is no meaningful alternative.” That information may support political pressure for competition, regulation, or public provision. It does not lower the bill by itself.

The strongest consumer benefits appear where alternatives exist but friction prevents people from using them.

That is a large part of the economy.

The agent can turn latent competition into effective competition by making switching credible.

Sellers will still practice price discrimination because differences in willingness to pay are economically real and sometimes useful. Airlines will not charge every passenger the same fare. Hotels will vary prices with demand. Promotions will target particular customers. Subscription companies will make retention offers. Luxury brands will protect price architecture. Local services will quote based on circumstances.

The goal is not a world with one universal price.

It is a world where the buyer has a representative capable of understanding the deal.

Fairness becomes less about sameness and more about agency.

Did the consumer know the relevant terms? Could she compare alternatives? Was sensitive context exploited unfairly? Did the agent reveal information it should have protected? Was the offer discriminatory on a prohibited basis? Did the platform steer the transaction for its own benefit? Can the decision be reconstructed afterward?

These are the price questions of delegated commerce.

The public price will survive because it remains useful.

But around it will grow a private market of bids, discounts, rules, thresholds, and negotiations conducted by machines.

The price has been watching the consumer for years.

The consumer is about to watch back.
