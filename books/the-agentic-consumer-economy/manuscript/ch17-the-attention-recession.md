# The Attention Recession

Advertising has always been a tax on not knowing exactly what you want.

That is too harsh as a complete definition and useful as a starting point.

A person who knows the exact make, model, size, seller, and acceptable price of an object needs very little persuasion. She may need information that the product exists or that it is available nearby. She may need reassurance that the seller is legitimate. But most of the machinery of modern advertising becomes unnecessary once the preference is stable, the product is specified, and comparison is cheap.

The advertising industry became enormous because human preferences are rarely that tidy.

People do not wake with fully formed demand for every purchase they will make. They discover products. They imitate friends. They notice fashions. They become curious. They get bored. They encounter a problem and do not know the category of product that solves it. They are moved by status, fear, humor, beauty, aspiration, anxiety, novelty, convenience, repetition, and sometimes by a straightforward demonstration that something works.

The commercial internet learned to industrialize these moments.

Search advertising caught people when intention was already visible. Social advertising tried to create or redirect intention earlier. Retail media placed paid influence close to the shelf. Influencers folded promotion into entertainment and identity. Affiliate marketing paid intermediaries for steering demand. Recommendation systems blurred the line between discovery and merchandising. Everywhere attention gathered, a market formed around the possibility of changing what the attentive person would do next.

Consumer agents interrupt that sequence.

Not completely. But enough to threaten a great many business models.

Suppose a household agent is told to keep the kitchen stocked with a list of ordinary products under a monthly budget, substituting only when quality and dietary constraints are preserved. The agent does not need to see a cereal commercial. It does not become hungry after midnight. It does not linger over a sponsored photograph. It does not feel social pressure because a famous person is holding a detergent bottle. If a brand wants to win that purchase, the useful levers are different: price, measured quality, availability, delivery, returns, preference fit, trust, sustainability claims the system can verify, or whatever other criteria the household has authorized.

For that purchase, attention is no longer the scarce input.

The person has delegated the attention.

If delegation spreads, parts of consumer capitalism experience something like an attention recession: not a collapse in total human attention, but a decline in the amount of human attention available for routine commercial capture.

The phrase matters because digital advertising is built on a peculiar abundance and scarcity at once. Humans produce hours of attention every day, but any one advertiser receives almost none of it. Platforms organize the scarcity, auction access, measure reactions, and return a fraction of the revenue to creators or publishers whose content attracted the audience.

An agent can remove the audience from the transaction.

The consumer still buys the shoes. The merchant still sells the shoes. But the person may never visit the page where the advertisement would have appeared.

This is not science fiction in embryo. Commerce platforms in 2025 and 2026 began constructing exactly the infrastructure required for purchases to occur inside AI surfaces rather than on merchant websites. OpenAI and Stripe's Agentic Commerce Protocol supported checkout within ChatGPT. Google and Shopify introduced the Universal Commerce Protocol as an open standard spanning discovery through checkout. Shopify described product catalogs syndicated into AI channels, with merchants becoming discoverable in conversations where a conventional storefront might never be loaded. Amazon's Buy for Me demonstrated a different route: an established shopping platform using agentic software to complete purchases on external merchant sites.

These systems are early, partial, and subject to change. The important structural fact is simpler.

The webpage is no longer guaranteed to be the place where commerce happens.

That has consequences far beyond retail design.

A large share of the web was financed on the assumption that human visits could be monetized. A review site ranked vacuum cleaners and earned affiliate fees when readers clicked through. A travel publisher attracted people researching destinations and sold ads beside the advice. A recipe site wrapped a useful answer in display advertising. A search engine returned links and auctioned placement above them. A creator recommended products inside entertainment. A newspaper supplemented subscriptions with advertising because readers were present anyway.

What happens when the consumer says to an agent, “Find a reliable vacuum for a house with two dogs under three hundred dollars,” and the agent synthesizes the available evidence without sending the person through the pages that produced it?

The consumer saves time.

The information economy may lose a payment event.

This is one of the central distributional problems of agentic consumption. A system can make commercial information more useful to the consumer while weakening the institutions that financed the creation, testing, reporting, photography, reviews, and communities from which the information came.

The problem is familiar from earlier digital transitions. Search engines made information easier to find while capturing a large share of commercial discovery. Social platforms made publication easy while centralizing distribution. Streaming made music and video more convenient while rearranging how creators were paid. Generative systems add another layer because the interface can answer without visibly transferring the user to the underlying source.

Agentic commerce intensifies the problem by attaching money directly to the answer.

A recommendation can become a transaction.

Once that happens, the question of who is paid for influencing the recommendation becomes unavoidable.

Advertising will not disappear. Capitalism has never been that considerate.

It will move.

The obvious destination is the agent itself.

A merchant can pay for inclusion, prominence, enhanced data, special offers, preferred fulfillment, or some other advantage inside the system that constructs the recommendation. The commercial vocabulary may differ from advertising because the interface is conversational and the product may be purchased without a visible ad unit. Economically, the key issue is whether payment changes the probability of selection.

This distinction is more important than whether a little box says Sponsored.

Imagine three arrangements.

In the first, a consumer agent searches the market according to the user's criteria, produces its ranking independently, and then shows a clearly separate sponsored offer after the ranking. The ad buys attention but not the recommendation.

In the second, sponsored products are eligible for a larger or faster candidate set. Payment changes which products the system seriously considers.

In the third, merchants bid directly for the probability that the agent will recommend them, subject to constraints about relevance and quality.

All three can be described as advertising.

They create very different agents.

The third system begins to resemble an auctioneer wearing the clothes of a fiduciary.

Consumers have lived with conflicted intermediaries before. Travel agents received commissions. Brokers sold products with differing compensation. Comparison websites accepted referral fees. App stores ranked paid placements. Credit-card rewards altered payment incentives. The old lesson is not that compensated recommendation is inherently corrupt. It is that incentives must be legible enough for users and regulators to understand whose interest the intermediary is serving at each moment.

Agentic systems make legibility harder because the recommendation can be generated rather than selected from an obvious list.

A human may ask, “What is the best carry-on for me?” and receive three paragraphs of apparently personalized reasoning. If commercial payments affected the candidate set, the ranking, the evidence considered, or the phrasing, where should disclosure live? Before the answer? Beside the product? In a transaction log? In a machine-readable record another agent can audit?

The traditional ad disclosure was designed for an eyeball.

The agentic disclosure may need to be designed for another machine.

This becomes especially important if consumer agents themselves learn to filter persuasion. A user might instruct an agent to exclude paid recommendations, or to show them only when the paid offer is cheaper than the best organic option, or to disclose the expected commission in dollars. A regulator might require platforms to expose when compensation influenced ranking. Independent auditing agents could compare recommendations across paid and unpaid modes.

Advertising then becomes partially adversarial software.

The seller's systems search for ways to become legible and attractive to buyer agents. The buyer's systems search for ways to separate genuine value from paid influence. Platforms write the rules governing what counts as acceptable influence. Regulators try to distinguish merchandising from deception. This is not the end of marketing. It is marketing moving from psychology toward protocol design, data quality, reputation, offer construction, and machine-readable evidence.

Human psychology does not leave the picture.

It moves up the funnel.

An agent can select among products according to a person's preferences, but it cannot necessarily decide what the person wants her life to mean. A teenager may ask for shoes from the brand his friends wear. A homeowner may insist on the appliance associated with reliability because her parents bought it. A traveler may choose the beautiful hotel despite a worse objective value score. A collector may pay for provenance. A gift buyer may want the object whose story carries affection.

Brands that survive agentic optimization may become more cultural, not less.

If machines commoditize functional comparison, meaning becomes one of the dimensions humans retain.

This produces a split in advertising.

Performance advertising aimed at squeezing a click from a routine purchase could weaken as agents absorb the transaction. Cultural advertising aimed at shaping desire, identity, and category preference may remain powerful because the principal still supplies the values the agent optimizes.

A detergent company may struggle to persuade a procurement agent with a cinematic commercial. A luxury house may still persuade the human to tell the agent, “Only show me this brand.”

The brand has bypassed the buyer agent by modifying the buyer's objective function.

That is an extraordinary sentence and an old business practice.

Advertising has always tried to change preferences before the purchase. Agentic commerce simply makes the separation between preference formation and purchase execution easier to see.

The largest platforms may therefore compete for both layers.

One layer knows what the person watches, reads, searches, says, photographs, and asks. Another layer executes purchases. If the same firm controls both, it can observe preference formation and monetize fulfillment. The economic attraction is obvious. So is the regulatory concern.

A platform that controls attention and agency can close the commercial loop.

It can show the aspiration, infer the desire, recommend the product, process the payment, track delivery, and remember the outcome. The traditional marketing funnel becomes one database.

This is the dream of vertical integration in digital capitalism.

It is also why consumer-agent competition matters. If people can choose independent agents, move their preferences and histories, and route purchases across multiple providers, commercial influence remains contestable. If the agent is welded to the operating system, identity account, payment wallet, and dominant content platform, switching becomes harder precisely as the agent becomes more useful.

The better it remembers you, the more expensive leaving can become.

An attention recession also affects creators in less obvious ways.

Many small creators depend on product discovery as a revenue stream. A photographer reviews cameras. A mechanic explains tools. A parent compares strollers. A runner tests shoes. Their economic value is not merely that they place affiliate links. They create trust through repeated visible judgment. A consumer agent may use their work as evidence while intercepting the conversion.

If that pattern becomes widespread, new compensation systems will be necessary or the supply of high-quality evidence may deteriorate.

One possibility is licensing. Agent platforms pay publishers, data providers, or expert communities for structured access.

Another is attribution-based commerce. A machine recommendation carries provenance and revenue shares to sources that materially contributed to the purchase.

Another is subscription. Consumers pay directly for trusted information because advertising becomes less effective.

Another is merchant-funded referral, reconstructed in machine-readable form.

And another is the familiar ugly equilibrium: enormous amounts of low-cost, commercially motivated content designed primarily to be ingested by agents.

Search-engine optimization becomes agent optimization.

The web has seen this movie before.

Once ranking creates economic value, people optimize for the ranking. Product descriptions become machine-targeted. Reviews are generated at scale. Merchants structure claims around whatever fields agents privilege. Reputation services emerge to certify data. Fraudsters manufacture signals. Platforms change ranking methods. Everyone complains that the old system was better, forgetting what they complained about in the old system.

The first generation of agent optimization will probably look embarrassingly mechanical in retrospect. Businesses will stuff product feeds with attributes, publish elaborate machine-readable FAQs, solicit structured reviews, and chase whatever citation patterns appear correlated with recommendations. Some practices will genuinely improve data quality. Others will become a new species of spam.

The distinction depends on whether the information improves the consumer's decision or merely manipulates the intermediary.

That is the same distinction consumer policy has wrestled with in human interfaces. The OECD's work on dark commercial patterns documented how online design can steer, deceive, nag, hide information, create false urgency, and exploit default effects. In 2024, an international review cited by the OECD found possible dark patterns across a large majority of examined commercial websites and apps. The figures do not imply that every pattern caused harm in every instance, but they show how much commercial effort is already spent shaping the architecture of choice rather than simply competing on product quality and price.

Agents can neutralize some of these tactics.

A countdown timer does not make software panic.

A prechecked box can be noticed automatically.

A buried fee can be added to total cost.

A difficult cancellation flow can be traversed without ruining an afternoon.

This is a genuine consumer benefit.

But manipulative design does not vanish. It moves from interface design to agent design.

The new dark pattern may be a default recommendation policy. It may be an undisclosed merchant exclusion. It may be a memory setting that quietly makes a user more dependent on the platform. It may be an “optimization” objective that weights platform revenue alongside consumer welfare. It may be a consent request whose consequences are too complex for a normal person to understand because the permission governs thousands of future autonomous decisions.

The visible screen becomes cleaner while the invisible choice architecture becomes more important.

Consumer protection will have to follow it.

This is a recurring theme of the agentic economy: the removal of human friction does not remove power. It can relocate power to defaults, protocols, permissions, rankings, and data access.

The attention recession may even increase the value of the remaining human attention.

If agents handle routine replenishment, subscriptions, travel rebooking, insurance comparison, and basic shopping, then the purchases still demanding direct human attention are unusual. They are new categories, identity goods, expensive commitments, gifts, experiences, and decisions where the user wants to enjoy the process.

A merchant that reaches a human in those moments has reached someone whose attention is less cluttered by administrative commerce.

The premium on genuine attention could rise.

Retail may polarize between invisible procurement and theatrical shopping.

At one end, commodities flow quietly through household agents: batteries, printer ink, recurring medicines where permitted, cleaning products, cloud storage, basic travel, routine insurance renewals, spare parts. The customer sees exceptions.

At the other end, brands invest in experiences worth human presence: stores, communities, events, craftsmanship, tastings, demonstrations, stories, prestige, entertainment, belonging.

The middle—the endless scroll of moderately differentiated products fighting for a click—may be the vulnerable zone.

If that happens, the advertising industry will not shrink uniformly. It will reallocate from capturing transactions toward influencing the preferences that agents later execute.

The political consequence deserves attention.

Advertising finances more than commerce. It finances journalism, entertainment, social platforms, search, maps, email, free apps, creator economies, and a large portion of the public internet. A technological system that improves consumer welfare by suppressing commercial attention can simultaneously weaken cross-subsidies that fund noncommercial information.

There is no law saying the money saved from detergent advertising will migrate to local reporting.

Markets do not preserve useful institutions automatically when their old revenue mechanism breaks.

This is why the agentic consumer economy should not be evaluated only at checkout. A five-dollar household saving may be real while a broader information ecosystem loses revenue. That does not mean the inefficient ad should be preserved as a public subsidy. It means transition accounting must include second-order effects.

The internet spent thirty years making attention measurable, tradable, auctionable, targetable, and retargetable.

Consumer agents introduce the possibility that a growing share of buying power travels through the economy without dragging human attention behind it.

For consumers, this can feel like liberation.

For merchants, it can feel like losing the storefront.

For publishers, it can feel like losing the visitor.

For platforms, it is an invitation to build a new auction inside the agent.

The 2030s may therefore contain an odd contradiction. People could experience fewer advertisements during routine commerce even as commercial competition over the software representing them becomes more intense.

The ad does not disappear.

It moves to the place where the decision is made.
