# The Customer Who Never Sleeps

In the spring of 2026, the future of shopping looked surprisingly administrative.

There was no robot walking the aisles. No household had surrendered its bank account to a chrome-plated butler. The visible changes were smaller: a new checkout button in a chat window, a product catalog exposed through an API, a payment credential that could be scoped to one transaction, a digital mandate recording what a user had authorized, a shopping assistant that could search a brand’s website and complete an order the brand itself did not sell through a large marketplace.

The pieces were arriving separately because commerce has always been more complicated than the act of choosing an object.

Finding a product is one problem. Knowing whether it fits the need is another. Confirming the current price is another. Checking inventory, delivery, taxes, returns, warranties, loyalty eligibility, payment method, fraud risk, identity, and permission creates a chain of smaller problems that human shoppers solve mostly by moving through interfaces. A person reads a page, clicks a variant, notices that delivery changed, enters an address, rejects an upsell, remembers which card has the travel benefit, checks a box, and eventually presses the button that matters.

For software to become a customer, those little acts have to become machine-readable.

That is what was happening.

OpenAI and Stripe developed the Agentic Commerce Protocol. Google and a group of retailers and platforms developed the Universal Commerce Protocol. Google’s Agent Payments Protocol introduced a way to represent delegated purchasing authority; by 2026 it was being moved into the FIDO Alliance, along with related work on verifiable intent. Mastercard was building Agent Pay and later a machine-oriented payment system meant for transactions that might happen continuously and in very small amounts. Stripe introduced a Machine Payments Protocol for agents that needed to pay other digital services. Shopify began treating its catalog not merely as a database for storefronts but as infrastructure that other agents could search. Amazon’s Buy for Me experimented with the heretical idea that Amazon could help a customer buy from a brand’s own website when the product was not sold by Amazon.

Each announcement could be mistaken for a product feature.

Together they described a change in the shape of the market.

For a century, most consumer commerce has assumed that a person will be present at the decisive moments. Even the automated parts are usually arranged around that assumption. A recommendation engine suggests; a human chooses. A saved card waits; a human confirms. An auto-renewing subscription charges because the human authorized a repeating rule earlier. A grocery service reorders a fixed list. A travel site bundles flights and hotels after a person selects dates. The software narrows the work, but the human remains the interpreter of intent and the bearer of permission.

An agentic consumer system tries to move the boundary.

The person says, in effect, what outcome is wanted. The machine performs more of the translation between intention and transaction.

“Find a quiet hotel near the conference, under three hundred dollars a night, refundable because the dates may change, and avoid properties where recent reviews mention construction.”

“Replace the printer when the cost per page is lower than repairing it, but use the toner we already own if possible.”

“Buy school supplies from the teacher’s list, reuse what we have, and keep the total under one hundred and fifty dollars.”

“Switch the broadband plan if the introductory rate is ending and there is a comparable service with no installation appointment.”

These are not shopping queries in the old sense. They are compact procurement policies.

The difference becomes clearer if the system is allowed to wait.

A human shopper usually searches when the need becomes urgent. The agent can begin earlier. It can watch inventory, prices, deadlines, product releases, delivery windows, and subscription terms. It can notice that a preferred coffee is discounted without requiring the owner to remember the ordinary price. It can wait for a flight fare to enter an acceptable range. It can prepare three replacement options for an appliance before the appliance fails. It can monitor a limited-run ticket sale or a hotel cancellation and act the moment a preauthorized condition is satisfied.

That is why the customer never sleeps.

The phrase does not mean literal autonomy in every transaction. Most people are unlikely to begin by giving a general-purpose model unlimited authority to spend. Delegation grows by domains and thresholds. Reorder household staples up to a monthly ceiling. Renew the train pass unless the price rises more than ten percent. Book the usual hotel if it is available below the approved rate. Buy replacement filters only from certified sellers. Do not purchase alcohol. Never change a medical subscription without asking. Always request confirmation for anything above five hundred dollars. The practical agent is surrounded by rules.

Those rules are economically important because they turn preferences into infrastructure.

Today a preference often has to be expressed again at every transaction. The shopper dislikes a particular ingredient, wants ethically sourced coffee, prefers repairable electronics, will pay more for a direct flight, avoids a certain marketplace, values a generous return policy, or refuses subscriptions with annual billing. The information exists in the person but not necessarily in a form the market can query. Sellers learn pieces of it through history and surveillance. The buyer continually reconstructs it through choice.

A persistent agent can carry those preferences forward.

This is not merely convenient. It changes the bargaining position of the consumer because memory has economic value.

Consider a familiar broadband bill. A company remembers the exact month the promotional price expires. It knows the customer’s payment history, service usage, address, equipment, support calls, and likely cost of cancellation. The customer may remember that the bill “seems higher.” The asymmetry is profitable because attention is scarce. An agent that stores the original offer, notices the expiration, compares local alternatives, estimates switching friction, and asks the provider for a retention offer before the new price takes effect has converted memory into negotiating capacity.

The same logic applies to insurance renewals, software subscriptions, credit-card benefits, warranties, airline credits, mobile plans, loyalty balances, medical reimbursements, electricity tariffs, car maintenance, and the dozens of low-drama contracts surrounding a modern household.

The largest early gains from consumer agents may arrive in places that are boring enough to be neglected by futurists.

A family does not need artificial general intelligence to benefit from remembering every return deadline.

It needs competent administration.

Businesses have spent enormous sums on exactly this kind of competence. Procurement software exists because firms learned long ago that buying is not a trivial act. A purchasing department maintains vendor records, negotiates terms, enforces budgets, checks invoices, follows contracts, consolidates orders, approves exceptions, and measures supplier performance. The sophistication rises with the size of the organization because the savings justify the overhead.

Households live below that threshold.

The paradox of agentic commerce is that software may let tiny economic actors adopt practices previously reserved for large ones. A household with fifty thousand dollars of annual consumption can behave, in limited ways, like a firm with a procurement system. A sole proprietor can compare insurance, software, shipping, office supplies, travel, and subcontractors without creating an operations department. A small restaurant can have an agent watch wholesale prices and delivery terms. A farmer can have software compare parts, fertilizer, weather services, and transport offers. The administrative scale advantage of the larger organization narrows.

Whether the savings actually reach the buyer is another matter.

A new intermediary always creates the possibility of a new tollbooth.

The web’s first generation of price-comparison tools offers a warning. In the late 1990s and early 2000s, researchers studied “shopbots,” services that automatically compared products and prices across online retailers. The expectation of frictionless competition was intuitive: if everyone can instantly find the cheapest seller, prices should converge toward the competitive minimum.

They did not.

Online prices were often lower than offline prices, and shopbot use could reduce prices and dispersion. Yet substantial differences persisted. Brand mattered even when the underlying book or compact disc was identical. Consumers were willing to pay more to a retailer they trusted because price was only one part of the transaction. Delivery reliability, returns, reputation, fraud risk, familiarity, and service were harder to compare. A market could become radically easier to search without becoming perfectly competitive.

The lesson matters because an AI agent is a more ambitious shopbot.

It can compare more than price. It can read return policies, summarize reviews, estimate delivery reliability, inspect seller reputation, and apply the buyer’s preferences. But every attempt to compare a messy human market requires somebody to choose what counts.

Suppose an agent ranks running shoes. Does it prefer the lowest price, best fit, longest expected life, most favorable professional reviews, highest customer rating, lowest injury risk, lowest environmental impact, easiest returns, fastest delivery, strongest brand reputation, local availability, or some weighted combination inferred from the user’s history?

There is no neutral answer.

The ranking is a model of the consumer.

This is where the customer who never sleeps becomes a political-economic character rather than a software feature. Whoever designs the agent helps decide which properties are legible and which are ignored. Whoever supplies the product data affects what the agent can see. Whoever controls the payment credential influences where the agent can act. Whoever owns the consumer memory possesses a map of preferences that may be more valuable than the purchase history alone.

The system can serve the buyer faithfully and still become a gatekeeper.

This tension is visible in the infrastructure choices being made now. Open standards can reduce dependence on any single platform. Merchant-of-record models can preserve a direct legal relationship between seller and customer. Scoped payment tokens can limit the damage from a compromised agent. Verifiable mandates can create a record of what the person actually authorized. Catalog protocols can let a small merchant expose products without negotiating a proprietary integration with every assistant.

But standards do not eliminate power. They organize it.

A railway gauge can be open while the station remains privately controlled. A web standard can be universal while a search engine captures most discovery. A payment network can interoperate across banks while its rules still shape merchant economics. The presence of a protocol tells us that a market is becoming possible; it does not tell us who will earn the rents.

By 2026 the contest was already visible.

Google had search intent, advertising infrastructure, a payments system, an AI assistant, and relationships with retailers. Amazon had transactional trust, fulfillment, Prime, product data, seller infrastructure, and decades of purchase history. Walmart had stores, groceries, logistics, membership, and enormous household frequency. Shopify sat behind millions of merchants and could turn merchant catalogs into a shared machine-readable layer. Payment networks had identity, fraud systems, merchant acceptance, and decades of experience resolving disputes. AI companies had the conversational interface and, increasingly, the place where consumers began deciding what to buy.

Each participant could plausibly describe itself as infrastructure.

Each could plausibly become the interface above the others.

The consumer’s interest is not identical to any of theirs.

A merchant wants access to demand without surrendering margin or customer relationships. An agent platform wants to be the preferred place where intention is expressed. A payment company wants agentic transactions to flow through trusted rails. A marketplace wants to preserve the advantages of aggregation. An advertiser wants a way to influence the recommendation. The consumer wants the system to understand what she means, protect her money, preserve her choices, and not quietly auction her intention to the highest bidder.

That last possibility deserves attention.

Human attention has been the scarce resource around which much of internet capitalism was built. Search advertising monetized the moment a person declared interest. Social advertising inferred interest from behavior. Retail media monetized the last steps before purchase. Affiliate links monetized referral. Influencer marketing monetized trust and identity. An agent can compress the visible journey from dozens of pages and ads into three recommendations.

The commercial pressure to get inside those three recommendations will be immense.

The advertising industry will not politely disappear because the customer delegated research to software. It will adapt. Sellers will offer promotions structured for agents. Platforms will experiment with sponsored placements, direct offers, preferred integrations, commissions, and paid access to high-intent demand. Merchants will optimize product data not only for search engines but for models. Marketing departments will try to understand what evidence an agent treats as credible. Reputation management will increasingly include the machine-readable record of returns, fulfillment, complaints, safety, and price history.

A human can sometimes recognize an advertisement because it looks like one.

An agentic recommendation can blend discovery, analysis, and commercial influence in a sentence.

This makes disclosure harder, not less important.

The deepest change is that the commercial battleground moves closer to intention. A search engine usually receives a query. A good agent may receive a life context. It knows that the user is pregnant, moving, divorcing, training for a marathon, caring for a parent, starting a business, losing a job, planning a wedding, or trying to reduce debt. Each condition changes what the person is likely to buy. The intimacy that makes the assistant useful also makes its mediation extraordinarily valuable.

There is an optimistic version of this arrangement.

The agent acts as a fiduciary-like representative of the consumer. It refuses hidden commissions. It distinguishes sponsored offers from its own ranking. It carries portable preferences and purchase history. It can leave one platform for another. It negotiates aggressively, remembers every promise, detects dark patterns, and makes markets more competitive by increasing effective search.

There is also a landlord version.

The agent is free because merchants pay for access. Its memory cannot be exported. Its ranking system is opaque. Premium users receive cleaner recommendations while free users receive commercially shaped ones. It can technically compare the whole market but disproportionately sees merchants participating in its protocol, payment system, cloud, or advertising auction. The consumer’s personal context becomes the richest targeting profile ever created.

Both versions can use the same underlying technology.

Business model and governance decide which one emerges.

This is why it is too early to celebrate the agentic consumer as a victory for buyers. The buyer has gained a staff, but the staff may work for somebody else.

The economics will become clearer as agents move from episodic assistance to persistent responsibility. Today a person might ask for the best television under a thousand dollars. Tomorrow the same system may know the dimensions of the room, the existing sound system, the household’s streaming services, whether somebody is sensitive to glare, the preferred installation date, the credit-card warranty benefits, and the fact that the family tends to keep televisions for nine years. It can decide whether waiting six weeks for a new model is worth it. It can arrange delivery and recycle the old set.

At that point the store is no longer the beginning of the shopping journey.

The household’s intention is.

The market must come to it.

For retailers, this feels like a loss of control. The homepage, email campaign, branded app, search ad, merchandising layout, and checkout funnel were built to shape a human journey. An agent can enter through a machine endpoint, request structured facts, compare the offer against competitors, and leave without admiring the photography.

For consumers, it feels like relief.

Much of shopping is work wearing the costume of choice.

The future will not eliminate the pleasurable parts. People will still wander markets, browse bookstores, try on clothes, argue about furniture, smell fruit, test bicycles, and buy irrational objects because they are beautiful. The agentic economy grows first in the parts people are happy to delegate: replenishment, comparison, paperwork, monitoring, coordination, and the endless defense against forgetting.

That is enough to matter.

A customer who never sleeps does not have to buy everything to change prices. It only has to make enough buyers more willing to search, wait, switch, and negotiate that sellers adapt their behavior in anticipation.

The employee does not need to threaten to quit every week for the possibility of quitting to affect wages. A country does not need to impose every tariff for the option to influence trade. A consumer does not need to switch every subscription for credible switching to alter retention economics.

The agent makes credibility cheaper.

That may be the first durable economic fact of the new consumer era.

The second is more unsettling.

Once the machine becomes a credible customer, every seller will want to know how to persuade it.

The customer will never sleep.

Neither will the market around it.
