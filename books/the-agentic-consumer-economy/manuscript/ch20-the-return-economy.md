# The Return Economy

The consumer agent does not stop working when the box arrives.

That sounds obvious and changes the economics of the purchase.

Modern e-commerce trained consumers to think of checkout as the decisive moment. The product is found, compared, ordered, paid for, shipped, and delivered. Everything after that feels like exception handling: perhaps the shirt does not fit, the blender is louder than expected, the chair arrives cracked, the television is too large for the wall, or the customer simply changes her mind.

For retailers, the exception is already an industry.

The National Retail Federation projected that American consumers would return roughly $850 billion of merchandise in 2025. Its survey with Happy Returns estimated that 15.8 percent of annual retail sales would come back and that online purchases would be returned at an even higher rate, about 19.3 percent. Those numbers come from industry research rather than a national administrative census, so they should not be treated as measurements precise to the decimal. Their scale is enough for the argument. Reverse commerce is not a rounding error attached to forward commerce.

It is a second supply chain running in the opposite direction.

A returned object may go back to a store, a consolidation point, a warehouse, a refurbisher, a liquidator, a recycler, a donation partner, a secondary marketplace, or a landfill. Somebody pays to inspect it, classify it, transport it, clean it, repair it, restock it, repackage it, mark it down, or discard it. The value of the object after the return depends on how quickly and accurately those decisions are made.

The customer sees a refund.

The economy sees logistics.

Agentic consumption can increase returns and reduce them.

That contradiction is the beginning of the chapter.

The increase is straightforward. If returning an object becomes almost effortless, consumers will return more objects at the margin. The agent has the receipt. It knows the deadline. It can generate the label, schedule pickup, select the correct reason code, track the parcel, notice when the refund is late, and escalate if the merchant loses the return. A task that once cost twenty minutes may cost the consumer twenty seconds.

Lower the cost of exercising an option and people exercise it more often.

Retailers know this mechanism already. Free returns are popular partly because they reduce perceived purchase risk. In the NRF's 2025 study, 82 percent of surveyed consumers described free returns as an important shopping consideration, and 71 percent said a poor return experience made them less likely to shop with the retailer again. The return policy is not merely a post-purchase service. It is part of the product being sold at checkout.

A consumer agent makes the option more valuable because the option becomes easier to use.

That can lead to a bad equilibrium. Consumers order three sizes because returning two is trivial. Agents learn that ordering several close substitutes and keeping the best one maximizes the consumer's expected satisfaction. Merchants absorb higher reverse-logistics costs, then raise prices, narrow free-return policies, or discriminate among customers based on return behavior. The consumer receives convenience while the system moves more boxes, uses more packaging, consumes more transport, and destroys more residual value.

The private optimum can be socially expensive.

The agent can also move the market the other way.

A surprisingly large share of returns begins with bad information before purchase. Size charts are inconsistent. Product photographs conceal scale. compatibility is unclear. specifications are incomplete. reviews describe different versions of the item. A person buys a cable that does not fit, furniture that does not fit through the door, a replacement part that looks correct but has the wrong connector, clothing whose nominal size bears little relationship to another brand's size, or an appliance incompatible with the available electrical connection.

A competent agent can ask the boring questions first.

What is the exact model number of the device already owned?

What are the measurements of the doorway?

Which shoe last fit well in this brand?

Does the part supersede an older part number?

Is assembly required, and will the finished object fit the intended room?

Does the return policy cover opened packaging?

The web has long contained this information in fragments. The difference is the cost of gathering and connecting it.

An agent that remembers the household can prevent mismatches that a one-off product page cannot.

This gives merchants a reason to share better data.

If an agent can predict return probability, a product with precise dimensions, reliable fit data, real photographs, accurate compatibility fields, and honest quality history becomes more valuable than a product optimized only to win the click. The merchant has historically borne some return cost, but the consumer also bears hassle. Agentic comparison can include the expected cost of failure explicitly.

The cheapest item is no longer the one with the lowest sticker price.

It is the item with the lowest expected total cost after the probability of return, replacement, downtime, and disappointment.

That calculation can reward quality.

It can also reward restrictive return policies if the agent optimizes too narrowly. A merchant with a harsh policy may report fewer returns because consumers are trapped, not because the product is better. A trustworthy agent therefore needs to distinguish observed return rates from returnability. A low rate can mean good fit or high friction.

This is the same identification problem that appears throughout digital capitalism. An observed behavior does not reveal the consumer's underlying preference when constraints differ.

Reverse logistics gives the agent an unusually rich feedback loop.

Most recommendation systems know that a purchase happened. A household agent can know what happened next. The shoes were returned because the heel slipped. The replacement was kept. The vacuum was kept but required two warranty claims. The cheaper phone case cracked after three months. The cookware was returned because the handle became hot, not because the color was wrong.

Over time, the agent learns from outcomes rather than conversions.

That is a better commercial objective if the system genuinely works for the consumer.

A merchant may prefer the purchase probability.

A consumer prefers the successful-use probability.

The distance between those metrics contains much of what is dysfunctional about digital merchandising.

This is why the return economy belongs in a book about agentic capitalism rather than a logistics manual. The agent creates the possibility of optimizing beyond the sale.

The merchant's response will be equally agentic.

Retailers already use software to route returns, detect fraud, decide whether an item should be shipped back, and determine whether a refund can be issued immediately. NRF reported in 2025 that 85 percent of surveyed retailers said they were using AI to detect or prevent return fraud. That figure comes from a retailer survey and should be read as industry-reported adoption rather than a verified census, but the direction is unsurprising. Return fraud is a classification problem attached to money, inventory, identity, and behavior. It attracts automation quickly.

The seller agent will ask whether this return is legitimate, whether it is economical to retrieve the item, and what disposition maximizes recovery.

A twenty-dollar object may cost more to transport and process than it is worth. The retailer can tell the customer to keep it and issue the refund.

A high-value electronic product may be routed to a specialized refurbisher.

An unopened item may return directly to saleable inventory.

A damaged object may be offered to the consumer at a partial refund rather than shipped back.

A merchant with excess stock may prefer not to receive another unit at all.

When these choices are automated, the return becomes a negotiation about residual value.

Consumer and merchant agents can sometimes find an outcome better than the standard binary of keep or return.

The customer says the table has a scratch on one side.

The merchant knows the table costs ninety dollars to retrieve and resell, that the scratch will face a wall, and that the customer paid four hundred dollars.

A seller agent can offer sixty dollars back to keep it. The consumer agent knows the household's preferences and may accept.

No truck moves.

No warehouse touches the table.

The customer pays less for an imperfection she can tolerate. The merchant avoids a larger loss.

This is a small example of a broader possibility: agentic commerce can make after-sale bargaining cheap enough to preserve value that crude policies destroy.

The circular economy enters at the same point.

An object returned by one consumer is not necessarily waste. It can be repaired, refurbished, resold, harvested for parts, recycled, donated, or redirected to a buyer with a different willingness to accept wear. Reverse logistics determines whether that residual value survives.

The European Union's Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation and related digital-product-passport work point toward a world in which more information about materials, repairability, provenance, and product characteristics can travel with goods. The exact implementation schedule and covered product categories will evolve, but the institutional direction matters. If products become more legible throughout their life cycle, agents can make choices after purchase that today require specialist knowledge.

A household agent could know that a broken appliance has a replaceable module rather than assuming replacement is necessary.

It could locate an authorized repairer, compare repair cost with replacement cost, identify whether the warranty still applies, and preserve the record for resale.

It could sell an unused object automatically when household policy permits.

It could bundle several low-value items into one resale shipment rather than letting them sit in a closet until they become worthless.

The consumer agent becomes a tiny asset manager for physical goods.

This sounds comically grand until one considers how much capital households store in objects they rarely track as assets. Phones, laptops, tools, furniture, appliances, sports equipment, children's gear, musical instruments, cameras, bicycles, and clothing all have residual values that decay partly because selling is annoying.

Transaction cost turns assets into clutter.

Agents can lower the transaction cost.

That could expand secondary markets substantially.

A person no longer needs to photograph the stroller, write a listing, answer messages, negotiate with strangers, decide whether a buyer is legitimate, coordinate pickup, and remember to remove the listing after sale. The agent can handle most of the clerical work while preserving human approval where safety or personal interaction matters.

Primary sellers will notice.

If resale becomes easier, durable products become more economically attractive because their residual value is easier to realize. A premium tool that holds value can beat a disposable tool on lifetime cost. A manufacturer with a strong certified-refurbishment channel can capture some of its products' second lives. Brands may begin to publish expected resale value the way automobiles have long been discussed in terms of depreciation.

The agent can incorporate depreciation into purchase choice.

That changes competition again.

A thousand-dollar phone that can be resold for five hundred dollars after two years may be cheaper in economic terms than a seven-hundred-dollar phone worth one hundred dollars later. Humans understand this for cars and houses because the sums are large. Software can apply it to smaller goods where manual accounting would be absurd.

The product's future becomes part of its present price.

This could reward repairability and punish planned fragility, but only if the necessary data is trustworthy.

Manufacturers may have incentives to exaggerate durability. Resale platforms may selectively report high prices rather than actual transaction distributions. Repair scores can become marketing. Agents will need independent evidence, and regulators may need rules around environmental and durability claims just as they police other commercial representations.

The circular agent is not automatically green.

It optimizes the objective it is given.

If the household asks for minimum immediate cost, the agent may buy a disposable product. If free returns make over-ordering cheap, it may create more transport. If resale is effortless, consumers may buy more because they expect to recover value later. Efficiency can increase throughput.

Jevons-like rebound appears in consumption as well as energy.

Lower the effective cost of a behavior and people may do more of it.

This is why environmental accounting cannot stop at a story about smarter logistics. The relevant question is net material throughput: fewer failed purchases, more repair and resale, perhaps more ordering and faster turnover.

The outcome will differ by category.

Furniture is not fashion. Electronics are not books. Perishable goods have different reverse paths from durable equipment. Luxury resale has different incentives from low-value household objects. Local density matters because a returned or resold item is cheaper to move when another buyer is nearby.

Agentic systems can exploit that geography.

A returned product need not travel back to a central warehouse if a nearby consumer wants the same item and the merchant can preserve guarantees and chain of custody. A retailer could redirect unopened returns locally. A repair network could aggregate work by neighborhood. Secondary-market agents could match supply and demand before objects enter long reverse journeys.

The reverse supply chain becomes a network rather than a trip home.

This is easier to imagine than to implement because fraud and quality uncertainty are formidable.

A customer can claim an item is unopened when it is not. A buyer can misrepresent damage. A seller can pass off a used product as new. Serial numbers can be swapped. Counterfeit goods can enter return streams. The NRF's 2025 returns work estimated that fraud represented a meaningful share of returns and documented retailers' concern about empty boxes, counterfeit substitutions, and other abuse.

Agents can make fraud cheaper too.

A malicious system can generate claims, exploit inconsistent policies, coordinate identities, identify merchants with weak controls, and operate at a scale a human fraudster cannot.

The defense will be better provenance.

Serial identity. purchase records. tamper evidence. payment history. condition verification. authenticated photographs. device telemetry where appropriate and consented. Product passports in some categories. Reputation carried across transactions.

Each defense raises a privacy question.

A retailer would love to know everything that happened to a product after sale. A consumer may not want a television, car, appliance, or phone continuously reporting its life to a commercial database.

The circular economy can become a surveillance economy if provenance is solved carelessly.

The useful principle is proportionality. The system should prove enough to support the transaction without making ownership conditional on permanent observation.

The return economy also changes accounting inside companies.

A company that optimizes gross sales while ignoring returns can congratulate itself for transactions that destroy value. Retailers increasingly measure net sales, return rates, disposition recovery, refund speed, fraud, and customer lifetime effects because the economics demand it. Agentic commerce will push these metrics closer to the recommendation layer.

A product with high conversion and high return may be a bad recommendation.

A product with slightly lower conversion and almost no returns may create more profit and more consumer satisfaction.

If agents learn from the entire transaction lifecycle, marketing and operations stop being separable.

The recommendation contains a logistics forecast.

That is another way the merchant becomes an API. The agent does not merely ask, “Can I buy this?” It asks, “What happens if it fails?”

Return policy becomes structured data.

Repair policy becomes structured data.

Warranty becomes structured data.

Resale value becomes data.

The best merchants may make these facts unusually easy to inspect because doing so signals confidence.

The worst may prefer ambiguity.

Consumer agents can make ambiguity expensive by penalizing unknowns.

That creates a potential quality ratchet. Sellers who publish precise terms become easier for agents to trust. Competitors respond. Product data improves. Total-cost comparison becomes more meaningful.

Or platforms can standardize the wrong things and create another gaming surface.

A merchant learns that the ranking system rewards a thirty-day return window and sets one, then makes the process unpleasant in practice. A product receives a repairability score that ignores unavailable parts. A refurbisher claims a grade whose meaning varies by seller.

Machine-readable is not the same as true.

The consumer agent needs enforcement behind the schema.

This is where public institutions and private markets meet. Consumer law defines warranties and deceptive practices. Standards bodies define product information. merchants define policies. logistics networks execute returns. insurers price fraud. payment systems handle disputes. agents coordinate all of them.

The consumer sees one sentence: “Send it back.”

The economy behind the sentence is large enough to change what gets manufactured.

That is the deeper significance of the return economy.

Forward commerce rewards making a sale.

Reverse commerce reveals whether the sale worked.

For decades digital systems became spectacularly good at optimizing the first event because the event was easy to measure. Agentic consumers can carry the measurement farther. They can remember the object, the promise, the failure, the remedy, the resale, and the next purchase.

A capitalism that learns from outcomes rather than clicks would be a different machine.

The box arriving was never the end of the transaction.

We merely lacked the staff to keep watching.
