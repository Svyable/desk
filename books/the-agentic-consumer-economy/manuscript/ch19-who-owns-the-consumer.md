# Who Owns the Consumer?

The most valuable file in the agentic economy may not be a credit-card number.

It may be a memory.

Not a transcript exactly. Not a browser history. Not a CRM profile. A working model of the person: what she prefers, what she avoids, what she can afford, what she has already bought, which promises companies made to her, which subscriptions she regrets, which sizes fit, which medications are irrelevant to a shopping task and therefore should remain private, which brands she trusts, which tradeoffs she accepts, how much hassle she will tolerate, where she will not compromise, and what she is trying to do next.

A good consumer agent becomes useful by accumulating this context.

That usefulness creates the central ownership problem of agentic capitalism.

If the agent knows you better every month, can you leave it in an afternoon?

The question sounds like data portability and is larger than data portability.

People have already lived through several generations of digital lock-in. An email provider became sticky because the address was printed everywhere. A social network became sticky because friends were there. A phone operating system became sticky because photos, purchases, messages, devices, and habits accumulated around it. A cloud suite became sticky because documents, workflows, identities, and coworkers depended on it. A marketplace became sticky because payment, reviews, order history, fulfillment, and trust compressed a complicated transaction into one tap.

An agent adds something more intimate.

It learns the policy behind the taps.

This difference is easy to underestimate. A purchase history says that someone bought three pairs of the same shoe. A consumer agent may know why: wide toe box, durable sole, no leather, acceptable for work, runs half a size small, and the buyer will pay twenty dollars more to avoid trying a new fit before an upcoming trip.

That explanation is portable economic power.

Without it, the next agent has to learn again.

The incumbent therefore acquires what economists call switching costs through a deeply human mechanism: accumulated understanding.

The better the product gets, the more expensive exit can feel.

Companies will describe this as personalization. They will not be wrong. A system that remembers preferences can save enormous amounts of time. It can prevent mistakes. It can notice that a proposed purchase conflicts with a budget or repeats something already owned. It can maintain continuity across years rather than forcing the user to restate basic facts.

The danger is not memory itself.

The danger is memory becoming tenancy.

If a person rents access to her own accumulated context from the platform that captured it, the relationship resembles a landlord more than a toolmaker.

This is where ownership language becomes useful even though personal data is not a simple piece of property. Legal systems generally treat privacy, access, processing, consent, and portability through rights and duties rather than declaring that every datum is owned like a bicycle. That complexity should not stop us from asking the economic question.

Who controls the asset that makes the agent valuable?

The answer can be divided into several layers.

There is raw history: purchases, messages, searches, receipts, warranties, returns, calendars, loyalty accounts, and financial transactions.

There is structured preference: sizes, dietary constraints, favored materials, shipping priorities, ethical preferences, budget ranges, recurring needs.

There is inferred preference: the system's conclusions about what the user tends to value even when she never stated it directly.

There is procedural memory: how the person wants decisions made. Ask before spending over two hundred dollars. Never auto-renew an annual contract without comparison. Prefer repair before replacement. Use points only when their value exceeds a threshold. Do not buy gifts automatically.

And there is relationship history: which recommendations the user accepted, rejected, regretted, or praised.

Moving the raw data is not the same as moving the agent.

This is why a download button can satisfy the letter of portability while failing the practical test.

A person may receive a giant archive of JSON files and still be unable to recreate the working relationship elsewhere.

The meaningful standard is credible exit.

Could another provider, with the user's permission, import enough context to become useful quickly without requiring the person to spend months retraining it?

Could the person keep critical memories locally and allow competing agents to use them?

Could preferences be stored in interoperable forms rather than proprietary embeddings that no other system can interpret?

Could the user selectively transfer “household purchasing policy” without handing the new provider ten years of private conversation?

These questions sound technical because technical design determines whether the answers are yes.

They are competition policy because switching costs determine whether the answers matter economically.

Europe's Digital Markets Act offers one useful precedent. The law was not written specifically for consumer AI agents, but its focus on gatekeepers, interoperability, and data portability captures the underlying problem: platforms with powerful network effects and ecosystem control can turn useful integration into durable dependence. By 2026, European Commission enforcement work had pushed mobile operating-system gatekeepers toward more interoperability and portability, including work concerning AI services. The details will evolve, but the principle is durable.

A market remains more contestable when users can leave without abandoning the value they helped create.

Agentic commerce makes that principle urgent.

The platform that becomes the consumer's general representative may sit above search, retail, travel, finance, subscriptions, utilities, insurance, entertainment, and household administration. It need not manufacture any of those products to exert enormous influence over them. It needs only to decide which options the consumer sees and to hold the memory that makes another representative inconvenient.

This is a different kind of platform power from the classic marketplace.

Amazon became powerful partly by bringing buyers and sellers into one venue and controlling important rules inside it. Google became powerful partly by mediating discovery across the web. Apple and Google became powerful partly by controlling mobile operating systems and app distribution. Meta became powerful partly by controlling social graphs and attention.

The agentic platform can mediate intention.

That is higher in the stack.

It hears “I need to move in June” before any moving company, realtor, utility, insurer, furniture store, bank, or internet provider does.

It hears “We are having a baby” before most commercial categories know demand exists.

It hears “My father should not be driving much longer” before the family researches transport alternatives, housing, care, insurance, or medical equipment.

Intent is commercially precious because it arrives before the transaction is specified.

The agent that receives it can shape the market the consumer encounters.

This creates a conflict that cannot be solved by a privacy policy alone.

The agent may be paid by the consumer.

It may also earn merchant commissions.

It may belong to a company that sells advertising.

It may belong to a commerce platform.

It may belong to a bank or payment network.

It may be bundled with an operating system or device.

It may use a model provider whose economics depend on usage growth.

Each business model creates different temptations.

A consumer-paid agent can still be biased or incompetent, but its revenue aligns more directly with the user. A merchant-funded agent can be free to the user but inherits conflicts over recommendation. An advertising-funded agent may face pressure to generate monetizable attention. A bank agent may optimize for products within the institution. A retailer's agent may be excellent inside that retailer and strategically incomplete outside it.

There is no business model without incentives.

The question is whether the incentives are visible and contestable.

The word fiduciary will inevitably enter this debate.

Financial advisers, lawyers, trustees, and other professionals sometimes operate under duties requiring them to place a client's interest ahead of certain conflicting interests. It is tempting to say a consumer agent should simply be a digital fiduciary.

The analogy is useful and incomplete.

A general agent may perform thousands of low-stakes acts across domains governed by different laws. “Best interest” is difficult to define when preferences are subjective. Is the best restaurant the cheapest, healthiest, closest, most enjoyable, most ethical, or the one the user specifically wants despite all the other metrics? Should an agent discourage an impulsive luxury purchase or respect autonomy? Should it maximize household savings if doing so eliminates purchases from local businesses the user values?

A machine cannot derive the principal's values from economics alone.

What it can do is represent declared interests faithfully, disclose conflicts, preserve user control, and avoid secretly substituting platform objectives for user objectives.

That is a more operational standard.

A trustworthy agent should be able to answer questions such as: Why did you recommend this? Which merchants were considered? Which were excluded and why? Did any party pay to influence the result? What data did you reveal to obtain this offer? What authority did you use to complete the purchase? What would have happened under a different constraint? Can I export the relevant memory?

These are not demands for a perfect explanation of every model weight.

They are demands for transactional accountability.

Commerce already creates records. Receipts, orders, contracts, authorizations, shipping notices, chargebacks, and warranties exist because disputes happen. Agentic commerce will need an additional layer: evidence of delegated intent.

Google's Agent Payments Protocol work explicitly emphasized mandates that can prove what a user authorized. Payment networks have similarly emphasized verifiable intent and registered agents. The immediate use case is preventing a machine from spending money outside its authority.

The same concept can support broader accountability.

A consumer should be able to distinguish “the agent searched and recommended” from “the user specifically approved this purchase” from “the agent was authorized to transact automatically under a standing rule.”

Without those distinctions, every dispute collapses into a philosophical argument about whether the AI did it.

Law needs a chain of responsibility.

So does trust.

Authority also needs limits.

A general consumer agent becomes dangerous if every permission is permanent and broad. The safe architecture resembles capability security more than a master key. The agent can spend up to a certain amount in a category, disclose particular information for a particular task, access location temporarily, or renew a service under defined conditions.

People will not configure every permission manually.

That fact is where defaults acquire political significance.

If the default is broad access in exchange for convenience, most users will grant broad access. If the default separates shopping memory from health or private communications, architectures will evolve around compartmentalization. If agents can perform routine actions locally on a device, some data may never need to leave. If every useful function requires centralized memory, centralization will win through convenience.

Privacy is often framed as what information a company is allowed to collect.

Agentic privacy includes what the company is allowed to infer and act upon.

An assistant that knows a person is grieving, unemployed, recently divorced, pregnant, financially stressed, or worried about a diagnosis possesses context that can improve service and intensify manipulation. A seller may have no legitimate reason to receive most of it. The consumer agent's job is partly to prevent the market from seeing everything the agent knows.

This is the information firewall at the heart of trustworthy delegation.

The agent should know more about the consumer than it tells the merchant.

That principle is the opposite of the data-broker instinct that defined much of digital advertising.

It could become a new competitive dimension.

One agent provider might advertise that all preference memory is local and encrypted. Another might offer cloud memory with rich cross-device continuity. A third might let users hold a portable personal data vault and authorize multiple agents against it. An employer-provided agent may be powerful but unacceptable for private purchasing because the boundary between work and personal context is too important.

Consumers will trade convenience, cost, capability, and privacy differently.

The existence of choices matters more than one universal answer.

This is why interoperability should not mean one mandatory global memory format containing everything about a person.

Good portability can be layered.

Basic profile. Purchase history. Active warranties. subscriptions. loyalty balances. household inventory. declared preferences. agent policies. vendor relationships. Each can be moved separately. Sensitive domains can remain segregated.

A person may want her shoe size portable and her private conversation history immobile.

The consumer economy has never had to think about identity at this resolution because the human carried the integration inside her head.

The agent externalizes it.

Once externalized, the integration becomes infrastructure.

This also creates a market for identity providers.

To transact reliably, an agent may need to prove that a user is an adult, lives in a certain jurisdiction, holds a valid payment method, belongs to a loyalty program, possesses a license, or has authorized a recurring transaction. The crude way is to reveal the underlying documents. The better way is often to prove the necessary fact while revealing less.

Digital identity systems, passkeys, tokenized credentials, and privacy-preserving proofs can reduce unnecessary disclosure if designed well.

They can also create new chokepoints.

If one identity system becomes required across commerce, whoever governs it acquires extraordinary exclusion power. A mistake, fraud flag, or political decision can prevent a person from acting across many markets at once.

Convenience centralizes failure.

That pattern appears throughout this book.

The agent unifies the household's commercial life, which creates leverage and a single point of dependence.

The agent remembers every promise, which creates accountability and a valuable surveillance record.

The agent routes across merchants, which increases competition and gives the router market power.

The agent protects the user from manipulative interfaces, which moves manipulation into less visible ranking rules.

The answer is not to reject integration.

It is to make integration reversible.

Reversibility is an underrated property of digital capitalism. A user who can leave disciplines a provider even if she never leaves. A merchant that can connect to several agent platforms bargains differently from one dependent on a single source of demand. A developer that can interoperate with the operating system competes differently from one waiting for permission from the platform's own service.

Exit is not the only form of power, but it is a durable one.

The best consumer-agent market may therefore look less like one omniscient assistant and more like an ecosystem around a user-controlled core.

One system may hold identity. Another may specialize in travel. Another may negotiate utilities. Another may manage household inventory. A general agent may coordinate them without owning every underlying datum. The user can replace a specialist without reconstructing her life.

This architecture will be harder to build than the integrated alternative.

Vertical integration is seductive because it works.

A single company can make the experience smoother when it controls model, memory, identity, payment, device, and commerce. Errors are easier to resolve internally. Authentication is seamless. Data flows without standards negotiations. Product teams can optimize the whole journey.

Competition policy often arrives precisely because seamless integration can become durable exclusion.

The challenge is not to make integrated products illegal by definition. It is to prevent integration from turning into captivity.

The 2030s could therefore produce a familiar political fight in unfamiliar language.

Platforms will say that safety requires trusted agents, integrated credentials, controlled stores, and certified commerce endpoints.

Competitors will say those requirements conveniently exclude them.

Privacy advocates will argue that central memory is dangerous.

Consumers will choose the easiest product.

Regulators will try to write rules that preserve safety without freezing incumbents into place.

Merchants will complain about fees.

Startups will promise portability.

Most people will care only when something breaks or the price rises.

That is how infrastructure becomes political: slowly, then all at once.

The most important ownership question may ultimately be neither legal title nor database access.

It may be agency in the older sense of the word.

Does the system actually act for you?

If it chooses a cheaper product because you asked it to, it is your agent.

If it chooses a more profitable product for the platform while telling you it chose the best one, it is something else.

If it remembers your life but makes leaving practically impossible, it is something else again.

The agentic consumer economy deserves its name only if the consumer remains the principal.

Everything else is digital capitalism wearing your preferences as a disguise.
