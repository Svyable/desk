# Chapter briefs and falsification notes

## Chapter 1 — The Customer Who Never Sleeps

**Central question:** What changes when the buyer can search, compare, remember, and act continuously rather than only when a person pays attention?

**Evidence used:** 2025–2026 agentic-commerce product launches and protocol work; classic search-cost economics; current payments infrastructure.

**Counterevidence:** Most consumer use may remain assistive rather than autonomous, and many markets are constrained by scarcity or monopoly rather than search effort.

**What would make this false?** If agent-mediated shopping remains a niche interface with little measurable impact on search time, switching, prices, or transaction completion.

## Chapter 2 — Before the Agent

**Central question:** Why did earlier “frictionless commerce” predictions only partly come true?

**Evidence used:** Bakos on electronic marketplaces; Brynjolfsson and Smith on internet price dispersion; the rise of search, marketplaces, comparison tools, and trusted intermediaries.

**Counterevidence:** Earlier digital commerce may be an imperfect analogy because modern agents can execute rather than merely display information.

**What would make this false?** If autonomous execution proves economically equivalent to old comparison tools and creates no new intermediary power or consumer behavior.

## Chapter 3 — The Consumer Gets a Staff

**Central question:** Can households acquire procurement capabilities once economical mainly for firms?

**Evidence used:** Search-cost theory; subscription/cancellation policy; payment and identity tooling; examples of household administrative tasks.

**Counterevidence:** Setup, trust, supervision, and error correction may consume much of the time agents appear to save.

**What would make this false?** If measured household time savings and financial gains remain trivial after supervision and mistake costs are included.

## Chapter 4 — The Machine Customer

**Central question:** What does a merchant need to know when the immediate buyer is software acting for a person?

**Evidence used:** Mastercard Agent Pay materials; Google AP2; Visa Trusted Agent Protocol; merchant discussions of agent verification and delegated intent.

**Counterevidence:** Merchants may continue to require visible human approval for most transactions.

**What would make this false?** If agent identity, authorization, and machine-specific trust never become economically important merchant concerns.

## Chapter 5 — The Death of the Product Page

**Central question:** What happens when discovery and checkout occur inside AI surfaces instead of merchant-controlled pages?

**Evidence used:** OpenAI/Stripe ACP and Instant Checkout; Shopify Agentic Storefronts and Catalog; Google UCP; Amazon Buy for Me.

**Counterevidence:** Brand-controlled websites may remain central for rich products, complex configuration, and high-consideration purchases.

**What would make this false?** If merchant-site traffic and conversion remain dominant even after agentic integrations become widely available.

## Chapter 6 — Brand After Browsing

**Central question:** Does machine comparison weaken brands or make trust and meaning more valuable?

**Evidence used:** Historical persistence of brand and trust in lower-friction online markets; machine-readable product attributes; reputation systems.

**Counterevidence:** Agents may reduce brand premiums even in categories where identity and trust once mattered.

**What would make this false?** If brand concentration falls uniformly across functional, luxury, trust-sensitive, and identity categories as agent use rises.

## Chapter 7 — Advertising to an Algorithm

**Central question:** Where does advertising move when human attention is no longer required for routine transactions?

**Evidence used:** Existing sponsored-ranking models; agentic checkout; OECD dark-pattern research; commercial incentives around recommendation and discovery.

**Counterevidence:** Advertising may remain mostly human-facing because preferences are formed socially before agents execute purchases.

**What would make this false?** If agent platforms avoid merchant-funded ranking and advertising spend does not migrate toward agent discovery or placement.

## Chapter 8 — The Price Has Been Watching You

**Central question:** What happens when individualized seller pricing meets individualized buyer search?

**Evidence used:** FTC surveillance-pricing study; dynamic pricing and revenue-management history; consumer-agent comparison mechanisms.

**Counterevidence:** Legal, reputational, or operational constraints may keep individualized pricing rare.

**What would make this false?** If personalized pricing and promotion do not meaningfully expand, or if agents cannot detect or counter them.

## Chapter 9 — Negotiation Comes Back

**Central question:** Can low-cost agents make bargaining economical in markets that adopted posted prices because negotiation was too expensive?

**Evidence used:** Search-cost theory; dynamic offers; programmable transactions; examples from travel, telecom, subscriptions, and durable goods.

**Counterevidence:** Consumers and merchants may prefer stable public prices for simplicity and fairness.

**What would make this false?** If agent-mediated negotiation remains rare and transaction terms stay largely standardized.

## Chapter 10 — Loyalty Against Optimization

**Central question:** How do loyalty programs change when the counterparty never forgets a point, benefit, credit, or status rule?

**Evidence used:** Structure of modern loyalty programs, credit-card benefits, and machine tracking capabilities.

**Counterevidence:** Emotional loyalty, status, and ecosystem benefits may be stronger than narrow optimization.

**What would make this false?** If agent use does not increase benefit redemption, switching, or pressure on loyalty-program economics.

## Chapter 11 — The End of Consumer Inertia

**Central question:** How much business value depends on consumer inattention, delayed cancellation, and switching friction?

**Evidence used:** FTC negative-option and cancellation enforcement; OECD dark-pattern evidence; recurring-service economics.

**Counterevidence:** Regulation may remove much of this friction before agents do, or consumers may intentionally retain familiar providers.

**What would make this false?** If agent adoption does not materially change churn, cancellation, renewal pricing, fee avoidance, or claim rates.

## Chapter 12 — Trust by Proxy

**Central question:** Can a consumer safely let software choose which merchants and claims to trust?

**Evidence used:** Google AP2 and Mastercard agent-payment/intent work; Visa Trusted Agent Protocol; FIDO Alliance agentic-authentication standards work; Stripe Machine Payments Protocol; reputation, fraud and dispute structures.

**Counterevidence:** Trust may remain too contextual for reliable automation, especially for high-stakes or novel purchases.

**What would make this false?** If users consistently refuse delegated merchant selection even when technical security improves.

## Chapter 13 — The Household Treasury

**Central question:** What happens when an agent optimizes spending across categories rather than one transaction at a time?

**Evidence used:** Household budgeting logic; loyalty and payment interactions; recurring billing; financial inclusion infrastructure; a 2025 BIS experiment on GenAI agents managing simulated wholesale-payment liquidity as a limited analogy for sequencing obligations under constraints.

**Counterevidence:** People may resist cross-domain financial access or prefer separate specialist tools; wholesale payment-system experiments do not establish safe or desirable autonomous household financial control.

**What would make this false?** If cross-market household agents do not improve cash flow, fee avoidance, benefit use, or purchasing decisions compared with category-specific tools.

## Chapter 14 — The Platform Above the Store

**Central question:** Does the agent layer become the next dominant digital gatekeeper?

**Evidence used:** Historical platform economics; current AI-commerce integration; EU DMA logic around gatekeepers, interoperability, and portability; OECD’s 2026 analysis of agentic AI, consumer agency and competition/consumer-policy overlap.

**Counterevidence:** Open standards, multi-homing, local models, and rapid model commoditization may keep the layer competitive.

**What would make this false?** If users routinely switch agents, merchants reach many agents cheaply, and no small set of platforms controls high-intent demand.

## Chapter 15 — The Merchant Becomes an API

**Central question:** What capabilities matter when merchants must sell through structured machine interfaces as well as human storefronts?

**Evidence used:** Shopify Catalog/UCP; product-feed infrastructure; checkout protocols; inventory and fulfillment systems.

**Counterevidence:** Scraping and general model capabilities may reduce the need for formal merchant integration.

**What would make this false?** If structured merchant data does not improve discovery, conversion, reliability, or agent participation enough to justify investment.

## Chapter 16 — When Sellers Have Agents Too

**Central question:** What happens when buyer agents face equally automated seller pricing, negotiation, and retention systems?

**Evidence used:** FTC surveillance-pricing findings; Google, Mastercard and Visa agent payment/identity work; Stripe machine-payment infrastructure; algorithmic-pricing literature; existing revenue-management systems.

**Counterevidence:** Sellers may choose simple posted prices and standardized policies because automated bargaining creates distrust or regulatory risk.

**What would make this false?** If seller-side agents do not materially change pricing, bundling, negotiation, or information strategy in markets with consumer agents.

## Chapter 17 — The Attention Recession

**Central question:** What happens to advertising-funded commerce and media when routine purchases require less human attention?

**Evidence used:** Agentic checkout infrastructure; affiliate and retail-media economics; OECD dark-pattern work; platform advertising history.

**Counterevidence:** Human-facing advertising may remain powerful because it shapes preferences before agents execute them.

**What would make this false?** If human commercial attention, merchant-site visits, affiliate conversion, and advertising spend remain structurally unchanged as agentic transactions grow.

## Chapter 18 — The Global Shopping Machine

**Central question:** Can agentic commerce lower the fixed cost of cross-border participation for consumers and small firms?

**Evidence used:** WTO World Trade Report 2025; WTO-ICC AI adoption survey; World Bank Digital Progress and Trends 2025; Global Findex 2025; current commerce standards.

**Counterevidence:** Logistics, payments, regulation, infrastructure, and digital-skill gaps may dominate the savings from AI coordination.

**What would make this false?** If agent adoption does not expand cross-border market participation by smaller firms or primarily benefits already digitized large companies.

## Chapter 19 — Who Owns the Consumer?

**Central question:** Who controls the memory, permissions, and representation that make a consumer agent valuable?

**Evidence used:** Platform switching-cost history; EU DMA portability/interoperability enforcement; OECD 2026 competition/consumer-policy analysis; delegated payment mandates; privacy and identity architecture.

**Counterevidence:** Portable local memory, open standards, and multi-agent architectures may prevent durable lock-in.

**What would make this false?** If consumers can move between competing agents with little loss of accumulated usefulness and agent-market concentration remains low.

## Chapter 20 — The Return Economy

**Central question:** What changes when the consumer agent manages the product after checkout—return, repair, warranty, resale, and disposal—as actively as it managed the purchase?

**Evidence used:** NRF 2025 returns estimates and merchant survey; reverse-logistics research; EU ecodesign, repairability, and digital-product-passport direction; lifecycle transaction logic.

**Counterevidence:** Easier returns may increase bracketing, shipping, fraud, and material throughput enough to offset gains from better matching and resale.

**What would make this false?** If agent use does not measurably reduce failed purchases, improve recovery value, increase repair/resale, or alter merchants’ return economics after controlling for increased return volume.

## Chapter 21 — Credit Has an Agent

**Central question:** What happens when a household agent can shop and coordinate the financing attached to consumption rather than accepting the credit product placed at checkout?

**Evidence used:** CFPB BNPL market and borrower research; fair-lending and consumer-finance rules; payment choice; telematics/data portability research; household cash-flow logic.

**Counterevidence:** Credit availability, underwriting regulation, lender access controls, and household financial constraints may dominate any search advantage. Easier financing may increase debt use rather than welfare.

**What would make this false?** If consumer agents do not reduce effective borrowing costs, late fees, harmful loan stacking, or financing friction—or if any savings are offset by higher debt and loss rates.

## Chapter 22 — The Consumer Union in Software

**Central question:** Does cheaper coordination allow consumers to aggregate demand temporarily and acquire bargaining power that individual optimization cannot create?

**Evidence used:** Community choice aggregation as a concrete demand-pooling precedent; purchasing-group economics; platform competition; consumer transaction and reputation data.

**Counterevidence:** Heterogeneous preferences may destroy scale economies, antitrust limits may constrain some forms, and the aggregator itself may become a powerful gatekeeper.

**What would make this false?** If software-formed buying coalitions fail to produce persistent savings or operational efficiencies outside a narrow set of already-aggregated markets.

## Chapter 23 — Digital Capitalism in the 2030s

**Central question:** Which market equilibria are plausible, and what institutional choices distinguish them?

**Evidence used:** All prior chapters; current protocol and regulatory trajectories; historical platform transitions; machine-payment developments; lifecycle, credit, and collective-demand extensions.

**Counterevidence:** The strongest alternative is bounded agency: AI remains mainly assistive, humans confirm most transactions, and market structure changes incrementally rather than dramatically.

**What would make this false?** If the book’s proposed signposts—agent-initiated transaction share, merchant dependence, portability, autonomous authorization, advertising migration, switching effects, lifecycle management, credit shopping, and demand aggregation—show little movement through the early 2030s.
