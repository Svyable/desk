# Research brief — The Agentic Consumer Economy

## Thesis under test

The book tests a specific claim rather than assuming an inevitable “AI shopping” future:

As the cost of delegated search, comparison, memory, negotiation, authorization, transaction execution, post-purchase administration, and coalition formation falls, ordinary consumers can acquire capabilities that were historically economical mainly for firms and professional procurement organizations. In competitive markets this can reduce search costs, switching costs, administrative friction, and some forms of seller-side information advantage. At the same time, the agent or platform representing demand can become a powerful new intermediary because it controls consumer context, merchant access, ranking, payment authority, future obligations, and the first interpretation of intent.

The predicted distributional result is conditional: consumer welfare rises most where agent competition, merchant access, portability, recourse, privacy, and underlying product-market competition remain strong. The same technology can instead concentrate value in a small number of agent platforms when consumer memory is sticky, recommendations are commercially conflicted, merchant access is gated, financing is bundled, or meaningful alternatives are weak.

## What this thesis does not claim

This draft does not claim that autonomous agents replace all human shopping, that every purchase becomes price-minimized, that advertising disappears, or that a named 2025–2026 protocol becomes the permanent standard.

It does not treat current vendor forecasts for agentic-commerce volume as established facts.

It does not assume a consumer agent is automatically loyal to the consumer. Business model, defaults, commercial relationships, permissions, data custody, and portability are part of the argument.

It does not assume lower search cost eliminates price dispersion. Research on early internet commerce is an explicit historical counterexample: online markets reduced many frictions while brand, trust, awareness, differentiation, and market power continued to matter.

It does not assume all friction is harmful. The manuscript distinguishes manipulative or administrative friction from reflective friction that supports consent, reversibility, safety, or deliberate choice.

It does not assume an easier return, a cheaper loan, or a larger buying group is automatically welfare-improving. Reverse logistics creates physical costs, credit shifts spending into future income, and collective purchasing can create buyer power that harms suppliers or becomes a new gatekeeper.

It does not assume better consumer representation can bargain away physical scarcity, legal monopoly, infrastructure constraints, geography, skilled-labor shortages, or a household's lack of financial slack. In those settings an agent may make the constraint more legible without removing it.

## Core empirical tests for later editions

A later revision should be able to answer these questions with measured data rather than product announcements:

1. What share of consumer transactions are initiated or completed inside agentic interfaces rather than merchant-controlled websites or apps?
2. How often do consumers authorize agents to transact autonomously versus requiring confirmation?
3. Do consumer agents measurably reduce total prices, fees, renewal costs, cancellation time, return friction, or search time?
4. Do they increase switching rates in insurance, subscriptions, telecom, banking, utilities, travel, and other friction-heavy categories?
5. Does agent-mediated demand reduce or increase price dispersion after product quality, delivery, returns, and service are included?
6. How concentrated are consumer-agent platforms, catalog providers, trust registries, and payment-authority layers?
7. Can consumers export useful preference memory and standing purchasing policies between providers in practice, not merely download raw archives?
8. Can merchants participate in several major agent ecosystems without prohibitive integration costs or commercial terms?
9. Does sponsored influence affect agent candidate sets, rankings, reasoning, or transaction outcomes, and can users detect that influence?
10. Do brands lose power in functional commodity categories while gaining importance in identity, trust, luxury, and experience categories?
11. Do small firms gain cross-border demand, or does machine-readable commerce mainly advantage firms with stronger digital infrastructure?
12. What new fraud, bot-defense, identity, chargeback, and dispute costs arise as autonomous transactions scale?
13. Do machine-to-machine transactions create substantial new productive activity or mainly expand defensive and intermediary activity?
14. Does agentic commerce weaken advertising-funded publishers and creators by intercepting visits and affiliate conversions?
15. Does household use of agents narrow or widen welfare gaps by income, geography, disability, language, and digital skill?
16. Do agents reduce failed purchases and return rates through better matching, or does lower return friction increase bracketing and reverse-logistics volume?
17. Does easier resale and repair materially change product durability, residual values, and primary-market purchasing decisions?
18. Do consumer agents lower effective borrowing costs and reduce late fees or instead increase debt use by making financing frictionless?
19. Does agent shopping increase competition among lenders and insurers without worsening discriminatory segmentation or data lock-in?
20. Do temporary consumer buying groups create measurable savings from lower acquisition and operating costs, and who captures those savings?
21. Does demand aggregation improve small-consumer bargaining without creating a dominant buyer-side intermediary?

## Evidence lanes

### Search costs and electronic marketplaces

The historical baseline comes from electronic-marketplace research, especially Yannis Bakos’s work on buyer search costs and Brynjolfsson and Smith’s study of internet retail pricing. These sources prevent the manuscript from treating cheap comparison as a novel economic mechanism or assuming that lower search cost automatically produces perfectly competitive prices.

### Live agentic-commerce infrastructure

The 2025–2026 evidence includes primary product and protocol documentation from OpenAI, Stripe, Google, Shopify, Mastercard, Amazon, Visa, and participating standards efforts including the FIDO Alliance. These sources establish that structured product discovery, delegated payment authority, agent checkout, machine-readable commerce, merchant recognition, agent authentication, and machine-to-machine payment infrastructure are being built now. They do not prove mass adoption or long-run market structure.

### Consumer protection, pricing, and interface manipulation

FTC surveillance-pricing research and OECD work on dark commercial patterns support the chapters on differentiated pricing, cancellation friction, manipulative interfaces, and the migration of choice architecture from webpages into agent policies.

### Competition and platform power

European Commission Digital Markets Act materials provide a current case study in interoperability, portability, gatekeeper obligations, and the policy logic of contestability. OECD’s 2026 paper on competition and consumer policy in digital markets adds a directly agentic-AI lens: it treats consumer agency, transparency, intermediation, self-preferencing, and the overlap between competition and consumer protection as linked questions. These sources are used as policy precedents and analytical frames, not as claims that existing rules automatically determine future consumer-agent markets.

### Global trade and inclusion

The WTO World Trade Report 2025, WTO-ICC business survey material, World Bank Digital Progress and Trends Report 2025, and Global Findex 2025 ground the discussion of trade costs, small-firm adoption, infrastructure gaps, language/context, digital payments, and international inequality.

### Payments, authority, and trust

Primary materials on Agent Payments Protocol, Mastercard Agent Pay and Verifiable Intent, Visa Trusted Agent Protocol, FIDO Alliance agentic-authentication work, Stripe’s Machine Payments Protocol, Agent Pay for Machines, and related token/mandate concepts support the discussion of verifiable intent, registered agents, delegated authority, merchant recognition, scoped payment authority, and machine-scale payments. These are emerging architectures and must be updated as standards mature.

### Household treasury and cash management

The household-treasury chapter uses a 2025 BIS working paper on GenAI agents for intraday liquidity management in wholesale payment systems as a deliberately limited analogy. The experiment shows a reasoning model handling simulated sequencing and liquidity constraints; it does not establish that consumer agents should autonomously control household finances or that wholesale-bank operations map cleanly onto family budgeting.

The distributional extension is grounded separately in CFPB evidence. Its overdraft/NSF research links frequent fee exposure with greater household financial vulnerability, while its BNPL work documents simultaneous obligations and borrowing across multiple providers. Those sources support the narrower claim that a household-wide agent can improve visibility and sequencing while still being unable to manufacture liquidity, assets, income stability, or affordable credit.

### Returns, repair, and reverse logistics

National Retail Federation research on the 2025 returns landscape provides a current industry estimate of return volume, online return rates, consumer expectations, fraud, and merchant priorities. NRF is an industry body and the figures are survey-based, so the manuscript uses them for scale and direction rather than treating them as an official national ledger. European ecodesign and digital-product-passport policy provides a separate institutional lane for repairability, product information, and lifecycle legibility.

### Consumer credit and financial choice

CFPB research on buy-now-pay-later use supplies measured evidence about fragmented installment borrowing, simultaneous loans, and market growth. It is paired with broader fair-lending and consumer-finance law rather than used to argue that installment products are inherently harmful. Research on telematics and consumer data is used as counterevidence to simplistic portability claims: proprietary information can create consumer value as well as lock-in.

### Collective purchasing and organized demand

Existing community choice aggregation programs show that small consumers can gain procurement leverage by pooling electricity demand under enabling law. The chapter uses this as a concrete precedent for demand aggregation, not as proof that every consumer market can or should be collectivized. The broader claim remains a hypothesis: software may lower the administrative cost of temporary buying coalitions enough to make them viable in more categories.

## Counterevidence and alternative paths

Several developments would materially weaken the strongest version of the book’s argument:

- Consumers may prefer AI-assisted research but reject autonomous purchasing for most categories.
- Security, fraud, liability, or chargeback costs may make broad delegation uneconomical.
- Merchant websites and apps may remain central because consumers enjoy browsing, brands insist on controlled experiences, or agent access is blocked.
- Open protocols may keep the agent layer unusually competitive and prevent durable gatekeeper rents.
- Preference memory may remain shallow enough that switching costs never become severe.
- On-device models and user-controlled data stores may decentralize consumer context.
- Advertising may adapt successfully through clearly separated sponsored offers without materially corrupting recommendations.
- Sellers may respond to consumer optimization by lowering posted prices and simplifying offers rather than escalating personalization and negotiation.
- Regulators may standardize cancellation, portability, fees, and disclosure so effectively that some of the consumer-agent advantage becomes unnecessary.
- Logistics, product quality, trust, and physical service may dominate purchase decisions enough that digital search improvements yield smaller price effects than expected.
- Agent tools may diffuse more slowly in low-income countries, making the global story primarily one of rich-country convenience rather than broad trade inclusion.
- Easier returns may increase physical throughput enough to offset gains from better matching, repair, and resale.
- Lenders may restrict agent access, or the most attractive financial offers may remain unavailable to households that most need them.
- Demand aggregation may remain confined to a few regulated or standardized markets because heterogeneous preferences destroy group economies of scale.
- Collective consumer agents may become another concentrated intermediary rather than a counterweight to platform power.
- In scarcity-bound markets, better search may mainly improve allocation or document the bottleneck rather than lower prices or expand supply.

## Falsification discipline

Every chapter brief contains a “What would make this false?” note. These are not rhetorical gestures. A later edition should update or remove claims that fail them.

When a vendor supplies a forecast, market-size estimate, adoption statistic, or conversion claim, treat it as the vendor’s evidence unless an independent source verifies it. Prefer observed transaction data, field experiments, regulatory records, and cross-platform measurements when available.

Distinguish capabilities from adoption. A protocol supporting autonomous checkout is not evidence that consumers broadly use autonomous checkout.

Distinguish technical openness from practical contestability. An open specification can coexist with concentrated catalogs, identity, distribution, payment rails, or default placement.

Distinguish consumer surplus from transfer. A lower merchant margin may be a transfer to consumers; reduced search time can be newly created value; a lower fee that causes a service to disappear may have a longer-run cost. The book should keep these mechanisms separate.

Distinguish transaction convenience from lifecycle welfare. A product that is easy to buy can be costly to return, repair, finance, or dispose of. The agentic consumer thesis is strongest when the system optimizes the whole relationship rather than merely conversion.

Distinguish removable friction from underlying scarcity. Better representation can reduce search, administrative, and switching costs without creating housing, infrastructure, skilled labor, physical capacity, or household financial slack.
