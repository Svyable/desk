*How Neurons Are Changing the Biotech Stack*

A narrative nonfiction book about the moment living neural systems stop being passive specimens and become active infrastructure. From induced pluripotent stem cells and self-organizing brain organoids to multi-electrode arrays, closed-loop assays, remote wetware platforms, patient-specific drug screens, and the first commercially deployable biological computers, the book follows a new stack taking shape underneath biotechnology.

The central claim is deliberately narrower than the hype around “brains in a dish.” Neurons matter because they are stateful experimental substrates. They respond, adapt, remember prior stimulation, reorganize their connectivity, and can be interrogated repeatedly while an experiment is running. Once that property becomes reliable and standardized, the biotech stack changes: the assay becomes interactive; the instrument becomes a two-way interface; software starts encoding stimuli rather than merely analyzing outputs; pharmacology can measure effects on plasticity and information processing; and a patient’s own cells can become a functional model that behaves over time rather than a static molecular snapshot.

This is not a prediction that wetware will replace silicon, nor a claim that current neural organoids are conscious. The more immediate revolution is in biotechnology itself: a shift from measuring biology to running closed-loop experiments with biology.

## What this is

A complete first-pass trade-book manuscript organized around a new systems thesis: living neural tissue is becoming an interfaceable, programmable-by-feedback, human-relevant layer in biotechnology. The book distinguishes 2D biological neural networks, neural spheroids, brain organoids, assembloids, organs-on-chip, and commercial “biocomputing” systems rather than collapsing them into the inaccurate phrase “mini-brains.”

The reporting spine begins with the 2006–2007 induced-pluripotency breakthrough and the 2013 cerebral-organoid paper, then follows the emergence of high-density electrophysiology, DishBrain, Brainoware, the Organoid Intelligence program, FinalSpark’s remotely accessible Neuroplatform, Cortical Labs’ CL1 and Cortical Cloud, the FDA’s 2025–2026 New Approach Methodologies push, the NIH Standardized Organoid Modeling Center, and 2025–2026 work on functional drug screening and organoid-based tactile classification.

## Fact boundary

This book describes a fast-moving research and commercialization frontier. Company descriptions of energy efficiency, learning speed, product capabilities, and future applications are treated as claims unless independently demonstrated. Current organoids do not reproduce a complete human brain, and the scientific community explicitly discourages terms such as “mini-brain” and “brain in a dish.” The 2025 ISSCR guidelines state that there is currently no biological evidence of consciousness or pain perception in CNS organoids that warrants specialized oversight, while also advising researchers to remain alert as models become more complex. Regulatory use of organoids and other New Approach Methodologies is expanding, but validation, reproducibility, context-of-use, and acceptance requirements remain active constraints.
