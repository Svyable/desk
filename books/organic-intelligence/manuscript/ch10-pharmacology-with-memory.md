# Pharmacology With Memory

A drug does not enter an empty system.

It enters a body that has eaten, slept, aged, adapted, compensated, been treated before, and perhaps lived with disease for years. Receptors have been regulated up or down. Circuits have reorganized. Metabolic pathways have adjusted. The patient’s current biology is partly an accumulation of previous biology.

Drug development knows this in principle and often simplifies it in practice.

The simplification is necessary. A screening cascade cannot reproduce a lifetime. Early assays ask controlled questions because controlled questions can be answered. Does the compound bind the target? Does it inhibit the enzyme? Does it kill the cell? Does it alter a channel? Does it trigger a known safety signal?

Neural wetware creates a way to add a small amount of history back.

Not a human history. An experimental one.

A living neural network can be recorded before exposure, challenged during exposure, and observed after exposure. The same tissue can carry the consequence of earlier stimulation into the next measurement. A compound can be evaluated not only for what it does to activity but for what it does to adaptation.

This is pharmacology with memory.

The phrase does not require a new branch of pharmacology. Chronic exposure, tolerance, sensitization, withdrawal, receptor adaptation, synaptic plasticity, and long-term changes are established concepts. What changes is the availability of human-derived neural systems in which some of those dynamics can be measured repeatedly at network level.

Safety pharmacology offers an immediate example.

A central nervous system drug can alter neuronal excitability in ways that raise seizure risk. Human induced-pluripotent-stem-cell-derived neurons grown on multi-electrode arrays are already used as tools for examining electrophysiological effects of compounds, including changes in firing and network bursting. The value is not that an in-vitro network reproduces a human seizure. It is that a human neural system can reveal compound-induced shifts in excitability and coordination that a molecular binding assay alone would not show.

A closed-loop platform can make the challenge more demanding.

Instead of observing spontaneous activity before and after dosing, the experiment can apply standardized stimulation and measure how the network responds across concentrations and time. A compound that leaves baseline activity within an acceptable range may nevertheless lower the threshold for runaway bursting under challenge. Another may suppress all activity and look “calm” while functionally impairing the network.

The difference between stabilization and silencing matters.

This becomes particularly important in disease models.

In 2025, researchers reported a multi-organoid drug-screening system combining microfluidics with three-dimensional electrophysiology. Among its demonstrations was an SCN2A-related epilepsy model derived from patient-specific cells. The platform could expose multiple brain organoids to compounds and record dose-dependent changes in network activity.

The experiment points toward a future in which treatment response is measured against a pathological functional phenotype rather than a generic molecular target.

That is a different development strategy.

Traditional target-based discovery begins with a mechanism the scientist believes matters. A compound is designed or selected to change that target. Downstream assays test whether the desired biology follows. Phenotypic discovery begins with a system that behaves abnormally and searches for compounds that correct the behavior, sometimes before the full mechanism is known.

Patient-derived neural wetware expands the phenotypic option.

A disease-associated network can become the thing to rescue.

This is attractive in neurological disease because causal chains are often long. A mutation may alter a channel. The channel alters excitability. Excitability changes network development. The network develops compensations. Symptoms emerge at the organism level. A drug aimed only at the molecular defect may not restore the network state that disease created.

A functional assay can reveal the gap between target engagement and system recovery.

That does not make molecular understanding obsolete.

A compound that “fixes” an electrophysiological score by nonspecifically shutting down the network is not necessarily therapeutic. Researchers still need mechanism, toxicology, pharmacokinetics, selectivity, and clinical evidence. The functional assay is another layer of evidence, not a replacement for the rest of pharmacology.

Its value comes from integration.

The neural network integrates channels, receptors, synapses, cell types, developmental state, and prior activity into behavior that can be measured. The assay may expose an effect that no single target readout predicts because the network has done the integration biologically.

This is the same reason whole-cell phenotypic screens can discover unexpected mechanisms.

Organic intelligence adds time to that integration.

Consider tolerance.

A drug may produce a strong response on first exposure and a weaker response after repeated dosing. In a living neural assay, that changing response can become part of the dataset. The mechanism could involve receptor desensitization, homeostatic adaptation, network plasticity, altered expression, or other processes. The platform does not need to know which mechanism is responsible before it can observe the trajectory.

Consider washout.

After the compound is removed, does activity return to baseline? Immediately? Slowly? Does the network overshoot? Does a learned response reappear? Does a prior exposure make the network more sensitive to the next dose?

Those are ordinary pharmacological questions made experimentally accessible at a different level.

Consider combinations.

Neurological patients often take multiple drugs. Two compounds that look acceptable alone may interact at network level. A stateful assay can test order and timing as well as simultaneous exposure. That flexibility creates a combinatorial explosion, but adaptive experimental design can help prioritize conditions.

The platform can become a place where pharmacology and control theory meet.

A control engineer asks how a system responds to input, how stable it is, whether perturbations die out or grow, and how feedback changes behavior. A pharmacologist asks how a compound changes biological function across dose and time. A neural wetware assay lets both questions be asked on the same tissue.

The resulting language may eventually become more quantitative.

A compound could be described by how it shifts the network’s response curve, recovery time, excitability threshold, synchronization regime, or adaptive capacity. Those functional parameters could be compared across donors, disease models, and treatments.

This is not yet a universal pharmacological vocabulary.

It is one reason standardization matters so much.

A recovery time measured on one platform may not match a recovery time on another if stimulation, electrode geometry, maturation, or analysis differs. Before dynamic neural metrics can become widely trusted, the field needs reference protocols and shared benchmarks.

Known drugs can provide some of those anchors.

Compounds with established excitatory, inhibitory, sedative, or seizurogenic effects can be tested across platforms. If a neural assay consistently detects expected categories and differentiates mechanisms where appropriate, confidence grows. If results change unpredictably with batch, line, or instrument, the assay remains exploratory.

Validation will be earned compound by compound and context by context.

The same logic applies to developmental neurotoxicity.

A chemical that affects the developing nervous system may alter proliferation, migration, neurite outgrowth, synapse formation, network maturation, or plasticity. No single in-vitro model captures all of those processes. Researchers have proposed combining organoid models with organoid-intelligence approaches so that the ability to adapt under feedback becomes an additional functional endpoint.

The proposal is compelling precisely because it can fail.

If known developmental neurotoxicants impair defined plasticity measures while negative controls do not, and if the result reproduces across lines and laboratories, the assay gains value. If every chemical changes a complex network in some idiosyncratic way, interpretation becomes impossible. If plasticity metrics correlate poorly with meaningful developmental outcomes, the additional complexity is not justified.

A good new assay should make itself vulnerable to falsification.

The commercial pressure can push in the opposite direction. Platform companies benefit from broad claims: more human-relevant, more intelligent, more predictive, more efficient. Drug developers need narrower ones.

Which liability did the assay predict?

Which candidate did it rank correctly?

Which decision changed?

Which late-stage failure might it have caught?

The answer to those questions will determine whether neural functional screening becomes infrastructure or remains a specialist technique.

There is a path to value even if the assay never predicts clinical efficacy directly.

Drug development contains many intermediate decisions. A platform can be useful by identifying compounds with undesirable network effects, selecting doses for further study, stratifying mechanistically distinct disease models, revealing whether a gene correction restores function, or prioritizing candidates before expensive animal studies.

Incremental predictive value can be worth millions if it arrives at the right decision point.

This is why the FDA’s movement toward New Approach Methodologies matters beyond animal ethics.

The agency’s 2025 roadmap and 2026 draft guidance create more room for human-relevant in-vitro systems to contribute to drug-development evidence when they are validated for a defined use. Organoids are part of that larger family of methods. Neural wetware could eventually offer functional endpoints within that framework.

But regulatory openness is not a lowered bar.

A complex assay can face a higher validation burden because there are more ways for it to vary.

The field will need to show precision, repeatability, reproducibility, sensitivity, specificity where relevant, robustness to routine variation, and a clear relationship between the assay output and the decision being supported. Machine-learning components may need version control and locked performance characteristics. Biological lots may need predefined acceptance criteria.

Pharmacology with memory becomes regulatory science with memory too.

Every platform update can matter.

A new differentiation protocol may improve maturation and change historical baselines. A new electrode array may increase signal quality but alter feature distributions. A better decoder may classify old recordings differently. A new medium may extend tissue life while shifting excitability.

Software companies celebrate continuous deployment.

Validated biotechnology cannot always move that way.

Once an assay is used to support consequential decisions, changes need bridging studies. The platform may need to maintain old methods while proving that new ones are equivalent or better. Innovation becomes slower precisely because the output matters more.

This is not bureaucracy fighting progress.

It is what happens when an experiment becomes infrastructure.

There is another reason memory complicates pharmacology: the disease itself may be developmental.

A patient-specific organoid grown in vitro does not carry the patient’s complete life history, but it can reproduce aspects of developmental trajectory. A treatment applied early in organoid maturation may have a different effect from the same treatment applied later. Researchers can use that timing experimentally in ways impossible in patients.

The platform can ask counterfactual developmental questions.

What happens if the molecular defect is corrected before the network phenotype appears? What happens after? Which changes are reversible? Which persist even when the original trigger is removed?

Those questions can separate cause from scar.

A disease may begin with one molecular event and later become maintained by a reorganized system. Treating the original cause late may therefore be insufficient. Neural organoids will not answer this perfectly, but they can provide a controlled human model in which timing is manipulated rather than inferred.

That is a powerful scientific use of biological memory.

It also reframes the promise of personalized medicine.

The naive version says: grow a patient’s neurons, try every drug, and choose the best one.

The realistic version is more selective. Create a patient-derived model when the disease biology can be reproduced in vitro, when the functional phenotype is measurable, when candidate treatments can be tested on a clinically relevant timescale, and when differences among treatments are large enough to justify the effort.

The platform does not need to become a universal avatar.

It needs to become a good experiment.

Memory helps when the clinical problem itself depends on history.

That could include epilepsy, neurodevelopmental disorders, neurodegenerative disease, chronic exposure, and treatment adaptation. It may be less useful for questions that can already be answered by simpler assays.

The field should welcome that limitation.

A technology that knows where it is unnecessary has a better chance of becoming necessary somewhere else.

The neurons do not make pharmacology magical.

They make it temporal.

A compound arrives. The network changes. The next stimulus lands on a different network from the one that existed before the compound.

The assay remembers because the biology does.

That is the beginning of a drug model that can fight back.

There is one pharmacological boundary a dish cannot negotiate away: exposure in the dish is not exposure in the brain.

A nominal concentration in culture medium can be useful for comparing compounds, but it is not automatically the concentration a human neuron would encounter after a patient swallows or receives the drug. In a person, absorption, distribution, protein binding, metabolism, transport across the blood-brain barrier, active metabolites, clearance, and tissue sequestration all shape exposure.

The organoid sees only what the experimental system delivers.

That means functional response has to be interpreted alongside pharmacokinetics.

A compound that rescues an organoid phenotype at one hundred micromolar may be uninteresting if safe human exposure never approaches that level. A compound that appears inactive at the nominal test concentration may be much more potent under clinically relevant unbound exposure conditions. A metabolite absent from the assay could be the species that matters in patients.

The living model does not remove PK/PD.

It makes the PD layer richer.

This distinction can sharpen screening design. Instead of sweeping arbitrary concentrations for maximum effect, researchers can prioritize ranges connected to plausible human exposure. Where protein binding in culture medium differs from plasma, unbound concentration can become more informative than nominal concentration. If metabolism is important, upstream liver models, microsomes, or organ-on-chip systems may need to generate metabolites before the neural tissue is exposed.

The biotech stack can become multi-organ before it becomes organismal.

A liver model can transform a compound.

A barrier model can estimate transport.

A neural model can measure function.

No individual model becomes a patient, but the chain can reproduce more of the causal path than one isolated organoid.

This is another reason the future is likely hybrid rather than dominated by one spectacular tissue.

The same logic applies to rescue.

A good electrophysiological outcome should be defined in more than one dimension. If a disease organoid shows pathological bursting, the easiest way to remove the phenotype may be to suppress activity globally. That is pharmacologically trivial and clinically unsatisfying.

A stronger rescue preserves function while reducing pathology.

The assay might therefore measure baseline activity, evoked responsiveness, pathological burst frequency, recovery after challenge, and perhaps viability together. A candidate that improves one dimension while destroying another is not a clean rescue.

This creates a response surface rather than a single score.

One drug may reduce hyperexcitability but slow recovery.

Another may leave spontaneous bursting modestly elevated while restoring stimulus discrimination.

A third may normalize the network only after repeated dosing.

Choosing among them requires a theory of which dimensions matter clinically.

The dish can reveal tradeoffs.

It cannot decide their medical importance by itself.

History also makes dosing schedules experimentally interesting. Two regimens with the same total amount of drug can produce different network trajectories if peak concentration, interval, and recovery matter. Chronic low exposure may induce adaptation that an acute pulse does not. Intermittent treatment may allow recovery or create sensitization.

A stateful assay can test schedule as part of pharmacology.

That becomes valuable when clinical dosing already involves repeated cycles or titration.

The experiment can ask not merely “Which drug?” but “Which pattern of exposure produces the most durable functional rescue without progressive loss of responsiveness?”

Again, this is not a replacement for clinical pharmacology.

It is a human neural layer that can make schedule hypotheses more informed before patients carry the risk.

The deepest validation question is whether a neural result changes when exposure realism improves.

If the effect disappears when the assay moves from nominal concentration to clinically plausible unbound exposure, the original finding was a laboratory artifact of dosing. If a metabolite produces the network phenotype while the parent compound does not, the assay has taught the development team which chemical species matters. If a barrier model limits exposure below the effective range, the compound may need chemistry or delivery changes before the neural rescue is relevant.

This is where organic intelligence becomes part of a pharmacology stack rather than a standalone screen.

The output should travel with an exposure statement: what chemical species reached the tissue, at what free concentration, for how long, under which medium conditions, and with what recovery interval. Without that context, a beautiful neural trajectory can be impossible to translate.

Reference pharmacology should travel with it too.

A platform can test compounds whose mechanisms and clinical effects are already well characterized, not because those drugs need rediscovery but because they reveal what the model can distinguish. Does a known sedative reduce activity in a way the assay separates from toxicity? Does a known pro-convulsant alter the challenge threshold? Does a channel modulator produce the expected direction of network change? Do effects scale with plausible exposure?

These references create a functional coordinate system.

They also make negative results more informative. If the platform fails to detect a known class effect, the problem may be the model, the exposure, or the readout. That failure should be investigated before an unknown compound is interpreted confidently.

A development team can then use the assay as one rung in an evidence ladder.

Target engagement says the compound reached its molecular mechanism.

Exposure modeling says the relevant concentration is plausible.

The neural assay says the human-derived network changed functionally.

Other models say whether systemic biology introduces liabilities the neural tissue cannot see.

Clinical data, eventually, say whether any of it mattered to patients.

No rung is sufficient by itself.

The value of the neural rung is that it can reveal a functional consequence between molecular mechanism and whole-organism outcome.

The deeper promise of pharmacology with memory is therefore not that organoids remember drugs the way people remember events.

It is that treatment history, exposure realism, and network function can become controlled variables rather than afterthoughts.

Dose, order, challenge, washout, recovery, re-exposure, and metabolite context can be designed deliberately.

The resulting trajectory may contain information that a terminal endpoint throws away.

That information becomes valuable only when it is connected back to the pharmacological realities outside the dish.
