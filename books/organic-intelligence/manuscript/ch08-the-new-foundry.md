# The New Foundry

A foundry is a promise that someone else has mastered repetition.

In semiconductors, a designer can specify a chip and send the design to a manufacturing system built around astonishing control. The foundry does not guarantee that every atom will be where a human imagined it, but it has reduced enough variation that engineers can build complex products on top of a predictable process.

Biotechnology has spent decades building its own versions of foundries.

Contract manufacturers produce therapeutic proteins. Cell-therapy facilities expand living cells under controlled conditions. Sequencing centers turn samples into standardized data. Biobanks collect, characterize, and distribute biological material. Organoid platforms are now moving toward the same industrial question: can a self-organizing living model be manufactured with enough consistency that the user can trust what variation remains?

The word *manufactured* can sound wrong for an organoid.

The cells do much of the organizing themselves. They divide, differentiate, migrate, secrete signals, respond to neighbors, and build tissue-like structures. A technician cannot place each cell according to a blueprint.

But manufacturing has never required total control over every microscopic event. It requires control over the process variables that determine whether the output meets specification.

That is the new foundry problem.

In the early years of organoid research, success itself was the important result. Could a three-dimensional human neural structure form? Could it reproduce a developmental feature? Could a patient-derived model reveal a disease phenotype? Individual laboratories optimized protocols around those questions.

Once the answers became yes, a different set of failures became visible.

One batch behaved differently from another. One induced-pluripotent-stem-cell line differentiated beautifully while another resisted. Some organoids grew large and organized; others remained small or stressed. Different laboratories used slightly different matrices, media, timing, agitation, and analysis. A result that was dramatic in one setting could be difficult to reproduce elsewhere.

This is not evidence that organoids do not work.

It is evidence that the technology has reached the stage where process engineering matters as much as biological discovery.

In 2024, researchers reported what they called high-quantity brain organoids designed specifically to improve scale and reliability. They generated thousands of organoids across several induced-pluripotent-stem-cell lines and demonstrated applications including microcephaly modeling, glioma invasion, cryopreservation, and a medium-throughput drug screen.

The title of the work emphasized reliability.

That choice is a marker of maturity. A frontier field begins by celebrating possibility. An industrial field begins asking about coefficients of variation.

The U.S. National Institutes of Health made the same transition visible in 2025 when it announced the Standardized Organoid Modeling Center. The agency committed $87 million over the first three years to a national effort aimed at developing standardized organoid models that could reduce reliance on animal systems and improve reproducibility.

A government does not create a standardization center because organoids are a novelty.

It creates one because organoids are becoming important enough that inconsistency has become a national research bottleneck.

The foundry has several layers.

The first is the cell bank.

A reliable organoid process begins with a reliable source of pluripotent cells. Lines have to be authenticated, tested for contamination, examined for genomic integrity, and tracked across passages. If patient-specific models are involved, donor provenance and consent become part of the manufacturing record. If gene editing is used, edited clones need characterization against appropriate controls.

The second layer is differentiation.

Growth factors, small molecules, extracellular matrix, media composition, timing, and physical conditions push pluripotent cells toward particular neural identities. Small changes can alter which cell types emerge and in what proportions. Reagents from different suppliers or lots can behave differently. Manual handling can introduce operator effects.

The third layer is assembly.

Cells may be aggregated into embryoid bodies, patterned, embedded, transferred into dynamic culture, fused into assembloids, or combined with other cell types. Size matters because diffusion matters. Geometry matters because gradients matter. The number of starting cells can influence later organization.

The fourth layer is maturation.

Time becomes a manufacturing input.

A silicon wafer does not need to spend ninety days becoming more itself. Neural tissue does. Networks form gradually. Synapses mature. Electrical activity changes. Cell populations continue to develop. Some phenotypes emerge only after long culture periods.

This makes inventory expensive.

A company cannot always respond to a sudden order by turning a dial and producing mature organoids tomorrow. It may need cohorts already growing, with expected attrition and quality variation. The production system begins to resemble a nursery crossed with a semiconductor fab.

The fifth layer is qualification.

What makes an organoid good enough to use?

The answer depends on the application. A developmental model may require particular cell-type markers and spatial features. An electrophysiological assay may require a minimum number of active electrodes, stable burst behavior, or defined responses to reference stimuli. A drug screen may need a narrow baseline distribution so that treatment effects can be detected. A computing experiment may care about responsiveness and plasticity more than anatomical fidelity.

The specification is downstream of the use case.

This is why “more brain-like” is an inadequate quality metric.

An organoid can be biologically complex and operationally useless. A simpler neural spheroid can be superior if it produces the relevant phenotype consistently. Industrial biology rewards fit-for-purpose models.

The sixth layer is cryopreservation.

Freezing biological material is one of biotechnology’s great logistical technologies because it separates production time from experiment time. Cell banks are useful partly because a characterized batch can be stored. Organoids are harder. Freezing three-dimensional tissues without damaging architecture or function can be difficult, and recovery may alter state.

The high-quantity organoid work included cryopreservation because reliable storage can transform the economics of a platform.

If standardized organoids can be produced in large batches, qualified, frozen, shipped, thawed, and recovered with acceptable functional consistency, laboratories no longer need to grow every model from the beginning. The biological supply chain becomes more modular.

If they cannot, production remains more local and time-bound.

The seventh layer is automation.

Manual organoid culture is a tax on scale. Every pipetting step creates labor cost and opportunity for variation. Every judgment call made by eye creates dependence on tacit expertise. Automated liquid handling, imaging, environmental control, and machine-learning-assisted quality assessment can convert craft into process.

But automation has to be designed around biology rather than imposed on it.

A robot can pipette the wrong volume with perfect reproducibility. A vision model can classify malformed organoids consistently while misunderstanding what matters. Automation removes operator variation only if the automated protocol itself is biologically sound.

This is why foundries need metrology.

Semiconductor manufacturing depends on measurement at every stage. Defects are identified before later processing adds more cost. Neural organoid manufacturing needs an analogous culture of in-process measurement.

Size, morphology, marker expression, viability, oxygen stress, cellular composition, genomic integrity, imaging features, and electrophysiological activity can all contribute to a quality profile. The best measurements will be those that predict downstream assay performance without destroying the sample.

Non-destructive quality control is especially valuable because the product is also the experiment.

A sequencing assay can consume a sample. A neural organoid intended for longitudinal testing must survive qualification. Imaging, extracellular recordings, secreted biomarkers, and environmental telemetry may become ways to certify state while preserving the tissue.

That creates a feedback loop inside manufacturing itself.

If early measurements predict later failure, the foundry can cull poor organoids before expensive maturation. If one reagent lot shifts development, process-control systems can catch the drift. If a donor line requires a modified differentiation schedule, the process can branch in a controlled way.

The foundry becomes adaptive too.

There is a risk in the analogy to chip manufacturing. Semiconductor fabs chase extreme uniformity because every transistor of a given design is supposed to behave within narrow limits. Personalized organoid platforms may need controlled diversity.

A disease cohort should not be normalized until every patient looks the same.

The goal is to reduce process variance while preserving biological variance.

This is the central manufacturing problem of precision medicine.

Imagine a set of organoids derived from twenty patients with the same diagnosis. The platform finds that five show one electrophysiological phenotype, ten show another, and five show no clear abnormality. That spread could reveal meaningful subtypes. It could also be a batch artifact.

A foundry exists to make the artifact explanation less plausible.

The better the process control, the more confidently researchers can interpret remaining differences as biology.

This is why standardization can increase discovery rather than suppress it.

A badly standardized model produces so much noise that unusual biology is hard to see. A well-standardized model creates a quieter background against which patient variation becomes sharper.

The foundry also changes what counts as an experiment.

In an artisanal laboratory, a researcher may spend months generating the organoids before collecting the data that appear in a figure. In a platform model, production and experimentation separate. One team optimizes cell manufacturing. Another team develops interfaces. Another writes stimulation protocols. Another analyzes data. Another validates assays against clinical or animal outcomes.

Specialization begins.

That specialization is economically important because no single laboratory can be best at every layer of the stack.

A pharmaceutical company may not want to become an expert in neural differentiation. A wetware-computing company may not want to operate a large donor-consent program. An academic lab may invent a valuable assay but lack process-development resources. Contract development and manufacturing organizations, reagent suppliers, instrument makers, and specialized organoid producers can fill the gaps.

The ecosystem around the cells may eventually be larger than the companies selling “biological computers.”

This has precedent. Biotechnology platforms create picks-and-shovels industries. Polymerase chain reaction created enzymes, thermal cyclers, reagents, diagnostics, and workflows. Next-generation sequencing created library-preparation kits, sequencers, bioinformatics pipelines, cloud infrastructure, and clinical interpretation companies. Cell therapy created viral-vector suppliers, cell-processing equipment, cryogenic logistics, and specialized manufacturing.

Organoid intelligence can create its own supply chain.

Defined media. Matrices. Cell lines. bioreactors. microfluidic consumables. 3D electrode interfaces. imaging systems. environmental sensors. reference compounds. quality-control software. biological provenance systems. remote experiment platforms.

None of these requires wetware to beat conventional computing.

They require researchers to care enough about living neural models to buy reliable infrastructure.

Standardization will also determine whether regulators can trust the field.

A regulator evaluating a New Approach Methodology needs to know whether results are repeatable within a laboratory and reproducible across laboratories. If a platform’s performance depends on one expert technician, one proprietary reagent lot, or one unusually cooperative cell line, it will struggle to become part of a durable evidence framework.

The path to regulatory acceptance therefore runs through manufacturing science.

This is why the NIH Standardized Organoid Modeling Center could matter more to the long-term biotech stack than any single computing benchmark. A benchmark proves that one system can do something. A standardization program asks whether many systems can do the same biologically relevant thing often enough to support decisions.

The latter is less cinematic.

It is how medicine changes.

The foundry problem also sets a boundary on speed.

Software iteration is fast because copies are cheap and execution is immediate. Biological iteration is constrained by maturation. A failed differentiation may not reveal itself fully for weeks. A protocol change can take months to evaluate. The feedback cycle of product development is therefore slower than founders trained on software may expect.

Automation can shorten handling time. Better protocols can improve yield. Parallel batches can increase experimental throughput. Cryopreservation can buffer inventory. But a neuron still needs to become a neuron.

Biology retains the right to take time.

That constraint may become a source of competitive advantage for companies that learn to operate it well. If a platform develops a large bank of characterized lines, longitudinal performance data, robust differentiation protocols, and predictive in-process quality controls, competitors cannot copy that operating knowledge from a patent abstract.

The moat is partly biological memory stored in an organization.

That is a different kind of intellectual property.

The cells have memory. The company does too.

A mature foundry will encode thousands of small lessons about what predicts a good neural substrate: which morphological changes matter, which electrophysiological patterns precede failure, which reagent lots shift maturation, how long a recovered frozen organoid needs before testing, how donor lines behave, which stimulation protocols are robust to batch variation.

Most of that knowledge will never make a headline.

It will decide whether the headline technology works.

The organoid is self-organizing.

The industry around it cannot be.
