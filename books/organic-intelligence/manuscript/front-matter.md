## Author’s Note

There is an easy version of this book to write. It begins with a photograph of a translucent clump of cells, calls it a miniature brain, announces that scientists have taught it to think, and spends the next three hundred pages deciding whether the future belongs to silicon or biology.

That is not this book.

The cells are real. The interfaces are real. The experiments are real. The field is moving quickly enough that a remotely accessible platform can now let a researcher send electrical stimuli to living neural tissue from another country, and a commercial device can package neurons, electrodes, fluid handling, environmental control, and software into a box intended for laboratories. In 2026 researchers reported that responses from human forebrain organoids could be used to classify tactile Braille inputs, with an ensemble of three organoids improving on a single one. None of this needs fictional embellishment.

But the most interesting consequence is not that biology might become a new kind of computer. The more immediate consequence is that biotechnology is acquiring a new kind of experimental object.

A conventional assay is usually imagined as something done *to* biology. A compound is added. A gene is edited. A fluorescent marker changes. A concentration rises or falls. A microscope, sequencer, mass spectrometer, or plate reader records the result. The biological material can be complicated, but the logic of the experiment is often linear: perturb, wait, measure.

Neural systems complicate that logic because a functioning network has history. It can change after stimulation. Its future response can depend on what happened earlier. A chemical may not simply kill a cell or alter the abundance of a protein; it may change excitability, synchronization, plasticity, or the network’s ability to adapt under repeated feedback. The assay can become a conversation, although that word risks suggesting more psychology than the biology warrants. More precisely, the assay becomes a loop.

That loop is the subject of this book.

I use **organic intelligence** as a broad trade-book term for the emerging use of living neural systems as adaptive, information-processing components in biotechnology. Researchers use several more precise terms. **Biological neural networks** can refer to cultures of neurons connected and measured in vitro. **Synthetic biological intelligence** has been used for closed-loop systems built from living neurons and digital environments. **Organoid intelligence** is a field organized around three-dimensional neural organoids, interfaces, learning, and biocomputing. **Wetware computing** reaches still wider, encompassing computation performed in biological or chemical substrates.

Those categories overlap. They are not interchangeable.

The distinction between a two-dimensional neuronal culture and a three-dimensional neural organoid matters. So does the distinction between an organoid and an assembloid, in which organoids or specialized cell types are combined to model interactions. In 2022 a large group of researchers published a nomenclature consensus precisely because public language was running ahead of scientific precision. They discouraged phrases such as “mini-brain” and “brain in a dish.” I follow that guidance. An organoid can reproduce meaningful features of neural development or function without being a small version of a human brain.

The same restraint applies to intelligence. When an in-vitro neural network changes its behavior under feedback, that is scientifically interesting. It does not automatically establish understanding, experience, or consciousness. The word *learning* can describe measurable adaptation in a defined experimental protocol without carrying every implication it has when used about a child, an animal, or a person. Where the literature makes a stronger claim, I try to describe the experiment first and the interpretation second.

There is another reason to keep the terms clean. The field is not entering biotechnology through one door.

One door is drug discovery. Patient-derived induced pluripotent stem cells can be differentiated into neurons or organized into three-dimensional models, creating systems that carry a donor’s genetic background into the laboratory. Another door is toxicology, where regulators and research agencies are actively encouraging human-relevant alternatives to some animal studies. Another is disease modeling, particularly for conditions in which the relevant tissue is inaccessible during life and the differences between human and animal neurobiology matter. Another is instrumentation: multi-electrode arrays, microfluidics, 3D probes, imaging, and automated culture systems are making living neural networks easier to stimulate and monitor. Another is software: the experimental environment itself can be encoded, deployed, altered, and repeated.

Only after those doors comes the one that receives the most attention: computing.

That is why the subtitle refers to the biotech stack rather than the future of computers. A stack is useful because it forces us to ask what depends on what. Living neurons require a cell source, differentiation protocols, quality control, nutrients, gases, temperature, fluidics, sterile handling, electrodes, stimulation logic, recording hardware, signal processing, experimental software, statistical models, and regulatory interpretation. A biological computer that cannot keep its substrate healthy is not a computer for long. A personalized neural assay that cannot be manufactured reproducibly is not a platform. An elegant organoid that produces different results in every batch is not yet industrial infrastructure.

The hard engineering sits beneath the spectacle.

This book therefore moves from cell reprogramming to organoid formation, from electrophysiology to closed-loop experiments, from remote APIs to reproducibility, from pharmacology to patient-specific models, and finally into computation, economics, regulation, consent, and moral status. The order is intentional. It follows the dependency graph.

The argument is also falsifiable. If neural functional assays remain too variable, too expensive, too fragile, or too poorly predictive to win any important place in drug development or toxicology, then much of the thesis weakens. If patient-derived neural models add no useful information beyond genotype and conventional biomarkers, the personalized-medicine case shrinks. If regulators find that plasticity-sensitive endpoints add complexity without improving prediction, the closed loop stays mostly an academic instrument.

Conversely, the thesis does not require a warehouse of living neurons to beat a GPU cluster. It becomes true in a more modest and, I think, more consequential way if adaptive human neural models become routine in a few valuable corners of biotechnology.

That possibility is now close enough to examine without science fiction.

The story begins with a game of Pong.
