# Organic Intelligence — research brief

Checked and drafted September 4, 2026.

## Governing question

What changes in biotechnology when living neural systems become not merely biological samples to inspect, but adaptive experimental infrastructure that can receive structured inputs, alter their own state, and produce time-dependent outputs?

The book uses **organic intelligence** as a trade-book umbrella for the practical stack forming around living neural information processing. It includes work that researchers variously describe as biological neural networks, synthetic biological intelligence, wetware computing, and organoid intelligence. The term is not used to erase the scientific distinctions among 2D neuronal cultures, neural spheroids, brain organoids, assembloids, or transplanted organoids. Those distinctions matter and are maintained throughout the manuscript.

## Primary thesis

The near-term significance of neural wetware is more credible in **biotechnology** than in general-purpose computing. Current systems are tiny, variable, fragile, slow to manufacture, difficult to standardize, and nowhere near replacing digital compute for ordinary workloads. Yet several properties of neurons are already strategically interesting for life science:

- they are human biological material rather than an animal proxy;
- they operate as networks whose function can be measured electrophysiologically;
- they can change with stimulation, chemical exposure, and feedback;
- induced pluripotent stem cells make donor- and disease-specific models possible;
- multi-electrode arrays and microfluidics create a two-way interface;
- software can now deliver experimental environments and feedback rather than merely analyze a terminal readout;
- repeated measurements can expose plasticity, network stability, excitation/inhibition balance, and other functional phenotypes that static assays can miss.

That turns a conventional assay stack—sample → perturbation → endpoint measurement → analysis—into a closed loop: neural substrate → encoded input → network response → decoded state → feedback/perturbation → changed network → repeated measurement.

## Evidence spine

The historical sequence used in the manuscript is anchored to checkable milestones:

1. 2006–2007: defined-factor induced pluripotency creates a route from adult somatic cells to patient-specific pluripotent cells.
2. 2013: cerebral organoids establish a widely used 3D human model for aspects of brain development and disease.
3. 2022: a field-wide nomenclature consensus clarifies neural organoids and assembloids and discourages “mini-brain” language; Cortical Labs researchers report DishBrain, a closed-loop 2D neuronal system that improves performance in a Pong-like environment under feedback.
4. 2023: the Johns Hopkins-led Organoid Intelligence program and Baltimore Declaration formalize a research agenda around brain organoids, interfaces, learning, computing, disease modeling, and embedded ethics; Brainoware demonstrates brain-organoid reservoir computing for speech recognition and nonlinear prediction.
5. 2024: FinalSpark publishes a remotely accessible wetware Neuroplatform with long-lived forebrain organoids, stimulation/recording, microfluidics, a Python API, and remote experiments; work on OI for developmental neurotoxicity proposes plasticity-sensitive assays.
6. 2024–2025: higher-quantity and more reproducible organoid protocols, 3D electrode systems, patient-derived disease models, and functional drug-screening platforms make scaling and pharmacology more concrete.
7. 2025: Cortical Labs commercializes the CL1 as a self-contained biological-computing/research device and offers remote access; FDA and NIH accelerate New Approach Methodologies and human-based research; NIH announces an $87 million, three-year Standardized Organoid Modeling Center.
8. 2026: FDA publishes general draft guidance on validating NAMs for drug development and reports first-year implementation progress; a Frontiers study accepted August 6 reports open-loop tactile/Braille classification using human forebrain organoids, including improved accuracy from a three-organoid ensemble.

## Critical boundaries

### 1. “Learning” is protocol-specific

The DishBrain paper reports improved task performance under structured feedback and contrasts it with controls. That is evidence of adaptive network behavior in a specific experimental system. It is not evidence that a neuronal culture has human-like understanding. The paper’s use of “sentience” was defined narrowly and remains controversial in broader interpretation; the manuscript avoids using that word as a settled biological conclusion.

### 2. Organoids are not brains

The 2022 Nature nomenclature consensus explicitly discourages “mini-brain,” “brain-in-a-dish,” and “whole-brain organoid.” Neural organoids recapitulate selected features of nervous-system development or organization. They lack much of the architecture, vascularization, sensory environment, body coupling, developmental history, and scale of an intact brain.

### 3. Company performance claims are claims

Cortical Labs and FinalSpark make ambitious statements about data efficiency, power efficiency, and future computing performance. The manuscript separates independently published demonstrations from corporate marketing. Commercial availability is evidence that the interface stack is becoming productized; it is not proof that wetware has superior economics for mainstream computation.

### 4. Reproducibility is a stack problem, not a footnote

Organoid heterogeneity, maturation differences, cell-line effects, stress, and batch variability remain major constraints. Standardization work—including high-quantity organoid protocols and the NIH SOM Center—is central to the thesis because the transition from scientific curiosity to biotech infrastructure depends on reproducible biological manufacture.

### 5. Regulatory momentum is not blanket acceptance

FDA’s 2025 roadmap and 2026 draft NAM guidance open a pathway for more human-relevant nonclinical methods. They do not mean every organoid assay is validated or that animal studies have disappeared. Context of use, reliability, validation, reporting, and predictive performance remain decisive.

### 6. Ethics should follow capability, not rhetoric

ISSCR’s current guidance says there is no biological evidence that CNS organoids have consciousness or pain perception warranting specialized oversight, while warning that the issue should be revisited as systems become more complex. The book treats donor consent, commercialization, moral status, and welfare as governance questions that become more important if adaptive capability increases, without assuming that present systems are conscious.

## Falsification tests

The book’s strongest thesis should weaken materially if, over the next several years, most of the following occur:

- closed-loop neural assays fail to outperform simpler 2D, molecular, imaging, or organ-on-chip methods for any valuable drug-development or toxicology contexts;
- organoid and neuronal-network variability remains too high for cross-site validation despite standardization and automation;
- patient-derived functional models do not add actionable predictive value beyond genotype, biomarkers, and conventional phenotypic assays;
- neural wetware requires so much culture support, specialized labor, and quality control that remote/platform economics remain permanently boutique;
- regulators find that neural functional endpoints do not improve prediction enough to justify validation burden;
- the apparent advantages of adaptation and plasticity prove to be experimental noise more often than useful signal.

The thesis strengthens if neural functional assays become validated in narrow but valuable contexts—seizure liability, developmental neurotoxicity, disease-specific network phenotypes, compound ranking, or patient stratification—even if wetware never becomes a meaningful competitor to GPUs.

## Research standard

Concrete dates, performance figures, named products, regulatory actions, and study outcomes are tied to the accompanying source ledger. Company claims are labeled as such in the prose. No quotations from real people are invented. Where a source uses contested language, the manuscript describes the underlying experiment rather than inheriting the source’s strongest interpretation.
