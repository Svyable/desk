# Research audit — the 2025 AHI assessment changes the argument

This note is a bounded update for *Hear the Beam*. It does not settle the cause of anomalous health incidents. It prevents the manuscript from stopping its official-assessment chronology in 2023 and, more importantly, gives the book a better counterexample to its own mechanism-bridge thesis.

## Primary source

**National Intelligence Council, *Updated Assessment of Anomalous Health Incidents, as of December 2024*, ICA 2025-00008-B, approved for public release January 9, 2025.**

Official PDF: https://www.dni.gov/files/ODNI/documents/assessments/NIC-Unclassified-ICA-Updated-Assessment-AHI-December2024.pdf

The assessment is a particularly useful source because it separates two questions the manuscript already argues must not be collapsed:

1. **Did a foreign actor cause the reported events?**
2. **Could a foreign actor possess or have used a novel device capable of producing at least some reported effects?**

Those questions no longer produce a perfectly uniform Intelligence Community answer.

## What changed from 2023

The January 2025 public assessment says most of the Intelligence Community continues to judge it **very unlikely** that a foreign adversary is responsible for events reported as possible AHIs. Five components retain that judgment, based on intelligence collection, investigations of key incidents, and review of foreign research and weapons development. The assessment says years of collection and investigation still have not surfaced compelling intelligence tying a foreign actor to a specific reported event.

But two components changed judgments about the device/capability question after reviewing new reporting. One judges there is roughly an even chance that a foreign actor has used a novel weapon or prototype device to harm a small, undetermined subset of personnel or dependents who reported symptoms or sensory phenomena. Another judges roughly even chances that a foreign actor has developed a novel weapon or prototype device that could have caused such effects, while still judging it unlikely that a foreign actor was responsible for the reported events.

This is exactly the distinction the book needs to preserve. A capability judgment can move without an attribution judgment moving with it.

## What this does **not** establish

The 2025 assessment does not publicly identify an attacker, a specific incident caused by an attacker, a recovered device, a measured attack waveform, or a closed source → propagation → coupling → biological effect → clinical pattern chain. It therefore cannot be used as proof that a directed-energy attack occurred.

Nor should the majority judgment be treated as proof that such a device is physically impossible. The public assessment itself contains component-level disagreement about the capability question.

The manuscript should quote or paraphrase confidence language with dates and agency/component counts rather than writing that “the intelligence community concluded” as though the record were monolithic.

## Strongest counterargument to the book

The book's proposed “mechanism bridge” could itself become a skeptical shortcut. Microwave hearing may be a real laboratory effect that lends intuitive plausibility to larger claims, but it does not follow that every later technical assessment was psychologically contaminated by that effect. Intelligence components had access to classified collection and technical reporting unavailable to the public and changed some judgments in 2025 after reviewing new information.

The strongest version of the counterargument is therefore: **the apparent bridge may sometimes reflect new evidence rather than conceptual slippage**.

The book should allow that explanation to win wherever the documentary record supports it. To establish the historical mechanism-bridge thesis, later chapters need evidence that analysts, officials, investigators, journalists, or claimants actually used microwave-auditory or adjacent Cold War bioeffects work as a bridge to broader capability claims. Conceptual resemblance alone is not enough.

## A better evidence ladder for the contemporary chapters

For each official AHI assessment, record separately:

- publication date and institution;
- question being answered: mechanism feasibility, device existence, incident attribution, medical syndrome, or foreign responsibility;
- population or subset under discussion;
- judgment and confidence level;
- component agreement/dissent;
- whether the judgment rests primarily on intelligence collection, technical analysis, medical evidence, or absence of expected adversary signatures;
- what new evidence would cause reassessment;
- whether later assessments actually changed that same judgment or answered a different question.

This prevents “plausible mechanism,” “possible device,” and “responsible adversary” from becoming three phrasings of the same proposition.

## Manuscript implications

Chapter 1's current sentence that later assessments “generally” found foreign-adversary responsibility unlikely remains directionally defensible, but it is now too smooth for the most current public record. When that passage is revised, the 2025 split should replace generic language about assessments having “evolved.” The interesting fact is not merely evolution; it is that **some components became more open to a device while the majority still rejected adversary attribution**.

That fracture should become narrative pressure rather than a caveat. It demonstrates the book's governing rule in real time: mechanism, capability and attribution can move independently.

## Publication hold

Do not state or imply that the January 2025 ICA validates the National Academies' 2020 pulsed-RF hypothesis. The two documents ask different questions, use different evidence, and the public 2025 assessment does not identify the novel device contemplated by the dissenting components as RF. Do not turn “novel weapon or prototype device” into “microwave weapon” unless a primary source explicitly makes that identification.
