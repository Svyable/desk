# The Sound With No Speaker

In 1962 Allan H. Frey published four pages that should have remained four pages.

They did not.

The title was almost aggressively dull: “Human Auditory System Response to Modulated Electromagnetic Energy.” Frey was working at General Electric’s Advanced Electronics Center and Cornell. He described a phenomenon that sounds, even now, like something a person would invent after reading too much science fiction. Put a human being in the path of appropriately pulsed radio-frequency energy and the person can hear something. There is no loudspeaker beside the ear. No headphone. No ordinary pressure wave traveling from a cone through the room. The sensation appears to the subject as sound.

That much is real.

It is worth staying with that sentence because almost every bad argument in this territory either refuses to accept it or refuses to stop there.

Frey was not reporting telepathy. He was not reporting a machine that inserted thoughts. He was not demonstrating a weapon that could rewrite behavior from across a city. He was describing induced auditory perception under particular electromagnetic conditions. Later investigators reproduced the broad phenomenon. Physical work converged on an explanation in which rapid, tiny heating from a microwave pulse produces thermoelastic expansion and launches an acoustic transient inside tissue. The head, in effect, becomes part of the transducer.

That is strange enough. We should resist improving it.

The difficulty is that a real effect this counterintuitive does something to the imagination. It creates technical permission. Once a beam can make a person hear a click without a speaker, the distance between click and voice begins to feel smaller than it is. The distance between voice and command feels smaller still. Then between command and control. Between control and injury. Between injury and a weapon. Between a possible weapon and the cause of a particular unexplained illness.

None of those transitions is free.

Engineering is mostly the invoice for transitions people thought were free.

Consider what Frey’s observation actually demands. There must be a source. The source must generate electromagnetic energy with relevant carrier and pulse characteristics. Enough energy must arrive at the body, after distance and geometry and whatever walls or windows intervene. The body must couple to that field. The pulse must create a physical transient of sufficient magnitude and temporal structure. The auditory system must detect it. The subject must perceive it. Every one of those verbs hides numbers.

Now change the desired output. Do not make a click. Make intelligible speech. The waveform burden changes. Make the effect covert at operational distance. The antenna and propagation burden changes. Put the target inside a building. Geometry changes again. Demand neurological injury rather than perception. Now tissue dose and mechanism matter in a different way. Demand that the injury resemble a particular clinical syndrome. Another bridge. Demand that no one detect the emitter. Another. Demand that the operator know where the target’s head is. Another.

A mechanism is not a capability. A capability is not an operation. An operation is not an attribution.

That distinction should be boring. It has not been boring for sixty years.

Long before Havana became shorthand for mysterious illness, there was Moscow.

For years the United States Embassy in Moscow was illuminated with microwave radiation originating outside the compound. This is not folklore excavated from a conspiracy forum. The exposure became a matter of official concern, measurement, medical investigation, diplomacy and eventually public reporting. A declassified CIA Reading Room item preserving a 1977 account describes Soviet microwave radiation directed at the embassy and the U.S. government’s attempts to understand its possible health consequences. The episode became known as the Moscow Signal.

Here is where the story becomes dangerous in a more useful sense. The Soviet Union really was transmitting microwave energy toward an American embassy. American officials really did worry about what it meant. Scientists really did investigate biological effects. The United States really did possess a growing literature showing that radio-frequency energy could interact with biological systems in ways more interesting than “it gets hot.”

If you wanted to manufacture a myth of invisible weapons, you could hardly ask for better raw material.

If you wanted to investigate an actual invisible weapon, you would begin with much of the same material.

This is the problem.

The Moscow Signal does not need to have been a health weapon to matter. Its existence changed the prior probability inside government. Once an adversary has intentionally placed electromagnetic energy onto your diplomatic compound, questions that would otherwise sound unserious become legitimate intelligence requirements. What is the signal doing? Is it for surveillance? Is it interfering with equipment? Is it activating something? Is it an experiment? Is it harassment? Is it producing biological effects accidentally? Deliberately? What are the Soviets studying that we are not?

The correct response to uncertainty is investigation. Intelligence organizations, however, do not investigate inside a clean philosophy seminar. They investigate while trying not to be surprised.

That asymmetry matters. If the cost of dismissing a bizarre adversary capability is catastrophic and the cost of spending money on a dead end is merely embarrassing, bizarre ideas acquire budgets. The budget is then discovered decades later and offered as proof that the idea worked.

This is one of the recurring mistakes in the history of classified science. Government interest is evidence of government interest.

Sometimes it is also evidence that a foreign government showed interest first.

Sometimes the whole thing is a hall of mirrors.

Cold War technical intelligence was especially good at producing these rooms. One side sees a paper, a rumor, a laboratory, a strange instrument, an embassy signal. It asks whether the other side has found something. The other side notices the first side asking. Funding follows uncertainty. Intelligence reports document the funding. Years later the reports are declassified. A reader encounters twenty boxes of official paper and reasonably concludes that twenty boxes must contain a thing.

Twenty boxes can contain a question.

This book is about learning to weigh the box.

The microwave auditory effect is an unusually clean place to begin because it prevents cheap skepticism. You cannot dispose of the subject by saying electromagnetic energy cannot create an auditory sensation in a human being. It can. Frey’s early result was followed by work from Arthur Guy, C. K. Chou, James Lin and others investigating microwave-induced acoustic effects. The phenomenon became a legitimate object of bioelectromagnetics rather than a one-off curiosity.

But the same literature prevents cheap credulity. Once you read it as engineering rather than incantation, the effect becomes bounded. Pulse width matters. Peak power matters. Carrier frequency matters. Anatomy matters. Background acoustic noise matters. A laboratory demonstration is not an adjective—“microwave”—that can be pasted onto any symptom report.

This is why I dislike the phrase “directed energy” when it is used without a noun after it. A flashlight is directed energy. Radar is directed energy. A laser rangefinder is directed energy. Medical ultrasound is directed energy. So is a microwave oven in a loose enough argument. The phrase sounds specific while deleting the variables that decide what happens.

Frequency. Waveform. Intensity. Duration. Aperture. Range. Coupling. Absorption. Target geometry.

Put the variables back and the mystery either becomes an engineering problem or begins to evaporate.

In late 2016, American personnel in Havana began reporting unusual health experiences. The case history grew, spread geographically and became politically charged. The label changed over time; “Havana syndrome” became “anomalous health incidents.” Some people described abrupt sensory phenomena, pressure, sound, disequilibrium, pain or other acute effects, followed in some cases by persistent symptoms. The population was heterogeneous. So were the proposed causes.

In 2020 the National Academies published an assessment commissioned by the State Department. Its committee concluded that directed, pulsed radio-frequency energy appeared to be the most plausible mechanism among those it considered for the distinctive acute initial symptoms in the subset of cases it emphasized. The language matters. “Most plausible” is not “proven.” “Mechanism” is not “attacker.” “Consistent with” is not “caused by.” The committee also confronted missing information and heterogeneity.

That report is frequently cited as though a panel found the weapon.

It did not.

Later U.S. intelligence assessments complicated the picture further, generally finding foreign-adversary responsibility unlikely for most reported cases while leaving some uncertainty and continued investigation around a smaller residue. Those assessments have themselves evolved. Agencies have not always assigned identical confidence or interpreted the same residue identically. The administrative category has also had to carry people whose symptoms may not share one cause.

This is precisely the kind of evidence ecology in which people become certain too early.

One camp hears “microwave auditory effect” and “National Academies” and sees a solved directed-energy attack. Another hears “intelligence community” and “unlikely” and sees mass psychogenic illness with a government expense account. Each side takes the part of the record that makes the other side look stupid.

The record is more irritating.

A real RF auditory phenomenon exists.

A real Soviet microwave signal struck the U.S. Embassy in Moscow.

A real history of military and intelligence interest in radio-frequency biological effects exists.

Real U.S. personnel later reported disturbing acute and chronic symptoms.

A distinguished scientific committee considered directed pulsed RF plausible for a subset of the acute presentations it reviewed.

Publicly available evidence has not established a foreign RF device as the cause of those cases.

These statements can all be true at once.

If that feels unsatisfying, good. Unsatisfied is an epistemic state. It is not a defect to be repaired with confidence.

The more interesting question is what evidence would change it.

Suppose, for the moment, that someone wanted to build a covert system that caused a target to perceive sound using pulsed RF. We do not need to design the machine. We need only ask what reality would demand from it. The transmitter occupies space. It consumes power. An antenna has gain and beam geometry. The field falls and spreads according to physics. Buildings attenuate and reflect. A moving target changes alignment. A pulse train that matters biologically is also an electromagnetic event that instruments may detect. If the desired effect requires high peak fields, components and safety constraints become relevant. If the operator is distant, aperture grows or delivered intensity falls. If the target is behind unknown construction, uncertainty grows.

Invisible does not mean footprintless.

This is one reason the Moscow case is so useful. The signal was invisible to the diplomats’ eyes but not to instruments. Once you know to measure, radio-frequency energy is not supernatural. It is often easier to characterize than a rumor.

The modern cases are harder because the alleged exposure, if there was one, was transient and usually reconstructed after symptoms began. A detector installed tomorrow cannot measure yesterday’s field. Medical imaging can reveal injury but generally cannot read an RF waveform back out of tissue. Symptoms can be real while their initiating cause remains gone.

That asymmetry is a gift to both attackers and stories about attackers.

A covert energy weapon, if one existed and were used briefly, might leave less familiar forensic residue than a bullet or chemical contaminant. But a narrative about a covert energy weapon benefits from exactly the same absence. No fragment becomes evidence of sophistication. No detected source becomes evidence that the source was undetectable. Variable symptoms become evidence of variable settings. Failed replication becomes evidence that the classified system is better than the laboratory.

A hypothesis that converts every missing observation into a capability claim cannot lose.

Anything that cannot lose cannot tell us much.

So the book will force the beam to lose.

Not rhetorically. Quantitatively.

We will reconstruct what the early experiments actually required. We will follow the Moscow Signal as a measured electromagnetic event rather than a spooky anecdote. We will look at Project PANDORA and related Cold War research without confusing threat assessment with technical success. We will read Soviet and Russian claims with the same hostility toward flattering inference that we apply to American claims. We will examine psychotronics as an intelligence and cultural category before treating it as a scientific one. We will return to Havana and separate case definition, symptom, mechanism, source and attribution.

Then we will build the missing ladder.

Source.

Propagation.

Coupling.

Physical effect.

Biological response.

Perception or injury.

Clinical pattern.

Operation.

Attribution.

A claim can be strong on rung four and nonexistent on rung nine. This is not a paradox. It is how engineering works.

There is a second reason to do this carefully. People who report frightening symptoms deserve something better than being conscripted into somebody else’s cosmology. A person can be ill without knowing why. A government can mishandle a case without secretly knowing the cause. A clinician can document an abnormality without identifying an attacker. An intelligence service can investigate an adversary hypothesis without validating it. And a patient can be wrong about mechanism without being wrong about suffering.

The internet is terrible at these distinctions because mechanism is emotionally valuable. A cause turns an experience into a story. An intentional cause turns it into a moral story. A classified intentional cause explains why proof is scarce. By the time that structure is complete, uncertainty feels like betrayal.

Science has a less satisfying offer: keep the variables separate long enough that one of them can surprise you.

The surprise in this story may ultimately be that an exotic-seeming mechanism was real but irrelevant to the famous cases. Or that a subset of cases had an exposure mechanism that broad administrative grouping obscured. Or that the deepest historical continuity is not a weapon at all but a recurrent intelligence failure mode: adversaries studying effects because we study them because they study us.

I do not know yet.

That is why this is a book rather than a verdict.

Frey’s four pages are still on the desk. A human being can hear a beam.

Now we have to find out what, exactly, that fact is allowed to mean.
