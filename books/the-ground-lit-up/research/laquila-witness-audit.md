# L'Aquila opening witness audit

Scope: one bounded source check for Chapter 1's opening anecdote. This note does not revise the active manuscript while #2276 is open.

## Why this matters

Chapter 1 currently says that after the 6 April 2009 L'Aquila earthquake, "one resident's account" acquired an afterlife: he saw flashes before the main shock and took his family outside. That anecdote is unusually consequential because the chapter returns to it at the end and uses it to embody the difference between a compelling retrospective story and a usable precursor.

The primary published source supports a narrower and more interesting version than the current prose.

## Primary source checked

Cristiano Fidani, “The earthquake lights (EQL) of the 6 April 2009 Aquila earthquake, in Central Italy,” *Natural Hazards and Earth System Sciences* 10 (2010), 967–978. DOI: https://doi.org/10.5194/nhess-10-967-2010

The paper describes a seven-month post-earthquake collection of testimonials. Its abstract says reported luminous phenomena ranged from roughly nine months before the main shock to roughly five months afterward and extended over a large area, up to about 50 km north of L'Aquila. The catalogue is therefore retrospective and heterogeneous by construction; it is not a prospective observation network.

The relevant named witness is **Carlo Strinella**. In the paper's conclusion Fidani says Strinella had prior knowledge of earthquake-light reports and “took measures to protect his family after interpreting some flashes he had sighted before the main shock.” The source does **not**, in that concluding sentence, establish the stronger wording that he simply “took his family outside.” Do not add that physical action unless a traceable source supplies it.

This detail matters for more than pedantry. Prior knowledge of EQL changes the anecdote's epistemic meaning. Strinella was not a naïve observer who saw an unexplained flash and independently inferred an earthquake. He already possessed a hypothesis linking unusual lights and earthquakes, then interpreted flashes through that hypothesis. That makes his case simultaneously more relevant to warning behavior and less clean as evidence that the signal itself carried predictive information.

The same paper explicitly treats the catalogue as testimony gathered after the earthquake and reports a wide range of luminous forms. That makes Chapter 1's later denominator argument stronger: a memorable successful interpretation cannot tell us how often similarly interpreted flashes occurred without a damaging earthquake, how many earthquakes arrived without such flashes, or how prior belief affected reporting and action.

## Publication holds for the manuscript pass

- Name Carlo Strinella if the anecdote remains; “one resident” unnecessarily hides the feature that makes the case scientifically useful: he already knew the EQL hypothesis.
- Do not write that Strinella “took his family outside” from Fidani 2010 alone. The primary paper supports only that he “took measures to protect his family.”
- Preserve the chronology as an after-the-event testimonial catalogue, not a contemporaneously registered prediction.
- Do not imply that Strinella's action validates earthquake lights as a predictor. A successful decision is not a sensitivity/specificity estimate.
- Do not turn the paper's authorial suggestion that EQL education could save lives into an established public-safety recommendation. That conclusion is an inference from retrospective testimony, not evidence from a validated warning trial.

## Strongest counterargument

The witness story may be too weak to carry the opening at all. If the book's governing claim is that precursors require denominators, opening and closing on a retrospectively collected success story risks granting the anecdote narrative authority that the chapter spends its middle qualifying. The clean alternative is not to sneer at Strinella; it is to make his prior belief part of the scene and let the reader see the actual problem: he made a decision under uncertainty that happened to precede a destructive earthquake, but the published record cannot tell us the false-alarm rate of that decision rule.

If later primary or contemporaneous reporting reconstructs the exact flashes, timing, location, action, and pre-existing knowledge more tightly, the anecdote may deserve more weight. Until then, the narrower version is both more defensible and more intellectually alive.

## Editorial payoff

A later manuscript pass can improve the opening without adding drama. The useful pressure is already there: **Carlo Strinella knew the earthquake-light idea before the earthquake. He saw flashes, interpreted them through that idea, and acted to protect his family. Then the earthquake came.** The scientific question is no longer whether the story is seductive. It is what we would have needed to record before 6 April to know whether his rule worked rather than merely whether this instance ended memorably.

That gives the chapter a human decision, a hidden denominator, and a built-in counterargument in the same documented event.
