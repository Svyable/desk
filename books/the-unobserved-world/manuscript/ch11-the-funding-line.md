# The Funding Line

A scientific proposal is a claim about a future dataset.

Give us money, the researchers say, and we will build the apparatus, recruit the participants, hire the staff, collect the samples, run the model, sequence the organism, dig the site, survey the population, or observe the sky. At the end, if things go well, there will be evidence where there is currently only a question.

A funding decision therefore has unusual epistemic power.

It does not merely reward good science. It helps decide which possible science becomes observable.

This is obvious to anyone who has written a grant. A rejected proposal does not usually proceed at full scale through sheer force of belief. Some projects can be pursued with departmental money, smaller awards, personal time, cheaper methods, or another funder. Others cannot. A telescope, trial, longitudinal dataset, field expedition, or laboratory program requires resources. Without them, the hypothesis may remain a paragraph in a rejected application.

Later, the scientific community evaluates the literature.

The literature contains results from questions that crossed earlier funding lines.

Peer review is meant to make those lines intelligent. Experts assess significance, methods, feasibility, originality, prior work, investigator capability, and likely contribution. In a world of limited money, selection is unavoidable. Funding every plausible proposal would not create an ideal science. It would consume resources needed to do any project well.

The difficulty begins when judgment becomes more precise than the evidence supports.

Imagine a panel has fifty strong proposals and money for ten. Reviewers read, score, discuss, and rank. The top proposal is clearly stronger than the bottom one. Near the funding line, the distinctions can be thinner. Reviewers disagree. Scores contain noise. Small differences in framing, panel composition, reviewer assignment, or disciplinary taste can change the order.

Yet the budget requires a line.

The proposal ranked tenth is funded. The proposal ranked eleventh is not.

Ten years later, the funded project has publications, citations, trainees, follow-on grants, datasets, and a record that can justify the panel's faith. The unfunded project may have disappeared. The scientific system has generated much richer evidence about the winner.

This is the same selective-label structure we have seen elsewhere, but with an added twist: the output being selected is knowledge itself.

Science studies have long examined the reliability and biases of peer review. The evidence is complicated. Expert evaluation contains real information. Reviewers can identify fatal methodological flaws and distinguish serious proposals from weak ones. They can also disagree substantially, especially among strong proposals. Prestige, field norms, institutional reputation, topic popularity, and reviewer perspective can influence outcomes.

The question is not whether peer review is good or bad.

It is where peer review's ability to rank ends.

A growing set of funders and researchers has become interested in partial lotteries for exactly this boundary. The idea is not to throw every application into a hat. Proposals first pass a quality or eligibility threshold through review. When several are judged fundable and difficult to distinguish reliably, chance helps allocate the scarce awards.

This makes many scientists uncomfortable for understandable reasons. A grant can determine years of work and multiple careers. Applicants spend enormous time preparing proposals. To have the final decision made partly by lottery can feel like an insult to merit.

The alternative is not necessarily pure merit.

It may be noisy precision pretending to be merit.

Ferric Fang and Arturo Casadevall argued in 2016 for a modified lottery in research funding, proposing that peer review identify a pool of meritorious proposals and random selection allocate awards among them. Their argument emphasized limits of ranking and possible reductions in bias and administrative burden.

More recent research has tried to classify and assess the different forms funding lotteries can take. A 2024 article in *Research Evaluation* describes a taxonomy of random allocation systems and analyzes fairness questions. In 2026, *Nature* reported on the growing use and debate around randomization among fundable proposals, including institutions experimenting with lotteries when reviewers cannot reliably separate finalists.

The timing matters. This is not only a speculative reform proposed from outside science. Versions are being used.

For this book, the most interesting property of a partial lottery is not fairness in the ordinary sense. It is exploration.

If expert review systematically prefers familiar methods, established institutions, conventional topics, or applicants with strong track records, a deterministic ranking can repeatedly fund the same regions of scientific possibility. A lottery among proposals that all clear a serious quality threshold introduces variation without requiring reviewers to pretend they know which near-equal proposal will produce the most important science.

The randomness buys information.

Some of the funded projects will fail. That is not evidence the lottery was foolish. Some top-ranked grants fail too. The relevant comparison is whether a portfolio selected with bounded randomness produces different knowledge, researchers, and methods at acceptable cost.

This is difficult to evaluate because scientific value unfolds slowly and is hard to measure. Citation counts are convenient but incomplete. Breakthroughs are rare. A failed experiment can still eliminate an important hypothesis. A training grant can matter through people rather than publications. A field can be influenced by an instrument, dataset, or negative result that looks modest in standard metrics.

Science has unusually long labels.

That makes exploitation tempting. Established researchers and proven methods have evidence now. Young investigators and unfamiliar approaches promise evidence later. A panel trying to reduce immediate risk will naturally prefer legibility.

But research exists partly to create evidence where legibility is low.

If a funding system demands strong prior proof before every experiment, it can trap science in a logical loop. Preliminary data become necessary to receive the money required to produce data. Investigators need publications to receive the resources required to produce publications. New fields need evidence before the field has the infrastructure to generate evidence.

Funders already recognize this problem through early-career programs, exploratory grants, high-risk/high-reward programs, seed funding, and special calls. These mechanisms are institutional exploration budgets.

They protect uncertainty from the standards applied to mature work.

The design matters. A “high-risk” program can become a branding exercise in which reviewers still reward familiar investigators doing slightly less familiar projects. An innovation fund can select proposals that sound unconventional while remaining easy to justify. Human judgment is adaptive; applicants learn what reviewers consider adventurous and optimize toward it.

Randomness has one unusual virtue here: it is difficult to flatter.

Once proposals clear the quality threshold, a lottery refuses to extract fake information from tiny score differences. It converts acknowledged uncertainty into procedural variation.

That does not make it universally fair. Applicants with more resources may submit more proposals. Eligibility criteria can encode bias. The threshold itself can be noisy. A lottery can distribute chance unequally if some fields or institutions are overrepresented in the pool. Randomness after a biased gate does not cleanse the gate.

The value is narrower: it prevents a noisy final ranking from becoming a self-confirming knowledge policy.

Consider what happens under deterministic selection. A panel prefers established method A over uncertain method B. A receives funding. Researchers using A generate more papers, datasets, trained students, and successful examples. Future reviewers now have stronger evidence that A is productive. B remains thinly evidenced because fewer B projects existed. The next ranking reasonably favors A again.

Method A may truly be better.

But the funding system has difficulty distinguishing “A is better” from “A has been allowed to become better evidenced.”

A partial lottery among proposals using A and B, after both clear a competence threshold, creates the variation needed to learn.

This is the scientific version of the interview list.

The institution does not hire random science. It creates a second look for uncertainty near a boundary where ranking is unreliable.

There is a deeper reason lotteries fit science unusually well. Researchers already accept randomness as a tool for producing credible evidence. Random assignment is not considered anti-scientific inside an experiment. It is a method for preventing hidden judgment from contaminating comparison.

Using randomness in funding feels different because the researchers themselves become subject to it.

That discomfort is revealing.

Scientists readily recognize that patient assignment can require randomization when treatments are genuinely uncertain. They are less comfortable when their own grant outcomes acknowledge the same uncertainty. Merit feels individual; noise feels statistical.

A partial lottery makes institutional humility public.

It says: our review process can determine that these proposals are worth doing. It cannot reliably know which one will matter most.

That sentence may be more honest than a score of 92.4 versus 91.8.

Precision is especially dangerous when selection creates the future evidence. The funded proposal has the chance to justify its score. The unfunded proposal does not. Over time, deterministic ranking can make arbitrary early distinctions look prescient.

Science funding also shapes people before proposals are written. Young researchers observe what gets funded and choose fields. Departments hire around grant-rich topics. Universities build facilities where funders signal demand. Doctoral students select advisers with resources. Companies form around funded technologies. A funding priority changes the supply of future applications.

The gate alters the upstream population.

This is the same dynamic we saw with creators adapting to recommendation systems and applicants adapting to hiring criteria. A selection rule becomes a production rule.

Funding agencies know this and use it intentionally. Calls for proposals can build fields. Mission-driven agencies can direct attention toward a public problem. There is nothing inherently wrong with that. Society funds science partly to pursue priorities.

The issue is whether the system leaves enough room for questions the priority-setting process cannot anticipate.

Important discoveries often emerge from work whose later importance was difficult to state in advance. This observation is often romanticized into the claim that breakthroughs are unpredictable and therefore review is useless. That goes too far. Researchers can assess rigor and plausibility. Funders can make strategic bets. The fact that outcomes are uncertain does not erase judgment.

It argues for portfolios.

A portfolio can contain exploitation and exploration. Some grants extend strong programs with clear trajectories. Others test uncertain ideas. Some build infrastructure. Some support new investigators. Some respond to urgent missions. Some can be allocated randomly among proposals reviewers genuinely cannot separate.

The key is not to force every grant to justify itself using the same probability of near-term success.

An institution that funds only the projects easiest to defend can become very good at producing defensible science.

It may become worse at discovering indefensible-looking truths.

There is no metric that can prove the correct exploration share in advance. Too little exploration risks stagnation. Too much can spread resources across weak work and reduce the scale needed for strong programs. Different fields have different capital requirements, feedback times, replication needs, and societal stakes.

The funding line is therefore a governance choice, not a formula.

But one question can improve almost any funding system: where is the panel genuinely uncertain, and what does the process do with that uncertainty?

Does it hide uncertainty behind decimal scores? Does it resolve disagreement through status? Does the loudest reviewer win? Does the chair choose? Does the proposal from the famous institution receive the benefit of doubt? Or does the system sometimes admit that several options are good enough and let variation create evidence?

The answer determines more than fairness among applicants.

It determines which branches of science get the chance to become facts.
