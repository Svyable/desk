# A Field Guide to Missing Evidence

The easiest way to misunderstand a decision system is to begin with the data it has.

A better place to begin is with the decision that produced the data.

Before asking whether a model is accurate, ask who received the opportunity to generate an outcome. Before asking whether a policy worked, ask which alternative was never tried. Before asking whether a ranking improved, ask what fell below the line and therefore stopped producing evidence. Before asking whether a system is fair, ask whether some groups are judged with abundant outcome histories while others are judged mostly from inherited assumptions.

The central habit of this book is simple to state and difficult to maintain: treat missing outcomes as part of the decision system, not as empty space outside it.

An institution rarely sees the world in full. It sees the world after admissions rules, lending thresholds, diagnostic habits, recommendation policies, procurement practices, research priorities, staffing limits, legal constraints, budgets, and human attention have filtered it. Some of those filters are necessary. Some are beneficial. Some protect people from harm. But every filter also changes what can later be learned.

That is the first diagnostic question: what had to happen before this outcome became observable?

A repayment record requires a loan. A job-performance record requires a hire. A biopsy result usually requires a test or procedure. A song’s long-run listening pattern requires exposure. A student’s success in a higher-level course requires placement into that course. A public program’s measured effect requires implementation. A scientific idea’s later influence generally requires enough funding, publication, or attention to survive.

If an outcome depends on prior access, then the observed dataset is partly a history of access decisions.

The second question is who is systematically absent from that history.

Absence is not always evidence of exclusion. People opt out. Records fail. Outcomes happen elsewhere. Privacy rules intentionally prevent some linkage. Institutions share data imperfectly. A rejected borrower may obtain credit from another lender. A patient sent home may return to a different hospital. A candidate not hired here may thrive somewhere else. The important question is not whether the institution possesses every outcome. It is whether the pattern of missingness follows the earlier decision strongly enough to distort what the institution believes about the population it is judging.

This is where selective labels differ from ordinary missing data. The label is absent because the institution chose an action that prevented, redirected, or failed to expose the outcome it later wishes it knew.

The third question is whether the missing outcome matters to a decision the institution still has to make.

Not every unobserved path deserves exploration. A manufacturer does not need repeated evidence that an unsafe component should be used. A hospital does not need to randomize patients into plainly inferior treatment. A lender does not need to create harmful debt merely to improve a model. A government does not need to violate rights for the sake of a cleaner estimate.

The unobserved world is not a moral claim that every possibility deserves a trial.

It is a practical claim that uncertainty should be distinguished from prohibition.

Some options are outside the action set because law, safety, consent, rights, or basic ethics put them there. That is not an information problem to be optimized away. Exploration begins only after those boundaries are established.

Within the remaining space, the useful question becomes: where is uncertainty both consequential and reducible?

A model may be very uncertain in a region that almost never affects a decision. Learning there has little value. Another uncertainty may sit directly on a threshold used thousands of times per day. A small improvement in knowledge could change outcomes for many people. Institutions do not need maximal information. They need information that can change responsible action.

This is the logic behind value of information. The phrase can sound abstract, but the operational version is plain: if we knew the answer to this question, would we do anything differently?

If not, collecting more evidence may be busywork.

If yes, ask what is the least harmful, least costly, most reversible way to learn it.

Sometimes the answer is a randomized trial. Sometimes it is not.

Natural variation can reveal information when people or institutions behave differently for reasons that are plausibly unrelated to the outcome of interest. Thresholds can create useful discontinuities. Staged rollouts can create comparisons. Historical policy changes can create quasi-experiments. Independent data linkage can recover outcomes that one institution does not see. Carefully chosen audits can expose blind spots. Small pilots can answer enough of a question to justify a larger one.

The method matters less than the discipline of asking what comparison identifies the claim.

That word, comparison, is worth keeping close.

A prediction can be impressive without a useful comparison. A before-and-after chart can be dramatic without telling you what would have happened otherwise. A high success rate can coexist with missed opportunities if the institution only measures the cases it selected. A low error rate can conceal a population the system has learned to avoid.

The counterfactual is not a hidden spreadsheet waiting to be found. It is the outcome under an alternative action, and for one person at one moment we cannot observe both paths. Good research designs construct credible comparisons that let us estimate what those alternative outcomes would have looked like across groups.

That is why randomization is powerful when it is appropriate. It does not reveal the other life of one person. It makes groups comparable enough that the difference in their outcomes can be attributed, within sampling uncertainty and design limits, to the difference in treatment.

Randomness is therefore not magic and not morality.

It is an identification device.

The phrase exploration versus exploitation also becomes clearer when stripped of technical glamour. Exploitation means using what the system currently believes is best. Exploration means taking some action partly because the outcome will improve future knowledge. Most useful systems need both. A system that explores forever fails to use what it learns. A system that exploits perfectly can stop discovering where its beliefs are wrong.

The balance is domain specific.

In recommendation, small exposure experiments can be cheap and reversible. In hiring, interviews create cost but are far less consequential than randomizing final employment decisions. In medicine, many interventions are too consequential for opportunistic exploration, while testing strategies, diagnostic support, or already-equivalent accepted treatments may sometimes be studied under rigorous consent and oversight. In government, staged implementation can be feasible for one program and politically or legally inappropriate for another.

The appropriate unit of exploration is often smaller than the final decision.

That is a useful design move. If randomizing the high-stakes decision is unacceptable, look upstream for a lower-stakes action that produces information. Interview rather than hire. Review rather than fund. Audit rather than penalize. Test a message before changing eligibility. Run a shadow model before delegating authority to it. Reserve a small traffic slice before redesigning the whole platform.

Learning does not require recklessness. It often requires decomposition.

The next diagnostic question is whether the system has an exploration budget.

Every institution already spends resources on uncertainty, although it may not use that name. It keeps spare capacity. It funds prototypes. It interviews candidates who are not obvious choices. It grants exceptions. It reviews borderline applications. It runs quality audits. It tests backup suppliers. It gives junior researchers small grants. It pilots programs.

The question is whether those activities are designed to generate reusable knowledge or merely tolerated as exceptions.

An exploration budget makes the tradeoff visible. It can be expressed in interview slots, grant dollars, test capacity, traffic percentage, staff time, pilot sites, or review volume. The budget does not have to be large. It has to be deliberate enough that short-run optimization cannot silently consume all of it.

If a recommender assigns one hundred percent of exposure to the current predicted winners, exploration has been reduced to whatever accidents remain. If a hiring system interviews only the top-ranked profiles, the model receives abundant confirmation about the people it already understands. If a grant process repeatedly funds the same legible trajectories, the institution may become excellent at ranking familiar work while weakening its ability to discover unfamiliar work.

Exploration capacity is a kind of epistemic reserve.

Reserves matter most when the environment changes.

A system trained in one labor market, disease environment, interest-rate regime, demographic mix, competitive landscape, or cultural moment may appear stable until the world moves outside its observed range. An institution that preserved some variation will often notice the shift earlier because it has maintained contact with alternatives. An institution that optimized tightly around a single historical path may have more performance but less peripheral vision.

This is one reason holdouts can matter even after a system appears to work.

A holdout is not always a frozen test set. Operationally, it can mean preserving a comparison group, a legacy process, an independent audit sample, or a small allocation governed by a different rule. The point is not nostalgia for the old method. The point is to avoid a situation in which the new policy becomes universal before anyone can still estimate what it changed.

Universal rollout can erase its own control group.

That may be acceptable when evidence is already strong and the cost of withholding benefit is high. But it should be a decision, not an accidental consequence of enthusiasm.

The same caution applies to model retraining. Once a model influences who gets selected, the labels used in later retraining are no longer neutral observations from the old world. They are outcomes generated under a policy partly shaped by earlier versions of the model. The data-generating process has become endogenous to the system.

This does not make retraining invalid. It means lineage matters.

Record which policy generated which labels. Record major threshold changes. Record where humans overrode the model. Record when the applicant pool shifted. Record which populations had sparse labels. Record whether a new data source changed who was admitted into the observed sample.

Without that history, later analysts may treat policy artifacts as facts about the world.

A mature evidence record should preserve not only what was decided but why the decision was believed to be reasonable at the time.

What evidence supported it? Which assumptions were essential? What alternatives were considered? Which populations were weakly observed? What result would have changed the decision? What new condition should trigger re-evaluation?

These questions are a practical defense against institutional amnesia.

They also make negative results useful.

Organizations often preserve successes because successes become programs. Failures disappear because nobody wants to maintain a system that was abandoned. Yet a failed pilot can be more valuable to future decisions than a successful one if the institution records what was tested, how it failed, and under what conditions.

The loss of negative evidence creates a peculiar form of repetition. New staff encounter the same plausible idea without access to the old disappointment. The institution appears innovative because it keeps rediscovering its own discarded experiments.

A publication-quality learning system therefore needs an archive of failed expectations, not merely an archive of adopted policies.

The next question is whether learning changes authority.

Evidence can exist without affecting action. A study may be rigorous and operationally irrelevant because the people who understand it cannot alter procurement, staffing, eligibility, software, or budget. An organization can become excellent at producing reports while remaining behaviorally fixed.

The bridge from evidence to policy needs an owner.

That owner does not need absolute authority. The institution does need a known process for deciding what happens when evidence contradicts the current rule. Who can reopen the threshold? Who can authorize another pilot? Who decides whether the result generalizes? Who documents the reasons for rejecting the evidence? When will the question be revisited?

If those answers are missing, experimentation becomes theater.

Another field question concerns external validity: where should we expect this result to travel?

A program that worked in three cities may fail nationally. A hiring model trained inside one firm may not describe another occupation. A recommendation experiment on one platform may depend on product design, user intent, or catalog composition. A medical result may depend on prevalence, referral patterns, or clinical capacity.

The correct response is neither automatic generalization nor permanent skepticism.

Scale is another empirical question.

Staged commitment turns that problem into a sequence. A pilot asks whether the mechanism works under close attention. Replication asks whether the result survives another setting or team. A staged rollout asks whether implementation quality holds when the system grows. Monitoring asks whether effects persist as participants and operators adapt.

Each step should be large enough to answer the next important uncertainty and small enough that failure remains tolerable.

This is a more useful description of institutional experimentation than the mythology of moving fast.

The pace should follow reversibility and stakes.

The final field question is the one most easily lost in technical discussion: who bears the cost of learning?

Information is not free merely because it improves a model.

An applicant may spend time on an exploratory interview. A patient may experience anxiety from additional testing. A community may carry the inconvenience of a pilot. A creator may receive exposure but also become part of a platform experiment. A borrower may face real financial consequences. A worker may be subjected to a policy whose uncertainty benefits the institution more than the worker.

The distribution of learning costs is part of fairness.

Consent, transparency, compensation, appeal, privacy, independent oversight, and the ability to exit can matter as much as statistical efficiency. Some experiments should not happen even if they would generate excellent data. Some data should remain uncollected. Some outcomes should remain deliberately unlinked. Some uncertainty is the price of respecting human boundaries.

That is why the aim of this book is not total observability.

A totally observable society would be intolerable.

The aim is to notice when institutions confuse their field of vision with the world itself.

The practical vocabulary is modest. A potential outcome is what would happen under a particular action. A counterfactual is the alternative outcome not observed for the same unit at the same moment. Selective labels arise when earlier choices determine whose later outcomes become available. Exploration is purposeful information acquisition under uncertainty. Exploitation is using current knowledge to choose what appears best. Regret is the performance lost while learning compared with an ideal policy that already knew the answer. A holdout preserves an independent comparison. External validity concerns whether a result applies beyond the setting that produced it. A natural experiment uses variation not deliberately created by the researcher when that variation supports a credible comparison.

The words are less important than the habit behind them.

When a system reports what it has learned, ask what it had to select in order to learn it.

When a system reports high confidence, ask where its labels are dense and where they are thin.

When a policy reports success, ask what comparison makes that claim believable.

When a model improves, ask whether the improvement came from better understanding or from narrowing the world it encounters.

When standardization increases, ask what useful variation disappeared with it.

When exploration is proposed, ask who pays, what boundary contains it, and what decision the information will change.

When evidence is produced, ask who has the authority and memory to use it.

And when an institution says there is no demand, no talent, no risk, no benefit, no viable idea, or no alternative, ask one final question before accepting the absence as fact.

Did the world fail to produce it?

Or did the system fail to look?
