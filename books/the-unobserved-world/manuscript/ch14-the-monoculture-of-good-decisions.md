# The Monoculture of Good Decisions

A monoculture does not require everyone to make bad decisions.

It can be built from good decisions repeated by everyone.

Imagine a hundred employers trying to improve hiring. Each studies its historical data and discovers that certain credentials correlate with success. Each buys a screening tool trained on large datasets. Each adopts structured processes, reduces idiosyncratic judgment, and concentrates interviews on candidates with the strongest predicted outcomes.

At the level of the individual firm, this may be an improvement.

Across the labor market, the same kind of applicant can become systematically unobserved.

Now imagine a hundred lenders adopting similar credit-risk models. A hundred investment funds using similar risk controls. A hundred universities consulting similar rankings. A hundred hospitals buying similar decision-support systems. A hundred platforms optimizing similar engagement metrics.

Plurality of institutions does not guarantee plurality of decisions.

This is the monoculture problem.

The biological metaphor is useful because monocultures are efficient under familiar conditions. A crop variety chosen for yield can be planted at scale. Uniformity simplifies equipment, training, treatment, and harvesting. The vulnerability appears when a threat exploits the shared weakness.

Decision monocultures have a related vulnerability. They produce correlated blind spots.

If one employer undervalues an unconventional candidate, another employer can discover the mistake. The labor market learns through competition. If every employer uses the same underlying signals, the candidate may never receive the chance that would falsify the shared assumption.

A local false negative becomes a systemic non-event.

This is different from the usual concern about systemic risk, though they overlap. Financial systems worry about institutions holding similar assets or models because shocks can cause correlated losses. The unobserved-world concern is that similar decision systems can create correlated missing evidence even before a shock occurs.

Everyone looks prudent because everyone sees similar outcomes.

The danger is hardest to detect when the shared rule is genuinely strong.

Suppose a standardized medical guideline improves outcomes for most patients. Adoption spreads. Variation falls. This is good. If a small subgroup responds differently, however, the system now needs an explicit mechanism to discover that subgroup because ordinary practice no longer creates much variation. A monoculture of evidence-based care can still need exploration at the edges.

The alternative is not to preserve outdated care for the sake of diversity. It is to preserve scientific diversity after clinical standardization.

This distinction applies broadly. Standardization in aviation, engineering, food safety, accounting, and medicine has enormous value. Shared protocols prevent known errors. Compatibility lets systems work together. Best practices spread learning.

The phrase “monoculture” should not become a sneer at standards.

The question is what happens to unknown errors after the standard is universal.

A good standard has revision pathways. It collects incident reports. It supports exceptions under defined conditions. It updates when evidence changes. It does not interpret widespread adoption as proof of permanent correctness.

A bad monoculture mistakes compliance for validation.

Software makes the problem easier to scale because a decision rule can be distributed globally. One update changes millions of choices. That is part of software's power. It also means exploration can disappear in one release.

Human systems once had regional variation by default. Different branches used different processes. Managers interpreted rules differently. Hospitals had local protocols. Consumers encountered different inventories. This inconsistency could be unfair and inefficient, but it also created natural comparisons.

Centralized software can remove the inconsistency before the institution has designed a replacement source of learning.

This is an underappreciated cost of operational excellence.

A company celebrates that every branch now follows the same optimized workflow. Leadership gains visibility. Training becomes easier. Customers receive consistent service. Then the company loses the ability to observe whether a slightly different workflow might perform better in a different region because no branch is allowed to deviate.

Centralization converts local experiments into tickets.

The organization can compensate through formal experimentation. It can designate test sites, run controlled rollouts, encourage local proposals, or randomize eligible variations. But if no such structure exists, consistency becomes epistemic closure.

The same can happen through professional norms without centralized software. Consultants move across firms. Executives copy competitors. Regulators define acceptable practice. Accreditation bodies reward common procedures. Investors ask companies to present familiar metrics. Career risk encourages leaders to do what peers do.

A decision can be individually defensible precisely because everyone else makes it.

If the outcome is bad, no one looks uniquely foolish.

This produces a powerful incentive toward correlated exploitation.

Exploration is professionally asymmetric. The person who tries something unusual and fails owns the failure. The person who follows the industry standard and fails can point to the standard.

This is why institutions often need protected heterodoxy.

Research portfolios create it through multiple investigators. Democracies create it partly through federalism and local government. Markets create it through competition. Open-source ecosystems create it through forks. Universities create it through disciplines and academic freedom. Venture capital creates it through many funds making different bets. Courts create it through dissenting opinions and multiple jurisdictions.

These systems are messy.

Their mess can be a learning asset.

Federalism, for example, is sometimes described as enabling “laboratories” of democracy. The phrase is imperfect because citizens are not experimental material and states differ in ways that complicate comparison. Still, policy variation across jurisdictions can reveal alternatives a single national rule would hide. One city tries a street design. Another changes permitting. One state modifies a benefit. Researchers compare outcomes, with all the usual causal cautions.

Plural governance generates observations.

The benefit disappears if every jurisdiction buys the same policy package and implementation software.

This is not an argument against diffusion. When an intervention clearly works, copying is rational. The learning question is whether some independent variation remains at uncertain boundaries.

Markets provide a similar mechanism. If one firm wrongly rejects a customer segment, a competitor can serve it. If one publisher misses an author, another can publish the book. If one investor refuses a technology, another can fund it. Market diversity turns missed opportunities into potential competitor advantage.

But markets can converge.

Common data sources, shared infrastructure, benchmarking, regulation, and imitation can cause competitors to make similar choices. If every venture fund chases the same sector, if every lender uses the same score, if every recruiter buys the same screening service, competition in ownership may coexist with similarity in epistemology.

The system needs diversity of decision rules, not merely diversity of logos.

This becomes especially important as artificial intelligence becomes a general-purpose layer inside institutions. A model provider may not make the final decision, but its outputs can shape drafting, ranking, analysis, customer service, coding, research, and strategy across thousands of organizations. If many actors ask similar systems the same questions, they may receive different wording around similar priors.

The risk is not that one model controls society.

It is that shared cognitive infrastructure can reduce the variance through which society discovers model blind spots.

This is one reason open models, multiple vendors, human expertise, domain-specific systems, and local experimentation matter even when one system appears technically superior. Diversity can have option value.

That sentence should not be used to defend inferior systems indefinitely. A dangerous medical model should not remain in use merely to diversify the ecosystem. Security standards should not preserve known vulnerabilities. An airline should not keep an unsafe procedure alive because variation is educational.

Pluralism operates inside a safety envelope.

The same boundary appeared in exploration. The objective is not maximum variance. It is enough independent variation to detect unknown weaknesses.

How much is enough?

There is no universal number. The answer depends on the cost of correlated failure, the stability of the environment, the reversibility of decisions, and the availability of external evidence. A low-stakes recommendation system can afford constant experimentation. A nuclear-plant control system should rely heavily on validated standards and carefully isolated testing.

The book's argument gains credibility by accepting this asymmetry.

Some domains should be monocultural in operation and plural in research.

Others can be plural in operation.

A medical device can use a standardized safety protocol while multiple research teams test improvements offline. A bank can comply with common capital rules while experimenting with lawful underwriting products. A school can maintain curricular standards while testing different support systems. A city can follow building codes while piloting permitting workflows.

The design goal is layered diversity.

This is also a reason audit and red-team functions matter. They create institutional disagreement on purpose. The main system optimizes. Another group is paid to find ways it could be wrong. Security teams do this routinely because a system that only tests itself under expected conditions will miss adversarial failures.

Decision systems need epistemic red teams.

Instead of asking only “can someone exploit the model?” they can ask “what population does this policy rarely observe?” “Which assumptions are shared across all our vendors?” “What would a competitor have to believe differently to serve the people we reject?” “Which categories of outcome cannot reach our dashboard?”

These questions search the negative space.

A monopoly is not required for the negative space to become large. Coordination can create it. Industry best practice can create it. Regulation can create it. Benchmarking can create it. Professional education can create it.

This should make regulators particularly cautious when turning one model or metric into a standard. Regulation often seeks consistency for good reasons: equal treatment, safety, transparency, auditability. But a mandated scoring system can freeze an uncertain model into law. If every institution must use the same rule, no market actor can generate evidence from an alternative.

A well-designed regulation can distinguish minimum constraints from permitted experimentation.

Safety and rights can be standardized while methods compete.

The difference is crucial. A regulation can say what harms are unacceptable without prescribing one predictive technology forever. It can require evaluation, reporting, and fairness while allowing multiple models. It can create sandboxes or pilot pathways for new methods under supervision.

Governance can protect both reliability and learning.

The monoculture problem also appears inside data. If everyone trains on the same public benchmarks, model development can overfit to the benchmark's worldview. Researchers improve measured performance while weaknesses outside the benchmark remain underexplored. New evaluations help because they expose systems to different tasks and distributions.

Benchmarks are the scientific equivalent of familiar roads.

They are useful precisely because they are shared. They become dangerous when shared measurement is mistaken for complete measurement.

The answer, again, is not to abandon common standards. It is to keep adding tests from outside the current frame.

A resilient system has both memory and mutation.

Memory preserves what worked. Standards encode lessons. Repetition makes competence cheap. Mutation produces difference. Exploration exposes new possibilities. Independent institutions prevent one error from becoming universal.

Too much mutation and the system cannot accumulate knowledge. Too much memory and it cannot adapt.

Civilization has always balanced these forces imperfectly. The novelty of modern prediction is that it makes convergence faster. A good model can be copied instantly. A metric can spread across an industry. A recommendation architecture can become a default. A vendor can embed one assumption into thousands of workflows.

The speed of convergence increases the value of deliberate divergence.

Not contrarianism for show. Not random opposition. Not a committee member assigned to say no.

Real alternative policies, backed by enough resources and observation to produce evidence.

The future is safer when one good decision is allowed to remain one good decision long enough for another good decision to compete with it.
