# The Pilot That Never Happened

Governments are asked to decide at the wrong scale.

A minister announces a national program. A legislature passes a law. A city council adopts a rule. An agency changes a benefit, a form, an inspection regime, a school policy, a road design, a procurement standard. The language of government makes policy sound singular because public authority eventually has to produce a rule.

Before the rule, there is often uncertainty that would look familiar in any laboratory.

Will people understand the new form? Will the reminder increase payment? Will the benefit reach the intended group? Will the traffic change divert congestion or merely move it? Will a new permitting process reduce delay without lowering safety? Will an educational intervention survive when ordinary schools implement it rather than researchers?

Too often, these questions are argued as politics before they are tested as operations.

The result is a peculiar form of unobserved world: the pilot that never happened.

A policy is debated in its largest imaginable form. Supporters defend the best version. Opponents attack the worst. Because the national decision is consequential, uncertainty becomes threatening. Each side has an incentive to speak as though the outcome is already known.

A small experiment would admit ignorance.

That admission can be politically expensive.

The United Kingdom's Cabinet Office and Behavioural Insights Team published *Test, Learn, Adapt* in 2012 with Ben Goldacre and David Torgerson, arguing for much more systematic use of randomized controlled trials in public policy. The title contains the whole institutional idea. Do not decide once and then evaluate ceremonially. Build a process in which policy can generate evidence before it hardens.

Governments have expanded experimental capacity since then, in Britain and elsewhere. Cities run field experiments. Agencies pilot notices, tax interventions, job programs, educational tools, and administrative changes. Development economics has made randomized field trials common in many policy domains. Digital government can A/B test interfaces and communication.

The existence of these tools makes the places they are not used more visible.

Why does a policy sometimes move directly from argument to scale?

There are legitimate reasons. Laws may need uniformity. Emergencies can make delay costly. Small pilots may not capture network effects or political response. A national tax cannot always be tested in one neighborhood without avoidance. Infrastructure can require scale before benefits appear. People may object to unequal treatment. Agencies may lack legal authority to randomize. A pilot can create winners and losers in ways that violate expectations of equal public service.

Experiments are not a constitutional trump card.

There are also less admirable reasons. Leaders fear a result that weakens a preferred policy. Agencies lack staff to design trials. Procurement cycles make variation difficult. Evidence arrives after the political window closes. Managers are rewarded for launch, not learning. A failed experiment is visible; a failed counterfactual is not.

The political asymmetry resembles the organizational one from Chapter 5.

If a pilot fails, somebody authorized a failure. If no pilot occurs and a mediocre policy becomes standard, the alternative remains hypothetical.

The person who experiments can be blamed for what happened. The person who declines to experiment is rarely blamed for what nobody got to see.

Recent research has started to examine this directly. Guglielmo Briscese and John List studied the political economy of field experiments in policymaking. Their work includes evidence that policymakers can update beliefs after an experiment reveals an ineffective intervention, yet disappointing results may also reduce demand for further experimentation. In other words, evidence can teach the policy lesson while making the method politically less attractive.

That response is humanly understandable.

An experiment that contradicts a leader's belief does two things at once. It says the program may not work and reminds everyone that the leader did not know. The second message can hurt more than the first.

A learning government has to protect uncertainty from becoming humiliation.

This is one reason scientific institutions use protocols. A trial is not treated as a personal wager by the investigator every time. The field has norms for uncertainty, pre-registration, review, and null results. Government often lacks the same cultural buffer. A pilot can look like indecision because policy leadership is expected to project certainty.

The language needs to change from “we are trying this because we do not know what we are doing” to “we are sequencing commitment because the outcome depends on evidence.”

That is not spin. It is a different model of competence.

Competence in an uncertain environment includes knowing what must be learned before scale.

The strongest version of this argument is not about cute behavioral nudges. It is about architecture.

A policy can be designed so that rollout itself creates comparisons. Regions can phase implementation at different times. Capacity constraints can create lotteries among eligible participants. Thresholds can support credible quasi-experiments. Administrative data can track outcomes before and after changes. Independent evaluators can specify measures in advance. Pilots can test implementation rather than only theory.

These designs make evidence a property of execution.

The weaker model is to implement everywhere and commission an evaluation later. By then the policy has constituencies, vendors, staff, forms, software, and political identity. Reversal becomes costly. The evaluation is no longer informing a choice between open options. It is reviewing an institution.

The timing of evidence matters.

An experiment after lock-in can tell you a policy is weak and still fail to change it.

Stefano DellaVigna, Woojin Kim, and Elizabeth Linos followed 67 U.S. city departments that collectively ran 73 randomized controlled trials with a national nudge unit. Their work asks a second question after “did the experiment produce evidence?”: did the organization adopt the result? Follow-on adoption of the tested nudge treatment occurred in only a minority of cases in their analysis. The strength of the experimental evidence had limited influence on adoption; organizational features, including retention of the staff member connected to the trial, mattered.

Generating evidence and using evidence are different capabilities.

This is the chapter where the book's title expands. The unobserved world can disappear at two points.

First, the institution never runs the alternative and therefore never creates evidence.

Second, the institution creates the evidence and then fails to preserve or act on it, effectively making the learned alternative unobservable to future practice.

A learning institution needs both exploration and memory.

Government is especially vulnerable to the memory problem because people rotate. Elections change leadership. Civil servants transfer. Vendors change. Reports move to archives. Programs are renamed. A pilot can be successful, then vanish because the person who knew how it worked leaves.

This is not a failure of statistics. It is a failure of organizational continuity.

A 2026 NBER paper by Patrick Agte, Daniel Morales, Christopher Neilson, Sebastián Otero, and Gautam Rao adds another layer. The researchers studied scaling an educational program in the Dominican Republic that had previously shown effectiveness in a randomized trial. When the government ordered implementation at national scale, actual verified implementation was much lower than in the original trial. Interventions such as sharing research evidence, modest financial incentives, or implementation assistance did not close the gap in that setting.

The result is sobering because it separates “we know this can work” from “we know how to make a bureaucracy do it at scale.”

Scale is a new treatment.

That sentence is worth sitting with.

A policy tested with researchers, special attention, motivated staff, and a bounded sample is not identical to the same nominal policy rolled through ordinary administration. The implementation environment changes. A successful pilot produces evidence about a version of the program, not a guarantee about the national system.

This is often used as an argument against experiments: pilots do not generalize.

The better response is that scale itself needs to be studied.

A phased rollout can test implementation. Multiple sites can reveal heterogeneity. Process measures can identify where the mechanism breaks. Replication can distinguish a lucky first setting from a robust intervention. Evidence should travel with uncertainty attached.

The unobserved world is not defeated by one randomized trial.

It is managed by a sequence of comparisons.

Public policy also demonstrates why randomness can be politically sensitive even when scientifically attractive. Citizens may ask why one eligible person receives a service and another does not. In some settings the answer is unacceptable. In others, scarcity already forces rationing. A lottery among equally eligible people can be fairer than opaque discretion and can create a credible evaluation.

The ethical difference depends on the baseline.

If a program can serve everyone, withholding it for research can be difficult to justify when strong evidence already supports it. If capacity is limited and some fair allocation rule is necessary, randomization can both allocate and learn. If uncertainty is genuine, a trial may protect future participants from a policy that only sounds good.

Randomness is most defensible when it replaces arbitrary scarcity, not when it manufactures deprivation.

The same principle applies to phased implementation. A government may be unable to launch in every region at once. If the order of rollout is determined transparently and the phases create a comparison, learning can be built into a constraint that already exists.

This is exploration without extra cruelty.

There is another political reason pilots matter: they allow ideas to lose without losing everything.

A national reform tied to an ideology is difficult to abandon because reversal can be interpreted as ideological defeat. A local pilot can fail as a design. The smaller commitment reduces identity costs.

Reversibility creates psychological room for evidence.

This does not mean every policy should be incremental. Some problems require decisive action. Rights are not experiments. A government should not pilot whether equal protection is desirable. Emergency response can require acting under uncertainty before clean evidence exists. Climate, war, pandemics, financial crises, and infrastructure can involve time horizons where waiting has costs.

The discipline is to distinguish value choices from empirical mechanism questions.

Democracy decides goals through politics. Evidence can help determine whether a particular mechanism advances them.

Confusing the two creates endless conflict. A randomized trial cannot tell a society how much redistribution is just, but it can test whether a benefit-delivery design increases take-up. It cannot determine how much privacy citizens should trade for security, but it can measure whether a screening process detects threats. It cannot decide whether a city should prioritize drivers or pedestrians, but it can estimate how a street redesign changes crashes and travel time.

The experiment answers a narrower question than the politics.

That narrowness is a strength.

It gives disagreement somewhere to go besides rhetoric.

The best public systems create a route from uncertainty to bounded trial to measurement to adaptation to scale. The worst jump from uncertainty to permanent implementation and then treat the resulting institution as evidence that the original idea was inevitable.

Once a policy exists, it creates data. That data can make the policy easier to model and administer than alternatives. Vendors specialize. Staff develop expertise. Citizens adapt. Metrics accumulate. The familiar policy becomes increasingly legible.

The alternative becomes more expensive to imagine.

This is why the pilot that never happened matters even when the existing program is not a disaster.

A society can become trapped in adequate systems because no alternative ever received enough reality to become credible.

Experimentation is one way to keep policy counterfactuals alive.

Not all of them. Not forever. Not without ethical boundaries.

Just enough that “we have never done it that way” stops masquerading as evidence that it would not work.
