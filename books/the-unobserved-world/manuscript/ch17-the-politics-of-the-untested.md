# The Politics of the Untested

A policy can survive because it works.

It can also survive because nobody has arranged a fair contest.

The distinction is difficult to maintain in politics because policy is never only a technical mechanism. It expresses values, distributes resources, creates rights and obligations, rewards some behavior, restricts other behavior, and becomes attached to parties, leaders, institutions, and identities.

An experiment asks a narrow question. Politics hears a broader one.

Test a job-search reminder and someone may hear an argument about whether unemployed people are trying hard enough. Test a policing strategy and the experiment carries the history of state power. Test an education intervention and parents may hear a claim about their children. Test a tax notice and the mechanism sits inside a dispute about taxation itself.

This is why the phrase “just test it” can be politically naive.

There is no laboratory outside society.

Still, the opposite mistake is costly. When every empirical mechanism becomes fused to a value dispute, policies can remain untested precisely because testing feels like conceding uncertainty about the values.

A society can know what it wants and still be unsure how to get there.

You can believe children deserve a strong education without knowing which tutoring model works best. You can believe taxes should be collected without knowing which notice improves compliance. You can support cleaner air without knowing which incentive changes household behavior. You can believe eligible citizens should receive a benefit without knowing which application design maximizes take-up.

The goal is political. The mechanism is empirical.

Keeping those categories separate creates room for learning without pretending evidence can settle every political disagreement.

This is harder than it sounds because successful policies acquire moral stories. A program that has existed for years is not merely a mechanism; it has employees, beneficiaries, opponents, vendors, procedures, and symbols. An alternative threatens all of them.

The untested policy therefore begins with a political disadvantage.

The existing system has facts. The alternative has forecasts.

The existing system can point to people it serves, jobs it supports, buildings it occupies, reports it publishes, and routines that officials understand. The alternative can point to models and examples from elsewhere. Even when the status quo performs badly, it has the epistemic advantage of existence.

This is status quo bias with infrastructure.

A pilot can reduce the disadvantage by giving the alternative a small piece of reality.

That is why experimentation can be politically transformative even when the experiment itself is mundane. It changes the argument from “this could never work here” to “this worked under these conditions in these places.” The alternative becomes discussable in the language institutions trust.

But experiments can also threaten political actors because they create the possibility of public falsification.

Guglielmo Briscese and John List's work on the political economy of field experiments points to this tension. In their study, policymakers updated beliefs after an experiment showed an intervention was ineffective. Yet evidence contradicting expectations could also reduce demand for further experimentation.

The mechanism is not hard to imagine. If experimentation repeatedly exposes overconfidence, leaders may learn to avoid the instrument rather than improve the beliefs.

A thermometer that keeps bringing bad news can become unpopular.

This is not unique to government. Companies stop running tests that threaten a favored product. researchers avoid experiments likely to produce null results. Individuals stop weighing themselves when the number hurts.

Information has political costs.

A learning institution must therefore make bad news survivable.

That requires norms separating the failure of a hypothesis from the failure of a person. Science aspires to this imperfectly. A null result can be a valid result. Engineering treats a test that reveals a flaw before deployment as a success of the testing process. Aviation investigates incidents because uncovering weakness is better than preserving reputation until catastrophe.

Politics often does the reverse. An experiment that fails can be weaponized as evidence of incompetence.

The rational politician learns not to experiment.

Institutional design can change the incentive. Independent evaluation bodies can own methods. Pilots can be routine rather than attached to one leader's personality. Pre-specified criteria can reduce post hoc spin. Cross-party support for trial infrastructure can make the method less ideological. Civil servants can maintain experimentation capacity across administrations.

The goal is to make uncertainty bureaucratically normal.

This sounds dull. That is an advantage.

When experimentation is a routine part of administration, each trial carries less symbolic weight. A form redesign can fail without becoming a referendum on the minister. A city can compare reminder messages without pretending the test determines the morality of enforcement. A school district can evaluate a tutoring schedule without turning every implementation detail into an ideological identity.

Routine lowers the ego cost of learning.

There are limits that should remain political rather than experimental. Rights should not be allocated as though their desirability were an uncertain treatment effect. A government should not randomize whether one group receives due process to see if it improves efficiency. Equal protection is not a hypothesis. Consent and dignity matter even when violating them would produce useful data.

This boundary is essential because the rhetoric of evidence can become technocratic arrogance.

An experiment can answer “what happens if we do this?” It cannot answer “should we value that outcome?” without importing a value judgment.

Suppose an experiment shows that a surveillance practice reduces a certain kind of crime. That does not settle whether the privacy cost is acceptable. A policy can be effective and unjust. Another can be inefficient but legally or morally required.

Evidence informs politics. It does not replace it.

The unobserved-world argument therefore applies most cleanly to mechanism uncertainty inside democratically chosen boundaries.

Once a society decides that an outcome matters and certain interventions are permissible, experimentation can compare how to achieve the outcome. The political system remains responsible for the goal and constraints.

This separation also helps with fairness objections to randomized policy. People reasonably resist being treated differently by government for reasons they cannot control. Random assignment can look arbitrary, especially when benefits are valuable.

The baseline matters.

If government has enough capacity to give a proven benefit to everyone, withholding it solely to create a control group may be hard to justify. If capacity is scarce and some people must wait, a lottery can be fairer than discretion and can create evidence. If two interventions are both considered acceptable and genuine uncertainty remains, randomized assignment may be defensible with appropriate oversight.

Randomness does not create ethical permission.

It can improve allocation inside permission.

This distinction will anchor the next chapter. In politics, it also changes public explanation. A government should not say, “We are experimenting on you.” It should be able to say, accurately, “We have several permissible ways to deliver this service and do not know which works best. We are comparing them under independent rules, measuring outcomes, and will change policy based on the result.”

That is a higher bar than a press release promising innovation.

It creates accountability for learning.

One underappreciated political value of pilots is that they make revision legitimate. A permanent policy announcement creates an expectation of persistence. A pilot contains an expiration date. Everyone knows the rule can change.

Temporary institutions are easier to learn from because they have not yet become entitlements, contracts, or identities.

Sunset clauses can play a similar role, though they are often ignored or routinely renewed. A real sunset forces reconsideration. Staged authorization requires evidence before expansion. Regulatory sandboxes permit bounded alternatives. Waivers allow local experiments while preserving a national baseline.

These are legal mechanisms for keeping counterfactuals alive.

They do not guarantee learning. A pilot can be designed to succeed. Metrics can be cherry-picked. Temporary programs can become permanent through political inertia. Sandboxes can become loopholes. Evaluation can be delayed until nobody cares.

Institutional form is not enough.

The evidence system needs adversarial capacity.

Someone must be allowed to ask whether the measured outcome is the right one, whether harms were distributed unevenly, whether implementation differed from the plan, whether the comparison was credible, and whether the policy can generalize.

This is another reason plural institutions matter. Universities, auditors, journalists, civil-society groups, opposition parties, inspectors general, courts, and independent agencies can examine different parts of the evidence.

A government evaluating itself entirely through its own chosen metrics has the same circularity as a model learning only from labels its own policy produced.

Democracy is partly an architecture of external feedback.

Opposition is expensive, noisy, and often frustrating. It also produces information the governing coalition would not choose to generate.

This is not to idealize political conflict. Partisan incentives can distort evidence, and opposition can become automatic denial. The point is structural: independent actors make it harder for one decision rule to control both policy and its evaluation.

The unobserved world shrinks when many institutions can look.

The politics of the untested also includes people excluded before policy formation. A benefit that requires a complicated application receives feedback from successful applicants. People who never apply may be absent from administrative data. A transit meeting hears from residents who have time and knowledge to attend. A public comment process records people aware of the rule. Participation is selected.

Government can mistake the people it hears for the public.

Outreach, surveys, administrative linkage, community organizations, and randomized contact can widen the sample. Again, the goal is not total observation. Democratic privacy and freedom include the right not to be constantly measured.

The goal is to avoid equating silence with satisfaction.

This is the public-policy version of “no test is not a negative test.”

No complaint is not proof of no harm. No application is not proof of no demand. No participation is not proof of no preference. The absence may be produced by the design of the channel.

Politicians understand this intuitively when it helps their argument. A low take-up rate can be framed as lack of demand or as evidence the application is too hard. A small crowd can be framed as apathy or as poor outreach. The same observation supports different causal stories.

Experimentation can sometimes separate them.

Simplify the form for a randomized group. Send outreach to a sample. Change the deadline. Compare response. Small interventions can reveal whether the barrier was preference or process.

This is the humble face of experimental government.

It does not replace grand political choice. It prevents unnecessary ignorance about how the machinery works.

The larger ambition is cultural. A democracy that cannot admit uncertainty will accumulate policies whose main evidence is their own survival. A democracy that treats every policy as provisional will exhaust citizens and undermine stability.

The balance resembles everything else in this book.

Exploit what is well established. Explore where uncertainty matters and comparison is ethical. Preserve rights outside the experiment. Build evidence before lock-in where possible. Keep evaluation independent of the policy's authors. Make revision a sign of learning rather than humiliation.

The politics of the untested is ultimately the politics of being allowed to change your mind.

Evidence has little value in a system where changing your mind is punished more than being quietly wrong.
