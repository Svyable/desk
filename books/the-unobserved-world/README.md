# The Unobserved World

## How Prediction Shrinks the Future by Erasing the Roads We Never Try

Every consequential decision creates two histories. One becomes a record. The other disappears.

A company interviews one applicant and rejects another. A lender approves one borrower and turns away another. A physician orders a test for one patient and sends another home. A school advances one student and places another in remediation. A recommendation system puts one song in front of millions of listeners while another remains almost unheard. Later, the institution studies the outcomes and calls what it sees data.

But the data was partly manufactured by the earlier decision.

*The Unobserved World* is about that missing half of modern evidence. Its central argument is that prediction systems do not merely forecast a future that exists independently of them. When predictions are used to allocate access, attention, money, testing, employment, treatment, education, or exposure, they help decide which futures will occur often enough to become learnable. A system that becomes very good at exploiting yesterday’s evidence can therefore become worse at producing tomorrow’s evidence.

This is not an argument against prediction. It is an argument for recognizing a second job that decision-making institutions have always performed, usually without naming it: they do not merely choose among known options. They decide what society gets to learn next.

The book follows that problem from the fundamental problem of causal inference into hiring, lending, medicine, education, recommendation systems, science funding, public policy, and organizational strategy. It asks when randomization is not indecision but instrumentation, when fairness requires exploration rather than equal treatment of a frozen model, and how institutions can preserve enough variation to discover people, treatments, ideas, and policies that their existing evidence has not yet learned how to value.

## Editorial principles

1. **Start with the missing outcome.** Each chapter should make the absent evidence concrete before introducing formal language.
2. **Distinguish prediction from learning.** A model can predict well on observed cases while the institution remains ignorant about cases it systematically excludes.
3. **Treat algorithms as part of a decision system.** The relevant causal chain includes policy, incentives, human overrides, access, feedback, and measurement—not just model architecture.
4. **Do not romanticize randomness.** Exploration has costs. Randomization is appropriate only within ethically and operationally defensible boundaries.
5. **Preserve domain differences.** Hiring, medicine, lending, education, science, and culture do not share the same stakes or lawful interventions.
6. **Prefer experiments and primary research.** Use causal evidence where available, and label observational associations as observational.
7. **Name boundary conditions.** Every chapter’s research notes should record what would make its central claim false, narrower, or setting-specific.
8. **End with institutional design, not a slogan.** The practical question is how a system can make good decisions today without consuming the evidence it will need tomorrow.
