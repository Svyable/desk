# Chapter 11 research note — funding lotteries as bounded exploration

## Why this note exists

Chapter 11, “The Funding Line,” argues that partial lotteries can be useful when peer review can identify proposals worth funding but cannot reliably defend fine-grained rankings among near-equals. The current manuscript makes that argument carefully, but its evidence for actual operational use leans partly on a 2026 *Nature* feature. This note adds a first-party funder source so the chapter can distinguish proposal from practice.

## Primary-source anchor

The Health Research Council of New Zealand (HRC) described its Explorer Grant process in an October 1, 2015 call notice. HRC said applications were short and anonymised, assessors judged whether they were transformative and viable, and all applications clearing that gate were equally eligible for funding. Successful proposals were then selected through a random process.

HRC’s stated rationale matters. The agency said random allocation was particularly suited to Explorer Grants because high-risk applications with less predictable outcomes may not be meaningfully rankable by conventional scoring. This is a direct institutional example of the mechanism Chapter 11 describes: judgment sets a quality boundary; randomization operates inside the remaining uncertainty rather than replacing judgment altogether.

HRC’s current research repository also records Explorer Grant awards in 2026, showing that the grant category remains operational. The repository entry for a 2026 paediatric-epilepsy project records a NZ$150,000 Explorer Grant with a 24-month duration. That current record confirms continuity of the program category, but it should not be used by itself to infer that every current Explorer Grant round uses exactly the same random-selection procedure documented in 2015 unless the current call rules are separately checked.

## Strongest counterargument and boundary

The HRC case does not establish that lotteries outperform peer review generally. Explorer Grants are deliberately unusual: they target transformative, exploratory, or unconventional research and use a thresholded process designed for proposals whose outcomes are especially difficult to rank. A mechanism that is sensible there may be inappropriate for large infrastructure awards, tightly specified mission programs, or proposals with large and defensible differences in expected value.

The stronger claim is narrower: when a funder has already judged several proposals to be viable and worthwhile, and admits that fine ranking would extract more precision than review can support, bounded randomization can convert acknowledged uncertainty into portfolio variation.

## Editorial implication

A later prose pass can replace the chapter’s generic sentence that “versions are being used” with the HRC example. The detail improves narrative specificity without changing the chapter’s argument: the lottery is not a rejection of merit; it is a refusal to manufacture certainty after merit review has reached its evidentiary limit.
