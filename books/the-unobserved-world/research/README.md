# Research Brief — The Unobserved World

## Purpose

This book examines a specific epistemic cost of decision systems: choices determine which outcomes become observable, so a system can improve at selecting from known evidence while quietly degrading its ability to learn about people, treatments, ideas, products, and policies it rarely chooses.

The argument joins several mature research traditions that are usually discussed separately: potential-outcomes causal inference, selective labels, exploration versus exploitation, contextual bandits, organizational learning, experimental policy, recommender-system diversity, and research-funding design. The manuscript should not imply that any one paper proves the book’s whole thesis. The book’s contribution is the synthesis and the institutional question that follows from it.

## Evidence hierarchy

Prefer, in order:

1. Peer-reviewed original research and authoritative working papers with transparent methods.
2. Randomized experiments, natural experiments, and well-identified quasi-experiments for causal claims.
3. Government, regulator, standards-body, or first-party operational evidence for system-specific facts.
4. High-quality observational studies for associations, with causal limits stated in the prose.
5. Reputable reporting for chronology or context, never as the sole support for a technical mechanism when the original is available.

## Claim rules

- The potential-outcomes framework does not imply that every social decision is practically or ethically randomizable.
- Selective labels are a data problem created when outcomes are observed only for cases selected by an earlier policy. Do not use the term for ordinary missing data without that decision dependence.
- A predictive model may be accurate on the distribution it sees. The book’s concern is what happens when deployment changes that distribution and therefore changes the evidence available for future learning.
- Exploration is not synonymous with novelty, diversity, affirmative action, or randomness. It is purposeful information acquisition under uncertainty.
- Do not claim that a contextual-bandit policy is appropriate in high-stakes domains merely because bandits are mathematically relevant.
- Never treat denial of medical care, unsafe lending, or other harmful exposure as an acceptable experiment. Ethical constraints bound the action set before exploration begins.
- Distinguish individual benefit from institutional learning. A decision that generates information may still be wrong for the person subjected to it.
- Separate short-run predictive performance from long-run learning performance.
- When a result comes from one firm, hospital system, city, platform, or country, say so.
- Recommendation-system diversity research often mixes observational and experimental components. Preserve the authors’ causal caveats.
- Partial lotteries in research funding operate after eligibility or quality screening; do not describe them as replacing judgment with pure chance.

## Chapter source trails and falsification notes

### Chapter 1 — Every Choice Deletes a Dataset

Core sources: Holland (1986) on causal inference and potential outcomes; Lakkaraju et al. (2017) on selective labels; Kleinberg et al. (2018) on human decisions and machine predictions.

**Falsification note:** A rejected option does not always contain valuable information. Many actions are dominated, unsafe, illegal, or too costly to sample. The chapter’s claim is narrower: when institutions learn from outcomes generated by their own prior selections, systematic exclusion can create systematic ignorance.

### Chapter 2 — The Fundamental Problem

Core sources: Holland (1986); Rubin’s potential-outcomes tradition as summarized there; standard experimental-design logic.

**Falsification note:** Counterfactual outcomes are not literally hidden records waiting to be retrieved. They are conceptual quantities used to define causal effects. Experiments estimate distributions and average effects under assumptions; they do not reveal both outcomes for one individual.

### Chapter 3 — When Prediction Becomes Policy

Core sources: Lakkaraju et al. (2017); Kleinberg et al. (2018); Mullainathan and Obermeyer (2019/2023 revision).

**Falsification note:** Prediction alone need not alter the data-generating process. The feedback problem arises when predictions influence action, exposure, measurement, or access.

### Chapter 4 — Learning From the People You Let Through

Core sources: Lakkaraju et al. (2017); Li, Raymond, and Bergman (2026); Auer, Cesa-Bianchi, and Fischer (2002).

**Falsification note:** Exploration can be statistically useful and still institutionally inappropriate. The chapter must keep separate the mathematical value of information and the normative legitimacy of obtaining it.

### Chapter 5 — Success Can Make You Blind

Core sources: March (1991); Auer et al. (2002); Li et al. (2026).

**Falsification note:** Exploitation is not a pathology. Mature systems should use reliable knowledge. The danger is a persistent imbalance that makes short-run optimization crowd out the variation required for learning.

### Chapter 6 — The Interview List

Core source: Li, Raymond, and Bergman, “Hiring as Exploration,” Review of Economic Studies 93(2), 2026.

**Falsification note:** The empirical results come from professional-services recruiting within one Fortune 500 firm and should not be generalized to every labor market. Diversity effects depend on the applicant pool, historical data, objective, action set, and implementation.

### Chapter 7 — Credit for the Known

Core sources: Björkegren and Grissen (2018) on digital credit and alternative data; selective-label literature for the general problem; domain-specific lending literature should be used cautiously because repayment is observed principally after credit is extended.

**Falsification note:** Lenders also learn through bureau data, other institutions, collateral, macroeconomic variation, and rejected applicants who later borrow elsewhere. A single lender’s labels are not the whole credit market.

### Chapter 8 — The Untested Patient

Core source: Mullainathan and Obermeyer, “Diagnosing Physician Error,” NBER Working Paper 26168, revised 2023; selective-label literature for testing decisions.

**Falsification note:** Medicine is not an arena for unconstrained exploration. Diagnostic testing has costs, false positives, downstream procedures, and patient burdens. Natural variation and carefully governed trials can supply evidence without treating patients as arbitrary experiments.

### Chapter 9 — Placement

Core source: Bergman, Kopko, and Rodriguez, NBER Working Paper 28948, a seven-college experiment in algorithmic placement.

**Falsification note:** The reported selective-label effect differs by subject in that study; it substantially affected prediction error for college English and had little effect for college math. Do not turn a setting-specific result into a universal law of education.

### Chapter 10 — Discover Weekly

Core sources: Anderson et al. (2020) on diversity of consumption at Spotify; Hansen et al. (2021) on shifting consumption toward diverse content.

**Falsification note:** The 2020 study explicitly warns that lower diversity in algorithmically driven listening is not, by itself, evidence that recommendations caused users to become less diverse. User preference influences both mode and consumption. The chapter uses the work to show a measurable exploration tension, not a blanket indictment of recommenders.

### Chapter 11 — The Funding Line

Core sources: Liu et al. (2024) on grant-funding lotteries; Fang and Casadevall (2016) on modified lotteries; Nature reporting in 2026 on operational adoption and debate.

**Falsification note:** Lotteries do not prove that peer review is useless. Partial lotteries are generally proposed for proposals already judged fundable or too close to rank reliably. They trade one kind of uncertainty and discretion for another.

### Chapter 12 — The Pilot That Never Happened

Core sources: UK Cabinet Office, “Test, Learn, Adapt” (2012); DellaVigna, Kim, and Linos (2022); Briscese and List (2024).

**Falsification note:** Not every policy can be piloted cleanly, and experiments can have external-validity, spillover, political, timing, and implementation limits. A failed pilot may reject an implementation rather than an underlying mechanism.

### Chapter 13 — Feedback Without Exploration

Core sources: March (1991); Auer et al. (2002); Lakkaraju et al. (2017).

**Falsification note:** Feedback loops can improve performance when the observed sample remains representative of future use. The problem is not feedback itself but endogenous exposure combined with insufficient correction or exploration.

### Chapter 14 — The Monoculture of Good Decisions

Core sources: March (1991) and empirical examples from hiring, recommendation, and policy chapters.

**Falsification note:** Standardization often improves safety, reliability, and fairness. The chapter concerns correlated decision rules that remove informative variation, not standardization as such.

### Chapter 15 — The Vanishing Weird

Core sources: Spotify diversity research; March (1991); science-funding literature.

**Falsification note:** Unusual options are not valuable merely because they are unusual. Most novelty fails. The argument is about maintaining enough bounded exposure that rare high-value departures can be discovered.

### Chapter 16 — When Fairness Requires Curiosity

Core source: Li et al. (2026), plus selective-label and education-placement research.

**Falsification note:** Exploration is not a substitute for anti-discrimination law or fairness constraints. A system can explore while remaining discriminatory, and an exploration bonus can create unequal burdens if poorly designed.

### Chapter 17 — The Politics of the Untested

Core sources: Briscese and List (2024); DellaVigna et al. (2022); UK experimental-government guidance.

**Falsification note:** Political resistance to experiments can reflect legitimate concerns about unequal treatment, consent, capacity, or trust—not merely irrational aversion to evidence.

### Chapter 18 — Randomness With a Reason

Core sources: Auer et al. (2002); funding-lottery research; classical randomized-trial logic.

**Falsification note:** Randomization is an instrument, not a moral solvent. It is most defensible when the relevant options clear a prior threshold of safety or merit and when genuine uncertainty remains.

### Chapter 19 — Institutions That Learn

Core sources: March (1991); DellaVigna et al. (2022); Agte et al. (2026) on barriers between evidence and implementation.

**Falsification note:** Generating evidence does not guarantee adoption. Learning institutions need memory, authority, incentives, implementation capacity, and mechanisms to revisit decisions.

### Chapter 20 — Keep the Future Observable

Synthesis chapter. Reuse primary sources above rather than introducing a late cascade of new claims.

**Falsification note:** The book does not argue for maximizing observability. Privacy, safety, dignity, rights, and irreversible harms can properly make some futures unavailable for experimentation. The design objective is enough ethical variation to prevent self-confirming ignorance.

## Central synthesis to preserve

The manuscript repeatedly returns to four distinctions without turning them into a branded framework:

- **Observed outcome versus counterfactual outcome:** causal inference begins with what cannot be jointly observed.
- **Prediction versus intervention:** a forecast becomes epistemically consequential when it changes exposure or access.
- **Exploitation versus exploration:** using knowledge and producing new knowledge are different objectives.
- **Decision quality versus learning quality:** a system can improve one while degrading the other.

The ending should not demand that institutions become laboratories. It should ask them to recognize that they already are laboratories, because every repeated policy determines which evidence the future will contain.
