# The May Order

Executive orders are written to look like decisions.

The good ones are decisions.

The dangerous ones can also be invitations to find out how much power everyone thinks the president has.

On May 1, 2025, Donald Trump signed Executive Order 14290, titled *Ending Taxpayer Subsidization of Biased Media*. Its argument was compact. Government funding of news media was unnecessary in the modern media environment. NPR and PBS, in the president’s judgment, did not provide a fair and accurate presentation of the news. Taxpayers should not be forced to subsidize what the order described as biased coverage. The administration therefore directed federal actors to end support for the two organizations to the maximum extent consistent with law.

The order sounded simple because its target was familiar.

Its legal object was not.

The president did not control NPR.

He did not control PBS.

He did not ordinarily control CPB as though it were an executive agency.

Congress had created the corporation precisely to prevent federal officials from using public money as a line of editorial command.

The order therefore contained its own caveat: action had to be consistent with law.

That phrase transformed a proclamation into a jurisdictional puzzle.

What, exactly, could the executive branch stop?

The administration could direct agencies under presidential control to examine their grants and contracts. It could tell officials to identify direct or indirect flows of federal money to NPR and PBS. It could instruct executive departments to avoid creating new support where statutes left discretion. It could take legal positions about CPB’s obligations. It could use the bully pulpit to define public broadcasting as an illegitimate subsidy. It could prepare a rescission request to Congress.

What it could not obviously do was order an independent congressionally chartered corporation to disregard the law governing its distribution of appropriated funds.

That boundary became the dispute.

The order’s political function did not depend on the boundary being clear.

Presidential directives operate in several registers at once. They tell agencies what to do. They tell allies what the president values. They tell opponents where the administration intends to push. They generate legal arguments. They create deadlines. They force institutions that prefer obscurity into public positions. They can also establish a narrative in which every resistance to the order becomes evidence of entrenched bureaucracy.

For public broadcasting, the order did something else.

It converted an appropriations fight into a speech fight.

For decades, conservatives had argued that CPB funding should end because public media was unnecessary, biased, or both. Presidents had proposed eliminating the appropriation. Congress had chosen whether to agree.

The May order named NPR and PBS and linked the funding action directly to the president’s assessment of their coverage.

That specificity created the constitutional problem.

The First Amendment does not generally require the government to subsidize private speech. If Congress creates a grant program for highway construction, a newspaper cannot demand highway money because newspapers engage in protected expression. If Congress abolishes an arts program, artists cannot ordinarily compel continuation of the appropriation merely because art is speech.

But once government creates a benefit, it does not have unlimited freedom to distribute or withdraw that benefit based on hostility to protected viewpoints.

The doctrine is sometimes described through the language of unconstitutional conditions. Government may not always do indirectly through a benefit what it could not do directly through coercion. The details vary by context, and not every funding condition violates the Constitution. Governments routinely define programs that support some speech and not other speech. A grant for veterans’ oral histories need not finance a cooking show. A science agency can fund science rather than political campaigns.

The public-broadcasting order was different because it did not merely define a program.

It identified speakers.

The president’s complaint was editorial.

That gave NPR and PBS a straightforward theory when they sued: the administration was retaliating against them for constitutionally protected journalism.

The government’s response had its own force. The president was making a policy judgment about federal spending. The broadcasters did not possess a constitutional entitlement to taxpayer money. The executive order repeatedly acknowledged statutory limits. The administration could argue that ending a subsidy does not censor anyone; NPR and PBS remained free to speak.

The clash exposed why constitutional law often turns on characterization.

Call the action “ending a subsidy,” and the case sounds like spending policy.

Call it “punishing disfavored press,” and it sounds like retaliation.

The facts had to determine which description fit.

The White House made that task harder for itself by saying exactly what it believed.

The order did not announce a neutral review of all federal media support. It did not set a general rule that the government would no longer fund national news organizations. It focused on NPR and PBS and explained the decision through allegations of bias.

Political clarity became legal evidence.

This is a recurring tradeoff in executive power. A president wants supporters to know why an action is being taken. Lawyers often prefer a narrower, more neutral explanation that leaves fewer handles for judicial review. The more explicitly an administration ties an adverse government action to protected speech, the easier it can become for plaintiffs to argue retaliatory motive.

Trump’s political style rarely favored ambiguity.

The order was a message.

It was also a record.

The public broadcasters reacted quickly.

NPR and PBS emphasized their independence, public-service missions, and the local station system that would be affected by the policy. They disputed the bias charge and warned that the administration was trying to use government power against journalism it disliked. The litigation that followed would ask the courts to preserve not only money but a principle about how presidents may interact with publicly supported media.

CPB faced a stranger problem.

The corporation had been structured so that it could plausibly say the president was not its boss.

That proposition sounds almost provocative in an era accustomed to political fights over the scope of presidential authority. If federal money supports an organization and the president appoints members of its board, how can the president not direct it?

The answer lay in the statute and the purpose behind it.

Congress had placed CPB outside the usual executive-department structure. The corporation’s role as a buffer would be meaningless if a president could dictate grant recipients according to editorial approval. Federal law also restricted government interference in programming. The architecture reflected a deliberate distrust of direct political control.

The Trump administration’s broader theory of executive power pushed in the opposite direction.

Across the federal government, the second Trump term contested forms of independence that limited presidential control over personnel and institutions. Public broadcasting therefore became part of a larger constitutional argument: how much authority can Congress place beyond direct presidential supervision when the institution performs functions connected to federal law and money?

That argument was already visible before the May order.

In April, the White House had sent termination notices to three members of CPB’s board.

CPB disputed the removals and went to court.

The board fight and the funding order were legally distinct. Together they revealed the administration’s approach. One track challenged who controlled the corporation. Another challenged where its money could go. A third, soon to become decisive, asked Congress to remove the money itself.

The redundancy was strategic even if it was not planned as one formal three-part operation.

If the administration won the board fight, greater control over CPB could change the institution from within.

If it won the executive-order fight, agencies and CPB might have to stop funds from reaching NPR and PBS.

If Congress granted the rescission, the broader appropriation would disappear regardless of how the first two legal disputes ended.

Institutional resilience is weakest against redundant attacks because defenders tend to specialize.

Lawyers fight the removal case.

Another legal team fights the executive order.

Government-relations staff fight the rescission.

Station managers prepare budgets.

Fundraisers call donors.

Each actor sees the threat most relevant to the actor’s job.

The attacker only needs one pathway to produce irreversible effect.

The May order was therefore important even though it did not, by itself, dissolve CPB.

It changed expectations.

Expectations are not merely psychological in a grant-funded system. They affect contracts, hiring, borrowing, capital expenditures, negotiations, and donor behavior. A station manager who believes federal support is likely to vanish may freeze a position before Congress votes. A vendor may ask different questions about a multi-year agreement. A foundation may accelerate emergency support. Employees may start looking for work. National organizations may prepare cost reductions.

Policy uncertainty can spend money before the policy does.

The order also widened the target beyond CPB’s formal appropriation.

Its language about direct and indirect federal funding invited agencies to look for other relationships with NPR and PBS. Public broadcasters participate in a world of federal grants, contracts, educational partnerships, emergency communication, and station-level support. Even if CPB’s core appropriation had survived, the order signaled that federal agencies should scrutinize those ties.

This feature later mattered in court because the executive branch could affect plaintiffs through funding channels Congress had not explicitly rescinded.

The March 2026 injunction would block agencies from enforcing the order’s speech-based restrictions even after CPB itself was gone.

That is why the later ruling was not meaningless.

The federal government still had relationships with public broadcasters.

The court could protect those relationships from an unconstitutional condition.

It simply could not restore the extinct corporation.

The order also forced defenders to confront a rhetorical problem that had grown for decades.

If they said public broadcasting was independent of government, critics asked why government money was necessary.

If they emphasized dependence on federal funding, critics asked how the journalism could truly be independent.

The architecture had an answer: independence comes from rules separating the funder from editorial control, not from the absence of public money.

That answer is familiar in other settings. A scientist can receive a National Science Foundation grant without the president choosing the result. A university can receive federal research money without becoming a federal university. A defense contractor can receive public money without every employee becoming a civil servant. A public defender is paid by government to oppose government prosecutors.

Funding and control can be separated.

They can never be made completely unrelated.

The anti-subsidy argument exploited the residue.

If Congress can stop the money, then journalism ultimately depends on political tolerance. That dependence may be attenuated by CPB, grant formulas, advance appropriations, and a decentralized station system. It remains dependence.

The May order attempted to shorten the distance.

The broadcasters’ legal response attempted to restore it.

In the meantime, the administration continued working through channels that did not require the court to accept its theory of CPB control.

One was regulatory.

The Federal Communications Commission had opened an inquiry into whether underwriting announcements on NPR and PBS member stations crossed the legal line into prohibited commercial advertising. FCC Chair Brendan Carr wrote to NPR and PBS leadership in January and again publicly in April about the investigation. The inquiry was not itself proof of retaliation. Public broadcasters are subject to communications law, and underwriting rules are real rules. Enforcement questions do not become illegitimate merely because the administration criticizing the institution also pursues them.

The context nevertheless mattered.

A system under attack from the White House was simultaneously defending its funding, its board structure, and its compliance practices before an agency whose chair had openly questioned aspects of its model.

Institutional pressure can be cumulative even when each component has an independent legal rationale.

That is one reason intent is difficult to discuss responsibly. A critic sees coordinated punishment. An administration sees multiple legitimate exercises of authority. A court may examine one action under one cause of action and leave the broader political pattern to historians.

This book has a different task.

It has to describe the pattern without pretending that pattern resolves every legal question.

The May order was not the law that rescinded CPB’s appropriation.

The FCC investigation was not the rescission.

The attempted board removals were not the executive order.

The House hearing was not any of these.

Together they formed the environment in which a long-standing conservative objective moved from aspiration to implementation.

The sequence accelerated in June.

Under the Impoundment Control Act of 1974, a president can propose that Congress rescind previously enacted budget authority. The statute was itself a product of conflict with Richard Nixon, who had tried to withhold money Congress had directed the executive to spend. Congress responded by restricting presidential impoundment while preserving a process through which the president could ask lawmakers to cancel funds.

The irony was historical and structural.

A law written after Nixon-era battles over presidential spending power would become the procedural vehicle by which another president asked Congress to cancel public-broadcasting funds.

The distinction was crucial.

The president could propose.

Congress had to agree.

On June 3, Trump transmitted a rescission package that included CPB money. The Government Accountability Office later summarized the request. Among the targeted budget authority was the advance appropriation that would have supported CPB in fiscal year 2026, and the broader package ultimately encompassed roughly $1.1 billion of public-broadcasting funding across two fiscal years.

Now the administration no longer needed a court to accept the proposition that CPB was subject to direct presidential command.

It needed votes.

The shift from executive order to rescission request was a shift from contested unilateral power to Article I power.

Congress owns the purse.

This was the route public broadcasting had survived before. Its defenders knew how to count votes. Rural Republicans had stations. Senators from large states had viewers. Local broadcasters had relationships with representatives who might dislike NPR’s national politics but value a community licensee. Advance funding had historically encouraged a bipartisan coalition.

The old defense was still available.

But the order had changed the frame in which the vote would occur.

The president was not asking Republicans to make an isolated judgment about a communications grant.

He was asking them to support an administration priority already defined as ending taxpayer support for biased media.

A vote for CPB could therefore become a vote against the president’s stated objective.

Party alignment converts policy details into loyalty tests.

The more a president makes a small program symbolically important, the harder it becomes for legislators of the president’s party to treat the program as locally useful trivia.

That was the new danger for rural public-media supporters.

A member could believe a station back home did valuable work and still decide that breaking with the president over a relatively small federal grant was politically irrational. The local benefit was concentrated but modest. The national signal of defection could be immediate.

The rescission procedure added urgency.

Unlike ordinary appropriations, where delay and procedural friction can preserve a status quo, a rescission request arrives with statutory timing rules. Congress does not have infinite time to decide. The package becomes a discrete object. Amendments can be offered. Bargains can be made. Leadership can count.

Public broadcasting was no longer defending an abstract future budget.

It was defending named money on a clock.

This is the point at which the May order should be understood less as an unsuccessful attempt to do everything and more as one stage in a campaign whose decisive authority would come from Congress.

The order defined the target.

The rescission request defined the amount.

The legislature would decide whether the architecture kept its fuel.

The First Amendment case could continue in parallel.

So could the board litigation.

In another political configuration, those lawsuits might have bought time until the funding coalition reassembled. A preliminary injunction might have preserved enough stability for Congress to reject the cuts. A board decision might have clarified CPB’s independence. Public pressure might have separated local stations from national brands in lawmakers’ minds.

But law does not operate outside politics.

It operates while politics is moving.

By July, the relevant question was no longer whether the president could personally order CPB to stop funding NPR and PBS.

It was whether Congress would take the money away from CPB altogether.

The administration had moved from the contested edge of presidential authority to the center of legislative power.

The executive order would eventually lose.

The strategy would not.
