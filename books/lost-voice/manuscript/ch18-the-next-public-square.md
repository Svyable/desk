# The Next Public Square

A country can end a funding model without ending the need the model was built to address.

That is where this story ends.

Not with a judgment that public broadcasting should have remained exactly as it was.

Not with a judgment that the market will inevitably replace everything worth keeping.

With a harder question.

What does a democratic society want from media that is neither state-controlled nor governed entirely by commercial return?

The answer in 1967 was public broadcasting.

The answer in 2026 is less settled.

The technological world that produced CPB is gone. Spectrum scarcity no longer defines access to information. A person with a phone can publish globally. A teenager can learn calculus from a free video library, watch a city-council meeting online, listen to a history podcast, stream a symphony, follow a war through satellite imagery, and receive an emergency alert without touching a radio dial.

No serious defense of public media can pretend this abundance is irrelevant.

The market did solve problems the architects of the Public Broadcasting Act expected public institutions to solve.

It solved them spectacularly.

It also created new failures.

Abundance made attention scarce.

Distribution became cheap while trust became expensive.

National content exploded while local reporting collapsed in many communities.

Commercial media found profitable ways to serve enormous audiences and weak incentives to cover a school board with forty viewers.

Platforms made publication universal and discovery dependent on algorithms built for engagement.

News became easier to produce and harder to sustain as a local institution.

The public-media question therefore survived its medium.

That is why the next public square may not look like public broadcasting.

It may be a network of nonprofit newsrooms, public-radio stations, university media centers, library systems, digital publishers, statehouse reporting cooperatives, documentary producers, emergency-data services, community archives, and educational platforms that share infrastructure without sharing one federal funding source.

Or the country may eventually decide that some national financing mechanism remains useful and build one differently.

The 2025 dismantling creates a rare opportunity to ask the design question from first principles.

What problem are we actually trying to solve?

If the answer is rural communication, fund rural communication.

If the answer is local journalism, design policy around local journalism rather than broadcast licenses alone.

If the answer is children’s educational media, measure access and quality across television, streaming, schools, and libraries.

If the answer is emergency resilience, support redundant communication infrastructure explicitly.

If the answer is cultural preservation, fund archives and production directly.

If the answer is independent journalism, confront the unavoidable problem of how government can finance speech without governing it.

The old system bundled these purposes together.

Bundling made administration easier and political defense harder.

A future system could unbundle them.

That would create clarity and new transaction costs.

The history of CPB suggests several principles any successor would need to respect.

The first is that funding independence cannot rest on goodwill alone.

Presidents will criticize media. Newsrooms will make mistakes. Political coalitions will change. A system that depends on everyone agreeing not to use money as leverage will eventually encounter someone willing to use the leverage.

If public funds support journalism, the rules separating funding from editorial decisions need to be explicit, durable, and judicially enforceable.

The March 2026 ruling supplies part of that protection. Agencies cannot turn the president’s dislike of journalism into a government-wide funding penalty for named speakers.

A future funding mechanism would still need structure.

The second principle is that structure cannot make public money apolitical.

Congress controls appropriations.

Even an endowment or trust begins with a political decision. Board appointments have politics. Eligibility rules have politics. Definitions of public service have politics. The goal is not to create a sphere untouched by democracy.

The goal is to keep political accountability at the level of rules rather than stories.

Congress should be able to decide what a program is for, how much it costs, who qualifies, and whether it should continue.

It should be much harder for an official to decide that one newsroom receives less because yesterday’s story was unwelcome.

That boundary is the essence of editorial independence.

The third principle is that public legitimacy matters as much as formal independence.

A firewall can prevent direct control.

It cannot force citizens to trust the institution behind it.

Public media’s loss of conservative trust was not a minor public-relations problem. For an institution partly financed through Congress, broad legitimacy was part of the funding model whether leaders liked it or not.

That does not mean journalism should flatter every political constituency.

It means institutions claiming to serve the whole public need to examine whether their culture, hiring, language, assumptions, and story selection make parts of the public feel they are being observed from outside rather than represented fairly.

The correct response to this problem is not partisan quota journalism.

A newsroom does not become trustworthy by hiring one Republican to balance one Democrat or by treating every political claim as equally valid.

The deeper objective is epistemic diversity: people who notice different things, ask different questions, know different communities, challenge shared assumptions, and recognize when professional consensus has become social homogeneity.

Public media had this problem before 2025.

The funding fight made the cost impossible to ignore.

The fourth principle is that local inequality has to be treated as a design variable rather than a moral afterthought.

Private funding follows wealth.

That is not an accusation. It is arithmetic.

A station in an affluent metropolitan area has more potential members, more major donors, more foundations, more underwriting prospects, and more institutional partners than a station serving a sparse or poor region.

If a future system relies entirely on voluntary support, services will tend to reflect that geography unless some redistributive mechanism counteracts it.

Maybe states will provide the mechanism.

Maybe national philanthropy will.

Maybe universities and tribes will.

Maybe a future Congress will.

Maybe technology will reduce fixed costs enough that the inequality matters less.

The point is to model it rather than assume it away.

The fifth principle is that infrastructure deserves a constituency even when it lacks an audience.

Programs can raise money.

Pipes cannot.

The transition from CPB showed how shared distribution, music rights, archives, and technical systems can become vulnerable when funding is organized around visible content. A future public-media field needs institutions capable of financing common goods that no single member wants to pay for alone.

This is the same problem open-source software, scientific research, roads, standards bodies, and public-health systems face.

Everyone benefits from maintenance.

Maintenance has terrible marketing.

A sixth principle is that independence from government is not independence from power.

Private donors have power.

Foundations have power.

Platforms have power.

Universities have power.

Corporate underwriters have power.

Audiences have power.

A post-federal public-media system should not romanticize private funding as neutral simply because the First Amendment problems are different.

The advantage of plural funding is that no single source has to dominate.

The best surviving stations may become more diversified, not less: membership, philanthropy, underwriting, university or state support where appropriate, events, services, investment income, and digital revenue.

The goal is not purity.

It is resilience against any one funder becoming a command channel.

The seventh principle is that sunset and review should be built into institutions that want legitimacy across generations.

One reason the fight over CPB became existential is that supporters often defended continuity while critics attacked permanence. A system created in 1967 had accumulated so much institutional history that questioning the structure could sound like questioning the mission itself.

A future model could separate the two.

Require periodic independent review.

Measure local service.

Measure ideological and geographic reach without trying to regulate opinion.

Measure how much money goes to infrastructure, local journalism, education, administration, and national programming.

Publish clear evidence about who benefits.

Allow Congress to renew the structure on a deliberate timetable rather than forcing every argument into an abolition threat.

This would not eliminate politics.

It could make politics more informative.

The eighth principle is that government should not confuse funding neutrality with content neutrality.

Journalism cannot be neutral in the sense of avoiding every conclusion. Facts produce conclusions. Investigations expose wrongdoing. Science reporting sometimes describes one claim as false. Courts rule. Elections have winners. History contains perpetrators and victims.

A requirement that publicly supported journalism never offend a political constituency would destroy journalism faster than defunding it.

The proper standard is procedural: accuracy, transparency, correction, evidence, independence, and good-faith efforts to represent relevant perspectives fairly.

The government should not enforce these standards story by story.

Institutions should.

That means governance matters.

Public media’s future may depend as much on boards and editorial systems as on money.

The ninth principle is that digital transition should not become an excuse to abandon universal access.

Streaming is cheaper in some ways and more capable in others. It also shifts costs to households through broadband and devices. Over-the-air broadcasting remains unusually accessible because the receiver does not need a subscription, identity, account, or monthly data plan.

The future may be digital-first.

It should not casually become broadband-only where broadband remains unequal.

WEIU’s transition from over-the-air television to digital distribution illustrates both the opportunity and the tradeoff. The organization could continue producing video after losing a funding model that could no longer sustain broadcast television. The mission survived. One form of universal access did not.

A national policy interested in public media should care about both.

The tenth principle is the simplest.

Do not build a system whose survival depends on nobody ever using the legal powers available to them.

The old architecture had many safeguards.

It survived Nixon.

It survived Reagan.

It survived Gingrich.

It survived Trump’s first term.

Its supporters understandably came to see that record as proof of durability.

But the design still allowed Congress to rescind future appropriations.

Once a president who wanted elimination possessed a congressional majority willing to act, the architecture had no deeper financial protection.

The system was robust against conflict between branches.

It was fragile against alignment.

That is the institutional lesson beyond public broadcasting.

American governance often relies on veto points.

The president cannot do everything alone.

The House can resist.

The Senate can resist.

Courts can intervene.

Independent boards can slow control.

Advance appropriations can buy time.

These protections work best when political power is divided.

When one coalition controls several veto points and agrees on a structural objective, institutions protected mainly by fragmentation can become vulnerable very quickly.

The correct response is not to make every institution impossible for democratic majorities to change.

That would replace democracy with entrenchment.

The correct response is to be honest about which protections are rights, which are statutes, which are norms, and which are simply expectations that the other side will not use all of its available power at once.

CPB’s history had all four.

The First Amendment supplied rights.

The Public Broadcasting Act supplied statutes.

Bipartisan deference supplied norms.

Advance appropriations supplied expectations of time.

In 2025, the rights survived, the statute’s operating institution did not, the norm collapsed, and the time was shortened through rescission.

That is the anatomy of the lost voice.

The title does not mean Americans stopped hearing public media.

It means the old federal voice in the financing arrangement—the national promise that noncommercial local broadcasting was a public good worth underwriting through an independent buffer—fell silent.

Other voices grew louder.

Listeners wrote checks.

Foundations offered support.

States considered new policies.

Universities made choices.

Stations merged, cut, streamed, and improvised.

NPR and PBS redesigned their economics.

Courts protected constitutional boundaries.

The field became more plural and more unequal at the same time.

That is a plausible future for many American institutions.

Federal withdrawal does not always produce absence.

It produces devolution.

Devolution can create experimentation.

It can also make public capacity depend more heavily on local wealth and political geography.

The post-CPB years will reveal which force dominates.

There is one final reason public broadcasting matters beyond its modest budget.

The fight was a test of whether the country can support institutions that are public without being governmental.

That category is precious and unstable.

Civil society sits there.

Universities sit partly there.

Nonprofit hospitals, museums, libraries, research institutes, standards organizations, legal services, cultural groups, foundations, and public-interest newsrooms live in the space between market and state.

They often depend on public policy without belonging to government.

They can perform functions markets undersupply without becoming bureaucracies.

Their legitimacy rests on a bargain: some public support, some private autonomy, enough accountability to justify the support, enough independence to prevent political capture.

The bargain is never permanently settled.

Public broadcasting became one of the clearest places to watch it fail.

The failure may be instructive enough to improve the next bargain.

That would require both sides of the 2025 argument to learn something they would rather not learn.

Defenders would have to admit that institutional independence without broad legitimacy is fragile, that old media structures should not be preserved merely because they are old, and that public-service organizations can become culturally narrow while sincerely believing themselves universal.

Critics would have to admit that removing public funding does not remove power from media; it reallocates power toward donors, markets, states, universities, and platforms, and that local public goods can disappear even when national content remains abundant.

Supporters of executive power would have to accept the court’s boundary: political leaders cannot turn government-wide funding discretion into retaliation for disfavored journalism.

Supporters of public funding would have to accept Congress’s boundary: a subsidy created by statute remains subject to democratic reconsideration.

Those concessions are not compromises in the sense of splitting the difference.

They are facts.

A durable next system would have to be built around them.

Perhaps no federal replacement will ever emerge.

Public media may continue evolving into a largely privately supported federation with selective state and institutional funding. If that model succeeds, the 2025 rescission may eventually be remembered as a forced modernization that separated public-service journalism from a politically toxic subsidy.

Perhaps the losses will accumulate unevenly enough that a later Congress rebuilds national support under a different name. If so, the new architecture may look less like broadcasting policy and more like civic information infrastructure.

Perhaps both happen: private success in wealthy markets, public intervention in high-cost or underserved ones.

That hybrid outcome would fit the rest of American life.

The country rarely chooses market or government once and for all.

It layers them.

For now, the most important consequence of 2025 is that the old layer is gone.

The people who said it could never happen were wrong.

The people who said public media would disappear with it were wrong too.

The more interesting reality is still unfolding.

An institution can lose its federal center and continue speaking.

A court can defend the speech after Congress ends the subsidy.

A station can survive by becoming something different.

A community can discover what it values only when the bill arrives directly.

A national system can become a collection of local experiments.

And a political victory can prove less final than the legislation that produced it, because the needs underneath institutions outlive the institutions themselves.

The next public square is already being built.

It is being built by people who did not choose the conditions.

That is usually how the future starts.
