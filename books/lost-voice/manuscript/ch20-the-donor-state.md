# The Donor State

When public money disappears from a nonprofit institution, private money does not arrive as an ideology.

It arrives as a person.

A member increases a monthly gift.

A foundation officer calls.

A university president offers temporary support.

A corporation renews underwriting.

A wealthy donor creates a challenge grant.

A listener leaves a station money in a will.

The replacement can feel more voluntary, more local, and more democratic than a federal appropriation because every dollar carries an affirmative choice.

It can also concentrate power in ways a public appropriation does not.

The post-CPB system is therefore not a simple movement from state funding to independence.

It is a movement from one set of funders to another.

That change is the subject of this chapter.

The strongest argument for donor replacement begins with consent.

A taxpayer cannot opt out of CPB funding because he dislikes NPR. A donor can.

A viewer who values PBS can contribute. A listener who never uses public radio can keep the money. A community that values a station enough can support it directly. This model aligns payment with preference and reduces the grievance that government is forcing citizens to subsidize speech they reject.

The 2025 rescission created the clearest test of this argument in public broadcasting’s history.

Audiences responded.

Station after station reported emergency giving. The threat was concrete enough to change donor behavior. Public-media organizations that had spent decades asking members to “support the programs you love” could now ask a sharper question: what are these programs worth if the federal floor is gone?

For many listeners and viewers, the answer was more than they had been giving.

That result matters.

Institutions sometimes underestimate their private support because a subsidy dampens urgency. People who assume an organization will survive without them can rationally free-ride on other donors. A credible threat of loss changes the calculation.

Public radio had always depended on voluntary payment for a non-excludable service. Anyone could listen without donating. Enough people gave anyway. The post-CPB emergency simply increased the price signal.

This is one reason the early resilience of public media should not be treated as a miracle.

The system had spent half a century building donor lists, membership software, pledge-drive expertise, major-gift programs, underwriting relationships, foundation connections, and brands associated with public service. It was unusually well prepared to convert political threat into private support.

A new nonprofit newsroom without that infrastructure would have been far more vulnerable.

The old federal system had helped build the private one.

That creates a strange historical loop.

Public subsidy contributed to institutions strong enough to survive the loss of subsidy.

Critics can interpret this as evidence that the subsidy succeeded and became unnecessary.

Defenders can interpret it as evidence that public money created durable civic capacity worth preserving.

Both interpretations can be true depending on the station.

The donor model becomes more complicated when we ask who gives.

Public-media audiences are not a perfect cross-section of the country. Many stations, especially in large markets, draw heavily from educated, affluent listeners and viewers. Those people are more capable of replacing federal support than communities with lower incomes or smaller populations.

This produces a geographic and class problem.

Voluntary payment can efficiently reveal preference only among people with money to express the preference.

A poor community may value a local station intensely and still fail to replace a large grant.

A wealthy community may value a station modestly and replace the money easily.

Markets register willingness to pay, not abstract social value.

The old federal system had partially corrected for this by redistributing national money toward high-cost and small-market stations.

The donor state does not automatically do that.

It can, if philanthropy chooses to.

National foundations can create rural funds. Wealthy donors can support stations outside their communities. Collaborative funds can target news deserts. Donor-advised funds can move money across geography. A philanthropic system can be more flexible than congressional formulas and less vulnerable to partisan appropriations fights.

It can also be episodic.

Foundations have strategies.

Strategies change.

One year local journalism is a priority. Another year climate, health, artificial intelligence, democracy, education, or a new crisis receives attention. A station can lose a federal grant because Congress votes once. It can lose a foundation grant because a program officer changes the portfolio.

Neither source is guaranteed.

The difference is accountability.

A member of Congress can be voted out.

A foundation board cannot.

A private donor has the right to spend money according to private values. That is part of civil society’s strength. It is also why replacing public money with philanthropic money changes the democratic character of the institution.

Public funding distributes influence through elections and law.

Philanthropic funding distributes influence through wealth and donor intent.

This does not make public funding superior.

It makes the tradeoff visible.

Consider editorial independence.

A federal appropriation routed through CPB created an obvious political risk: elected officials might use funding to pressure journalism. The old architecture attempted to reduce that risk through statutory distance, advance funding, local governance, and professional norms.

A foundation grant creates a different risk. The foundation can specify a topic, population, geography, or method. It may fund climate reporting, health reporting, statehouse reporting, arts coverage, or investigative work. The newsroom accepts because the work aligns with its mission.

No one needs to call an editor and demand a story.

Funding priorities shape what capacity exists before story selection begins.

The effect can be subtle and entirely legitimate.

A donor who funds environmental reporting does not have to interfere in coverage to make environmental reporting more abundant.

A donor who funds rural reporting increases rural coverage.

A donor who funds criminal-justice reform creates capacity around one set of questions.

Money shapes attention even when it does not shape conclusions.

This is why nonprofit newsrooms often publish donor lists and conflict policies. Transparency is the substitute for electoral accountability.

Public broadcasting after CPB will need more of it.

If major gifts replace federal dollars, audiences should know who the major funders are, what restrictions accompany grants, how editorial firewalls work, and whether donors can support general operations rather than only preferred projects.

The legitimacy problem did not disappear with Washington.

It moved to the development office.

Membership money has different incentives.

Thousands of small donors can diversify power. No single member controls much. Recurring gifts create predictable revenue. A station accountable to many listeners may be more responsive to its community than a station accountable to a federal formula.

This is the strongest democratic case for the donor model.

The people who use the institution support it.

The model also contains a feedback loop that can narrow audiences.

Membership fundraising rewards intensity.

People give when they love a service or fear losing it. Political threat can increase both. A station whose strongest donors are liberal may discover that framing itself as a defender against conservative attack raises money. A conservative nonprofit media outlet learns the mirror image from its audience.

Fundraising can therefore reward polarization even when journalists prefer moderation.

The federal appropriation had created a reason to maintain broad political legitimacy beyond the donor base.

Without it, a station may rationally optimize for the constituency that pays.

This does not mean public media will become more partisan after defunding.

It means one institutional incentive for broad coalition maintenance has weakened while another incentive for donor engagement has strengthened.

The effect should be measured rather than assumed.

Underwriting adds another layer.

Corporate sponsors support public media because audiences are attractive and association with the institution has reputational value. Underwriting is not identical to commercial advertising under broadcasting rules, but it still connects content institutions to corporate budgets.

Sponsorship can diversify revenue away from government and wealthy donors.

It can also fluctuate with the economy.

NPR’s financial difficulties in 2023 were driven partly by a sponsorship downturn before the federal rescission. That history matters because it shows private revenue is not a stable replacement simply because it is private.

A recession can reduce underwriting at the same moment membership falls.

A controversial story can make a sponsor reconsider.

A technology platform can change the ad market.

Private diversification reduces political risk and introduces market risk.

Universities create a different form of donor state.

A university licensee can support a station through central funds, facilities, staff, or fundraising. This relationship can make the station resilient because the parent institution has scale. It can also make the station dependent on university leadership and state politics.

The 2026 Illinois press-protection law for professional public-media employees at state institutions reflected concern about exactly this vulnerability. If public media becomes more dependent on state universities after losing federal support, editorial independence may need protection at the university level.

Federalism does not eliminate political pressure.

It multiplies jurisdictions.

State funding can be more responsive to local needs than national funding. It can also vary dramatically by party control and fiscal capacity. A wealthy state can replace federal money. A poorer state may not. A governor can support public media one year and oppose it the next.

A nationally pooled system smooths these differences.

A state-based system expresses them.

The donor state therefore tends toward pluralism and inequality at the same time.

There will be more funding models.

There will be less uniformity.

Some stations may thrive through membership and philanthropy. Others may become university departments. Others may receive state support. Others may merge into regional networks. Others may leave NPR or PBS affiliations to reduce costs. Others may go digital-only. A few may close.

The system becomes an archipelago.

The question is whether islands can still share enough infrastructure to feel like a public-media system.

National philanthropy may become the new bridge.

Several foundations and donors had supported public media long before 2025. After the rescission, their role became more strategic. A foundation can fund technology that benefits many stations. A consortium can finance legal services, local journalism, shared data, or infrastructure. Philanthropy can move faster than Congress and experiment more freely.

This flexibility is real.

So is philanthropic fashion.

Public institutions designed around private grant cycles can become excellent at describing themselves in the language funders currently reward.

Innovation.

Equity.

Community engagement.

Civic information.

Resilience.

Democracy.

Technology.

The words can describe genuine work. They can also become a vocabulary of dependency, just as congressional institutions learn the vocabulary of appropriations.

Every funder creates a dialect.

The question is whether the newsroom begins thinking in the dialect before deciding what the public actually needs.

This is not a uniquely philanthropic danger. Government programs create their own bureaucratic vocabularies. Corporate markets create metrics. Membership creates audience categories. Algorithms create engagement incentives.

No funding system permits pure mission.

Institutions choose among distortions.

The old CPB model had one large advantage in this respect: flexible station grants could support general operations.

General operating support is boring and valuable.

A station can use it for electricity, insurance, engineering, salaries, rent, technology, and the unglamorous functions required to exist. Foundations often prefer projects because projects are easier to explain and evaluate. Donors prefer programs they can hear or see. Capital campaigns prefer buildings. General operations remain the thing everyone depends on and nobody wants named after them.

Federal grants helped pay the boring bill.

Replacing them with philanthropy may therefore change not only how much money stations receive but what the money can be used for.

A station with the same total revenue can become less flexible if more of the revenue is restricted.

This is another reason gross replacement numbers can mislead.

One million dollars of unrestricted recurring membership revenue is not equivalent to one million dollars of one-year restricted foundation grants.

Both appear as revenue.

Only one can reliably pay next year’s engineer.

The post-CPB research agenda should therefore track revenue quality, not only revenue quantity.

What share is recurring?

What share is unrestricted?

How concentrated are major donors?

How much comes from state or university support?

How much depends on underwriting?

How large are reserves?

How much is one-time emergency giving?

How much is tied to specific projects?

These variables will determine which stations survive the second and third year after rescission.

The donor state also changes who public media imagines as its public.

Under the federal model, every taxpayer could plausibly claim standing to complain, even if the claim was politically annoying. The institution was partly theirs through national policy.

Under the donor model, the moral constituency can become the people who contribute.

That shift may improve responsiveness.

It may also make non-donors easier to ignore.

A rural low-income listener who never gives still receives the broadcast. A station optimizing for members may prioritize the preferences of people capable of giving. Public service requires resisting that temptation because the whole point of noncommercial media is partly to serve people who are not attractive customers.

The federal grant had subsidized that resistance.

Without it, mission has to defeat math directly.

Some stations will succeed because their donors understand the mission.

Others will rationally follow the money.

This is not corruption.

It is organizational survival.

The most promising version of the post-federal model therefore looks less like replacing one big check with one rich donor and more like radical diversification.

Thousands of members.

Several foundations.

Underwriters.

Earned revenue where appropriate.

University or state support with editorial protections.

Investment income.

Events.

Digital products.

Major gifts capped in influence by transparent governance.

Shared national services financed cooperatively.

No single funder large enough to become a veto.

This is harder to manage than a federal floor.

It may produce stronger institutions because the revenue base becomes more resilient to one political decision.

That is the best case for the donor state.

The worst case is oligarchic local media.

A small number of wealthy donors decide which institutions survive. Foundations define which topics receive reporting capacity. Rich cities sustain strong public media while poor regions lose service. Stations become more ideologically aligned with the people most willing to give. General operations become chronically underfunded. Emergency campaigns become normal budgeting.

Nothing in the end of CPB guarantees either outcome.

That is why the first-year donor surge should be treated as a beginning, not a verdict.

The federal system was criticized for making public media dependent on politicians.

The replacement system will be judged by whether it can avoid becoming dependent on patrons.

The constitutional risks are different.

The democratic risks remain.

A healthy public square needs money from somewhere.

The question is always what the money is allowed to buy besides the work itself.
