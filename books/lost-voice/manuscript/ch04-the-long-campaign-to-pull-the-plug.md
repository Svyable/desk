# The Long Campaign to Pull the Plug

The campaign to end federal support for public broadcasting was older than many of the people who voted to do it.

That matters because institutions are rarely dismantled by arguments invented the week before the vote. They are dismantled when old arguments acquire new power.

Public broadcasting had attracted political suspicion almost from the moment it acquired a national structure. The suspicion came in several forms that later generations tended to collapse into one complaint about bias.

One was constitutional and cultural: government should not subsidize speech.

Another was economic: broadcasting belonged in the market, and noncommercial institutions should support themselves through philanthropy, universities, foundations, memberships, or other private sources.

Another was ideological: public broadcasters leaned left in their personnel, assumptions, guest selection, story framing, and cultural sensibility, then used public money to amplify that tilt.

Another was institutional: a recipient of federal appropriations that claimed editorial independence could enjoy public support without meaningful public accountability.

Another was technological: whatever scarcity might have justified public broadcasting in 1967 had been erased by cable, satellite, the internet, streaming, podcasts, and social media.

These arguments did not always travel together. A libertarian could oppose the subsidy while enjoying NPR. A conservative could defend a rural public station while loathing national coverage. A liberal could criticize public broadcasting for elite cultural assumptions while supporting the appropriation. A fiscal hawk could see a tiny program that was not worth a political fight. A culture warrior could see the same tiny program as symbolically intolerable.

The coalition against public broadcasting became powerful when the symbolic argument outran the budgetary one.

The Corporation for Public Broadcasting was never a serious driver of federal deficits. Its appropriation could disappear entirely without changing the long-run fiscal problem facing the United States. Anyone claiming otherwise was confusing a budget with a morality play.

That smallness did not protect it.

In some ways it made elimination easier to imagine. A program too small to matter fiscally can still matter ideologically, and cutting it carries fewer direct nationwide costs than changing Social Security, Medicare, defense, or the tax code. The political reward can therefore be larger than the budget reward.

Richard Nixon understood the symbolic power early.

His administration’s conflict with public television emerged while the system was still being assembled. Nixon and his aides objected to public-affairs programming they believed reflected liberal establishment attitudes and worried that a federally supported national system could become politically hostile. The fight culminated in a 1972 veto of legislation that would have provided longer-term funding.

The details of the Nixon era resist easy translation into the politics of 2025. Public television was younger. The institutions were still changing. The relationships among CPB, stations, producers, and the emerging national distribution organizations were less settled. Yet the core problem was already visible: an administration could insist that it was defending neutrality while public broadcasters saw the funding threat as an attempt to influence programming.

That ambiguity never left.

Every government-funding dispute over journalism contains two questions that sound similar in campaign language and behave differently under law.

Should the government fund this institution?

Should the government punish this institution because of what it says?

A person can answer no to the first and no to the second.

Politics often tries to merge them.

Nixon’s pressure helped teach public broadcasters and Congress why funding structure mattered. The eventual use of advance appropriations was partly an answer to the danger of immediate political leverage. Put time between the controversial program and the next available funding decision. Make retaliation slower. Turn annual anger into a legislative process rather than a phone call.

The solution worked well enough to become invisible.

Then Ronald Reagan arrived with a broader project.

Reagan’s challenge to public broadcasting was nested inside an effort to cut domestic spending and redefine the federal role. The argument was not only that public media tilted politically. It was that a country with private broadcasters and charitable institutions should not assume Washington must finance a noncommercial alternative.

The administration proposed deep reductions in public-broadcasting support early in the first term. Once again, the system mobilized. Once again, Congress altered the outcome. Federal support continued.

Survival produced adaptation. Public stations expanded fundraising, underwriting, and other sources of income. The mixed model became more sophisticated. The federal share became one part of a broader revenue stack rather than the dominant national source.

This looked like independence.

It also created the argument that would later be used against the subsidy.

If public broadcasting could raise so much money privately, why did it need Washington?

The answer from stations was that federal support created the stable base that helped leverage the rest. The answer from critics was that successful leveraging showed the market and civil society could provide the money.

The dispute was not resolvable by arithmetic alone because the counterfactual was unknowable. One could observe the system with federal support. One could later observe it after support disappeared. One could not run both histories at the same time.

The 1990s pushed the conflict into a more openly partisan era.

When Republicans captured the House in the 1994 elections, Speaker Newt Gingrich made ending federal support for CPB part of the new majority’s agenda. His language was blunt. The public-broadcasting appropriation became a test of whether the new Congress would dismantle parts of the old liberal policy order.

The effort failed to eliminate CPB.

It succeeded in changing the political meaning of the issue.

From then on, “defund public broadcasting” could function as a recognizable conservative commitment even when it did not produce enacted law. The issue joined a category of proposals whose repetition mattered. Each attempt built a record. Each hearing supplied examples. Each controversy became precedent for the next accusation. Each survival also taught defenders a script.

Public broadcasting learned how to survive the ritual.

Explain that the appropriation is small.

Explain that most of it goes to local stations.

Show rural dependence.

Invoke educational programming.

Remind lawmakers that stations have constituents.

Mobilize listeners and viewers.

Separate the national newsroom under criticism from the local grant at risk.

Wait for the coalition to fracture.

For years, it worked.

The ritual became so familiar that the failure mode was difficult to recognize when it arrived.

The early 2010s provided another rehearsal. In 2011, a hidden-camera controversy involving an NPR fundraising executive intensified Republican criticism. The executive was recorded making disparaging comments about Tea Party supporters during a conversation with people posing as potential donors. NPR condemned the comments and the executive departed. Around the same period, NPR had fired commentator Juan Williams after remarks about Muslims, provoking a separate backlash over ideological tolerance.

The controversies mattered beyond their immediate facts because they supplied a narrative.

To critics, public broadcasting was not merely staffed by people who happened to lean left. It was an institution whose internal culture could become contemptuous of the citizens helping to fund it while punishing dissent within its own ranks. Federal support transformed newsroom culture from a private personnel issue into a taxpayer grievance.

The House voted that year to prohibit federal funding for NPR, though the measure did not become the final abolition of the broader CPB system.

Again, the institution survived.

Again, the argument accumulated.

The important feature of accumulated grievance is that later participants need not remember every episode accurately for the narrative to retain power. The details dissolve. The conclusion remains.

They are biased.

We have tried before.

They always protect themselves.

Why are taxpayers paying for this?

By the time Donald Trump entered presidential politics, that conclusion fit naturally into his broader relationship with the press.

Trump did not treat media criticism as an occasional response to coverage. Conflict with journalists was a central part of his political identity. He named outlets, attacked reporters, described coverage as dishonest, and repeatedly presented major news organizations as political actors rather than neutral observers.

Public broadcasting occupied a particularly vulnerable place in that worldview because it combined journalism with a traceable federal appropriation.

A president cannot normally defund a private newspaper because he dislikes its coverage.

He can propose eliminating a federal subsidy.

Trump’s first-term budgets repeatedly sought to end CPB funding. Congress declined. The pattern resembled previous conservative administrations: White House proposal, congressional resistance, institutional survival.

A person looking only at enacted appropriations could conclude that the threat had again been normalized.

That conclusion missed a change in the Republican Party.

Over time, hostility toward mainstream news organizations became less a factional attitude and more an organizing feature of conservative politics. Republican voters’ trust in major media institutions fell. Alternative conservative media grew larger and more sophisticated. Podcasting, social platforms, cable commentary, independent newsletters, and partisan digital outlets weakened the old assumption that national journalism operated from a small set of common institutions.

Public broadcasting’s claims of neutrality entered a market in which neutrality itself was contested.

This was not simply a conservative phenomenon. Across the political spectrum, audiences increasingly selected news sources that reflected social identity, ideology, lifestyle, and community. Trust fragmented. News became easier to produce and harder to authenticate collectively. Institutions that once possessed authority because they were institutions had to justify it anew.

NPR and PBS did not escape that change.

Their audiences remained broad by the standards of many media organizations, but their reputations became politically asymmetric. Surveys repeatedly found stark partisan differences in trust toward news outlets. Conservative critics saw public broadcasters as cultural institutions of educated urban liberalism. Public-media employees often lived and worked in professional environments where the distribution of political belief differed from the country as a whole. Debates over language, race, gender, immigration, policing, climate, and Trump himself became tests of whether editorial norms were reporting reality or encoding ideology.

The accusation of bias was not automatically proved by partisan differences in trust.

Neither could it be dismissed because the accusers were partisan.

A newsroom can be attacked unfairly and still have blind spots.

A critic can identify a real blind spot and still use it as a pretext for political retaliation.

These possibilities are uncomfortable because political argument prefers exclusive explanations.

The 2025 dismantling becomes unintelligible without both.

In the years before Trump returned to office, NPR endured an internal and external debate over ideological diversity. One of the most damaging critiques came not from a Republican officeholder but from Uri Berliner, a longtime NPR editor who published an essay in 2024 arguing that the organization had lost viewpoint diversity and public trust. Berliner pointed to story selection, language, and a newsroom culture he believed had become too homogeneous.

NPR disputed important parts of his characterization and emphasized its commitment to serving a broad audience. The controversy nevertheless gave critics something unusually potent: criticism from inside the institution by someone with decades of experience there.

For opponents of federal funding, the essay was not a reason to reform NPR.

It was evidence for a verdict already reached.

This difference in remedial imagination matters.

When supporters of public media heard criticism of ideological homogeneity, they tended to ask how hiring, standards, editorial process, local coverage, and audience engagement could improve. When the strongest opponents heard the same criticism, they asked why taxpayers were involved at all.

Reform assumes legitimacy.

Abolition withdraws it.

By 2025, a meaningful faction of the governing party had moved from one category to the other.

The March congressional hearing made the shift visible.

Officially titled around the claim of “anti-American airwaves,” the hearing summoned NPR chief executive Katherine Maher and PBS chief executive Paula Kerger before a House Oversight subcommittee associated with the Department of Government Efficiency agenda. The title announced the prosecution before testimony began.

Republican members focused on bias, editorial choices, transgender issues, Gaza, Hunter Biden, racial language, and public trust. The hearing also drew attention to NPR’s handling of controversies that conservatives believed had been distorted or ignored. The executives defended their organizations, described editorial standards, emphasized local service, and disputed the characterization that public media functioned as partisan propaganda.

The hearing did not cut a dollar.

It changed the atmosphere around the dollars.

Congressional hearings are often dismissed as theater because they rarely persuade the people performing in them. That underestimates theater’s political function. A hearing identifies protagonists, names a grievance, creates clips, forces institutions to answer under hostile framing, and converts diffuse dissatisfaction into a legislative object.

By the time the hearing ended, “NPR and PBS” had become easier to connect rhetorically to “federal funding” than the underlying CPB structure warranted.

That compression was useful.

The administration’s later executive order did the same thing more formally. It named NPR and PBS, described them as biased, and ordered the federal apparatus to stop supporting them directly or indirectly to the maximum extent consistent with law.

The phrase “consistent with law” was doing heavy work.

CPB’s whole architecture existed because the law did not make it a subordinate bureau of the president. The administration could announce a policy against funding named broadcasters. Whether it could compel CPB to implement that policy was another question.

The order nevertheless mattered even before the courts addressed it.

It said that the long conservative campaign had entered an executive phase.

A campaign to eliminate federal support no longer lived mainly in think-tank papers, budget proposals, symbolic votes, or speeches. The president was instructing agencies to act, challenging the institutional boundaries around CPB, and preparing to ask Congress to retrieve the future appropriation.

The difference between 1995 and 2025 was not that the critique had become more sophisticated.

Much of it was familiar.

The difference was coalition and procedure.

A Republican president held office. Republicans controlled the House. Republicans controlled the Senate. Party discipline was strong enough to move a rescission package through votes that left almost no margin for defection. The administration had a statutory route designed for presidents to propose taking back enacted spending. The target was small enough in the federal budget to avoid the constituency warfare of entitlement reform but culturally salient enough to produce political reward.

The old campaign finally possessed all the necessary pieces at once.

This is a recurring feature of policy change. Ideas can remain unsuccessful for decades not because they disappear but because the institutions required to enact them are misaligned. Then an election alters the alignment and what had looked like permanent background noise becomes a legislative program.

Political scientist John Kingdon famously described policy change through streams of problems, policies, and politics that sometimes join in a window of opportunity. The public-broadcasting fight does not need a formal model to show the pattern. The problem, as conservatives defined it, had been named for years: biased media supported by taxpayers. The policy was available: eliminate CPB funding. The politics finally aligned.

The surprise belonged mostly to people who had mistaken repeated failure for lack of intent.

The intent had been stated plainly.

The more difficult question is whether the public-media system recognized that its old defense had weakened.

Large institutions often respond to recurring attacks by professionalizing the response. They hire government-relations staff. They maintain fact sheets. They cultivate bipartisan supporters. They know which local stories persuade which members of Congress. They develop language about economic impact, rural service, emergency communication, and educational benefit.

Professionalization makes survival more likely under familiar conditions.

It can also make a new condition look familiar for too long.

The 2025 coalition did not merely seek a smaller appropriation.

It rejected the legitimacy of the funding arrangement.

That is a different threat.

If an opponent says your budget is too large, evidence of efficiency can help.

If an opponent says your institution should not receive federal money under any circumstances, efficiency is beside the point.

If the complaint is that your newsroom is biased, a list of rural transmitters can look evasive.

If the response is that most federal money goes to rural transmitters, the critic can say the national newsroom has hidden itself behind them.

The two sides were increasingly answering different questions.

Public media defended what the system did.

Its opponents attacked why the system was entitled to federal support at all.

That distinction explains the emotional asymmetry of the 2025 debate. Defenders treated rescission as the destruction of a useful public institution. Critics treated rescission as removal of an improper privilege.

A privilege does not deserve incremental reform.

It deserves withdrawal.

Once federal support was understood that way inside a governing majority, the long history of public broadcasting’s survival offered less protection than anyone wanted to admit.

Nixon had tested the buffer.

Reagan had tested the subsidy.

Gingrich had tested the coalition.

The 2011 controversies had tested legitimacy.

Trump’s first term had tested congressional resistance.

The second term combined the lessons.

None of the earlier attempts had been meaningless simply because it failed.

Each showed where the system was strong.

Each also showed the next opponent where to push.

By 2025, the federal architecture was still standing.

So was a fifty-year campaign to remove it.

Only one of them needed the political conditions to change.
