# The Emergency Alert Argument

The most effective defense of public broadcasting in July 2025 had almost nothing to do with journalism.

It had to do with what happens when the lights go out.

As the Senate worked through amendments to the rescission package, opponents of the public-broadcasting cuts repeatedly tried to protect functions connected to public safety, rural service, tribal communities, and emergency alerts. The motions failed. The votes were not close enough.

The debate exposed an old political problem hiding inside a modern media fight.

A system can be controversial because of what it says and indispensable because of what its infrastructure does.

Those are different functions living inside the same institution.

Broadcast towers are not op-ed pages.

Microwave links are not political analysis.

Satellite interconnection is not a position on immigration, climate, or Donald Trump.

Yet a funding architecture built around stations finances all of these things through the same organizational budgets. A congressional majority angry at national journalism can therefore reduce the money that maintains technical capacity with no editorial content at all.

The reverse is also true.

Defenders can invoke emergency capability to protect funding that supports national journalism their opponents dislike.

Each side can accuse the other of hiding the real issue behind the part of the system voters find easiest to defend or attack.

This is what bundling does to politics.

The emergency-alert argument deserves scrutiny precisely because it was emotionally powerful.

Public broadcasters have long participated in national, state, and local emergency communication. Radio and television stations are part of the Emergency Alert System. Public television has developed datacasting capabilities that can transmit digital information over broadcast spectrum. Local stations maintain relationships with weather services, emergency managers, schools, public-health agencies, and communities that need trusted channels when conditions become dangerous.

Broadcasting has technical properties that remain useful in disasters.

A transmitter sends one signal to everyone in range. It does not become overloaded because one million people tune in at once. A battery-powered radio can work when household internet is down. An antenna can receive television without a subscription or broadband connection. In remote regions, broadcast coverage may reach areas where cellular service is inconsistent.

These properties are not nostalgic.

They are engineering.

The existence of mobile alerts does not make broadcast redundancy meaningless. Modern emergency systems are layered because every channel can fail differently. Cellular networks depend on towers, backhaul, power, device batteries, carrier infrastructure, and network capacity. Internet services depend on power, local access, routing, and working devices. Broadcast systems have their own vulnerabilities but fail along different pathways.

Redundancy is valuable because systems do not coordinate their failures for our convenience.

The strongest version of the public-media defense therefore said: Congress is not merely cutting programs. It is weakening a layer of national emergency resilience, especially in rural and tribal areas where alternative communication systems may be thinner.

The strongest response from supporters of rescission was equally important.

If emergency communication is essential, fund emergency communication.

Do not use public safety as a moral shield for an entire media ecosystem.

The federal government already spends money through the Federal Emergency Management Agency, the Federal Communications Commission, the National Weather Service, the Department of Homeland Security, state emergency offices, broadband programs, public-safety grants, and other systems. Nothing about hurricane warnings logically requires Congress to subsidize NPR programming or PBS national content.

That argument cuts through a lot of advocacy.

An institution should not be immune from reform because one of its functions is valuable.

Hospitals perform emergency care and still face scrutiny over pricing. Universities conduct scientific research and still face questions about administrative cost. Defense contractors make essential equipment and still face procurement review. A station that participates in emergency alerting can be asked to justify why the same federal architecture must also support every other function attached to the licensee.

The question is whether functions can actually be separated at lower total cost.

That is an institutional-design problem, not a slogan.

Imagine a small public radio station serving a mountainous region.

The station has a studio, a transmitter, backup power, an engineer, a local host, national programming contracts, insurance, music rights, tower rent, internet service, membership staff, and perhaps two reporters. During normal weeks, these components produce radio. During a wildfire, the same transmitter and engineer become emergency infrastructure. The local host may become an information conduit. The reporters verify closures and evacuation orders. The backup generator matters more than the playlist.

Now suppose Congress decides it wants to preserve only the emergency function.

It could create a grant for backup power.

It could subsidize tower maintenance.

It could pay for alert equipment.

It could finance datacasting.

These are all possible.

What it cannot cheaply do is fund the station only during the emergency.

The capability exists because the organization exists between emergencies.

The engineer has a job because equipment needs maintenance every week.

The audience knows the frequency because it listens before the fire.

The station has relationships with local officials because it reports on the community during ordinary times.

Preparedness is a byproduct of continuity.

This is why public-safety arguments often rely on institutional bundles. The emergency function is cheaper when supported by an organization doing other things every day.

Bundling can be efficient even when the bundle creates political cross-subsidy.

Commercial broadcasters demonstrate the same principle. A local television station does not finance its weather radar solely with revenue from tornado coverage. The whole business supports the capability. When the emergency arrives, the fixed investment becomes public-service capacity.

The question for public media is why taxpayers should finance the fixed investment when commercial broadcasters also exist.

The answer varies by geography.

In a dense market, several television and radio stations may provide strong emergency coverage. Public broadcasting is one layer among many. In a sparse market, a public station may be one of relatively few locally staffed institutions with extensive reach. Tribal and remote communities can face even thinner commercial incentives.

A national subsidy can therefore be redundant in one county and material in another.

This is the same geographic inequality that appeared in Community Service Grants.

The old CPB system addressed it indirectly.

It did not ask Washington to decide which tower was essential for which emergency. It supported stations under general rules, and the stations maintained capabilities within local budgets.

The price of this simplicity was imperfect targeting.

Money supported the institution, not only the emergency function.

A reformer can reasonably want better targeting.

The danger is assuming that a targeted replacement exists because one could be designed.

In 2025, the rescission preceded the replacement.

That sequence transferred the burden of redesign to stations and states after the money disappeared.

The Senate’s failed emergency-related motions make the sequence visible. Lawmakers were explicitly warned about public-safety consequences. They chose not to carve those functions out of the package.

That vote does not prove the functions were harmed.

It proves Congress accepted the risk that they could be.

The risk then had to be managed elsewhere.

Some of the most interesting post-CPB innovations grew out of this pressure.

Public television stations continued exploring datacasting and partnerships with emergency agencies. State governments considered or enacted support for local public media. Stations used philanthropy to maintain technical systems. National associations worked to preserve shared infrastructure. In some places, the federal role could be replaced without audience-visible change.

This is evidence for the critics’ claim that CPB was not the only possible coordinator.

It is also evidence that coordination still had to occur.

The function did not vanish merely because the federal line did.

Emergency systems reveal a broader principle about infrastructure economics.

The value of resilience is difficult to monetize because it is concentrated in bad days.

A backup generator can look wasteful for years.

A second communication path can look redundant.

A staffed local newsroom can look inefficient compared with an automated feed.

Then a flood destroys the first path, the cellular network fails, rumors spread, and the local institution suddenly looks cheap.

Markets can finance resilience when customers are willing to pay for it. Governments often finance resilience because customers systematically underpay for events they expect not to experience.

The classic problem is prevention.

Success looks like nothing happened.

That makes prevention politically weak during normal periods.

Public broadcasting had bundled a small amount of resilience spending inside a service people used daily.

The bundle made the resilience easier to sustain and harder to identify.

The rescission separated the political argument from the engineering reality.

Congress could say it was ending a media subsidy.

Stations still had to maintain towers.

The emergency-alert argument also tested the meaning of “essential.”

Public broadcasters sometimes use the word too easily.

A service can be valuable without being essential.

A community can survive without classical radio. It can survive without a documentary series. It can survive without a national political program. If advocates describe every output as essential, the word loses force when applied to evacuation information or emergency communication.

The better case is differentiated.

Some functions are amenities.

Some are cultural goods.

Some are civic goods.

Some are redundancy.

Some are genuinely connected to life and safety.

A funding model does not need to treat them all the same.

The 2025 fight did.

This is one reason a successor to CPB, if one ever emerges, may be more modular.

A rural-service fund could be justified through geographic market failure.

An emergency-infrastructure fund could be justified through resilience.

A local-journalism fund could be justified through information externalities.

An education fund could be tied to measurable childhood or classroom use.

Cultural content could compete for grants separately.

National news could receive no dedicated subsidy at all.

This architecture might reduce the political vulnerability created when a controversial national newsroom and a rural transmitter share the same federal funding story.

It would also create several new problems.

Different agencies would administer different programs.

Stations would spend more time applying for grants.

Eligibility could become politically contested in multiple places rather than one.

Federal officials would need to define which journalistic or cultural activities count.

Small stations might lack administrative capacity to navigate the system.

Shared overhead would be allocated across grants.

The sum of targeted programs could cost more to administer than the old general support.

Institutional design rarely offers a clean swap between waste and precision.

Precision has overhead.

The old CPB model accepted some imprecision in exchange for local discretion.

That was one of the reasons critics called it unaccountable.

It was also one of the reasons stations called it independent.

Emergency communication exposes the tradeoff sharply because Washington cannot predict every local use of capacity.

A station manager knows which transmitter is fragile.

A local emergency director knows which valley loses cellular service.

A tribal broadcaster knows which roads become impassable.

A federal formula can give them resources without requiring the federal government to micromanage those facts.

The price is trust.

The federal government has to trust local institutions to use flexible support for public purposes.

When political trust in the institutions collapses, flexible grants become difficult to defend.

That is what happened to CPB.

The bias argument contaminated the infrastructure argument because both depended on trust in the same grantees.

This is the deeper meaning of the Senate votes on rural, tribal, and emergency protections.

Public broadcasting had lost enough national legitimacy that even its least ideological functions could not assemble a coalition sufficient to preserve federal funding.

That is an institutional failure whether one blames the broadcasters, the politicians, polarization, or all three.

A public system whose emergency capabilities depend on people setting aside their anger at national news has a design problem.

A future system should not make the same mistake if it can avoid it.

The simplest response is to remove journalism from the federal funding relationship entirely and finance only infrastructure.

That would make the constitutional risk smaller because the government would be funding technical capacity rather than editorial work.

It could also make the infrastructure less useful by separating it from institutions that maintain audiences and local knowledge.

Another response is to create broad eligibility across commercial and nonprofit broadcasters. Any station meeting public-safety standards could receive infrastructure support. That would reduce the sense that public broadcasters possess a privileged subsidy.

Commercial stations might welcome the support.

Taxpayers might ask why profitable companies need it.

A third response is to use competitive procurement. Government could pay providers to guarantee coverage and redundancy in high-cost regions.

That approach treats emergency communication like a service contract rather than a media subsidy.

It may be the cleanest economically.

It also creates a government buyer choosing among communication providers, raising different questions about competition and dependency.

None of these options restores the old CPB compact exactly.

That is the point.

The emergency-alert argument is not evidence that the old system must return.

It is evidence that abolition did not answer every function the old system had accumulated.

When Congress removes a bundle, somebody has to decide which pieces are worth rebuilding.

The post-2025 system has been making those decisions ad hoc.

This is a natural feature of political change. Reform rarely arrives with a complete replacement architecture because the coalition for destroying the old arrangement is often larger than the coalition for designing the new one.

People can agree that a subsidy should end for different reasons.

One wants no government media funding.

Another wants only local support.

Another wants emergency infrastructure preserved.

Another wants NPR punished.

Another wants deficit reduction.

Another wants power returned to states.

These motivations can pass one rescission bill.

They cannot necessarily pass one replacement bill.

The result is a policy vacuum filled by existing institutions.

States act where they can.

Foundations fund what they value.

Stations cut what they cannot afford.

National organizations preserve common systems where members will pay.

Private vendors take over functions that can be commercialized.

Some gaps remain.

The emergency function is especially unforgiving of gaps because the test arrives without notice.

A station can survive a year with reduced cultural programming and rebuild later.

A communication failure during one wildfire cannot be rerun after funding improves.

This asymmetry argues for measurement.

The post-CPB period should be studied not only through financial statements but through resilience outcomes.

How many transmitters lose backup capacity?

How many stations reduce engineering staff?

Which rural areas lose over-the-air service?

How do public-media stations perform during hurricanes, wildfires, floods, winter storms, and other emergencies after the funding transition?

Do state and federal emergency agencies replace lost CPB-supported functions?

Does datacasting expand or contract?

Are commercial alternatives sufficient?

These questions can turn a symbolic argument into empirical policy.

That is what the public-broadcasting fight often lacked.

The competing sides talked past each other because one side emphasized offensive journalism and the other emphasized useful stations.

Both chose the function that made their preferred policy easiest to justify.

The emergency-alert argument forces the functions into the same frame.

Suppose NPR’s national newsroom is biased in exactly the way its strongest critics claim.

It does not follow that a rural transmitter is unnecessary.

Suppose a rural transmitter is important during disasters.

It does not follow that Congress must finance NPR’s national newsroom.

The policy challenge is the space between those statements.

The old system occupied that space through cross-subsidy and institutional trust.

The trust broke.

The cross-subsidy went with it.

That is why the 2025 dismantling should be studied as more than a media story.

It is a story about bundled public goods.

Governments build institutions that accumulate functions because combining them can be administratively efficient. Over time, one visible function may become politically toxic while less visible functions remain broadly valued. Abolition then becomes a crude instrument.

The Department of Agriculture does not only subsidize farms. Universities do not only teach undergraduates. Libraries do not only lend books. Transit agencies do not only run trains. Public broadcasters did not only broadcast political journalism.

Institutional bundles create resilience and opacity at the same time.

Critics see opacity and suspect waste.

Defenders see interdependence and fear cuts.

Both can be right.

A good reform identifies the bundle before cutting it.

The 2025 rescission identified the appropriation.

The wind-down identified the bundle afterward.

That order is the most important criticism of the policy that does not depend on believing federal public-media funding should have continued forever.

Congress had authority to end the subsidy.

The administration had a legitimate policy argument that the old media environment had changed.

But ending a mature system without a full inventory of shared functions creates avoidable transition risk.

CPB’s orderly wind-down reduced that risk through transfers and closeout work.

The success of that process should not obscure the design lesson.

A future government trying to dismantle any institution should ask what the institution does on its least visible day.

Not the day of the controversial program.

The day the hurricane arrives.
