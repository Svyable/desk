# A Machine Built to Be Hard to Kill

The federal government entered public broadcasting through a contradiction.

It wanted the benefits of public money without the editorial consequences of government ownership.

That was harder than it sounded.

By the middle of the 1960s, television had become the country’s dominant mass medium. Commercial broadcasting was powerful, profitable, and constrained by the simple economics of advertising. Programs capable of attracting large audiences had an advantage. Programs serving small audiences, children, classrooms, local culture, experimental art, long-form public affairs, or communities with little purchasing power had a weaker commercial case.

Noncommercial educational stations already existed, but the national structure was thin. Many had weak signals, limited schedules, uncertain funding, and little ability to share expensive programming. A station could be valuable to its city and still be unable to afford the kind of production or distribution that made a national service possible.

The policy problem was not simply that good programs cost money.

It was that broadcasting had characteristics economists would recognize as awkward for an advertising market. Once a radio or television signal was transmitted, another viewer could receive it at almost no additional cost. Educational and cultural value did not always correlate with an audience advertisers wished to purchase. Rural coverage could cost more per person while producing less revenue. Some benefits—an informed citizenry, children’s education, emergency communications, preservation of culture—were diffuse enough that no single buyer had an incentive to pay their full social value.

A government could respond by owning the broadcaster.

Many countries did.

The United States mostly did not.

That choice reflected more than American affection for private enterprise. The First Amendment made a government news service politically and constitutionally uncomfortable. A publicly owned national broadcaster whose chief executive ultimately answered to a president would raise a question obvious enough to require no legal training: what happens when the news displeases the person controlling the institution?

The solution that emerged in the Public Broadcasting Act of 1967 was architectural rather than philosophical.

Congress created the Corporation for Public Broadcasting.

The word *corporation* did work. CPB was not established as a cabinet agency. It was chartered as a nonprofit corporation intended to support and develop public telecommunications while protecting broadcasters from direct federal control. Its board would be politically appointed, because federal money cannot be made wholly apolitical by changing the label on the recipient. Yet the organization would stand between appropriators and programmers. It would distribute grants, help build a system, and leave editorial decisions outside ordinary executive command.

This was not independence in the sense enjoyed by a newspaper financed by its owners and readers.

It was mediated dependence.

That distinction became the core design feature of American public media.

Congress could decide how much money to provide. CPB could allocate support according to law and policy. Local stations could own their licenses and make programming choices. Later, PBS and NPR would operate as separate nonprofit membership organizations, receiving programs from producers and serving stations rather than acting as federal bureaus.

The result was cumbersome.

It was supposed to be.

Cumbersome structures are often what democracies invent when they distrust both concentrated public power and pure market outcomes. The complexity creates friction. Friction slows interference. Nobody can simply telephone the minister of broadcasting because there is no minister of broadcasting.

The arrangement also produced endless confusion.

To audiences, NPR could sound like a single national radio station. It was not. Listeners heard *Morning Edition* or *All Things Considered* on a local station that might be licensed to a university, a community nonprofit, a tribal organization, a school system, or another entity. The station paid NPR for programming and services, raised its own money, received underwriting, sought donations, and in many cases received a Community Service Grant supported by CPB.

PBS was similarly familiar as a national brand without being the owner of a single national chain of local stations. Member stations carried PBS programming but had their own governance, schedules, fundraising, and local obligations.

CPB sat further upstream.

It did not tell an NPR correspondent what to report at noon. It did not write the script of a PBS documentary. It supported a system in which those activities could occur.

That difference is the difference between funding infrastructure and controlling speech, though the boundary was never clean enough to make political conflict disappear.

Tax money has a memory.

Opponents could reasonably trace a federal appropriation to CPB, from CPB to a local station, from the station to dues paid to NPR or PBS, and from those dues to national programming. The fact that several institutions stood between Congress and a newsroom did not make the public contribution imaginary.

Defenders could trace the same route and reach the opposite conclusion: the layers were exactly what prevented the public contribution from becoming government editorial authority.

Both descriptions were true.

The question was what conclusion to draw from them.

The early history made clear why Congress had wanted insulation. Presidents did not become indifferent to broadcasting merely because CPB had been created.

Richard Nixon’s administration entered into open conflict with public television. The dispute involved funding, structure, programming, and the administration’s belief that the emerging national public-television apparatus was politically hostile. Nixon objected to what he saw as centralized public-affairs programming and attempted to redirect the system toward local production. In 1972 he vetoed public-broadcasting legislation amid a broader confrontation over its direction.

The conflict exposed a flaw in any system whose independence depends on annual appropriations.

A president does not need to order a producer to cancel a program if the threat of losing the institution’s money can shape everyone’s behavior upstream.

Congress responded over time by strengthening advance funding.

Advance appropriations sound like an accountant’s detail. They were a constitutional culture expressed as a calendar.

If Congress appropriates money for public broadcasting well before the fiscal year in which it will be used, a president or congressional majority angered by tonight’s program cannot as easily threaten next month’s payroll. The money is not beyond repeal. Congress can always legislate again. But the lag raises the political and procedural cost of retaliation.

It also helps local stations plan.

A television transmitter does not care which party won the midterm election. Neither does a multi-year educational series, a rights agreement, a rural relay system, or a contract with a producer. Stable institutions make commitments longer than a news cycle. Advance funding gave the public-media system enough visibility to behave like an institution rather than a succession of emergency campaigns.

That design did not eliminate politics.

It converted politics into episodic stress.

Ronald Reagan entered office in 1981 promising to reduce the size of the federal government. Public broadcasting appeared in the budget-cutting effort. The administration proposed substantial reductions in future support. The fight tested whether a system built during the Great Society would survive a conservative governing project explicitly skeptical of federal spending and public-sector expansion.

It survived.

A decade and a half later, Newt Gingrich became the face of a more direct threat. With Republicans taking control of the House in 1995, Gingrich spoke openly about eliminating federal support for CPB. To many conservatives, public broadcasting was a relic and a partisan irritation. To defenders, the promise looked like a threat to institutions ranging from *Sesame Street* to rural radio.

Again, the system survived.

These episodes established a dangerous kind of institutional confidence.

If something survives enough near-death experiences, people begin treating the near-death experience as proof of immortality.

The public-broadcasting funding fight became recurring theater. A conservative administration proposed elimination or deep cuts. Public broadcasters mobilized viewers and listeners. Programs beloved across ideological lines were invoked. Rural stations explained their dependence. Lawmakers restored money or rejected the deepest reductions. CPB emerged bruised but intact.

The ritual had incentives for everyone.

Politicians could signal hostility to perceived media bias. Public broadcasters could mobilize donors and supporters. Legislators could cast themselves as protectors or budget hawks. The existence of a recurring threat did not mean the threat was fake. It meant the system had repeatedly found enough political support to survive it.

During Trump’s first term, the White House again proposed eliminating CPB funding in budget requests.

Congress did not comply.

That fact later became part of the puzzle. Trump’s hostility to federal public-media funding was not new in 2025. The political capacity to realize it was.

A machine built to be hard to kill is not a machine that cannot be killed.

The distinction lies in what its designers expected an attacker to have.

The original public-broadcasting architecture assumed conflict between branches and across parties. It assumed that one elected official might dislike a program, so power was distributed. It assumed Congress would continue to see enough public value in the institution to maintain funding. It assumed advance appropriations would make impulsive retaliation harder. It assumed a board structure and corporate form would resist ordinary presidential direction.

These were sensible assumptions in a political environment where institutional preservation commanded a coalition wider than ideological affection for the content.

They were less useful when the same political coalition controlled the presidency and both houses of Congress, regarded public broadcasting as illegitimate, and was willing to use a rescission process to retrieve money that had already been committed.

Institutional design does not fail merely because somebody discovers an illegal shortcut.

It can fail because somebody uses the legal doors in an order the designers did not expect.

That is the deeper significance of advance funding in 2025. The appropriation meant to create stability became a discrete asset on the federal balance sheet. Congress had put money aside for future public broadcasting. The Trump administration could identify that money, package it in a rescission proposal, and ask Congress to take it back.

The shock absorber was still attached to the machine.

Congress removed it.

To understand why that mattered, it helps to follow one federal dollar.

Congress appropriated money to CPB. CPB first operated under statutory and administrative constraints on how much it could spend on itself. The overwhelming majority moved outward. Community Service Grants went to qualifying local radio and television stations under formulas and eligibility rules. Other money supported system functions, content, interconnection, and initiatives that individual stations would find expensive to recreate alone.

A local station then mixed the federal money with other revenue.

Membership contributions might be substantial. Underwriting might matter. A university or state government might provide support. Foundations could fund particular projects. Major donors could become essential. The station might earn income from events, tower leases, production services, or investments. It could purchase national programming from NPR or PBS, produce local journalism, maintain transmitters, employ engineers, and participate in emergency systems.

The federal grant was rarely the whole budget.

That fact was central to the case for the system and to the case against it.

Defenders described CPB money as catalytic. Because federal support covered part of the stable base, a station could use it to attract and deploy local funds. CPB’s own materials emphasized that local stations typically leveraged each federal grant dollar with several dollars from other sources. The money also flowed disproportionately toward places where local fundraising was hardest.

Critics heard a different message. If stations could raise several private dollars for each federal dollar, perhaps they did not need the federal dollar. A mature media organization with loyal listeners and major donors should be able to stand on its own. Public broadcasting’s success at building private support could be presented as evidence against the subsidy that helped make the support possible.

That argument contains a genuine economic question.

When does a public subsidy crowd in private value, and when does it simply replace money supporters would otherwise contribute?

There was never one answer across the system.

Consider a large urban public-radio station with hundreds of thousands of listeners, a deep membership file, corporate underwriting, philanthropic relationships, and a strong local news brand. Federal support may be painful to lose without being existential. An emergency drive can produce millions. Major donors can bridge a gap. Costs can be cut without turning off the transmitter.

Now consider a small station serving a wide rural area with a sparse population. The transmitter still requires electricity. Equipment still fails. Engineers still have to reach remote facilities. Rights still cost money. The station may cover communities that commercial broadcasters do not see as sufficiently profitable. The number of potential donors is smaller, and their incomes may be lower. A federal grant that looks negligible from Washington can be a large fraction of the station’s flexible cash.

The same appropriation therefore performed different functions in different places.

It was national policy with local variance.

That variance also made the political argument susceptible to anecdotes. A critic could point to a wealthy public-media institution and ask why taxpayers in a poorer state should support it. A defender could point to an isolated rural station and ask why a national culture-war dispute should deprive a remote community of its only local service.

Neither anecdote described the full system.

Both described real pieces of it.

The architecture tried to reconcile them with formulas.

Community Service Grants included base and incentive structures that tended to matter more to smaller stations. CPB eligibility requirements encouraged nonfederal financial support, audience service, governance standards, and other obligations. Federal money was not simply divided equally among stations, nor did it flow only according to ratings.

That was the public-policy logic: use a national pool to support local institutions whose capacity to raise money varies.

It was also the conservative objection in another form: a federal formula was making distributive judgments the market would not make.

The 2025 fight eventually became a referendum on whether that difference was a virtue.

Long before it became a vote, however, public broadcasting accumulated another vulnerability.

The media environment changed beyond recognition.

In 1967 broadcast spectrum was scarce, channel choice was limited, and television distribution required expensive physical infrastructure. By 2025 almost anyone with a phone could publish video worldwide. Podcasts offered more audio than any listener could consume. Streaming services carried documentaries, children’s programming, foreign drama, lectures, concerts, and news. Nonprofit journalism had proliferated beyond the public-broadcasting system. Foundations could support digital news directly. Universities could publish without a transmitter. Local governments could send emergency alerts to phones.

The original market-failure argument did not disappear.

It became harder to state with the vocabulary of scarcity.

The relevant scarce resource was no longer a television channel.

It was attention, trust, local reporting capacity, and money for work whose return could not easily be captured by a commercial seller.

Public broadcasting adapted technologically. NPR became a podcast powerhouse. PBS streamed. Stations built websites, newsletters, apps, digital newsrooms, and on-demand archives. Yet institutional arguments are sticky. Critics could still look at the name *public broadcasting* and see a government-supported answer to a problem technology had solved.

Defenders increasingly had to argue for the functions rather than the medium.

Children’s education.

Local journalism.

Arts and culture.

Rural service.

Emergency communications.

National programming available without a subscription.

The strongest case for federal support by 2025 was no longer that Americans lacked media choices.

It was that abundant media choices did not guarantee universal access to these particular public goods.

That case may persuade someone who never listens to NPR.

It may fail to persuade someone who hears NPR’s national newsroom as ideologically one-sided.

The legitimacy problem sits there.

A public institution can survive hostility if enough opponents accept the institution’s rules even while disliking its output. That is what happened during many earlier funding fights. Conservatives who accused public media of bias still joined appropriations majorities that continued CPB funding. They might have valued local stations, feared constituent backlash, believed the federal share too small to justify a fight, or simply accepted continuity.

Once that institutional loyalty weakened, the technical protections had to carry more weight.

They were not designed to carry all of it.

CPB’s corporate form could resist a presidential command, but it could not tax.

Its board could dispute removal, but it could not appropriate.

Advance funding could buy time, but Congress could rescind it.

A decentralized station system could keep broadcasting after the federal hub disappeared, but decentralization could not make every local budget equally resilient.

The machine was hard to kill because power had been dispersed.

The same dispersion meant that when the center was removed, the death did not look like death.

Transmitters remained on.

Programs remained in feeds.

NPR and PBS logos remained familiar.

Membership drives continued.

People could reasonably ask what exactly had been dismantled.

The answer was the mechanism connecting a federal public purpose to locally independent institutions.

That mechanism had never been as visible as the programs it enabled. CPB itself was almost designed for obscurity. A successful buffer is not supposed to star in the show.

Its obscurity became a political liability. The public knew *Sesame Street*. It knew *Nova*. It knew *Morning Edition*, *Fresh Air*, *Frontline*, *PBS NewsHour*, *All Things Considered*, and local hosts whose voices had become part of daily life. Few people knew why CPB existed, how grants worked, or why an organization that produced almost none of the famous programming could matter so much to the system.

It is difficult to defend plumbing with a mascot.

Yet plumbing was the point.

Congress had not created CPB to be beloved. It had created CPB to make public support less dangerous.

By 2025, a new governing coalition concluded that the support itself was the danger.

Once that judgment took hold, every protective feature could be reinterpreted. Independence looked like unaccountability. Advance funding looked like insulation from voters. grant formulas looked like entrenched subsidy. The corporate buffer looked like an obstacle to presidential control. Public-service rhetoric looked like self-justification from institutions that had lost political neutrality.

The very properties designed to make the system durable became evidence, to its opponents, that it was too difficult to change.

That reversal is one of the recurring patterns in institutional politics.

A safeguard is praised as independence by the people who trust the institution and condemned as entrenchment by the people who do not.

The law cannot settle that political argument by itself.

It can only specify who has authority to act.

In 1967, Congress gave public broadcasting a peculiar form because it feared a direct line from government money to government speech.

In 2025, the country learned that breaking that line did not remove politics from the system.

It moved politics to the point where the money entered.
