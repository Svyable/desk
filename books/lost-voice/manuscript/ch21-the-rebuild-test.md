# The Rebuild Test

The cleanest way to understand what was lost is to imagine trying to rebuild it.

Suppose a future Congress decides that the country wants a national public-media funding system again.

Not because NPR won every argument.

Not because PBS became universally trusted.

Because enough lawmakers conclude that local journalism, rural service, educational media, cultural programming, emergency communication, and common infrastructure justify a federal role.

What would they have to create?

The answer is larger than an appropriation.

Money can be enacted quickly.

Institutions take time.

A future bill could appropriate five hundred million dollars tomorrow. Someone would still need to receive it, distribute it, define eligibility, audit recipients, manage common services, protect editorial independence, handle disputes, preserve geographic equity, and explain why elected officials cannot use the program to reward friendly coverage and punish hostile coverage.

That is the rebuild test.

If recreating the old functions requires rebuilding an institution, then the 2025–26 episode changed more than a budget line.

The first choice would be whether to recreate CPB itself.

Congress could theoretically charter a new Corporation for Public Broadcasting with the same name and many of the old statutory features. It could appoint a board, restore advance funding, recreate Community Service Grants, reestablish administrative capacity, and transfer shared functions back from the organizations that inherited them.

This path offers familiarity.

It also recreates the vulnerabilities the Trump administration exposed.

Who can remove the board?

How insulated is the corporation from executive control?

Can a future Congress rescind advance funding on an expedited timetable?

Does the structure privilege broadcasters over digital nonprofit media?

Can local grants indirectly support national organizations a governing coalition considers biased?

Would public legitimacy return simply because the old architecture returned?

A serious rebuild would have to answer these questions rather than assuming the 1967 design can be rebooted unchanged.

One option would be greater independence.

Congress could fund a trust or endowment large enough to support public-media grants without annual appropriations. Board terms could be staggered. Removal protections could be explicit. Grant formulas could be set in statute. A permanent capital base could reduce the leverage any one administration possesses.

The attraction is obvious after 2025.

The democratic problem is equally obvious.

How much money should one Congress be allowed to place beyond the practical reach of future Congresses?

An endowment large enough to create genuine independence would be a form of entrenchment. Future taxpayers and lawmakers would inherit a media-financing institution they could influence only indirectly. Critics who already regarded CPB as insulated from accountability would see the new model as worse.

Independence cannot simply be maximized.

It has to be balanced against legitimate democratic control.

Another option would be a dedicated revenue source.

Some countries finance public broadcasting through license fees or dedicated levies. The United States could theoretically impose a small communications fee, spectrum charge, platform assessment, or other revenue stream earmarked for civic media.

Dedicated funding reduces annual appropriations pressure.

It also makes the public-media tax more visible.

Visibility can increase accountability and opposition at the same time.

A household that never watches PBS may resent a dedicated charge more than an invisible share of general taxation. A fee tied to devices or broadband can become obsolete as technology changes. A platform levy raises questions about whether technology companies are being forced to finance journalism that covers them.

Every firewall is attached to something.

A third option would abandon the idea of one public-media institution entirely.

Congress could create competitive grants for defined public functions.

Local journalism.

Rural and tribal information service.

Emergency communications.

Educational content.

Cultural production.

Archives.

Shared technical infrastructure.

Any qualified nonprofit, university, station, consortium, library, or perhaps commercial provider could compete.

This approach addresses the strongest modernization critique of CPB. Why should broadcasting licensees receive privileged status in an age when public service can be delivered through podcasts, websites, streaming video, newsletters, and other digital formats?

Platform-neutral funding sounds future-proof.

It creates a difficult First Amendment and administrative question.

Who decides which journalism deserves a grant?

CPB reduced this problem by supporting local institutions under formulas rather than asking federal officials to choose the best investigative story. A competitive journalism fund can drift toward content selection even when reviewers act professionally.

Applications must describe editorial plans.

Panels must score them.

Government defines categories.

Rejected applicants can suspect viewpoint discrimination.

A system designed to avoid subsidizing legacy institutions can accidentally bring government closer to editorial content than CPB ever was.

The old buffer begins to look less archaic once one tries to replace it.

A fourth option would finance audiences rather than institutions.

Imagine a civic-media voucher. Every taxpayer or household receives a small amount that can be directed to qualifying nonprofit news, educational, or cultural organizations. The government supplies the money. Citizens choose the recipient.

This model reduces centralized editorial selection.

It makes public support voluntary at the level of allocation even if the tax financing is collective.

It can encourage pluralism.

It also favors institutions with strong brands and fundraising machines. National organizations may capture vouchers more effectively than tiny rural outlets. Polarized media can mobilize supporters. People uninterested in civic information may leave vouchers unused. Administrative costs could be substantial.

A voucher solves one legitimacy problem and creates a popularity contest.

A fifth option would place primary responsibility on states.

The federal government could provide block grants or matching funds. States would decide how to support local public media. This approach respects geographic variation and reduces national culture-war pressure.

It also reproduces geographic inequality.

A state hostile to public media may provide nothing. A wealthy state may build a strong network. A poor state may struggle. State governments can exert political pressure more directly on local institutions than Washington did through CPB.

Federalism distributes power.

It does not guarantee independence.

A sixth option would be philanthropic matching.

Federal money could match private donations to qualifying local media, perhaps with larger matching rates in low-income or rural areas. This would combine voluntary support with national redistribution. Stations that demonstrate community value receive more money while places with weak donor bases receive a more generous public match.

The design is attractive because it uses private giving as a signal without allowing wealth alone to determine service.

It also embeds existing inequalities in the base. A community unable to raise the initial dollar may never trigger the match. Rules would have to prevent wealthy donors from capturing disproportionate public subsidy. National political organizations could attempt to qualify. Government would still need an administrator.

The rebuild test keeps returning to administration because administration is the institution.

There is no funding mechanism without someone defining and enforcing rules.

That someone becomes the new target when political trust breaks.

This is the fundamental lesson CPB leaves behind.

The hard problem was never writing checks.

The hard problem was creating enough distance between the people who authorize the checks and the people who decide what gets said.

The 1967 system solved this through a corporate buffer and a decentralized field.

The Trump administration challenged the buffer.

Congress removed the money.

The court later protected the speakers from one form of executive retaliation.

A future system would have to solve all three problems simultaneously: funding, independence, and democratic legitimacy.

The rebuild test also clarifies why dissolution matters.

If CPB had simply been cut to a smaller appropriation, a future Congress could restore funding through a familiar account. Staff would remain. grant systems would remain. contracts would remain. board governance would remain. Institutional memory would survive.

After dissolution, reconstruction must begin earlier in the chain.

Recruit directors.

Hire staff.

Rebuild grant administration.

Create compliance systems.

Negotiate with stations.

Recreate databases.

Determine how transferred rights and infrastructure fit into the new organization.

Establish credibility.

Convince employees the institution will not disappear again after the next election.

That last problem may be the hardest.

Institutions depend on expectations.

A talented grant administrator may hesitate to build a career in a corporation that has already been abolished once. Stations may be reluctant to build long-term budgets around a federal promise proven reversible. Vendors may demand stronger contractual protections. A board may act more cautiously. Political actors may treat every appropriation as temporary.

Restoration can reproduce money faster than trust.

This is the hysteresis of institutional politics.

A temporary shock can have permanent effects because people change behavior after learning what is possible.

Banks lend differently after a default.

Companies invest differently after expropriation.

Employees plan differently after mass layoffs.

Stations will plan differently after the end of CPB even if some successor eventually appears.

The new baseline contains memory of the cut.

That memory can improve resilience. Stations may maintain larger reserves, diversify donors, share services, and reduce dependence on any one public source.

It can also make national coordination harder because members trust the center less.

The rebuild test therefore asks not only what Congress can create but what organizations will believe.

The same logic applies politically.

A future coalition wanting federal support must explain why the new system will not reproduce the legitimacy failures of the old one.

Conservative voters who believed NPR and PBS were hostile to them will not be persuaded by institutional nostalgia. The argument must show why the program serves citizens who do not share the cultural assumptions of public-media professionals.

That may require broader governance.

It may require new measures of viewpoint diversity.

It may require distributing more money directly to local institutions and less through national brands.

It may require explicit prohibitions on using federal grants for national programming dues.

It may require opening eligibility to digital and commercial partners.

Every concession changes the system.

A durable rebuild would therefore probably not be restoration.

It would be constitutional redesign.

The 2026 court ruling provides one foundation.

Any new system can begin with a clearer understanding that the executive branch cannot use otherwise available federal benefits to punish named media organizations because the president dislikes their protected speech.

Congress can codify stronger protections around that principle.

For example, eligibility decisions could be reviewable under transparent criteria. Grants could be formula-based where possible. Removal authority for governing boards could be explicit. No executive official could direct programming or condition support on favorable coverage. Agency investigations could remain available for ordinary legal compliance without becoming editorial supervision.

None of these protections would bind Congress to permanent funding.

That is the necessary democratic limit.

A future Congress must retain the authority to reduce or end the program.

The challenge is designing changes so that policy disagreement does not become story-by-story financial retaliation.

This is a subtle distinction and a crucial one.

A legislature can say, after debate, that the country will no longer finance public media.

A president cannot say, while other funding programs remain open, that NPR receives nothing because yesterday’s coverage was unfair.

The first changes policy.

The second disciplines speech.

The March ruling made that boundary clearer than it had been before the dismantling.

A successor could be stronger for having learned it.

The rebuild test also asks whether national public-media funding is the correct objective at all.

Perhaps the proper lesson of 2025 is not to rebuild.

Perhaps civil society can do the work better.

The strongest evidence for this position is the survival itself. NPR still reports. PBS still programs. Stations still broadcast. Audiences donated. Foundations responded. States experimented. Organizations cut costs. Digital distribution replaced some expensive broadcast functions.

The system proved more resilient than a centralized-institution story would predict.

Why recreate the political vulnerability?

Let the field evolve.

This is a serious conclusion.

It should be tested against distribution.

If survival concentrates in wealthy markets while rural and low-income communities lose service, private resilience will not have replaced the old national purpose. It will have changed the map of access.

If national journalism survives while local reporting contracts, the most visible institutions will have outlived the function Congress once had the strongest reason to subsidize.

If donor concentration increases ideological segmentation, independence from government may come with narrower public legitimacy.

If shared infrastructure fragments into expensive duplicative systems, the market solution may cost more collectively even while costing taxpayers less.

The no-rebuild case wins only if the replacement system performs the public functions well enough that national redistribution is no longer worth its political cost.

That is an empirical question.

A future Congress should have the evidence 2025 lacked.

Station-level revenue composition.

Geographic service maps.

Local journalism employment.

Emergency coverage performance.

Broadband substitution.

Donor concentration.

State support.

Membership durability.

NPR and PBS dues.

Infrastructure cost.

Audience trust across party and class.

The dismantling has created a before-and-after dataset.

It should be used.

This is one of the unexpected benefits of institutional rupture. Long-running policy debates often become unfalsifiable because nobody observes the alternative. Public broadcasting had been defended and attacked hypothetically for decades. Everyone had a model of what would happen without federal support.

Now the country can compare the models with reality.

The comparison should not be rushed.

The first year contains emergency money, transition grants, remaining reserves, and temporary accommodations. The second and third years will be more revealing. Capital maintenance cycles are longer. Donor fatigue takes time. Staff attrition accumulates. Mergers happen slowly. Institutions can spend balance sheets before changing service.

A station surviving September 2026 has not proved it can survive 2030.

The opposite is also true.

A painful first year may produce a stronger later organization.

Innovation requires time too.

The rebuild test should therefore be repeated.

In 2028: what functions remain missing?

In 2030: where did local public media strengthen and weaken?

In the next recession: which revenue model proves more resilient?

In the next major disaster: which communication systems work?

In the next administration hostile to a different set of media institutions: does the March 2026 legal precedent constrain retaliation effectively?

Institutions should be judged across stress, not only calm.

There is a final form of the rebuild test that has nothing to do with legislation.

Could the public itself rebuild the national function without government?

Stations could create a larger mutual fund financed by dues weighted toward wealthy members.

National philanthropies could capitalize an endowment for rural stations.

NPR and PBS could coordinate a systemwide infrastructure trust.

Major stations could cross-subsidize smaller ones in exchange for governance rights.

Audiences could contribute to a national solidarity fund during membership drives.

Universities could form regional public-media consortia.

None of these possibilities requires Congress.

They require the stronger stations to accept redistribution.

That is the question the old federal system answered through taxation.

Will private institutions voluntarily do the same?

The answer will reveal whether public media’s national solidarity was genuinely institutional or merely fiscal.

A wealthy station has rational reasons to keep its money local. Its donors gave for local service. A national organization has rational reasons to preserve dues. A foundation has a strategy. A university has a budget. Cross-subsidy always asks someone with options to support someone with fewer.

Government is good at this because taxation can compel participation.

Civil society is good at this when solidarity is strong enough to make compulsion unnecessary.

The next decade will test which description fits American public media.

If the field recreates national pooling privately, CPB’s dissolution may look less consequential over time. The federal institution will have disappeared while the coordinating function survives through voluntary organization.

If the field fragments, the loss will look larger.

That is the rebuild test in its simplest form.

Do the functions come back?

Not the logo.

Not the appropriation.

The functions.

A national floor for high-cost stations.

Shared technical infrastructure.

Editorial independence from the funder.

Common rights.

Local public-service capacity.

Universal access where the market is weak.

A mechanism for broad accountability that does not become political command.

If those functions reappear through another system, the country will have replaced CPB.

If they do not, the absence will become more visible with time.

The 2025–26 dismantling therefore should not be judged by whether one misses the Corporation for Public Broadcasting as an institution.

Most Americans never thought about it while it existed.

The proper measure is whether we eventually have to reinvent what it did.

That is how invisible institutions prove their value.

Someone removes them.

Years later, people discover themselves rebuilding the same pipes under different names.
