# The Case Against Public Media

The easiest way to write badly about the end of federal public broadcasting is to make its opponents stupid.

A book can then proceed with reassuring speed. Public media provides education, culture, local journalism, emergency communication, and programming the commercial market undersupplies. The federal appropriation is tiny relative to the budget. Therefore anyone trying to eliminate it must be motivated by hostility to journalism, philistinism, partisan grievance, or some combination of the three.

That account is emotionally efficient.

It is not sufficient.

The strongest case against federal public-media funding begins with a proposition that does not require anyone to hate *Masterpiece*, public radio, classical music, local weather coverage, documentaries, or children’s television.

Good things do not automatically become federal responsibilities.

The American economy contains millions of useful institutions that receive no dedicated congressional appropriation. Newspapers can perform civic functions without federal operating grants. Churches can provide community services without being government agencies. Museums, orchestras, universities, food banks, neighborhood groups, legal-aid organizations, investigative newsrooms, theaters, and libraries rely on different mixtures of private and public support. The fact that an institution creates social value begins a funding argument. It does not end one.

Public broadcasting’s defenders sometimes weakened their own case by treating value as entitlement.

The critic’s next question was predictable.

Why this institution?

Why this medium?

Why this funding architecture?

Why now?

In 1967, the answers had been easier to connect to the technology of broadcasting. Spectrum was scarce. Television production and distribution were expensive. A national noncommercial service could do things local educational stations could not. Advertising economics strongly favored mass audiences. Public policy could plausibly create a parallel system for educational, cultural, and civic content that the market might not produce in sufficient quantity.

By 2025, the abundance problem had reversed.

The country did not lack content.

It was drowning in it.

Anyone with a smartphone could choose among more news, documentary video, lectures, children’s programming, classical performances, history, science, interviews, and political commentary than a lifetime could absorb. YouTube alone carried free educational libraries that would have been unimaginable to the architects of the Public Broadcasting Act. Podcasts eliminated much of the distribution scarcity that had once defined public radio. Streaming services financed ambitious documentaries and drama. Universities published courses. Foundations funded nonprofit digital outlets directly. Local governments, weather services, wireless carriers, and social platforms could distribute emergency information without relying on a radio tower.

A federal program born in scarcity now had to justify itself in abundance.

The obvious defense was quality.

That was also dangerous.

If the government’s rationale became that NPR and PBS produced *better* journalism or culture than private alternatives, someone had to decide what better meant. Public funding would then depend on judgments about taste, editorial standards, educational value, and civic merit. The system had been created partly to avoid direct government control over precisely those judgments.

The more persuasive modern defense therefore shifted toward market failure at the margins rather than scarcity in the aggregate.

There may be endless podcasts, but not enough reporters at a rural county commission.

There may be dozens of streaming services, but most require subscriptions, broadband, or both.

There may be commercial emergency information, but a remote community still benefits from a resilient local transmitter.

There may be thousands of children’s videos, but quality educational content available free to every household remains a distinct good.

There may be abundant national commentary, but very little patient reporting from a region that advertisers have stopped supporting.

This defense was strong where the market failure was concrete.

It was weaker when attached to a national newsroom with a large audience, sophisticated fundraising, major philanthropic support, a valuable brand, and successful digital products.

The critics noticed.

A conservative member of Congress could ask why a federal appropriation meant to preserve rural service should create financial pathways that ultimately support an NPR newsroom the member considered politically hostile. The answer—that local stations voluntarily pay for national programming they value—did not remove the political fact that federal grants could strengthen the stations’ ability to pay those dues.

The indirectness that protected editorial autonomy also complicated accountability.

The second major argument was therefore not about technology.

It was about compelled subsidy.

Every appropriation spends money collected from people who disagree about its purpose. That is ordinary government. Taxpayers who oppose a war still fund the military. People without children fund schools. Drivers help pay for transit. City residents help finance rural roads. The public character of taxation means individual disagreement is not normally a veto.

Speech adds a different sensitivity.

If government funds a bridge, the bridge does not publish an investigation of the taxpayer.

A news organization does.

For conservatives who believed NPR and PBS leaned left, the funding arrangement felt like more than redistribution. It felt like the state forcing them to support institutions participating in political argument against them.

The analogy can be overstated. CPB grants did not transform NPR into a state broadcaster. Public-media journalism was protected by editorial rules and institutional distance. The federal appropriation was small relative to the whole system, and much of it went to local stations performing functions beyond national political journalism.

Yet the legitimacy problem did not depend on the subsidy being a majority of revenue.

A dollar can carry symbolic meaning larger than its percentage.

The White House’s May 2025 executive order rested heavily on this claim. It argued that government funding of news media in the modern environment was unnecessary and that the particular national public broadcasters were biased. From that perspective, eliminating the subsidy was not retaliation. It was restoring neutrality by taking the government out of the business.

That position contains a serious First Amendment tension.

The government is free in many contexts to decide what programs to fund. It does not follow that officials may identify particular speakers, condemn their viewpoints, and then deny otherwise available benefits because of those viewpoints. A decision to abolish a program for everyone is legally different from a decision to punish a recipient for speech within a continuing program.

The March 2026 federal court ruling turned on that difference.

But legal limits on executive retaliation do not dispose of the policy case against the program itself.

Congress can decide the public no longer wishes to finance a category of activity. If Congress can create CPB, Congress can change or terminate its appropriations, subject to the Constitution and the terms of enacted law. Public broadcasting never possessed a constitutional guarantee of federal money.

This is why the strongest anti-subsidy argument should be separated from the president’s rhetoric about specific coverage.

A person can believe the May executive order improperly targeted NPR and PBS because of their journalism while also believing Congress should end CPB funding prospectively.

A person can believe NPR exhibits ideological bias while also believing government should not use funding levers to dictate editorial outcomes.

A person can believe public broadcasting is excellent and still conclude that the federal government has no unique role in paying for it.

The political debate often denied citizens these combinations because combinations are poor campaign material.

The third argument against federal support concerned institutional age.

Programs develop constituencies. Constituencies develop routines. Routines become moral claims.

A grant created as a policy choice in one era can, after fifty years, be discussed as though removing it is an act of destruction rather than another policy choice. That asymmetry frustrates people who want government to be able to stop doing things.

Public broadcasting illustrated the problem unusually well because its defenders often described advance appropriations and institutional insulation as virtues. To critics, the same protections looked like mechanisms designed to make a subsidy politically difficult to remove.

Why should a Congress elected in 2024 be constrained by the preferences of a Congress that appropriated money in advance?

The legal answer was that an appropriation is law until changed.

The democratic answer was that a later Congress could change it.

That is what the rescission did.

From this perspective, the 2025 action was not dismantling an independent press institution. It was an elected government exercising ordinary control over federal spending after years of unsuccessful attempts.

The word *ordinary* deserves scrutiny.

The political campaign around the spending decision was explicitly entangled with complaints about journalism. The president named NPR and PBS, described their content as biased, and sought to restrict their access to money. House Republicans held a hearing whose title framed the broadcasters as “anti-American.” The policy objective was not formed in a vacuum by auditors ranking low-value programs.

Still, a motive can be politically aggressive without making every subsequent legislative act invalid.

Congressional spending decisions are saturated with political motives. Members vote for military bases, research grants, cultural programs, environmental projects, tax provisions, and disaster aid partly because they approve or disapprove of the activities being supported. Courts do not normally invalidate an appropriation vote because legislators criticized the recipient.

That separation between political legitimacy and legal authority is central to this story.

Opponents could be pursuing a lawful legislative objective through rhetoric that raised constitutional concerns when translated into executive action.

The fourth argument was that public media’s professional culture had become too narrow.

This critique requires more care than the word *bias* usually receives.

Every newsroom has a point of view in at least three senses.

It chooses what to cover.

It chooses which sources to treat as authoritative.

It chooses language.

Those choices do not prove partisan intent. Journalism without selection is impossible. But selection reflects professional norms, social networks, education, geography, class, and institutional culture. A newsroom can sincerely pursue fairness while sharing assumptions that a large part of its audience does not share.

Conservative distrust of NPR was not created by one story.

It accumulated through patterns.

Coverage of Trump.

Coverage of race and policing.

The handling of the Hunter Biden laptop story.

Language around sex and gender.

The origins of COVID-19.

The use of terms and frameworks associated with progressive academia or activism.

The relative ideological composition of newsroom staff.

Disputes over which claims were treated as misinformation and which uncertainties were emphasized.

Some conservative complaints distorted what NPR had actually reported. Some substituted disagreement with facts for evidence of bias. Some demanded a form of balance that would place well-supported claims and unsupported claims on equal footing.

Others identified real questions about viewpoint diversity.

Uri Berliner’s 2024 critique from inside NPR became powerful because it named that latter problem. He argued that the organization had lost ideological diversity and that this loss affected coverage and trust. His account was disputed and incomplete, but it could not be dismissed as the complaint of someone who had never seen the newsroom.

An institution dependent in part on public legitimacy should have treated the episode as more than a reputational inconvenience.

NPR’s problem was not that it failed to make every Republican approve of every story.

No serious newsroom can operate that way.

The problem was that many Republicans no longer believed the institution was even trying to describe them fairly.

That is a different level of distrust.

Public broadcasting’s defenders sometimes answered it with audience surveys, journalism awards, editorial codes, and examples of conservative guests. Those responses addressed professional performance. They did not necessarily address cultural distance.

The distinction can be illustrated without politics.

A national newspaper can cover rural hospitals accurately and still sound to rural readers as though nobody writing the stories has ever depended on one. A business publication can describe hourly workers with impeccable statistics and still reveal that its writers inhabit a salaried world. A sportswriter can know every statistic and still misunderstand the fan culture of a team.

Accuracy and affinity are not the same.

Public media’s broad mission made the gap more dangerous. An outlet that markets itself to a niche can afford to be culturally narrow. A taxpayer-supported institution claiming service to the entire public carries a heavier burden.

The fifth argument was competitive fairness.

Local newspapers spent decades watching advertising revenue migrate to digital platforms while public radio stations, some with federal support and nonprofit tax status, expanded local newsrooms and digital coverage. Commercial broadcasters competed for sponsorship and audience against institutions receiving grants and donations. New nonprofit news organizations built business models without a dedicated federal appropriation.

Why, critics asked, should one legacy sector receive a privileged funding channel?

The answer from public media was that it also carried public obligations, served markets commercial outlets abandoned, and produced noncommercial work precisely because the revenue model differed.

The answer from local newspapers could be less philosophical.

We do those things too.

This rivalry complicated broad claims that CPB funding simply “supported journalism.” It supported a particular historical network of institutions. If the public objective was local journalism, Congress could theoretically design a platform-neutral program. If the objective was children’s education, Congress could fund educational content competitively. If the objective was rural emergency communication, it could support infrastructure directly.

CPB’s defenders could respond that creating new programs to replicate pieces of a functioning system would be more expensive, politically vulnerable, or administratively complex. They could also point to decades of local investment already embedded in stations.

The critic could answer that incumbency is not an entitlement.

Again, there was no trivial argument on either side.

The sixth argument was about donor substitution.

If audiences truly valued public media, then ending federal support would test that value honestly. Membership drives already showed that listeners and viewers would contribute voluntarily. Foundations and wealthy individuals supported journalism. Public radio in particular had cultivated a prosperous, educated audience in many markets. Why should taxpayers with no interest in the programming subsidize those who loved it?

The first year after the rescission produced evidence for this claim.

Several stations reported extraordinary giving. Some told audiences they had replaced the federal loss through membership. Emergency campaigns attracted donors who understood that the funding model had changed. The cuts made the old slogan of “listener-supported radio” more literal.

This evidence deserves to remain in the story.

It is tempting for defenders to explain it away as a one-time sugar high. It may prove to be exactly that, but in 2026 nobody could know with confidence. A funding shock can reveal previously untapped willingness to pay. Stations may become more entrepreneurial. Donors may build recurring commitments. States and foundations may create durable replacements.

The opposite evidence also deserves to remain.

More than five hundred layoffs were reported across public media by mid-2026. Some stations cut programs. Some small organizations faced severe gaps. A public television station in Illinois shut down. PBS reduced costs and changed its programming. The loss was not absorbed without consequence.

A market test can produce both survivors and casualties.

That is what tests do.

The policy question is whether the casualties represent inefficient institutions that should disappear, or public goods that the market predictably underprovides.

One cannot answer that from the word *public*.

The seventh argument against federal support was less principled and more openly political: institutions that repeatedly alienate a governing coalition should not expect that coalition to finance them forever.

This is a dangerous principle for journalism.

It is also a fact of appropriations politics.

Public broadcasters sometimes spoke as though the firewall around editorial decisions also insulated them from the consequences of losing political trust. It did not. The firewall meant a lawmaker could not call an editor and demand a story be killed. It did not mean lawmakers had to vote forever for the appropriation.

The challenge, then, was not to please politicians.

It was to maintain enough legitimacy across political tribes that funding the system remained a shared institutional commitment rather than a partisan favor.

By 2025, that commitment had eroded.

The erosion was not solely the work of public media’s enemies. It reflected polarization, media fragmentation, conservative movement strategy, changing Republican attitudes toward institutions, Trump’s politics of direct conflict, and public broadcasters’ own cultural failures.

The temptation to assign a single cause is strong because a single cause produces a cleaner villain or a cleaner acquittal.

Institutions usually fail more messily.

The case against public media contained arguments strong enough to persuade people of good faith.

It also contained an unresolved danger.

Suppose taxpayers should not subsidize journalism. The clean policy is to end the subsidy through law, neutrally and prospectively.

Suppose instead the government identifies two news organizations, declares their journalism biased, and uses public power to stop money from reaching them because of that judgment.

The first proposition can be liberal in the classical sense: separate state and press.

The second can become a form of state pressure on the press.

In rhetoric, the two positions can sound almost identical.

In constitutional law, the difference matters.

In May 2025, the White House crossed from the long argument about whether public broadcasting should be funded into an executive command aimed at NPR and PBS by name.

The administration believed it was ending an improper subsidy.

The broadcasters believed they were being punished for their speech.

Both descriptions pointed toward the same money.

Only one could explain why the order named the speakers.
