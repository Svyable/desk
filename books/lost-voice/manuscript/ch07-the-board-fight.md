# The Board Fight

The email arrived before the executive order.

That sequence matters.

On April 28, 2025, three members of the Corporation for Public Broadcasting’s board received notices purporting to terminate them. The White House had decided that Laura Gore Ross, Diane Kaplan, and Thomas Rothman would no longer serve.

CPB’s answer was not a resignation announcement.

It was a lawsuit.

The corporation argued that the president did not possess the removal authority he was asserting. Congress had not built CPB as an ordinary executive agency whose directors served at presidential pleasure. The statutory design was intended to keep political officials at a distance from programming and grants. If a president could remove board members whenever public-media coverage angered him, the buffer would become conditional on presidential tolerance.

The administration saw a broader principle.

The president is responsible for the executive branch. Limits on presidential removal of officials exercising federal authority had already become one of the great constitutional questions of the second Trump administration. Across independent agencies and boards, the White House tested legal arrangements that insulated officers from direct presidential control. The theory drew on the Constitution’s assignment of executive power to the president and on a long-running conservative legal argument that too much of the federal government had become accountable to no elected executive.

CPB was an odd place to stage that argument because CPB insisted it was not part of the executive branch at all.

The corporation’s very existence forced a question that had slept inside the Public Broadcasting Act for decades.

What is an institution funded by Congress, chartered by federal law, governed by presidential appointees confirmed by the Senate, assigned a national public purpose, but deliberately not organized as a federal agency?

The usual answer was practical.

A buffer.

The law needed a more exact one.

The lawsuit over the board removals did not begin with a grand ruling that settled the constitutional status of every quasi-public corporation. It began in the ordinary way of institutional litigation: requests for temporary relief, statutory interpretation, arguments about irreparable harm, and the difficult question of what a court should do before the merits were fully resolved.

In June, the district court denied CPB’s request for a preliminary injunction on the posture presented. That was not a final declaration that the president possessed unlimited authority over the board. The court’s reasoning left important questions alive.

Public descriptions of legal cases often flatten this stage into winning and losing.

“Trump wins board fight.”

“CPB loses bid.”

The labels conceal the procedural reality. A preliminary injunction is extraordinary relief. A plaintiff must satisfy requirements before a full trial or final judgment. Losing that request does not necessarily mean losing the underlying legal theory.

For CPB, however, procedure had a cost.

Time.

A corporation fighting for the independence of its board was also approaching the congressional rescission fight that would determine whether the corporation had money to distribute.

Legal ambiguity is manageable when an institution has years.

It becomes dangerous when the budget clock runs in weeks.

The board dispute also exposed a hidden assumption in the original public-broadcasting design.

Congress can write independence into structure.

It cannot make political actors agree on what the structure means forever.

The CPB statute included unusual features. The corporation was established as a nonprofit. Its board membership was politically balanced by law. The arrangement reflected distrust of one-party domination. The federal government was barred from exercising control over public telecommunications in ways inconsistent with the statutory scheme. CPB had duties connected to the expenditure of federal funds, but it was designed to support private and local entities rather than to broadcast as the voice of the United States.

For decades, presidents appointed board members and the system operated without a decisive test of whether a president could simply fire them.

Institutional peace can masquerade as settled law.

Sometimes a power is absent because everyone agrees it is absent.

Sometimes it is absent because nobody has tried.

The Trump administration tried.

That decision fit a pattern wider than public media. The second Trump term moved aggressively against statutory and customary constraints on presidential control. Officials at independent agencies were removed. Legal disputes emerged over the Federal Trade Commission, National Labor Relations Board, Merit Systems Protection Board, and other bodies. The Supreme Court’s evolving separation-of-powers doctrine had already weakened some older precedents protecting independent officers.

CPB therefore entered court while the legal landscape around independent entities was moving.

Its strongest argument was not that all presidentially appointed board members are immune from removal.

It was that CPB was special by design.

The corporation existed to prevent exactly the kind of political control that ordinary executive supervision would create. Public broadcasting was not like rail regulation or labor adjudication. Its subject was speech. If presidential control over CPB became ordinary, then a president’s view of media content could influence the distribution of public funds to journalists and broadcasters.

The administration’s strongest response was institutional accountability.

If CPB exercised federal authority and spent federal money, why should presidential appointees become untouchable once confirmed? Congress cannot evade the Constitution by calling an entity a private corporation if the entity performs governmental functions. Independence can become a euphemism for power without democratic supervision.

Again, the conflict did not sort cleanly into good-faith constitutionalism on one side and political opportunism on the other.

The administration had a genuine theory of executive power.

It also had a president who had publicly attacked the journalism of the institutions CPB helped support.

The two facts were connected in practice even if a court could analyze them separately.

That connection mattered because boards are not abstract constitutional objects.

Boards decide things.

They select leadership. They approve budgets. They interpret mission. They authorize litigation. They establish grant priorities within law. They decide how aggressively an institution resists political pressure. Replacing three members of a small board could change the internal balance of an organization at exactly the moment its future was being contested.

The board fight was therefore about both doctrine and control.

To the White House, removal authority could be described as a basic feature of presidential accountability.

To CPB, the removals threatened the firewall.

To public broadcasters, the timing made the dispute impossible to treat as neutral constitutional housekeeping.

On May 1, only days after the termination notices, the president issued the executive order against NPR and PBS funding. The order made the administration’s substantive objective explicit. The board dispute could no longer be viewed in isolation from a policy that sought to change what CPB did with its money.

This is where the architecture of 1967 met the politics of 2025 most directly.

A buffer works by forcing anyone who wants to influence the endpoint to pass through institutions.

The White House attacked the institutions.

It challenged board independence.

It challenged funding decisions.

It challenged the subsidy itself.

The response from CPB illustrates why formal independence matters most when everyone stops assuming it.

The corporation did not comply with a presidential instruction merely because it came from the president. It argued that its statutory obligations controlled. It maintained that Congress, not the White House, had defined its role. The dispute moved to court instead of moving directly into a change in grant policy.

That is the friction constitutional design was supposed to produce.

Friction did not guarantee survival.

It bought process.

This distinction is easy to miss because people often judge safeguards by whether they ultimately prevent an unwanted outcome. If CPB dissolved months later, one might conclude that board independence was meaningless.

It was not meaningless.

The board resisted.

The administration could not simply replace disagreement with obedience overnight without litigation.

The executive order itself would later be enjoined.

The problem was that the institution had another dependency the board could not litigate away.

Appropriations.

Board independence determines who governs an organization.

Funding determines whether there is much organization left to govern.

The rescission campaign therefore reduced the strategic value of the removal fight. Suppose CPB had ultimately obtained a definitive ruling that the president could not remove the directors. That victory would protect the composition of a board overseeing a corporation whose federal appropriation had been cancelled.

The legal principle would matter.

The practical leverage would shrink.

This was the same temporal mismatch that later appeared in the NPR and PBS litigation. Institutional defendants were fighting on several clocks, and the congressional clock could outrun the judicial one.

The episode offers a broader lesson about checks and balances that schoolbook descriptions tend to hide.

Checks are not switches.

They are processes that consume time.

Congress checks the president by legislating, withholding money, conducting oversight, confirming appointees, and creating statutory limits. Courts check officials by hearing cases and issuing judgments. Independent institutions check political direction by relying on tenure, norms, professional obligations, and procedural requirements. Elections check all of them in different ways.

None of these mechanisms is instantaneous.

Political strategy can exploit the gaps between their speeds.

A president can act quickly.

A court can require months to reach a final merits decision.

Congress can sometimes act very slowly, but an expedited rescission process can compress the legislative timeline.

An institution can spend reserves temporarily, but employees and vendors respond to uncertainty before a court does.

The actor who creates facts fastest may shape the practical result even when another institution later establishes the legal boundary.

This is not the same as saying illegality wins if done quickly.

Illegal acts can be reversed. Officials can be reinstated. Money can sometimes be restored. Contracts can be honored. Courts can issue emergency relief.

The point is narrower: irreversible or path-dependent consequences can accumulate while legal questions remain open.

A newsroom that lays off twenty reporters cannot automatically reassemble the same staff a year later. A nonprofit that dissolves does not spring back into legal existence because a court criticizes an earlier order. A station that sells property or cancels a program may be able to rebuild, but rebuilding is a new act requiring new money and people.

Institutional destruction has transaction costs.

That is why the board fight cannot be understood as a side story.

It revealed the central strategic vulnerability of the public-broadcasting system before the decisive vote occurred.

The system had multiple firewalls but no independent revenue source.

Some public institutions solve this problem through endowments. Universities with enormous endowments can withstand temporary changes in government grants. Foundations can use investment income. Member organizations can raise dues. Subscription businesses can charge customers. Religious organizations can rely on congregants. Political parties can raise campaign money.

CPB was built around a federal appropriation.

It was not meant to become self-financing because self-financing would alter its function. If CPB accumulated a giant permanent endowment, Congress would lose much of its fiscal accountability. If CPB charged stations enough to replace federal support, it would shift costs toward the same local institutions the subsidy was meant to strengthen. If it sold commercial services, it would become something other than the buffer Congress designed.

Its dependence was therefore not an accidental weakness.

It was part of the democratic bargain.

Congress supplied the money.

CPB supplied distance.

The bargain worked only while Congress was willing to fund an institution it did not directly control.

That sentence gets close to the deepest conflict in the 2025 fight.

Democratic governments are often willing to finance independent institutions when major political coalitions trust the rules that make them independent. Courts, universities, central banks, scientific agencies, and public broadcasters all depend to some degree on a distinction between losing a particular political argument and losing the legitimacy of the institution itself.

Once a coalition treats independence as evidence of hostility, funding becomes leverage.

The Trump administration’s approach to CPB made that shift explicit. An institution designed to resist political control looked, from the administration’s perspective, like an institution resisting legitimate presidential authority.

Those are mirror-image descriptions.

Each makes the other side look anti-democratic.

The board fight also forces an uncomfortable question for defenders of public media.

How independent should a federally funded corporation be?

It is not enough to say “completely.”

CPB spent public money. Congress had a right to define conditions. Auditors had a right to examine compliance. The public had a right to transparency. Board members were not hereditary guardians. They entered through a political appointment process. The corporation existed because federal law said it existed.

Independence from editorial interference does not require independence from law.

The difficult work is drawing a boundary between accountability and control.

The Public Broadcasting Act attempted to draw it structurally. Congress would establish purposes and provide funds. CPB would distribute support within statutory constraints. Stations and national organizations would make editorial decisions. The president would appoint board members with Senate consent, but partisan-balance requirements and institutional norms would prevent the board from becoming a transmission belt for one party.

The model assumed imperfect actors.

It also assumed restraint.

No statute can specify in advance every way a future president might seek influence or every way a future Congress might respond. Legal text sits inside political practice. Where the text is ambiguous, practice can harden into convention.

Until someone refuses the convention.

The April termination notices were such a refusal.

They asked the courts to say whether presidential power extended into a space that prior presidents had largely treated as buffered.

The answer at the preliminary stage was not the clean institutional vindication CPB wanted.

Yet the corporation’s refusal to accept the terminations as self-executing had another effect.

It documented the conflict.

The dispute produced filings, factual records, statutory arguments, and judicial reasoning that later historians and lawyers could examine. Political pressure that might otherwise disappear into phone calls and private understandings became visible.

This is an underrated function of litigation.

A lawsuit can lose and still make power legible.

It forces parties to state authority.

Who appointed whom?

What did the statute say?

What did the White House send?

What did the corporation do next?

Which actions were claimed to be lawful?

What relief was requested?

The board case thus became an early map of the institutional battlefield.

The executive order soon added another map.

The rescission request would add a third.

By midsummer, nobody paying attention could plausibly say the end of federal public broadcasting resulted from one accidental budget line.

The administration had articulated a policy.

It had challenged the people governing the buffer.

It had instructed agencies to stop funding named broadcasters.

It had asked Congress to rescind the appropriation.

The machinery was being tested from multiple directions.

CPB’s board could defend its statutory independence.

It could not vote in Congress.

That is where the fight was going.
