# The Hidden Plumbing

Public broadcasting had a satellite problem before it had a constitutional problem.

Not chronologically.

Structurally.

A news program produced in Washington has to reach a station in Wyoming somehow. A live concert originating in New York has to arrive in Oregon on time. A station that buys a national show needs a reliable feed, scheduling information, rights, technical standards, and enough redundancy that a single failure does not turn a national service into dead air.

Audiences experience all of this as nothing.

The program begins.

That is infrastructure’s highest compliment.

The disappearance of CPB forced public media to name systems that had spent decades succeeding invisibly. The Public Radio Satellite System and related interconnection arrangements became urgent legal and financial objects. National public-television distribution had its own dependencies. Music licensing, emergency communications, station grants, archival responsibilities, and multi-party contracts all had to be examined for a world in which the central federal corporation would no longer exist.

This is where the phrase *dismantled the public broadcasting system* becomes literal enough to leave politics behind.

A system is not only the organizations audiences recognize.

It is the agreements among them.

The Trump administration and Congress did not instruct NPR to stop producing programs. Congress did not order PBS to remove a documentary. No law told a local station to turn off its transmitter.

But if the institution supporting the interconnection among those organizations disappears, the remaining organizations have to reconstruct the agreements.

The work is expensive precisely because the old agreements were already there.

Economists call one version of this problem a coordination problem. Everyone benefits from a shared system, but no single participant has the same incentive or capacity to pay for the whole thing. If the largest member takes control, smaller members may fear dependence. If costs are divided equally, small stations pay too much relative to their budgets. If costs are divided by size, large stations may resent carrying others. If no one coordinates, participants duplicate infrastructure or accept lower reliability.

CPB had solved parts of this problem with public money.

The solution did not have to be efficient in every detail to create value. Its main advantage was that the funding question was settled upstream. Congress appropriated. CPB allocated. Stations and networks used shared systems.

After the rescission, the question moved downstream.

Who pays now?

That question sounds less political than “Should taxpayers subsidize biased media?”

It may have been more consequential to a station engineer.

The public-radio interconnection fight provides the clearest example. NPR and CPB became involved in litigation and negotiations over how money and control associated with the satellite distribution system would be handled as CPB wound down. In the end, more than thirty-five million dollars was directed toward preserving the Public Radio Satellite Interconnection System and related distribution functions.

The number matters because it reveals an asset the culture-war argument barely mentioned.

A federal appropriation had not only supported journalism.

It had created and maintained a network utility.

When the institution disappeared, somebody had to carry the utility forward.

That is what makes public infrastructure politically peculiar. Its value becomes most obvious when ownership changes.

A bridge has a visible asset. A satellite distribution system is easier to treat as an operating expense until lawyers have to decide where contracts, cash, and authority go.

The same pattern appeared in rights.

Public broadcasting is full of licensed material: music, performances, photographs, archives, footage, syndicated programs, educational resources. A centralized agreement can allow many stations to use material on terms none could negotiate individually. The value is not only the discount. It is reduced transaction cost.

One negotiation replaces hundreds.

The federal layer supported some of that common capacity.

Once removed, the system had three broad choices.

National organizations could absorb more of the common cost.

Local stations could pay higher dues or fees.

Other institutions—foundations, associations, universities, state networks, new nonprofits—could step in.

Every choice redistributed control.

This is why replacing a public dollar with a private dollar is not always financially neutral.

Money has governance attached.

A congressional appropriation comes with statutes, oversight, public accountability, political risk, and restrictions. A foundation grant comes with a donor’s priorities and term. Membership money comes from the audience the station can persuade. University support comes with university governance. Corporate underwriting comes with reputational and market considerations. State money comes with state politics.

No funding source is free of influence.

The old CPB structure tried to make federal influence indirect.

The new system would have to decide which other influences to accept.

Consider station dues.

NPR and PBS derive meaningful revenue from member stations. The national organizations create programming and services that stations choose to buy because the content attracts audiences and helps define the station’s identity. The arrangement works when stations have enough revenue to pay.

When stations lose federal grants, dues become a pressure point.

A national organization can hold dues steady and risk losing members or forcing local cuts.

It can reduce dues and reduce its own revenue.

It can redesign the formula, shifting costs among stations.

It can cut national programming, fundraising support, technology, staff, or other services.

PBS chose relief. In 2026, its leadership said member dues would be reduced, easing pressure on stations while reducing money available to the national service.

This is a network feedback loop.

Federal money disappears locally.

Local payments fall nationally.

National capacity shrinks.

The consequences return locally through fewer or cheaper services.

An opponent of federal funding can describe this as rational market discipline. Organizations that provide sufficient value will retain members; others will cut unnecessary cost.

A defender can describe it as the unraveling of economies of scale that had allowed small stations access to national quality.

Both descriptions are compatible with the same mechanism.

The argument depends on whether the old scale was efficient or artificially sustained.

The only way to know is to observe what replaces it.

Infrastructure also complicates the claim that internet distribution made broadcast subsidy obsolete.

The internet eliminates some physical constraints but introduces different ones. Streaming requires servers, content-delivery networks, application development, platform relationships, digital rights, data systems, cybersecurity, identity management, payment processing, and broadband at the receiving end. Podcasts need hosting, distribution, measurement, and licensing. Apps require maintenance across devices. Digital does not abolish infrastructure.

It changes the invoice.

Large national organizations can pay that invoice.

Small local stations can struggle.

Shared services remain attractive even when satellites give way partly to fiber and cloud systems.

The question is still who coordinates.

One of CPB’s least glamorous functions had been supporting common projects whose benefits were spread across the field. When Congress removed the corporation, the public-media system had to discover whether those projects could be financed through clubs instead of government.

A club good is excludable. Members pay dues and receive the service. In theory, public-media infrastructure can operate this way. Stations that want a satellite feed pay for it. Stations that want music rights join a licensing collective. Stations that want digital tools subscribe.

The difficulty is that the poorest members are then most likely to drop out.

The federal model had allowed cross-subsidy.

The club model prices more of the service directly.

That can improve efficiency.

It can also reduce universality.

This tension appeared in emergency communications.

Public broadcasters and their defenders frequently invoked public-safety roles during the 2025 fight. Some stations participate in alerting systems and maintain resilient transmission capacity that remains valuable when other communications fail. Public television infrastructure has been used for datacasting and emergency partnerships. Radio can remain available during disasters when internet or cellular networks are overloaded or damaged.

Critics responded that emergency functions could be funded directly if they were truly necessary. There was no reason, in this view, to subsidize a national news organization in order to preserve an emergency relay.

That argument has merit.

It also understates the cost of institutional decomposition.

A system built for multiple purposes can share fixed costs. The same tower carries daily programming and emergency information. The same engineering staff maintains both. The same organization that has a relationship with emergency managers also produces local news. Separating the public-safety function into a narrow grant may preserve the equipment while losing the institution around it.

Sometimes modular policy is cleaner.

Sometimes it destroys the economies created by bundling.

The 2025 rescission did not settle which was true. It removed the broad subsidy before replacement modules existed.

That sequence matters.

Reformers often imagine that obsolete institutions can be disassembled and their valuable functions rebuilt more efficiently. The rebuilding is treated as a later technical task.

Technical tasks are where reform programs often discover that the institution had encoded knowledge.

Who has the contract?

Who knows which station needs which feed?

Who maintains the list of eligible grantees?

Who has the relationship with a rights holder?

Who understands the rural exception in the formula?

Who knows which system has to keep operating on the day after the corporation closes?

Institutional memory is not a statute.

It lives in people.

The wind-down therefore threatened more than money. CPB employees who left took with them experience accumulated across grant cycles, station crises, technical migrations, audits, legislative fights, and industry changes. Other organizations could hire some of those people. Records could be transferred. Procedures could be documented.

Not all knowledge survives a handoff.

This is one of the hidden costs of abolition compared with reform.

A reformed institution can preserve memory while changing policy.

An abolished institution forces the ecosystem to repurchase the memory in other forms.

The same logic applies to trust.

A local station that had worked with CPB for decades knew the process, deadlines, definitions, and contacts. Replacing the money with a patchwork of foundations may require new applications with new conditions. Replacing system services with vendor contracts may require procurement skills. Replacing CPB coordination with association governance may require stations to spend more executive time on collective decisions.

Transaction cost moves.

It rarely disappears.

The political case for abolition often counts the visible federal spending and not the replacement transaction cost because the replacement is paid by different actors.

From Washington’s perspective, that can still be a successful cut. The federal budget is smaller.

From the system’s perspective, cost may have been transferred rather than eliminated.

The transfer can be desirable if local actors make better decisions. It can be wasteful if hundreds of organizations duplicate work once done centrally. The answer is empirical.

The public-media transition offered examples of both.

Some stations collaborated more closely after the cuts. Shared newsrooms, engineering arrangements, and regional partnerships became more attractive. National organizations reconsidered dues and services. Philanthropies explored collective responses. Old assumptions about what each institution had to own were questioned.

A shock can accelerate useful consolidation.

It can also force cuts on the wrong timeline.

A station considering a merger voluntarily can negotiate governance, identity, staffing, and service over years. A station facing an immediate budget hole may accept terms from weakness. The resulting institution can be more efficient and less local at the same time.

Efficiency and localism are not synonyms.

Public broadcasting had spent decades trying to hold both.

The old system was decentralized in ownership but centralized enough in funding and infrastructure to achieve scale.

That combination was expensive to explain and easy to caricature.

It was also unusual.

Commercial networks solve scale through ownership contracts, advertising, affiliate agreements, and market power. Digital platforms solve it through software and global distribution. Public broadcasting solved it through membership, grants, shared infrastructure, philanthropy, underwriting, and federal support.

No one designed the whole thing at once.

It accreted.

Accreted systems look inefficient because every layer has a history.

They can also be resilient because no single failure ends everything.

The 2025 dismantling tested which property dominated.

CPB’s disappearance did not collapse NPR or PBS distribution overnight because the system had multiple institutions capable of taking over pieces. That resilience supports the critics’ view that the corporation was not indispensable.

The cost and negotiation required to transfer those pieces supports the defenders’ view that CPB was doing real work.

Both should survive in the record.

The satellite dispute is particularly revealing because it reduced a grand constitutional conflict to a practical question.

Who controls the pipes?

A judge could rule on First Amendment retaliation.

Congress could rescind appropriations.

The president could declare public media biased.

Stations still needed Monday morning’s program feed.

Institutional politics becomes real at the point where Monday morning has to work.

That is why wind-down teams matter. Their job is not to relitigate the wisdom of abolition. It is to prevent political decisions from producing avoidable technical failure.

The CPB transition team had to preserve obligations long enough for successors to emerge. Money that remained legally available had to be used according to law. Grants had to be completed or terminated correctly. Interconnection had to be secured. Corporate records had to be handled. Employees had to leave with benefits and paperwork. Assets and liabilities had to be resolved.

A clean institutional ending is a form of public service.

It is easy to overlook because it lacks a constituency.

People rally to save a station.

Nobody rallies to reconcile its grant closeout.

Yet a disorderly CPB collapse could have harmed the very local stations Congress said were free to continue without federal support. The corporation’s final act was therefore paradoxical.

It used the remaining capacity of the federal public-broadcasting system to make the post-federal system possible.

That transition helped prevent the rescission from producing immediate visible chaos.

The absence of chaos could then be cited as evidence that the old system had not been necessary.

This is another institutional paradox.

A successful wind-down conceals the value of the institution’s final competence.

If bridges are transferred without closing, people notice the new owner, not the engineers who kept traffic moving.

If satellite feeds continue after CPB, listeners hear a program and assume nothing fundamental changed.

The evidence of good transition is continuity.

Continuity can be mistaken for costlessness.

The public-media story after 2025 is full of these misleading surfaces.

The station is still on the air.

The program still exists.

The feed still arrives.

Therefore the federal cut did nothing.

Or:

Staff were laid off.

A station closed.

A program changed.

Therefore the entire system was destroyed.

Neither inference is disciplined enough.

The useful work is following the pipes.

Who paid before?

Who pays now?

Who owned the contract?

Who owns it now?

Which functions disappeared?

Which survived through transfer?

Which became more expensive?

Which became more efficient?

Which communities lost capacity?

Which discovered new support?

That ledger is less dramatic than a hearing.

It is where the truth of a funding cut accumulates.

By late 2025, CPB’s public story was mostly about closure dates and money.

Inside the system, the difficult question was succession.

The corporation could disappear.

The plumbing could not.
