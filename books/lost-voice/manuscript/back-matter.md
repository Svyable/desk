# Back Matter

## A note on language

This book uses *dismantled* to describe the removal of the federal funding and coordinating architecture created around the Corporation for Public Broadcasting, not the disappearance of NPR, PBS, or every local public-media organization. NPR and PBS continued operating after CPB dissolved. Local outcomes varied substantially, from successful emergency fundraising and continued service to layoffs, programming reductions, dues changes, mergers, and in at least one highly exposed case the end of over-the-air public television service.

The book also distinguishes three actions that were politically connected but legally different: the Trump administration’s Executive Order 14290, the attempted removal of CPB board members, and Congress’s enactment of the Rescissions Act of 2025. The March 31, 2026 federal court ruling permanently enjoined agency implementation of the executive order’s instruction to cease funding NPR and PBS. It did not invalidate Congress’s rescission of CPB appropriations or recreate CPB after its dissolution.

## Method

The manuscript was built from primary legal and governmental material wherever available: the Public Broadcasting Act and later statutory history; presidential documents; congressional hearing records; House and Senate roll calls; the White House rescission request; Government Accountability Office review; CPB statements and court filings; and the March 31, 2026 opinion in the consolidated NPR and PBS cases.

Contemporary reporting and public-media trade sources were used for the effects that primary legal documents cannot show well: layoffs, local-station budget responses, PBS and NPR restructuring, emergency fundraising, the WEIU-TV transition away from over-the-air broadcasting, station-dues changes, and the transfer of shared system functions.

The research trail in `../research/` records the core sources, chapter-to-evidence map, counter-evidence, and claims that should be revisited as the post-CPB system develops.

## Chronology

**1967** — Congress enacts the Public Broadcasting Act and creates the Corporation for Public Broadcasting as a private nonprofit corporation intended to support public telecommunications while insulating programming from direct federal control.

**June 30, 1972** — President Richard Nixon vetoes public-broadcasting legislation during a broader conflict over funding, structure, and public-affairs programming.

**1980s–1990s** — Conservative administrations and congressional leaders repeatedly seek reductions or elimination of federal public-broadcasting support. The system survives and increases its reliance on memberships, underwriting, philanthropy, and other nonfederal revenue.

**2011** — Another major congressional fight over NPR and federal public-broadcasting funding reinforces the long-running conservative case against the subsidy.

**April 2024** — Longtime NPR editor Uri Berliner publishes a critique arguing that NPR has lost viewpoint diversity, intensifying debate over ideological homogeneity inside public media.

**March 26, 2025** — NPR CEO Katherine Maher and PBS CEO Paula Kerger testify before the House Oversight Subcommittee on Delivering on Government Efficiency at a hearing titled “Anti-American Airwaves: Holding the heads of NPR and PBS Accountable.”

**April 28, 2025** — Three CPB board members receive notices purporting to terminate their positions. CPB and the directors sue the next day.

**May 1, 2025** — President Donald Trump signs Executive Order 14290, *Ending Taxpayer Subsidization of Biased Media*, directing CPB and federal agencies to cease direct and indirect funding of NPR and PBS to the maximum extent allowed by law.

**June 3, 2025** — The administration transmits a rescission request to Congress that includes CPB’s advance appropriation.

**July 16–17, 2025** — The Senate rejects amendments and motions that would preserve or protect portions of public-broadcasting funding. At 2:30 a.m. on July 17, the Senate passes H.R. 4, as amended, 51–48.

**July 18, 2025** — The House agrees to the Senate amendment through a 216–213 vote on the governing resolution and completes congressional action on the rescission package.

**July 24, 2025** — President Trump signs the Rescissions Act of 2025, eliminating roughly $1.1 billion in advance appropriations for CPB across fiscal years 2026 and 2027.

**August 1, 2025** — CPB announces an orderly wind-down. Most staff positions are scheduled to end September 30, with a smaller transition team retained for closeout work.

**January 2026** — CPB’s board votes to dissolve the corporation.

**January 11, 2026** — *PBS News Weekend* airs its final broadcast for the foreseeable future after PBS cancels the live weekend newscast amid the loss of federal public-media funding.

**February 27–28, 2026** — CPB files articles of dissolution; dissolution takes effect February 28.

**March 31, 2026** — Judge Randolph D. Moss permanently enjoins agency defendants from implementing or enforcing Executive Order 14290’s instruction to cease funding NPR and PBS. Other portions of the consolidated cases are resolved on mootness or other grounds.

**May 15, 2026** — WEIU-TV at Eastern Illinois University ends over-the-air television broadcasting after losing federal support that had represented approximately eighty percent of its budget; the organization continues through digital television and radio operations.

**May–June 2026** — NPR reduces newsroom staffing through layoffs, buyouts, and unfilled positions. PBS approves a fiscal-year 2027 budget reducing station assessments by 12.9 percent from the prior year.

**Summer 2026** — Public-media reporting documents more than five hundred layoffs across the field while also recording unusually strong listener and viewer giving at a number of stations.

## What remains unresolved

The post-CPB system is too young for a final verdict. The evidence through September 2026 shows both adaptation and loss. Some stations have replaced federal money. Others have cut staffing or service. National organizations have reduced cost. Philanthropic and state responses remain uneven. Digital distribution is allowing some institutions to preserve mission with less broadcast infrastructure, while making broadband access more important.

Future editions should revisit at least five questions:

1. Whether emergency donor gains persist after the first two post-rescission fundraising cycles.
2. Whether rural and tribal station closures, mergers, or service reductions accelerate relative to large-market stations.
3. Whether state funding and philanthropic replacement increase or reduce political and donor concentration risk.
4. Whether NPR and PBS regain broader cross-partisan trust when federal funding is no longer central to their identity.
5. Whether Congress eventually creates a new national mechanism for local journalism, educational media, emergency communication, or other public-media functions.
