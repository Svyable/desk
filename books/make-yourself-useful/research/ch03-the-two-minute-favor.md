# Chapter 3 Research — The Two-Minute Favor

## Chapter claim under test

Small acts of help can matter far more to the recipient than their duration suggests because they often arrive exactly where progress has stopped. But usefulness is not unlimited availability. The chapter should distinguish help that builds capability or trust from recurring unpaid labor, compulsory “citizenship,” and structural failures that organizations quietly transfer onto conscientious people.

The chapter's preferred arc is:

1. one small act of help
2. the real consequence behind the apparently small request
3. evidence that help can benefit relationships and propagate
4. the boundary problem: repeated helping can become fatigue or hidden job transfer
5. the structural move: turn recurring favors into processes, funded roles, clearer systems, and capability-building

Do not turn this into a reciprocity hack or a claim that favors reliably generate opportunities.

## Opening case: Charlie Brown and Zach McMahon

Primary reporting:

- Shannon Heffernan, WBEZ Chicago, “Library's cyber-navigators help digitally illiterate patrons access social services,” June 13, 2012: https://www.wbez.org/news/2012/06/13/librarys-cyber-navigators-help-digitally-illiterate-patrons-access-social-services
- Syndicated/reported version, North Country Public Radio, July 5, 2012: https://www.northcountrypublicradio.org/news/story/20096/20120705/filling-out-social-service-forms-without-digital-literacy

Usable details:

- Charlie Brown was an older Chicago resident relying on social services for housing and health care.
- He carried a large bag of papers and described himself as computer illiterate.
- Social-service offices increasingly sent him online for information and forms.
- Brown met weekly with Chicago Public Library cyber navigator Zach McMahon at Harold Washington Library.
- McMahon helped with government/social-service access and job-related digital tasks.
- A critical observed moment: when Brown pushed the keyboard toward McMahon out of frustration, McMahon pushed it back and encouraged Brown to continue rather than completing the task for him.
- The cyber-navigator model in Chicago was grant-funded and part-time at the time; do not imply McMahon was a librarian or social worker unless a source says so.

The keyboard moment is valuable because it embodies capability-building without requiring invented dialogue or interior motive.

## E-government and public libraries

Bradley Wade Bishop, Charles R. McClure, and Lauren H. Mandel, “E-Government: Service Roles for Public Libraries,” Public Libraries, April 29, 2013: https://publiclibrariesonline.org/2013/04/e-government-service-roles-for-public-libraries/

- Summarizes 2009–2010 Public Library Funding and Technology Access Survey findings.
- Two-thirds of public libraries reported being the only provider of free public computer/Internet access in their communities.
- 78.7 percent reported providing assistance to patrons applying for or accessing e-government services.
- Use these figures only as historical evidence that libraries had already become important access points for online government, not as current 2026 estimates.

The chapter uses the migration of government services online as an example of administrative work being transferred to citizens and then partly absorbed by trusted community institutions.

## Recurring character: Amanda Perry

Urban Libraries Council profile:

- “Digital Navigator Profile: Amanda Perry”: https://www.urbanlibraries.org/blog/digital-navigator-profile-amanda-perry

Usable details:

- Amanda Perry was an Associate Librarian with Salt Lake City Public Library and a Digital Navigator.
- The profile says she had been with SLCPL for about 20 years, beginning as a library aide and progressing to associate librarian.
- She worked in the socioeconomically diverse Marmalade District and led a Creative Lab makerspace.
- She was involved with the library's Committee for Digital Inclusion.
- During pandemic-era restrictions she identified the loss of in-person library access as a significant gap for people who relied on libraries for connectivity and digital help.
- Her practical advice to other digital navigators emphasized understanding local demographics, connectivity data, community needs, and available resources before trying to help.

This makes Perry a useful recurring character for Chapters 3 and 12 because her work sits between individual assistance and the creation of a replicable service model.

## Salt Lake City Digital Navigators program

Institute of Museum and Library Services grant record:

- Grant LG-248566-OLS-20: https://www.imls.gov/grants/awarded/lg-248566-ols-20
- Fiscal year 2020; federal funds: $411,084.
- Salt Lake City Public Library grant to develop a Digital Navigators Program in response to sudden pandemic-era need for trustworthy digital-inclusion services.
- Partners included National Digital Inclusion Alliance and Urban Libraries Council.
- Program design included one-to-one dedicated phone support.

IMLS grant spotlight:

- “Connecting Communities, Navigating Needs: The Importance of Digital Inclusion Programs”: https://www.imls.gov/grant-spotlights/connecting-communities-navigating-needs-importance-digital-inclusion-programs
- Describes assistance with home Internet/hotspots, devices, digital skills, phone support, and in some cases personalized/door-to-door assistance.

National Digital Inclusion Alliance model:

- Current Digital Navigator Model page: https://www.digitalinclusion.org/digitalnavigatormodel/
- Defines digital navigators as trusted guides providing ongoing individualized support with connectivity, devices, digital skills, and critical online services.
- Explicitly includes follow-up over time and notes that effective programs require training, dedicated resources, staff time, and support.
- Current NDIA resources include boundaries, confidentiality/data-sharing, hiring, training, service-model design, and fair-compensation considerations.

NDIA 2021 toolkit:

- https://www.digitalinclusion.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/Digital-Navigators-Toolkit-1.pdf
- Describes digital navigators helping residents access services including food support, housing, education, employment, childcare, and government benefits.
- Useful historical documentation of how the model moved beyond one-off tech support.

## 2026 NDIA evaluation

NDIA announcement and evaluation summary, June 4, 2026:

- https://www.digitalinclusion.org/blog/ndnc-program-evaluation-report/
- National Digital Navigator Corps operated through 18 community organizations across 15 states in rural and Tribal communities.
- Reported more than 6,500 community members served, more than 9,200 sessions, and more than 4,100 devices distributed over 31 months.

Caution:

- These are program outputs from an evaluation produced by the organization leading the initiative.
- Do not present them as independent proof of causal long-term outcomes.
- Use them only to establish scale and institutionalization of the service model.

## Research: everyday prosociality

Katherine Nelson-Coffey et al., “Everyday prosociality in the workplace: The reinforcing benefits of giving, getting, and glimpsing,” Emotion (2018).

- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28581323/
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1037/emo0000321
- Randomized workplace study with 111 employees in Spain assigned to giver, receiver, or control conditions.
- Givers performed acts of kindness toward designated receivers over four weeks.
- Both givers and receivers showed some well-being benefits; receivers subsequently performed substantially more prosocial acts than controls.

Caution:

- Small sample, one workplace context, designed intervention.
- Do not use the reported “278% more prosocial behaviors” as proof that an ordinary favor reliably creates reciprocity; it is an experimental finding in one setting.
- The chapter therefore describes the study as evidence that help *can* propagate, not a universal effect.

## Research: citizenship fatigue and the cost of compulsory helping

Mark C. Bolino, Hsin-Hua Hsiung, Jaron Harvey, and Jeffery A. LePine, “Well, I'm tired of tryin'! Organizational citizenship behavior and citizenship fatigue,” Journal of Applied Psychology 100(1) (2015), 56–74.

- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25111252/
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037583
- Multiwave study of 273 employees and peers.
- Relationship between organizational citizenship behavior and citizenship fatigue depended on context.
- Fatigue was more strongly positive where perceived organizational support was low; better team-member exchange and low pressure altered the relationship.
- Citizenship fatigue predicted lower subsequent citizenship behavior.

This is the main empirical boundary for the chapter. It supports the claim that “extra-role” help can cease to be sustainable when the organization pressures people to keep donating it without support.

### Additional recent evidence (optional, not necessary in manuscript)

Chenyang Li and Ahmed Mohammed Sayed Mostafa, “The outcomes of illegitimate tasks at work: A meta-analysis,” Journal of Occupational Health Psychology (2026).

- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42347775/
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1037/ocp0000435
- Meta-analysis, k = 141, N = 65,124, covering attitudinal, well-being, and behavioral outcomes of illegitimate tasks.
- Potentially useful later for the book's exploitation/invisible-work argument.
- Because publication is recent and the concept is broader than helping behavior, avoid overloading Chapter 3 with it unless needed in revision.

Andrea L. Hetrick and Ryan P. Jacobson, “Receiving help at work mitigates the negative consequences of performance pressure,” Journal of Social Psychology 165(1) (2025), 102–118.

- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38170635/
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1080/00224545.2023.2298890
- N = 268 critical-incident design; receiving help weakened the link between performance pressure and exhaustion.
- Could support a later revision if the chapter needs more on the receiver side.

## AI / automation thread

The chapter should keep the AI argument narrow and durable.

Claim:

- Better software can and should absorb many routine “two-minute favors” such as locating information, explaining procedures, translation, troubleshooting, or password recovery.
- Digital-navigation experience shows that the user's underlying obstacle may also involve devices, connectivity, literacy, disability, identity verification, fear of fraud, or repeated institutional failure.
- The useful human contribution today often lies in noticing when a correct answer did not restore progress.

Do not claim that AI cannot eventually detect or resolve these wider constraints. The correct formulation is that useful service is measured by achieved progress rather than answer generation, regardless of whether the helper is human or automated.

## Falsification / counter-evidence notes

- Do not imply every small favor builds trust. Unwanted help can feel controlling, manipulative, or insulting.
- Do not imply favors should be given to create future opportunities. The chapter explicitly rejects transactional networking arithmetic.
- Do not romanticize libraries absorbing failures created by government agencies or other institutions. Their service can be admirable while the upstream transfer of burden remains a design failure.
- Do not imply all digital-navigation interactions are two minutes; many are repeated, complex, and ongoing. The title refers to the class of small help requests and their disproportionate consequences.
- Do not portray digital navigators as social workers, attorneys, benefits adjudicators, or other licensed professionals unless their actual role supports that title.
- Preserve the distinction between helping someone learn and refusing necessary assistance. Capability-building is not a moral requirement to make vulnerable people struggle.
- Do not claim the NDIA corps evaluation proves long-term causal impact.
- Do not reduce citizenship fatigue to “helping causes burnout.” The research shows context and support matter.
- Do not let the chapter's ending become “always teach a person to fish.” Sometimes carrying the immediate burden is the humane response; judgment is central.
