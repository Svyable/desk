# Chapter 3 — Illegitimate tasks and the boundary of helping

## Claim under test

Chapter 3 argues that small acts of help can be valuable without becoming a standing transfer of unwanted work onto the most conscientious person in the room. The relevant boundary is not simply whether a task sits outside a formal job description. A stronger distinction is whether the work is reasonably connected to the role and situation, or whether employees experience it as unnecessary or unreasonable enough to violate legitimate expectations about what should be asked of them.

## Fresh evidence

Chenyang Li and Ahmed Mohammed Sayed Mostafa, “The outcomes of illegitimate tasks at work: A meta-analysis,” *Journal of Occupational Health Psychology* 31(4) (2026), 279–301. Online publication June 25, 2026; August 2026 issue.

Primary/authoritative records:

- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42347775/
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1037/ocp0000435
- University of Leeds accepted-version record: https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/239995/

The meta-analysis synthesizes 141 studies with a combined sample of 65,124 participants. It examines “illegitimate tasks” across two related categories—unreasonable tasks and unnecessary tasks—and assesses associations with attitudinal outcomes, well-being, and behavior. Outcomes considered include job satisfaction, work engagement, turnover intentions, negative affect, burnout, work-family conflict, job performance, innovative behavior, organizational citizenship behavior, counterproductive behavior, and withdrawal.

This is substantially stronger support for Chapter 3’s exploitation boundary than treating one older citizenship-fatigue study as if it carried the whole argument. The larger literature now supports the narrower proposition that work perceived as unreasonable or unnecessary is meaningfully associated with poorer employee outcomes across several domains.

## Counterevidence and limits

The meta-analysis does not establish that every task outside a worker’s normal duties is harmful, exploitative, or illegitimate. “Illegitimate” is a construct tied to employees’ appraisal of whether a task violates reasonable role expectations; it should not be collapsed into “extra work” or “helping.” Some unusual tasks are legitimate responses to emergencies, learning opportunities, temporary team needs, or genuinely shared responsibilities.

The evidence also does not justify a simple causal sentence such as “unreasonable tasks cause burnout.” The included literature spans designs and measures, and the meta-analysis primarily synthesizes relationships across the existing evidence base. Chapter 3 should therefore preserve language such as “is associated with,” “can become,” or “is more likely to” unless a specific causal design supports a stronger claim.

There is also disconfirming nuance in the broader 2026 literature. Some recent work argues that the effects of illegitimate tasks can vary with appraisal and worker characteristics, including situations in which a task may be experienced as a challenge rather than purely as a hindrance. That does not erase the meta-analytic pattern; it is a reason not to write the book as though role boundaries have one universal psychological effect.

## Editorial implication

The chapter’s strongest formulation is not “never do work that is not your job.” That would contradict the book’s own thesis. The more defensible distinction is between voluntary, situational usefulness and a recurring organizational habit of routing unnecessary or unreasonable work toward the people least likely to refuse it.

A later manuscript pass could use the meta-analysis to sharpen the paragraph where repeated favors become hidden job transfer. The useful line of argument is: generosity can build trust, but when an organization repeatedly converts generosity into an expectation, the research category changes. At that point the question is no longer whether helping is good. It is whether the institution has made avoidable or unreasonable work someone else’s unofficial job.
