# Chapter 1 counterevidence — low-promotability work

Chapter 1 argues that noticing unclaimed work can build judgment, trust, and opportunity, but it also warns that “usefulness” can become a way for organizations to export low-status work onto conscientious people. Linda Babcock, Maria P. Recalde, Lise Vesterlund, and Laurie Weingart provide unusually direct evidence for that boundary.

## Primary source

Babcock, Linda; Recalde, Maria P.; Vesterlund, Lise; Weingart, Laurie. “Gender Differences in Accepting and Receiving Requests for Tasks with Low Promotability.” *American Economic Review* 107(3), 2017, pp. 714–747.

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20141734

Author-hosted paper: https://sites.pitt.edu/~vester/aer_promotability.pdf

AEA replication archive: https://www.openicpsr.org/openicpsr/project/113029/version/V1/view

## What the paper shows

The authors study tasks that a group needs completed but that provide less career benefit than other available work, such as committee service or writing a report. In their first laboratory experiment, with equal incentives across participants, women volunteered about 50 percent more often than men. When participants knew they were grouped only with members of their own sex, the volunteering gap disappeared, which argues against treating the result as a fixed difference in preferences.

A later experiment added a person who could direct a request to one group member. In mixed-sex groups, women were asked 39 percent of the time and men 27 percent of the time. When directly asked, women accepted at a higher rate: 76 percent versus 51 percent for men. Both male and female requestors were more likely to direct the low-promotability task toward women.

The mechanism matters for this book. The authors argue that beliefs about who is likely to say yes can shape who gets asked, which can then become self-reinforcing if one group accumulates more low-promotability work and therefore has less time for work that advances careers.

## Boundary and counterargument

This evidence does **not** show that every small favor, invisible task, or piece of articulation work is career-damaging. The experiments deliberately model tasks that are necessary but relatively less promotable, and the authors note that accepting such a task can be individually rational when the alternative is that the group fails to complete it. The study also does not establish that the same gender effect has the same magnitude in every occupation, culture, team size, or repeated-work setting.

The useful distinction is therefore not “extra-role work is bad.” It is whether carrying the unclaimed task increases a person’s context, authority, skill, or leverage — or merely consumes time while preserving an unequal allocation everyone has learned to expect.

## Manuscript implication

Chapter 1 already contains the right moral boundary: an uncarried bag may be a learning opportunity, or it may be work whose real owner has discovered a reliable person who will absorb it for free. This paper gives that warning an empirical distributional dimension. A future prose pass can make clear that selective usefulness is not only about protecting the individual from burnout; it is also about noticing when “helpfulness” has become an allocation system that quietly diverts some people away from promotable work.
