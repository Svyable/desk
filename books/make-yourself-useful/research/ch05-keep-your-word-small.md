# Chapter 5 Research — Keep Your Word Small

## Chapter claim under test

Reliability is not the fantasy of never missing a deadline. It is the practice of making commitments at a scope and certainty the situation can support, keeping the other person informed when reality changes, and closing the loops you actually agreed to own.

The chapter argues that small kept commitments can become evidence of trustworthiness because they reduce uncertainty and the need for others to chase. It should not claim that punctuality is morality, that every delay is an individual failure, or that a person can promise outcomes controlled by other people or systems.

Preferred arc:

1. Amanda Perry's digital-navigation process: agree on a goal and remain in contact until it is met.
2. Reliability as the reduction of open loops and uncertainty.
3. Trust evidence: ability, benevolence, integrity.
4. Promise size: distinguish commitments from predictions and dependencies.
5. Early updates as useful information, not customer-service theater.
6. Broken commitments and recovery.
7. Capacity/system constraints: reliability is not independent of working conditions.
8. Automation should remember and surface commitments without creating fake closure.
9. Return to Perry's outcome-oriented follow-up model.

## Recurring character: Amanda Perry

Urban Libraries Council, “Digital Navigator Profile: Amanda Perry,” February 23, 2021:

https://www.urbanlibraries.org/blog/digital-navigator-profile-amanda-perry

Usable details:

- Perry was an Associate Librarian and Digital Navigator with Salt Lake City Public Library.
- The profile says she had been with the library for about 20 years, beginning as a library aide and progressing to associate librarian.
- She worked in the Marmalade District and was involved in the library's Committee for Digital Inclusion.
- Perry described the Digital Navigator role as similar to a technology case manager.
- Her process: receive intake information, contact the person, explain services, conduct a skills assessment, agree on needs and a goal, find the best resources, and “remain in contact until the goal is met.”
- That final phrase is short enough to quote if desired and is the central factual spine for this chapter.

Caution:

- The ULC profile is an interview/partner publication about a grant-funded program. Treat Perry's description as first-person evidence of her work, not an independent outcome evaluation.
- Do not claim she personally followed every participant until every goal was achieved; she described the intended process.
- Do not use her long tenure as proof of exceptional reliability. The chapter uses her documented process, not tenure, as evidence.

## Salt Lake City Digital Navigators workflow and follow-up

Same 2021 toolkit:

- Program model provided one-to-one support for connectivity, devices, and digital skills.
- Digital Navigators established relationships of trust, identified goals, presented options, formulated plans with clients, and worked individually toward those plans.
- Workflow included intake, skills assessment, exit survey, and follow-up survey.
- An unsuccessful interaction warranted a scheduled follow-up call.
- The follow-up survey was conducted about one month after initial intake.
- Navigators reported repeated interactions with many clients.
- The pilot served 585 community members over 10 months, exceeding an initial target of 450.
- Among respondents to the follow-up survey, 86.67% reported accomplishing their digital-literacy goals.
- 75.12% of recorded interactions lasted at least 15 minutes; 51.24% were recorded as longer than 30 minutes.

Caution:

- The 86.67% figure is among follow-up-survey respondents, not necessarily all 585 participants. Do not present it as a program-wide causal success rate.
- Self-reported goal achievement and confidence are not objective skill measures.
- The toolkit is produced by program partners and should be treated as operational documentation, not independent evaluation.
- The interaction-length data help correct the book title's temptation to romanticize all help as “two-minute” work: actual navigation was often sustained.

2025 NDIA toolkit:

https://www.digitalinclusion.org/resource/2025-dn-toolkit/

Use only to establish that the Digital Navigator model continued to be formalized and updated after the Salt Lake City pilot. The Chapter 5 narrative should remain anchored in the 2020–2021 program Perry actually described.

## Trustworthiness research

Jason A. Colquitt, Brent A. Scott, and Jeffery A. LePine, “Trust, Trustworthiness, and Trust Propensity: A Meta-Analytic Test of Their Unique Relationships With Risk Taking and Job Performance,” Journal of Applied Psychology 92(4) (2007), 909–927.

PubMed:
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17638454/

DOI:
https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.92.4.909

- Meta-analysis of 132 independent samples.
- Distinguishes trust from perceived trustworthiness and trust propensity.
- Uses the influential ability/benevolence/integrity dimensions of trustworthiness.
- Trustworthiness dimensions had strong relationships with trust, and results generalized across leader and coworker referents.

Chapter use:

- Small promises can provide behavioral evidence relevant to ability, benevolence, and integrity.
- A missed commitment can be interpreted differently depending on whether the failure suggests poor estimation/competence, disregard for the other person, or inconsistency between words and behavior.

Caution:

- Do not claim the meta-analysis studied micro-promises, deadline keeping, or email follow-up directly.
- Ability/benevolence/integrity is a conceptual lens for interpreting reliability, not a “three-part reliability framework” to brand in the book.
- Later measurement research notes that people do not always sharply distinguish these dimensions in practice.

Additional synthesis:

“What Drives Employees’ Trust at Work? A Meta Analysis of Antecedents Across Referents and Culture,” Academy of Management Proceedings (2020):
https://journals.aom.org/doi/abs/10.5465/AMBPP.2020.18267abstract

- Meta-analysis of 536 independent samples.
- Reports substantial relationships between trust and ability, benevolence, integrity, leadership, communication, HR practices, and work design.
- Useful as broader support that communication and situational design matter alongside individual character.

## Psychological contract breach

Hao Zhao, Sandy J. Wayne, Brian C. Glibkowski, and Jesus Bravo, “The Impact of Psychological Contract Breach on Work-Related Outcomes: A Meta-Analysis,” Personnel Psychology 60(3) (2007), 647–680.

https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1744-6570.2007.00087.x

- Meta-analysis examining psychological contract breach across eight work outcomes.
- Breach was related to mistrust/violation, job attitudes, citizenship behavior, and in-role performance; actual turnover was the exception in the reported relationships.
- The model treated affective reactions including mistrust as mediators.

Updated evidence:

Rebecca R. Rees, Katrina M. Brownell, and Jill Kickul, “Beyond Exchange and Affect: An Updated Meta-Analytic Review and Regulatory Framework for Psychological Contract Breach,” Journal of Business and Psychology (2026).

https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10869-026-10123-1

- Open-access updated meta-analysis published May 27, 2026.
- Reports 517 samples and 91 antecedents, consequences, and correlates.
- Broadly confirms negative associations between perceived breach and favorable attitudes/social-exchange indicators, with positive relationships to unfavorable outcomes.
- Finds that effects are not uniformly one-directional and emphasizes temporal and regulatory complexity.

Chapter use:

- Only as evidence that perceived unfulfilled obligations can change relationships, not as proof that a missed “send the deck by Thursday” commitment causes the same effects.
- The chapter explicitly notes the scale difference.

Caution:

- Psychological contracts concern perceived reciprocal obligations in employment relationships, often involving organizational promises and expectations. They are not literal task commitments.
- Do not translate correlations from this literature into a quantitative claim about individual reputation.
- The 2026 review emphasizes complexity and temporal directionality; preserve that nuance.

## Delay information and uncertainty

Rouba Ibrahim, “Sharing delay information in service systems: a literature survey,” Queueing Systems 89 (2018), 49–79.

https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11134-018-9577-y

- Open-access survey of research on delay announcements in service systems.
- Broadly, sharing useful wait information can reduce uncertainty and improve customer experience.
- Information also changes customer behavior, which can affect system performance.

Chapter use:

- Supports the narrower intuition that known delay and unknown delay are different experiences.
- The chapter applies this by analogy to proactive status updates at work, while explicitly saying a service queue is not a coworker relationship.

Caution:

- Do not claim delay-announcement research directly measures interpersonal trust among coworkers.
- Accuracy matters; poor or misleading estimates can themselves produce negative reactions.

D. A. Thompson, P. R. Yarnold, D. R. Williams, and S. L. Adams, “Effects of Actual Waiting Time, Perceived Waiting Time, Information Delivery, and Expressive Quality on Patient Satisfaction in the Emergency Department,” Annals of Emergency Medicine 28(6) (1996), 657–665.

PubMed:
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8953956/

- Telephone survey of 1,631 emergency-department patients.
- Satisfaction with information delivery and expressive quality was associated with overall satisfaction.
- Actual waiting time was not predictive of overall satisfaction in that study, while perceived/expected waiting and information mattered.

Caution:

- Do not convert this into “actual delay does not matter.” The study measured satisfaction in one hospital context.
- Do not use patient satisfaction as a synonym for trust.
- The chapter uses the result only to illustrate the burden of uncertainty and the value of information during delay.

## Trust repair after a missed commitment

Peter H. Kim, Donald L. Ferrin, Cecily D. Cooper, and Kurt T. Dirks, “Removing the Shadow of Suspicion: The Effects of Apology Versus Denial for Repairing Competence- Versus Integrity-Based Trust Violations,” Journal of Applied Psychology 89(1) (2004), 104–118.

DOI:
https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.89.1.104

- Two experiments examining apology vs. denial after alleged competence- or integrity-based violations.
- Trust repair differed depending on the type of violation and the response.
- Apology was more effective than denial for competence-related violations under the experimental conditions; integrity allegations behaved differently.

Peter H. Kim, Kurt T. Dirks, Cecily D. Cooper, and Donald L. Ferrin, “When More Blame Is Better Than Less: The Implications of Internal vs. External Attributions for the Repair of Trust After a Competence- vs. Integrity-Based Trust Violation,” Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes 99(1) (2006), 49–65.

https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0749597805000907

- Laboratory experiment with 189 participants (165 in final manipulation-correct sample) using an accountant scenario.
- Shows that attribution and violation type complicate trust repair.

More recent related experiments:

Shuhong Gao and Jinzhe Yan, “Verbal or Written? The Impact of Apology on the Repair of Trust,” Frontiers in Psychology (2022).

https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9083358/

- Three experiments; apology effects varied by whether the violation concerned competence or integrity and by apology form.

Chapter use:

- The practical conclusion should remain modest: “just apologize” is not a complete science of trust repair; the response needs to match what failed, and behavioral remediation matters.
- For ordinary missed commitments, use plain recovery: say what happened, what changed, what happens next, and then behave differently.

Caution:

- Most trust-repair studies use controlled scenarios, often around job applicants/accountants, not naturalistic project delays.
- Do not prescribe denial after integrity violations as a general tactic; denying truthful wrongdoing would be unethical and risky.
- Do not overfit laboratory differences between verbal and written apologies into workplace scripts.

## Capacity, systems, and the moralization of reliability

The chapter needs an explicit structural boundary: a person's ability to keep commitments depends partly on workload, authority, schedule stability, caregiving, transportation, staffing, and organizational design.

Do not use punctuality or availability as a proxy for moral worth.

Cross-reference later research if needed:

- Chapter 3 citizenship-fatigue research (Bolino et al., 2015) for the cost of unsupported extra-role behavior.
- Chapter 4 boundary-spanning research for role stress and overload when dependable people become the human glue.

The Chapter 5 claim is that useful people make more realistic promises from the capacity they actually have and make dependencies visible. It is not that individuals can personally overcome every unstable system through discipline.

## AI / automation thread

Durable claim:

- Software can reduce the clerical burden of reliability by remembering commitments, extracting action items, scheduling follow-ups, surfacing drifting work, and communicating known delays.
- This is good; the book should encourage it rather than romanticize human memory.
- Automation can also create “fake closure”: status messages can be timely while nothing substantive is happening, stale data can produce false “on track” signals, and action extraction can assign the wrong owner.
- Reliability is correspondence between communicated state and actual state, not message frequency.

Do not claim AI cannot manage commitments or understand dependencies. Better systems will increasingly do both.

The enduring human/organizational question is whether the promise is calibrated to authority, evidence, dependencies, and consequences—and who is accountable when those conflict.

## Falsification / counter-evidence notes

- Do not claim reliable people never miss deadlines. The chapter explicitly rejects this.
- Do not turn reliability into “always say yes.” A credible no is often more useful than an optimistic yes.
- Do not recommend systematic sandbagging/underpromising as a manipulation strategy. Accurate expectations are more useful than manufactured delight.
- Do not treat proactive updates as sufficient compensation for bad performance. Communication can reduce uncertainty; it does not make chronic failure acceptable.
- Do not imply every missed commitment is equally diagnostic. A one-off competence error, a capacity problem, an external dependency, and deliberate deception are different failures.
- Do not moralize lateness or equate reliability with privilege-blind punctuality.
- Do not imply the Digital Navigator program proves a causal theory of reliability. It is an operational example of explicit goals and follow-through.
- Do not quote program-produced participant praise as objective evidence of impact.
- Do not claim the psychological-contract literature directly studies micro-commitments between coworkers.
- Do not present trust-repair experiments as universal scripts.
- Do not let the ending become a motivational command. The chapter should earn its final line through accumulated examples and evidence.

## Recurring-character uses later

Amanda Perry remains useful for Chapter 12 if later reporting supports how individual navigator practice became shared training, workflows, standards, and a replicable model.

Her Chapter 5 role should stay narrow: she illustrates follow-through toward a defined goal. Chapter 3 used her to show when repeated favors become an institutional service. Avoid re-explaining the entire Digital Navigator history in both places.
