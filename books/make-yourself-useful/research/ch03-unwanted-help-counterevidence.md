# Chapter 3 counterevidence — when help is not wanted

## Why this note exists

Chapter 3 argues that small acts of help can restore progress, but its falsification notes already warn that help is not automatically welcome. This source makes that boundary empirical rather than rhetorical.

## Schulz et al. (2024), *Stress and Health* — “When help is not wanted: Frustrated needs and poor after-work recovery as consequences of unwanted help at work”

- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1002/smi.3415
- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38699947/
- Study 1 surveyed 279 employees; Study 2 used a two-wave design with 165 employees.
- Across the studies, being offered unwanted help from coworkers or supervisors was associated with frustration of autonomy and competence needs.
- In the time-lagged study, autonomy frustration was linked to greater later rumination and poorer psychological detachment from work.

## Use in revision

This is useful counterevidence against any version of the chapter that implies “more helping” is inherently better. Help can communicate support, but it can also communicate that the recipient is not trusted to handle the task, or remove their ability to choose whether and how assistance is given.

If the manuscript is revised around this evidence, preserve the distinction between *help that is requested or restores agency* and *help imposed on someone who does not want it*. The study does not show that ordinary helping is harmful, nor that unwanted help necessarily causes the later outcomes in every workplace; the authors report associations across a cross-sectional study and a two-wave study, with the longitudinal pathway clearest for autonomy frustration.

## Strongest counterargument

The same chapter already contains evidence that receiving help can reduce strain under performance pressure and that prosocial behavior can benefit both givers and receivers. The point is therefore not to replace a pro-help story with an anti-help story. The stronger claim is that useful help is sensitive to the recipient’s agency, competence, and actual bottleneck.
