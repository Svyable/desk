# Chapter 1 Research Packet — Find the Uncarried Bag

## Primary recurring character: Ana Melendez

Ana Melendez is the lead in Community Health & Supportive Services at El Centro de Servicios Sociales in Lorain, Ohio, and a cancer navigator connected to Case Comprehensive Cancer Center. Her documented work includes referrals, Medicaid and SNAP applications, online portals, interpretation, accompanying patients to appointments, connecting people to local resources, and coordinating with hospitals, churches, food providers, banks, nonprofits, and other navigators.

The opening chapter uses only details Melendez has described publicly through the Alliance for Equity in Cancer Care. Do not add dialogue, inner thoughts, sensory description, or a patient case that is not separately sourced.

## Patient-navigation history

The chapter places Melendez’s work in the longer history of patient navigation. Harold P. Freeman developed the first U.S. patient-navigation program at Harlem Hospital in 1990 after American Cancer Society hearings on cancer and poverty documented financial, communication, medical-system, fear, distrust, and logistical barriers that kept patients from timely diagnosis and treatment.

Use the history carefully. The early Harlem outcomes combined navigation with increased access to screening and other changes; do not attribute every improvement to navigation alone.

Sources:

- Harold P. Freeman and Rian L. Rodriguez, “History and principles of patient navigation,” Cancer 117 (2011): https://acsjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/cncr.26262
- National Academies, “Establishing Effective Patient Navigation Programs in Oncology: Proceedings of a Workshop”: https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25073/chapter/2
- President’s Cancer Panel, “Achieving Equity in Cancer Care: The Need for Navigation and the Promise of Technology”: https://prescancerpanel.cancer.gov/reports-meetings/enhancing-patient-navigation-2024/achieving-equity-cancer-care

## Administrative burden

The chapter uses the concept of patient administrative burden to make the “uncarried bag” legible: patients perform scheduling, information seeking, prior authorization, billing resolution, and insurance-premium tasks, and the burden includes time, effort, and stress as well as money.

Source:

- Michael Anne Kyle et al., “Patient administrative burden in the US health care system,” Health Services Research (2021): https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8522562/

## Claims used in the draft

- Melendez grew up on the same block as El Centro and watched her grandmother, who did not speak or write English, use its services.
- During the COVID period, Melendez left a job she had held for more than eleven years and entered El Centro through a receptionist opening.
- Her reported day can include nutrition classes, appointments, interpretation, online portals, Medicaid and SNAP applications, calls, walk-ins, and referrals.
- Melendez described El Centro as a team of roughly fifteen employees and said the organization served more than ten thousand people in 2025.
- The first patient-navigation program in the United States was established by Harold Freeman at Harlem Hospital in 1990.
- Patient navigation was designed to reduce barriers between an abnormal finding and timely diagnosis/treatment, and later expanded across the care continuum.

## Falsification and caution notes

- Do not frame patients who become lost to follow-up as careless. The chapter’s argument is that fragmented systems assign coordination labor to people already dealing with illness, work, family, transportation, and cost.
- Do not imply that Melendez invented the services or outcomes she coordinates. Her work sits inside a network of coworkers, clinicians, patients, families, agencies, funders, and community organizations.
- Do not treat patient navigation as proof that every organizational gap requires a new human coordinator. The chapter explicitly allows checklists, clearer ownership, better handoffs, automation, or redesign to eliminate recurring coordination work.
- Do not equate usefulness with unlimited unpaid labor. The draft distinguishes learning from an unclaimed problem from becoming the permanent dumping ground for it.
