# Learn the Whole Machine

During Tonya Hicks’s electrical apprenticeship, there were times when the people around her did not seem especially interested in helping her become an electrician.

She has described being sent to clean up or being put in the office trailer while male apprentices worked in the field. In another telling of the story, the office was one of the places she was sent when there was nothing else people wanted to give her.

Hicks did not pretend the treatment was fair. She also did something with the time.

She read the receipts.

The detail is easy to pass over because a receipt looks like the least important object on a construction site. It is evidence of something already bought. The interesting work appears to be outside the trailer, where electricians are pulling wire, bending conduit, terminating panels, reading drawings, testing circuits, and trying not to discover at four in the afternoon that a part needed at eight in the morning is still on somebody else’s truck.

But a receipt contains a different view of the job.

It tells you what the company buys. It tells you what things cost. It gives you supplier names, quantities, timing, sometimes job numbers, sometimes the small purchases that accumulate below anybody’s threshold for concern until they become real money. Read enough receipts and you begin to see that electrical work is also purchasing, estimating, inventory, cash flow, scheduling, vendor relationships, labor allocation, billing, and the constant question of whether the price promised to the customer still bears any relationship to the cost of finishing the work.

Hicks has said that reading those receipts gave her a sense of how the business ran, knowledge she later drew on after starting Power Solutions in Atlanta in 2000.

A person can spend years becoming excellent at one part of a system without ever seeing the system that makes the part possible.

This is not a criticism of specialization. Modern work depends on specialization. You probably do not want an electrician who is also improvising as the structural engineer, the fire inspector, the accountant, and the attorney because she has decided titles are limiting beliefs. Expertise exists for a reason.

The problem appears when specialization becomes blindness.

The best welder in a shop can still lose a day because the material was ordered late. The best analyst can still produce a report nobody can use because the decision-maker needed the answer before the data was complete. The best software engineer can still optimize a service that customers cannot access because the identity system belongs to another team. The best customer-service representative can still spend every afternoon apologizing for a billing rule she has no authority to change and no channel for explaining to the people who do.

Each person can be doing their job correctly while the whole experience remains broken.

This is one of the stranger properties of organizations. They divide work so people can become competent, then create problems that exist mainly in the gaps between those divisions.

The customer does not experience the organization chart.

A patient does not care whether the referral was lost by scheduling, insurance authorization, the specialist’s office, or the electronic record. A homeowner whose power is out does not experience generation, transmission, distribution, dispatch, vegetation management, field operations, customer communications, and billing as separate departments. They experience a dark house. A supplier waiting to be paid does not particularly care that purchasing approved the invoice but finance rejected the coding and the person who can correct it is on vacation.

The institution experiences functions. The person on the other side experiences an outcome.

Learning the whole machine means becoming interested in the distance between those two views.

Hicks’s receipts mattered because they widened the unit of attention. She was training to perform electrical work. The office trailer exposed the economics and logistics surrounding that work. Years later, accounts of her role as the head of Power Solutions describe days spent with potential clients, project stakeholders, other executives, negotiations, supply chains, and schedules. None of that makes the electrical knowledge less important. It reveals what happened when the craft acquired dependencies.

The wire still has to be right. So does the purchase order.

A person who understands both is not necessarily more senior. They are more dangerous to a bad handoff.

Organizational researchers have a dry name for some of this behavior: boundary spanning.

The phrase refers to work that crosses the edges of teams, departments, organizations, or knowledge domains. A boundary spanner searches for information outside the immediate group, coordinates with people elsewhere, represents one group to another, translates, negotiates, and brings resources or knowledge across the line.

The name sounds grander than the activity often looks.

It can be the warehouse employee who knows which sales promises routinely create impossible pick-and-pack deadlines. The payroll specialist who understands enough about timekeeping to see why one department’s overtime entries keep failing. The nurse who knows which transportation problem will make a perfectly written discharge plan useless. The facilities technician who understands why a finance decision about preventive maintenance is about to become an operations problem.

It is often the person who can answer a question nobody officially assigned to them: what happens next?

Research on boundary spanning is useful because it supports the intuition without turning it into a law of nature. A recent meta-analysis covering more than thirty years of team research, 85 primary studies, and 10,848 teams found a positive overall relationship between team boundary management and performance. The gains were stronger for activities such as coordination, representation, and information search than for simply protecting a team from outside interference.

But the same research complicates the romantic version of the boundary-spanning employee. The effects varied with who did the work and what kind of boundary work they performed. Formal leaders were often more effective carriers of some boundary-management activity than ordinary team members. Another meta-analysis of employee boundary-spanning behavior found benefits for performance, innovation, satisfaction, and commitment, but also higher role stress.

The human glue gets sticky.

Anyone who becomes known as the person who understands how three departments fit together eventually discovers the tax attached to that knowledge. Every unusual question starts flowing toward them. Meetings accumulate. They get copied because somebody thinks they may know. They become the unofficial exception handler for systems that were never redesigned because the unofficial exception handler kept saving them.

This book began with the uncarried bag. There is a related danger here: learn enough of the machine and people may begin handing you every bag in it.

So the objective is not to become universally available.

It is to build a better model.

A good model of a workplace is not a list of everybody’s responsibilities. It is a picture of dependencies.

Where does the information originate?

Who changes it?

Who is allowed to approve the next step?

What does that approval unlock?

What happens if it is late?

Who absorbs the failure?

Which team has an incentive to say the problem belongs somewhere else?

What does the customer see while the organization debates ownership?

These questions are less glamorous than strategy. They are often strategy with the lights on.

Consider a support desk after a large company installs a new enterprise software system. From the user’s perspective, a problem might be simple: an order will not process, a report looks wrong, a field is unavailable, an approval disappeared. Inside the organization, solving the problem may require knowledge of several business units and several technical systems at once.

Researchers studying support after an SAP implementation analyzed 23,450 requests handled by 36 information-systems workers for more than 4,500 employees. They looked at two things that sound similar but are not: how central a support worker was in the network, and how much experience that worker had spanning different business units and technical systems.

Both mattered. Boundary-spanning experience mattered more.

That result should not be stretched beyond the setting. Thirty-six support workers in one large organization do not prove that every employee should rotate through every department. But the mechanism is recognizable. Repeated exposure to different domains teaches a person what a problem looks like from more than one side.

A login error may not be an identity problem. It may be a human-resources record that did not update, a permissions rule attached to a cost center, a stale account in another system, or a workflow that was configured around an organizational structure that no longer exists.

The person who knows only the screen sees the error.

The person who knows the machine starts asking where the error could have been produced.

This is why some of the most useful people in an organization seem to have strange careers.

They have worked in customer service and operations. Finance and product. Field service and scheduling. Sales and implementation. Nursing and quality improvement. Engineering and support. Their résumés can look less tidy because their knowledge is arranged around interfaces rather than a single ladder.

There is a cost to that breadth. Depth is easier to signal. A credential, title, publication record, or long tenure in one specialty is legible. Cross-boundary knowledge often appears as a collection of unrelated experiences until a problem arrives that requires all of them.

Then the person who knows what the other department means becomes unusually valuable.

Jody Hoffer Gittell’s work on relational coordination gives a more human description of the same problem. In complex, interdependent work, coordination improves when people share goals, share enough knowledge of one another’s work, and treat one another with mutual respect. Those relationships support communication that is frequent, timely, accurate, and oriented toward solving problems rather than assigning blame.

Notice what the idea does not require.

The nurse does not need to become a pharmacist. The pilot does not need to become a mechanic. The salesperson does not need to become a software engineer. Shared knowledge means understanding enough of the other role to coordinate intelligently with it.

You need to know what information the next person needs, what constraints they are under, what a bad handoff costs them, and what part of your own work becomes their input.

This sounds obvious until you watch a real organization operate.

A department optimizes its response time by closing tickets that require action elsewhere. The dashboard improves. The customer’s problem does not.

A sales team books revenue by promising an implementation date. The quarter improves. Operations inherits the impossible schedule.

A procurement policy reduces unit costs by centralizing purchasing. A field team begins losing hours waiting for inexpensive parts that used to be bought locally.

A hospital improves bed turnover by accelerating discharge. Patients who cannot get medications or transportation reappear through another door.

None of these examples requires an incompetent person. In fact, local competence can make the problem worse because each group gets better at maximizing its own measure.

The whole machine is where local victories meet.

This is one reason the useful person often becomes a translator.

Translation is not merely converting jargon into plain English. It means carrying constraints across boundaries without losing their meaning.

The engineer says the change is not safe to deploy Friday because rollback has not been tested.

The commercial team hears delay.

The translator explains what rollback means in the language of customer risk and weekend support capacity.

Finance says the vendor cannot be paid because the purchase order was created after the invoice.

The vendor hears bureaucracy.

The translator explains the control the policy is trying to preserve, then identifies the exception path instead of forwarding another PDF.

A patient says she cannot make the appointment.

The scheduling system records cancellation.

The navigator asks whether the problem is transportation, work hours, childcare, fear, money, or the fact that the specialist’s office is two buses away.

This is the same skill Ana Melendez uses when she moves among patients, clinics, insurance, benefits, and community services. It is the same reason a cyber navigator at a library can become useful far beyond teaching someone where the browser icon is. The formal task sits inside a chain of consequences.

The person who sees the chain can intervene earlier and explain better.

There is a temptation at this point to turn system knowledge into another career maxim: rotate widely, meet everyone, learn every department.

That advice is too simple.

Boundary spanning consumes time. Context switching is real work. A person can become so broad that nobody trusts them with depth. They can confuse access with expertise. They can insert themselves into decisions that belong to people with more knowledge and more accountability. They can become a walking group chat, continuously routing information while producing very little of their own.

There are also organizations where cross-boundary curiosity is punished. Information is political. Departments protect authority. Managers interpret questions as challenges. A junior employee who starts interviewing other teams may be seen as helpful in one workplace and as bypassing the chain of command in another.

Useful people notice this too.

Learning the machine includes learning where authority actually lives.

The official process might say the department head approves an exception. In practice, the senior coordinator knows which documentation the department head will reject. The org chart may show two teams as peers. In practice, one owns the data the other cannot operate without. A policy may permit local discretion while the incentive system punishes anyone who uses it.

This is not an argument for office intrigue. It is an argument for reality.

A system is made of technical steps, incentives, permissions, habits, relationships, and memory. If you map only the formal workflow, you have drawn the roads and omitted the traffic.

That makes learning the whole machine partly an exercise in observation.

Watch where work waits.

Watch which question is asked repeatedly.

Watch what people check before they trust a number.

Watch who gets called when the documented procedure fails.

Watch what customers have to explain twice.

Watch which spreadsheet exists because the official system cannot represent an exception everybody encounters.

Watch which meeting would not need to exist if two systems shared a field correctly.

The shadow process is often the most honest diagram of the real one.

Hicks read receipts because that was what she had in front of her. A modern employee may have much more material: project channels, dashboards, ticket histories, process documentation, CRM notes, recorded meetings, knowledge bases, audit logs, and increasingly, software capable of searching and summarizing all of it.

This should make whole-machine learning easier.

In some ways it already does.

A person joining a new company no longer has to wait months to encounter every acronym organically. Search can surface old decisions. Language models can summarize long threads, compare documents, explain unfamiliar technical terms, and help someone trace a question across material written by different functions. Systems can connect a customer complaint to a ticket, a contract term, a product change, and a prior incident much faster than a human reading folders one by one.

It would be foolish to build a durable career argument around the idea that machines will never understand organizational context.

They will understand more of it.

The harder problem is that organizational context is not identical to organizational text.

A document can be outdated. Two authoritative systems can disagree. A ticket can record the workaround without recording why the workaround was considered acceptable. A model can synthesize five fragments into a clean answer even when the fragments describe different time periods, business units, or policy regimes.

NIST’s generative-AI risk guidance uses the word confabulation for systems that confidently produce false or internally inconsistent content. The risk becomes more serious in domains that require context or expertise, precisely because a fluent summary can look like understanding.

The answer is not to avoid the tool. It is to ask a more adult question: what would have to be true for this answer to be safe to act on?

Which source is authoritative?

How current is it?

Does the rule apply to this region, customer, product, employee type, or contract?

Is there an exception?

Who owns the decision if the sources conflict?

What is the consequence of being wrong?

These are whole-machine questions.

The value of the person changes as the tools improve. Memorizing where the policy document lives becomes less important. Understanding why the policy exists, how it collides with another process, and when the situation requires an accountable human decision becomes more important.

That may change again. Better systems will capture more tacit knowledge. They will watch workflows, learn patterns, notice repeated exception paths, and make some forms of translation routine. Research is already beginning to examine how generative systems can help organizations integrate knowledge that used to remain trapped in individual experience.

Good. Let the routine translation become routine.

The useful person does not need the machine to stay stupid in order to remain useful.

They need to keep moving toward the part of the work where the model is incomplete, the objective is contested, the exception matters, or someone has to take responsibility for a tradeoff.

That requires breadth without pretending breadth is omniscience.

One practical way to build it is to follow an ordinary piece of work farther than your job requires.

Take an invoice.

Who created the need for the purchase?

Who selected the vendor?

Who approved the price?

Who received the goods?

Who coded the cost?

Who verifies that the invoice matches what arrived?

Who pays it?

What happens to the supplier if payment is late?

What happens to your team if the supplier stops extending credit?

What does the customer price assume about all of this?

Or take a support ticket.

Who reported the problem?

What were they trying to do?

Which system produced the symptom?

Which team owns that system?

What changed recently?

Who has authority to correct the data?

What downstream process already consumed the bad value?

Who needs to know after the immediate issue is fixed?

The point is not to become the person who personally performs every step.

The point is to stop mistaking your step for the process.

There is a career advantage in that shift, but it is not as clean as the usual story about becoming indispensable. People who understand systems often gain access to more interesting problems because they can be trusted with ambiguity. They can sit in meetings between functions and hear what each side is missing. They can notice when a request is impossible before five people spend a week attempting it. They can explain consequences in language a decision-maker can use.

They can also become exhausted.

If every process depends on one person’s undocumented understanding, the organization has not discovered a star. It has discovered a future failure.

The mature form of whole-machine knowledge is therefore not hoarding context. It is making context travel.

Document the exception.

Explain why the field matters.

Introduce the people who should be speaking directly.

Change the form so it asks for the information the next team actually needs.

Put the source next to the metric.

Create the checklist that prevents the same handoff from failing next month.

Teach the new employee how to trace a problem instead of teaching them to call you.

The goal is not to become the only person who knows how the machine works.

It is to become one of the people who makes the machine easier to understand.

Tonya Hicks did not choose the office trailer because she wanted a broader business education. By her account, she was there partly because people were keeping her away from work she had entered the apprenticeship to learn.

That fact should not be polished into a lesson about gratitude. Discrimination is not a leadership-development program.

But the receipts were still there.

She read them.

Years later, the electrician who had been pushed toward the office was running a company whose work required her to think about clients, crews, suppliers, schedules, contracts, and the changing energy systems around all of them.

The receipt had not been the work she wanted. It showed her where the work came from, what it cost, and what had to happen before another electrician ever touched a wire.

That was part of the trade too.
