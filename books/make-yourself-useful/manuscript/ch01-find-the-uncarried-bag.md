# Find the Uncarried Bag

Ana Melendez wakes before her alarm.

The alarm is set for 5:30. She has told an interviewer that she is usually awake by four. There are three children to get moving, medicine to remember for her oldest daughter, a lunch to pack for the youngest, a school drop-off before eight, and a work calendar to check before the day has properly begun. Coffee comes first. In the car she may listen to Spanish music or talk with a friend. Then she gets to El Centro de Servicios Sociales in Lorain, Ohio, and the work starts widening.

Melendez is the lead in El Centro’s Community Health and Supportive Services department and also works as a cancer navigator through Case Comprehensive Cancer Center. A morning can include a nutrition class, interpretation at a medical appointment, an online patient portal that somebody cannot get into, a Medicaid application, a SNAP application, walk-ins, referrals, phone calls, and whatever arrived after the calendar was made. On Thursdays there may be lunch with seniors at El Centro. Someone makes café. There is pan con mantequilla. The official systems surrounding all of this have names and rules and portals and phone trees. The work of getting a particular human being through them is less tidy.

That is the part worth noticing.

A hospital can diagnose cancer. A state can run Medicaid. An insurer can adjudicate a claim. A clinic can schedule an appointment. A food bank can distribute groceries. Each institution can do exactly what its own process says it does and still leave a person stranded between them. The stranded person may have a suspicious test result but no idea which number to call next. She may have insurance but not understand why the portal says a referral is missing. He may qualify for help but lack a document, a ride, a translator, a password, a weekday morning off, or enough confidence that the next call will not end like the previous three.

The problem, from inside any single organization, can look small. From the patient’s side, the categories collapse into a single practical problem: getting care.

Melendez did not enter El Centro as a navigator. During the pandemic she left a job she had held for more than eleven years and applied for a receptionist opening after an aunt encouraged her to try. She had grown up on the same block as El Centro. Her grandmother, who did not speak or write English, had used services from the department where Melendez now works. The move from reception to community health and cancer navigation makes a useful career story, but the interesting part is not a tidy climb up an organizational chart. Reception is one of the places where an organization discovers what its chart has forgotten.

People call reception when they do not know whom else to call. They walk to the front desk when the website has failed them. They describe problems in the language of their own lives rather than in the categories an institution uses internally. The person behind the desk hears the same confusions before the people designing the process do. A receptionist at a clinic learns which department never answers. A hotel desk clerk learns which room key fails in cold weather. A school secretary learns which form parents routinely complete wrong. An office administrator knows which executive has a habit of sending a file over without realizing that three people have to repair it first.

These are not glamorous vantage points. They are unusually informative ones.

Every workplace has work that is assigned, measured, reviewed, and named. It also has another layer of work that appears only when reality refuses to follow the plan. Somebody notices that the delivery arrived but nobody has a pallet jack. Somebody remembers that the customer who is deaf needs a different way to receive the update. Somebody catches the invoice with the old bank details. Somebody knows that the new employee cannot enter the building on Monday because security has not created the badge. Somebody sees that two departments are each waiting for the other to act.

This book will call those problems uncarried bags.

The phrase is deliberately ordinary. At an airport, a bag sitting alone eventually attracts attention because everybody understands that luggage is supposed to belong to someone and to be going somewhere. Work is often less obvious. A necessary task can sit between roles for months because each person can reasonably explain why it is not quite theirs. Nobody has failed at the task they were assigned. The larger thing still fails.

Organizational researchers have been studying versions of this problem for decades. The sociologist Anselm Strauss used the term *articulation work* for the coordination required to keep interdependent work aligned when real conditions do not match the clean model. Susan Leigh Star and Strauss later wrote about the ecology of visible and invisible work: what organizations count as work depends partly on which indicators they choose to see. The completed surgery is visible. The phone calls that got the patient to the operating room can disappear. The shipped product is visible. The person who noticed the address discrepancy at 4:47 p.m. is not. The presentation is visible. The analyst who spent Thursday afternoon figuring out that two teams use the same word to mean different things may not appear anywhere in the project plan.

It is tempting to romanticize this hidden layer because almost everyone can remember a colleague who quietly held a place together. That would be a mistake. Invisible work can be a source of learning and trust, but it is also where institutions hide costs. When one conscientious person repeatedly compensates for a broken process, management may never feel the break. What looks like dedication from above can feel like exhaustion from below.

Usefulness therefore begins with a distinction.

There is work that is unclaimed because nobody has noticed it yet, and doing it can teach you the system. There is work that remains unclaimed because the organization has learned that a reliable person will absorb it for free. Those are not the same career opportunity.

Health care makes the distinction easy to see because its handoffs are consequential and unusually well studied. In 2021, Michael Anne Kyle and Austin Frakt published a study of administrative work performed by insured, nonelderly adults in the United States. They asked about five common tasks: scheduling, getting information, prior authorization, billing problems, and premium problems. Seventy-three percent of respondents had performed at least one of the tasks in the previous year. Roughly one in four respondents reported delaying or forgoing care because of administrative work.

Those are insured adults. The problem was not simply that people lacked coverage. They were doing work required to turn coverage into care.

Public-administration scholars Donald Moynihan, Pamela Herd, and Hope Harvey have a useful vocabulary for this. Administrative burdens can impose learning costs, compliance costs, and psychological costs. First you have to discover what exists and what is required. Then you have to provide the forms, documents, calls, appointments, proofs, signatures, passwords, or recertifications. While doing so, you may also absorb confusion, stigma, anxiety, and the particular frustration of knowing that the thing you need is theoretically available if only you can successfully navigate the procedure for obtaining it.

The categories sound bureaucratic. The experience is not. It is calling during a lunch break and spending forty minutes on hold. It is driving across town because a form cannot be accepted electronically. It is hearing that the document you brought is not the document the office meant. It is trying to remember a password while a child needs dinner. It is deciding whether to spend another morning attempting to resolve a bill or simply put the bill on a credit card and hope.

A good system tries to reduce these costs. A bad one often exports them.

Patient navigation grew out of exactly this kind of exported burden.

In 1990, surgeon Harold Freeman established what is widely described as the first patient-navigation program at Harlem Hospital Center in New York. The program followed national hearings on cancer among poor Americans and Freeman’s own experience treating women who were arriving with advanced breast cancer. The barriers were not mysterious. Money mattered. So did communication, information, fear, distrust, and the difficulty of moving through a fragmented medical system while sick.

The early Harlem results became famous. In an earlier cohort of poor patients treated at Harlem Hospital, five-year breast-cancer survival had been 39 percent and only 6 percent of patients had stage I disease. In a later period, after free or low-cost screening and patient navigation had been introduced, a much larger share of cancers were found early and five-year survival was reported at 70 percent.

This is the kind of statistic that self-help books like because it appears to prove a clean lesson. It does not.

The Harlem intervention combined navigation with improved access to screening, and the cohorts came from different time periods in a disease whose treatment was also changing. Freeman himself described the improvement as the result of multiple factors. Later, better-controlled studies gave navigation a more precise claim. In a multisite Patient Navigation Research Program involving more than ten thousand people with abnormal screening results and more than two thousand people with cancer or precancer diagnoses, navigation showed no advantage in the first ninety days but a moderate benefit afterward in diagnostic resolution and treatment initiation, with the greatest benefits in settings where ordinary follow-up produced the longest delays. A 2023 overview of systematic reviews concluded that navigation improves participation in cancer screening and can reduce the time from screening to diagnosis and from diagnosis to treatment.

The evidence is less cinematic than a claim that one navigator saved lives. It is more useful.

Navigation seems to matter most where the path is long enough, fragmented enough, or unfamiliar enough that people can fall out of it.

That is also why Melendez’s day contains such an odd mixture of tasks. She is not merely moving cancer patients from oncologist A to oncologist B. The obstacle to an appointment may be language. It may be transportation. It may be money. It may be that the person has not eaten properly in two days, cannot interpret a benefits letter, or is frightened enough by the possible diagnosis that every unfamiliar step feels like evidence of something worse. A neat job description wants to separate medical, financial, social, administrative, and emotional problems. The person living through them does not experience the borders so cleanly.

This is what useful people learn to see.

They notice the difference between the formal process and the actual journey.

That difference exists almost everywhere. Consider a restaurant. On paper, a server takes an order, a cook prepares food, and a runner moves plates. In a functioning dining room, somebody also notices that table twelve has been waiting too long, that the kitchen did not see an allergy note, that a child dropped a fork, that the bar is buried, and that the couple by the window has clearly finished but has not received a check. Good service is full of tiny acts of coordination whose value is obvious to the customer and difficult to capture in a task list.

A construction site has the same problem with different consequences. The electrical crew can arrive exactly when scheduled and still lose a day if the wall is not ready, the lift is elsewhere, the specified material has not arrived, or the previous trade interpreted the drawings differently. A useful apprentice eventually stops seeing only the task in front of her and starts noticing the conditions that make the next task possible.

Software teams dress the same problem in cleaner clothes. A ticket says an export button needs to be added. The button may take an hour. The work that matters may be deciding what data the export is legally allowed to contain, what format finance needs, why customers are exporting in the first place, or which service quietly times out when the file grows past a certain size. The code is the visible object. The surrounding judgment is the work that prevents the object from becoming tomorrow’s problem.

Technology tends to make this boundary more important, not less.

A paper process can be sloppy in a way a computer form cannot. A clerk can look at a document, understand that *St.* means *Street* in one field and *Saint* in another, and keep going. Software needs a rule. Databases need categories. Workflows need states. The more an organization automates its normal path, the more conspicuous the abnormal path becomes.

This is not an argument against automation. Quite the opposite. Good automation removes repeated work that no human being needs to keep doing. But every automated system also draws a boundary around what its designers expected. The cases outside that boundary do not disappear. They arrive as exceptions, escalations, overrides, manual reviews, support tickets, phone calls, and people standing at counters because they were sent there by the website.

The interesting career question is not whether machines will take tasks. They will, and they have for a long time. It is what happens to the work at the edge of the task once the center is cheap.

A scheduling system can display available appointments. It may not know that the patient cannot drive after the procedure, that her daughter works nights, that the interpreter is only available on Tuesdays, and that the clinic listed as twenty minutes away by car is two bus transfers from her house. A model can draft a benefits explanation. Somebody still has to notice that the explanation is technically accurate and practically useless to the person who received it. A workflow can route a referral. Somebody has to notice when the referral has technically arrived and functionally vanished.

As tools improve, some of these edge cases will move into the automated center. That is normal. The useful person does not defend a particular manual task as sacred. They keep moving toward the part of the problem where context is still thin, responsibility is ambiguous, and consequences are real.

That is one reason learning the tool is incomplete career advice. Tools matter. But the person who only learns the interface can become expert in a layer that changes. The person who learns what the system is trying to accomplish, where people get stuck, which exceptions matter, and who bears the cost of a bad handoff retains something more durable.

Melendez’s job contains technology everywhere: patient portals, benefits systems, scheduling, electronic records, communication tools. None of those systems is the enemy. In many cases they make possible a scale of coordination that paper could not. Her usefulness appears in the remaining distance between a system that has processed a case and a person who has actually made progress.

That distance is where many careers are built.

None of this requires heroic initiative. In fact, the heroic version is usually a warning sign.

Organizations sometimes celebrate the employee who stays until midnight to fix a recurring failure that should have been eliminated six months earlier. Hospitals praise nurses who improvise around missing supplies. Families praise daughters who become unpaid case managers for several institutions at once. Companies praise operators who keep a broken process alive through memory and favors. The gratitude can be sincere. The arrangement can still be bad.

The aim is not to become the person who catches every falling object forever.

The aim is to become good at seeing what is falling, understand why, and decide what kind of response the situation deserves.

Sometimes the response is simply to pick it up.

A customer emails the wrong person and you know the right one. Forwarding the message with two sentences of context takes forty seconds and saves a day. A new colleague is heading into a meeting without the document everyone else assumes she has read. Sending it is useful. A parent at a school event cannot find the correct entrance. Walking them there does not require a committee on signage.

These acts are small enough that measuring them would be absurd. They matter because they reduce friction at the moment friction appears.

Sometimes the response is to pick it up and leave a marker.

If the third new hire asks where to find the same template, the useful act is no longer merely sending the template. Put the link where the next person will look. If customers repeatedly misunderstand a line on an invoice, rewrite the line. If every Friday somebody manually combines the same three files, document the steps, then ask whether the files can be combined automatically.

The smallest structural repair is often more valuable than the largest display of effort.

And sometimes the response is to refuse the bag.

If a manager repeatedly hands you work because you are the person least likely to object, the problem is not unclaimed. It has an owner who has discovered a cheaper place to put it. If you are interpreting at medical appointments because the institution has not provided required language access, quietly absorbing the job may protect one person today while helping the institution avoid fixing the underlying obligation. If you are the only person who knows how a critical system works and nobody will give you time to document it, your indispensability may be less a compliment than a liability that the organization has transferred onto your calendar.

Usefulness without judgment becomes availability.

This is one reason the best useful people are often selective in ways outsiders do not immediately understand. They are not merely generous with effort. They develop a sense for leverage. They can tell when five minutes now prevents five hours later. They can tell when a recurring question is evidence of a design problem. They know which exception is genuinely exceptional and which exception is the normal process wearing a fake moustache.

That last category is everywhere.

Listen to the apologies in an organization: repeated apologies about the portal being strange, finance needing another day, instructions being stale, an online submission that still requires a phone call, or two teams using incompatible systems. An apology repeated often enough is operational data.

Watch what gets carried in people’s private tools. The spreadsheet that one person updates because the official dashboard is late. The notebook with vendor phone numbers. The email folder labeled *pending*. The chat thread everyone searches because the documented procedure is wrong. The handwritten sign taped over the machine’s printed instructions. These are not automatically signs of dysfunction; every real workplace develops local knowledge. But they are clues. They show where people have built unofficial bridges over gaps in the formal system.

Notice who gets interrupted.

The person everybody asks may be generous, or simply experienced. They may also be standing at a junction the organization has never acknowledged. Receptionists, executive assistants, dispatchers, nurses, foremen, customer-service representatives, office managers, librarians, and veteran operators often know more about the movement of work than their rank suggests because the movement passes through them.

Melendez’s career began at one of those junctions.

The receptionist opening at El Centro was not a lesser version of the work she does now. It was a place to learn what people actually brought through the door. Over time she gained more context, relationships, and responsibility. The current job lets her work across institutions that no single patient designed and no single professional fully controls. She can interpret not just language but process: which clinic, which resource, which next step, which person.

That knowledge compounds differently from technical skill.

A person who learns one task becomes faster at the task. A person who learns how tasks connect becomes useful in situations nobody has written down yet.

This is why the uncarried bag is a better starting point than the usual advice to find your passion or build your personal brand. Passion is private. Brands are impressions. An unclaimed problem is observable. You can see whether the confusion keeps happening. You can see whether somebody is waiting. You can see whether the same handoff fails every week. You can try a repair and find out whether life gets easier for the person on the other side.

The feedback is unusually honest.

The danger is equally observable. If every solved problem simply earns you another person’s neglected work, you are not building freedom. You are building a larger cage.

The question, then, is not “How can I do more than my job?”

A better question is: “What is trying to happen here, and what is preventing it?”

That change in wording matters. It pulls attention away from performance and toward consequence. The goal of the clinic is not to process referrals. It is to get a person to appropriate care. The goal of the construction crew is not to complete isolated tasks. It is to finish a safe building. The goal of a report is not to exist. It is to help somebody make a decision. The goal of a meeting is not to fill forty-five minutes. It is presumably to change what somebody knows, decides, or does.

Once the result becomes visible, so do the gaps.

You do not need authority to notice them. You do not always need permission to make the smallest one better. You do need judgment about when a local repair is enough, when the pattern should be made visible, and when the burden belongs back with the system that created it.

Melendez’s work is a useful first case because none of it sounds like the mythology of career success. There is no dramatic product launch. No viral post. No conference stage. There are portals, applications, referrals, calls, interpretation, food assistance, medical fear, and a team of roughly fifteen people that, by El Centro’s account, served more than ten thousand individuals in 2025. The scale appears only after you look closely at the small work.

At the end of her day, Melendez has said, she often drives home without music. The morning commute may have Spanish songs or a call with a friend. The trip back is quiet.

There is something appropriately unglamorous about that detail. Work that crosses other people’s emergencies does not become profound merely because a book notices it. It remains work. It can be meaningful and tiring at the same time.

The lesson is not that everyone should become a navigator.

It is that every complicated human system eventually produces navigation work, whether it admits this or not. When the process breaks into pieces, somebody has to carry context across the seams.

Sometimes that person has a title for it.

Sometimes they are just the first one who noticed the bag.
