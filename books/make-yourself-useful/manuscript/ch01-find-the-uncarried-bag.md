# Find the Uncarried Bag

Ana Melendez usually wakes before her alarm.

She sets it for 5:30 in the morning, but often she is up around four. There are three children to get moving, including an older daughter who needs medication for 22q deletion syndrome. There is lunch to pack for the youngest. Before leaving the house, Melendez checks her work calendar. By 7:50 she has dropped her youngest at school and is headed toward El Centro de Servicios Sociales in Lorain, Ohio.

The calendar is useful, but it does not tell her what the day will actually contain.

Melendez leads El Centro's Community Health and Supportive Services department and also works as a cancer navigator through Case Comprehensive Cancer Center. A morning might include a nutrition class, an online benefits portal, a Medicaid or SNAP application, a walk-in client, a referral from a clinic, an appointment that needs interpretation, or a patient who needs someone to accompany them because the system they are entering was not designed around the way they speak, travel, work, understand insurance, or absorb bad news. She checks in with her team when she arrives. Then the phone, email, voicemail, scheduled appointments, unscheduled needs, and human complications begin arranging the day for her.

None of this is unusual enough to make national news. That is partly the point.

Melendez grew up on the same block as El Centro. Her grandmother, who did not speak or write English, had used its services. During the COVID period, Melendez left a job she had held for more than eleven years and, encouraged by an aunt, applied for an opening at El Centro. The opening was for a receptionist.

A receptionist is supposed to answer the phone, greet people, route requests, make appointments, and keep the front door of an organization from becoming a pileup. Those tasks matter. But the front desk also has a peculiar vantage point. It is where the organization meets the parts of life that do not fit neatly into the organization chart.

A person does not walk into a clinic as an insurance problem, a transportation problem, a language problem, a childcare problem, a digital-literacy problem, or a medical problem. They walk in as one person carrying several of those things at once. Inside the institution, the problems are separated again. Billing has one piece. The clinician has another. A social-service agency may have another. An insurer has another. A pharmacy has another. Somewhere a portal has a password the patient cannot remember. Somewhere else an eligibility rule has changed. One office closes before another calls back.

The patient experiences one continuous problem. The system experiences six separate departments.

That difference is where a great deal of useful work hides.

When Melendez describes patient navigation, she does not describe a grand theory. She describes getting people through the next barrier. She connects patients to care teams and local resources. She helps Spanish-speaking clients understand a health system that largely operates in English. She helps people who are uninsured or undocumented find services they can use. She sometimes accompanies patients to appointments when the absence of interpretation or transportation would otherwise create another delay. At El Centro, if her team cannot provide a service directly, their practice is to connect the person to an organization that can.

The work is easy to underestimate because almost every individual act sounds small. Make a call. Explain a form. Translate a piece of information. Find the right office. Check whether transportation is covered. Help somebody into a portal. Follow up after an appointment. None of these acts resembles surgery, engineering, litigation, or any of the other forms of expertise that come with an obvious claim to importance.

But remove enough of them and the expert work never reaches the person who needs it.

The first patient-navigation program in the United States grew out of a similar observation. In Harlem in the late 1980s, surgeon Harold Freeman was treating women whose breast cancers were often advanced by the time they reached him. Screening alone did not solve the problem. Even when women received an abnormal finding, some disappeared between that finding and diagnosis or treatment. The medical system could identify a danger without successfully carrying the patient across the distance to care.

In 1989, while serving as president of the American Cancer Society, Freeman helped lead hearings in several American cities about what happened to poor people with cancer. The resulting picture included financial barriers, communication problems, difficulty navigating the medical system, fear, distrust, transportation trouble, and the ordinary logistical strain of trying to remain employed and keep a family functioning while sick. In 1990, Freeman started a navigation program at Harlem Hospital. Lay navigators helped patients get from an abnormal finding through diagnosis and treatment by dealing with the obstacles in between.

The idea was simple enough to sound almost obvious after somebody had done it: if the system keeps losing people in the gaps, put a person in the gaps.

That sentence contains much of what this book means by usefulness.

Every organization has work that is necessary but only partially owned. It lives between job descriptions, departments, software systems, vendors, shifts, and assumptions. Everybody can see a piece of it. Nobody is quite responsible for the whole thing. When it fails, the failure is often described as if it appeared from nowhere.

A customer has called three times, but each call reached a different representative. A new employee has a laptop but no permission to the folder where the actual work lives. A construction crew arrives and discovers the material for the next stage was never ordered because one person thought procurement owned it and procurement thought the project manager had already handled it. A school sends a family to a website that requires documents the family does not know how to obtain. A report is technically complete, except three different teams use the same term to mean three different things. A restaurant has enough employees on paper, but the schedule is posted too late for two of them to arrange childcare. None of these problems belongs entirely to the person who first encounters it. That is why it is tempting to pass it along.

The uncarried bag is the thing everyone is stepping around.

Sometimes it is visible because people keep apologizing for it. Sorry, our system does that. Sorry, you have to call them. Sorry, I know you already explained this. Sorry, that team owns the next step. Sorry, we need you to fill it out again. Repeated apologies are often a map of institutional design. They point toward the place where the process makes sense from inside the organization and stops making sense to the person trying to use it.

Sometimes the signal is repetition. The same question reaches the front desk every day. The same spreadsheet has to be repaired every Friday. The same approval arrives late. The same client sends the same missing information because nobody has changed the instructions. The same senior employee quietly catches the same error before it becomes visible to anybody else.

Sometimes the signal is motion without progress. A request moves through email, chat, a ticketing system, a meeting, and another email while the underlying problem remains untouched. People are busy. The work is not moving.

And sometimes the signal is a sentence: I thought they were handling that.

That sentence is worth developing an ear for.

Most people are trained to protect the boundaries of their assigned work. There are good reasons for that. Clear responsibility matters. Expertise matters. Nobody wants an electrician improvising medical advice or a receptionist authorizing a financial transaction they do not understand. Organizations become dangerous when everyone assumes goodwill is a substitute for competence.

Usefulness is not boundarylessness.

It is attention to the boundary.

A useful person notices when the handoff itself is the problem. They do not automatically take permanent ownership of everything that falls between roles. They first ask a more practical question: what is trying to happen here, and what is stopping it?

That question is different from, what am I supposed to do?

The second question is about compliance with a role. The first is about the result the role exists to help produce.

This distinction is small enough to miss and large enough to alter a career.

Consider what happened to Melendez's own work. She entered El Centro as a receptionist. Her value did not grow because answering the phone became beneath her. It grew because the front desk exposed her to the shape of the problems arriving through it. She could see what people did not understand, what language they needed, what resource they lacked, what agency they had already tried, what part of the health system frightened them, and which barriers kept returning. The role gave her a position from which to see the machine from the user's side.

The useful move was not to declare the machine broken. Large systems are always broken in some way, and saying so requires almost no skill. The useful move was to become better at getting a real person through it.

This is why the smallest solution matters.

People often notice an unowned problem and immediately promote themselves into reformer. The intake process is confusing, so they design a new intake platform. The team communicates badly, so they propose a new operating model. Customers ask repetitive questions, so somebody starts a six-month knowledge-management project. The instinct is understandable. Once you see the pattern, the pattern feels like the problem.

But the pattern is still an inference. The fastest way to learn whether you understand it is to help one person through the next step.

Call the patient back. Rewrite the confusing paragraph. Put the three documents in the order they are actually needed. Walk through the portal with the person who cannot use it. Ask the warehouse what arrives too late. Sit beside the employee who repairs the spreadsheet. Follow the returned product from the counter to the refund. Watch where the handoff stops.

Solve the smallest version before naming the revolution.

Small solutions have an advantage large plans do not: they produce information. If a plain-language checklist prevents three repeated questions, you have learned something about the instructions. If it does not, you have learned that the confusion is somewhere else. If a reminder call changes whether patients show up, the issue was not simply a lack of motivation. If the reminder call makes no difference because transportation is the real constraint, now you know what to work on next.

Usefulness is empirical in that sense. It prefers contact with the problem over a theory of the problem.

Researchers who have studied the administrative burden patients carry in American health care have described work that includes scheduling, finding information, obtaining prior authorization, resolving billing disputes, and fixing insurance-premium problems. The burden is not only financial. It is time, effort, stress, uncertainty, and the possibility that a person delays or abandons care because the work required to obtain care becomes one burden too many.

This administrative work is real work, but most of it does not appear on the patient's job description because the patient does not have one. The system has silently assigned them a coordination role.

The same thing happens outside health care. Customers become unpaid integrators of incompatible service departments. Employees become human middleware between software systems that do not share information. Parents become case managers for schools, insurers, specialists, and public agencies. Small-business owners become part-time compliance officers, IT support, collections departments, and logistics coordinators because no one else in the business exists to hold those functions separately.

One way to find meaningful work is to look for the unpaid coordinator.

Who is currently carrying the complexity created by everybody else?

Sometimes the answer is a customer. Sometimes it is the least senior employee. Sometimes it is an executive assistant who knows which promises in the meeting were real and which were ceremonial. Sometimes it is the experienced technician everybody calls before touching the official help desk. Sometimes it is a parent with a folder of forms. Sometimes it is a nurse who has learned which office will actually return a call. Sometimes it is the founder, still quietly doing work the company claims to have delegated years ago.

If you can reduce that person's coordination burden, you are usually close to something valuable.

This is not the same as volunteering for every loose end.

There is a version of "make yourself useful" that becomes an instruction to absorb everybody else's disorder. The reliable employee gets more work because the unreliable employee cannot be trusted. The person who knows how the process works is never allowed to stop doing it. The colleague who is kind gets turned into the emotional support desk for the entire team. Someone becomes indispensable in the least liberating meaning of the word: the organization cannot function without them, but it also will not give them more authority, more pay, more help, or room to leave.

That is not the destination of this book.

Carrying an unclaimed problem is useful when it teaches you something, helps someone through a real barrier, exposes a recurring failure, earns trust appropriate to the value created, or gives you enough understanding to improve the system. Carrying it forever because everyone else has learned you will is different. The point is not to become the floor onto which every unowned task falls.

A healthy progression looks more like this: notice the problem, handle the immediate case, understand why it repeats, make the pattern visible, then help decide where the responsibility should live.

Sometimes it should live with you. A new responsibility can be the beginning of a larger role. Sometimes it belongs in another team. Sometimes the answer is a checklist, a clearer form, a better handoff, a different schedule, a changed permission, a small piece of automation, or simply a name next to a task that previously had none. And sometimes the answer is to stop doing work that an organization has refused to recognize after benefiting from it for years.

The difference between usefulness and exploitation often appears after the first act of help. Does the system learn, or does it merely learn to use you?

That question will return later in this book. For now, it is enough to keep usefulness tied to agency. You pick up the bag in order to move something important forward and learn what it weighs. You do not promise to carry every bag forever.

This is also where technology complicates the picture in useful ways.

Software can eliminate some uncarried work. A well-designed form can prevent a missing document. A shared system can stop customers from repeating their story to four departments. Automation can route routine requests, translate basic information, summarize a file, flag a missing field, and remind someone about an appointment. Modern AI systems can perform some of these tasks with surprising fluency.

That makes the remaining boundary work more important, not less.

The difficult cases are rarely difficult because nobody can generate another paragraph of instructions. They are difficult because somebody has to decide which instruction applies, whether the person understood it, whether the rule conflicts with their circumstances, whether the missing information is actually missing, whether an exception is justified, whether the next office will accept the answer, and who is responsible if the handoff fails.

A machine can make a process more efficient while leaving the burden untouched. It can also move the burden somewhere less visible.

If a benefits portal becomes faster for the agency but harder for the applicant, the work did not disappear. It was reassigned. If an automated support system closes tickets more quickly while customers open three new ones to get the original problem solved, the metric improved and the situation did not. If an AI assistant produces a plausible answer that a frightened patient cannot act on, production happened without usefulness.

The useful person keeps asking where the burden landed.

Melendez's work makes that question concrete. A portal can contain the right information and still be unusable to the person standing in front of it. A transportation benefit can exist and still fail to move somebody who does not know it exists. A specialist can be excellent and still be effectively unavailable to a patient who cannot understand the referral. A clinic can complete its part of the process while the person goes home with no idea what happens next.

Organizations tend to measure completed actions. People live completed journeys.

That is why useful people often become translators even when translation is not in their title. They translate a specialist's language into a customer's decision. They translate an executive's intention into a sequence of work. They translate a field worker's complaint into something an engineer can fix. They translate a policy into the actual Tuesday afternoon when a person has to comply with it.

This translation work creates trust because it reduces the number of times someone has to start over.

Starting over is one of the hidden costs of a badly joined system. Explain the problem again. Find the account number again. Retell the medical history. Recreate the spreadsheet. Reconfirm the decision. Send the attachment one more time. Every restart tells the person on the outside that the organization is made of pieces that do not remember one another.

The useful person remembers enough of the thread to keep it moving.

Melendez works in an organization of roughly fifteen employees, yet El Centro reported serving more than ten thousand people in 2025. She credits the scale partly to partnerships: hospitals, churches, banks, food providers, nonprofits, and other navigators. That matters because the lesson is not that one unusually committed person should personally solve ten thousand problems. The lesson is almost the opposite. Useful people learn where capability already exists and become good at connecting it to the person who needs it.

The bag does not always need a stronger carrier. Sometimes it needs a shorter route.

This is one reason the people who become trusted inside organizations are not always the ones with the most impressive expertise in isolation. Expertise is valuable, but work happens in systems. A person who can see the dependency between two specialists can become more useful than either specialist expects. A person who notices that the problem is not the medical decision but the ride to the appointment can change an outcome without knowing anything more about oncology. A person who realizes the crew cannot start tomorrow because today's material order is incomplete can save hours without being the fastest craft worker on the site.

Useful people develop a kind of peripheral vision.

They notice what is adjacent to the task. What must be true before this can work? What happens immediately after I finish? Who has to understand this next? What information are they missing? Where is this likely to stall? Who will absorb the consequence if it does?

You do not need permission to begin asking those questions.

That is different from acting without permission. Authority still matters. A junior employee should not quietly make decisions that belong to somebody else simply because the official decision-maker is slow. But seeing the missing decision, preparing the information needed for it, and bringing it to the right person is useful before any title changes.

This is the first quiet advantage in the book: attention travels ahead of authority.

You can notice before you are senior. You can listen before you are an expert. You can close a small loop before someone gives you a larger one. You can make the next person's work easier without announcing a leadership philosophy.

Over time, people remember who reduces friction without creating new drama around the reduction. They remember who comes back with the missing detail. They remember who does not make them explain the same thing twice. They remember who sees the problem behind the request.

That memory is the beginning of reputation.

It would be easy to turn this into a rule about going above and beyond. That phrase has been damaged by overuse and by employers who use it to make understaffing sound like character development. The useful standard is more precise.

Do not ask how much extra work you can absorb. Ask what important burden is currently unowned.

Then get close enough to understand it.

If the same person is confused every week, sit with the confusion. If the same handoff breaks every month, follow it. If the same apology keeps appearing in customer emails, trace what produces it. If a colleague always stays late to perform a task nobody mentions in meetings, find out what would happen if they stopped. If a process works only because one person remembers ten exceptions, those exceptions are part of the process whether the manual admits it or not.

The uncarried bag is rarely hidden. More often, people have simply grown accustomed to walking around it.

Melendez's work is valuable because she does not walk around the person holding it.

She began at a front desk in an organization her own grandmother had once needed. From there she learned the routes between people and services well enough to help others cross them. The work widened: from receptionist to community-health lead, from routing calls to navigating cancer care, from knowing one office to maintaining relationships across a network of institutions. None of that requires pretending she alone created the outcomes. The clinicians, patients, families, social workers, hospitals, food banks, insurers, coworkers, and community organizations all carry pieces of the result.

Her particular usefulness is in seeing when the pieces have not yet become a path.

That is a skill available in almost any kind of work.

Find the thing everyone needs and nobody quite owns. Do not romanticize it. Do not immediately build a department around it. Pick up the smallest part that will tell you something. Carry it far enough to understand why it was on the floor. Make the next step easier for the next person.

Then pay attention to what happens.
