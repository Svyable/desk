# Become Fluent in Other People's Problems

In July 2014, Jannette Navarro generally knew only three days of her working future.

She was twenty-two, a Starbucks barista in San Diego, earning nine dollars an hour. Her son, Gavin, was four. The job mattered to her. She had worked at a Dollar Tree and a KFC franchise, and Starbucks offered benefits and the possibility of moving ahead. She was a few credits short of an associate degree in business. She had saved nine hundred dollars toward a car. The car would make an enormous difference because getting to work by public transportation could take three hours.

There was a problem with the plan, and it was located on a piece of paper that kept changing.

Navarro's hours moved from week to week. She might work early, late, or close a store only to return for an opening shift after very little time away. The schedule often arrived no more than three days before the next workweek. That left her a remarkably short interval to arrange someone to look after Gavin, preserve the relationships on which childcare depended, get herself to work, and determine whether there was enough money to cover the trips.

In the language of a staffing system, this could be described as flexibility. The store needed the right number of employees when customers appeared. A schedule made too far in advance might produce empty hours when demand fell and too few baristas when it rose. Good managers had to protect the business, and labor was an expense they could see every day.

From Navarro's side, the schedule was doing something else. It was making promises on behalf of other people. Her aunt might have to change her own plans. The childcare arrangement might have to bend. A class could not be chosen if its hours might collide with work. A bus fare could matter more than it should when take-home pay rarely exceeded four or five hundred dollars over two weeks. The same schedule that seemed efficient inside the store created an elaborate, unpaid scheduling operation outside it.

One organization could change its mind about Tuesday. A family had to change its week.

That July, Jodi Kantor of The New York Times followed Navarro's attempts to make the arrangement hold. Kantor's reporting captured disputes about childcare, strain on Navarro's living arrangements, and difficulties in her relationship with another Starbucks worker. That relationship ended during the reporting. The breakup had several causes, Kantor later emphasized, and it would be a dishonest use of Navarro's life to assign the whole event to work schedules. The work schedule nevertheless made an already complicated life harder to coordinate.

There was no villainous robot making decisions about Gavin. There was scheduling software, a business trying to match labor to sales, managers operating within the priorities and limits they had been given, and a young mother who had fewer places to put the resulting uncertainty. A machine had helped move a cost off one ledger and onto another. Nobody needed to be cruel for the cost to become real.

Much of the advice given to ambitious people begins with the interests of the person doing the helping. How do I become valuable? How do I show initiative? How do I earn more responsibility? Those are legitimate questions, and the first five chapters have spent time on them. There is a point at which the questions can become an obstacle of their own. A person can get very good at being helpful according to their own definition, and never notice the nuisance, humiliation, risk, or expense that another person is trying to escape.

An employee's scheduling problem might be described by management as a lack of availability. That is a different description from the employee's need to know whether she can collect her child at six. Both descriptions might be factually correct. Only one explains why the shift is difficult to work.

To become fluent in somebody else's problems, you have to learn what an apparently ordinary decision costs them when they leave your field of view.

Consider how much of Navarro's situation was absent from the usual numbers. A labor forecast knows something about customers per hour, average transaction value, and the number of people needed to work the bar. It may know who is qualified for the opening shift and which employees have declared themselves available. A manager might be evaluated against labor hours and sales. All of those numbers belong in a serious operation.

The forecast does not automatically know the price of asking someone to rearrange childcare for the third time that week. Nor does a store's payroll expense include the neighbor who covers a shift, the family argument about who was supposed to be home, or the class a worker cannot enroll in because the schedule refuses to settle. If the same employee eventually quits, the store may have a turnover number, but even that number arrives after most of the cost has been paid by other people.

The point is not that these experiences have never been measured. Researchers have been measuring them for years. The point is that a measure is only useful to a decision if it is allowed to enter the decision.

The trouble with a highly optimized organization is often that it optimizes the part of the world it owns.

Kantor began her reporting by talking to people who worked in childcare. A scheduling problem at a coffee shop could have been investigated entirely through labor policy, software, store managers, and the company that sold the scheduling system. Childcare workers had a different vantage point. They dealt with the consequences after a parent's shifts had changed and before the parent knew whether the arrangement would survive.

That reporting path eventually led Kantor to Navarro. Once the reporter had the story, she checked its parts separately with Navarro's family, partner, and childcare providers. She also spoke with workers at seventeen Starbucks locations after hearing company representatives describe scheduling practices that did not match what she was learning. This was an informal reporting check, never a representative sample of the chain. Some employees reported receiving as little as a day's notice. It was enough to challenge the comfortable assumption that one worker's circumstances were merely unusual.

This is a better model of empathy than most offices teach. Kantor did not have to imagine how a scheduling system felt to an hourly parent. She found people living with its effects, tested what they told her, and asked whether the company could account for the difference. Concern was the beginning of the inquiry, not its evidence.

Empathy has acquired a strange status in business language. A company can declare that it cares deeply about customers while training employees to close interactions quickly. Managers can be asked to develop empathy while being measured so narrowly that understanding a problem makes their performance numbers worse. Workshops teach staff to repeat what a customer says in reassuring language. Occasionally the customer would prefer that somebody fix the invoice.

Some forms of emotional understanding are indispensable. Others are theatrical. The useful distinction is what changes after you understand.

If a restaurant worker says she cannot stay late, a manager may hear a refusal to help. If the worker has a second job starting an hour later, the request conflicts with another commitment. If she depends on a fixed childcare pickup, an apparently generous offer of overtime may be impossible. If a team regularly loses staff on particular evenings, the problem may involve pay, unreliable scheduling, an awkward commute, or a manager who repeatedly mistakes willingness for availability. Asking employees will not automatically settle which explanation is correct. It can rule out some very silly ones.

Availability and willingness are different facts. Someone can be eager to work and unavailable at a particular hour. Someone else can be available but unwilling to take an extra shift. Conflating them makes the manager worse at both staffing and understanding people.

In a large survey of hourly workers at major food-service and retail chains, sociologists Daniel Schneider and Kristen Harknett found that routine instability was common. Their 2019 analysis drew on almost twenty-eight thousand workers surveyed in 2016 and 2017. About two-thirds reported getting schedules less than two weeks ahead; roughly one-third had less than a week. Workers exposed to more instability also reported greater psychological distress, poorer sleep, and less happiness. In the analysis, schedule instability was more strongly associated with these outcomes than low wages were. Work–life conflict explained an important part of the relationship.

These associations are not a verdict on any one employer, and surveys cannot by themselves show that scheduling caused every health outcome. But pay is an incomplete description of a job. Two jobs with identical hourly wages can occupy radically different amounts of a person's life if one permits planning and the other requires constant repair.

A wage is money paid for hours worked. A job also occupies the hours needed to make those hours possible.

A second study got closer to the way uncertainty enters a household. Economists Elizabeth Ananat and Anna Gassman-Pines asked ninety-two low-wage workers with young children to complete daily surveys for a month. The resulting data covered 2,221 worker-days. On 13.3 percent of those days, the worker experienced an unanticipated schedule change. Almost nine in ten parents experienced at least one such change during the month.

That detail matters because averages can make disruption sound manageable. Thirteen percent is a share of days, and on a dashboard it might pass as tolerable variation. A parent who needs childcare at a precise hour does not experience a percentage. She has to make a call. The change can occupy far more than thirteen percent of her attention, particularly when the person she calls is tired of being asked.

In the daily data, unexpected changes were associated with worse mood and sleep quality on those days. Some estimated effects were statistically marginal, and the sample was small. The study is strongest as a close view of how schedule changes occur, not as a universal coefficient for unhappiness.

This is where usefulness requires more than kindness. A manager might sympathize with an employee whose childcare has fallen apart and still make the same schedule the following week. If the staffing process rewards managers for matching hours to a forecast while punishing them for excess labor, sympathy may produce a pleasant conversation without a changed decision. The manager's intentions matter, but the rules under which the manager works matter too.

It is tempting to say that the answer is simply to give people stable schedules. For some jobs this is straightforward. For others, real uncertainty remains. A restaurant loses its lunch crowd when a storm arrives. A hospital staffs for emergencies that cannot be booked in advance. A small shop cannot buy unlimited excess coverage. Employees also want different things. One person needs the same hours every week; another values picking up shifts around classes or another job. A rigid schedule can be its own trap.

The more interesting question is how much instability is unavoidable, and how much comes from the way the business is organized.

A remarkable experiment with Gap stores put that question under pressure. From late 2015 through 2016, researchers working with the retailer studied scheduling across twenty-eight stores in the Chicago and San Francisco areas. Nineteen were randomly assigned to a treatment and nine served as controls. The project combined several changes: easier shift swaps, more consistent start and end times, some stronger expectations about weekly hours, and carefully targeted additional staffing. Both groups had already adopted two-week advance notice and eliminated on-call shifts across the company.

The researchers did not ask stores to ignore demand. They tested whether more stable scheduling could coexist with a business that still needed to sell clothes.

Their first report found median sales up about seven percent in treatment stores and labor productivity up around five percent. A later peer-reviewed analysis used a different statistical measure, reporting a 5.1 percent increase in productivity driven by higher sales and somewhat lower labor use. These are different estimates of different outcomes. They should not be blended into a miraculous number. The intervention was also a package of practices in a modest number of stores; it cannot establish that any one reform will increase profits in every restaurant or retailer.

Its most interesting result may lie elsewhere. Among those stores, changes in customer traffic explained only about thirty percent of week-to-week variation in payroll hours. Store managers cited inaccurate shipment information, promotional decisions arriving late, and visits from company leaders as other sources of instability.

Employees had been living with the uncertainty. Not all of it had begun with customers.

An organization can ask workers to be endlessly adaptable while treating its own last-minute decisions as natural events. The schedule ends up absorbing disruptions created by procurement, marketing, headquarters, and management, while the person working hourly wages is expected to arrange the rest of life around the result.

The experiment does not settle what Starbucks should have done in 2014. The stores sold clothing rather than coffee, and the changes required managerial effort, not merely a software setting. It establishes a possibility: the tradeoff between employee stability and business performance may be less fixed than the company assumes. Some of the flexibility managers believe they need could be flexibility the company has manufactured.

A different piece of research offered a modest view from the policy side. When Emeryville, California, adopted a fair-workweek ordinance requiring large food-service and retail employers to give earlier notice and compensation for certain last-minute changes, researchers followed workers with young children. Their before-and-after comparison found greater stability and improved well-being relative to similar workers at smaller, exempt businesses. Employees tended to work fewer days with longer shifts; their total hours remained roughly unchanged. Fewer commutes and childcare handoffs are plausible reasons these patterns might matter.

It was a small study, not a randomized trial. A city ordinance is neither a universal cure nor a complete account of the costs it imposes on employers. But it is evidence against the idea that chaos is simply the unavoidable price of serving customers.

Notice where the argument has moved. It began with an apparently personal problem, a mother struggling to organize her life. It has reached the organization's information supply chain, its scheduling incentives, the distribution of business risk, and the possibility that a different management process could help workers without harming the store. Navarro had not failed to appreciate flexibility. The employer had an incomplete account of the costs associated with it.

Listening becomes economically useful when it changes the model.

This is also why a popular prescription to train employees in customer empathy can be so disappointing. The person closest to the problem may already know precisely what is wrong and have no authority to change it. An assistant manager may spend an hour trying to reconcile competing requests, only for a regional staffing target to undo the solution. A compassionate supervisor still operates inside a system that allocates resources and decides which results are rewarded. Care is a real human quality. It is not a substitute for permission, time, staffing, or money.

There are costs of doing nothing as well. Continual turnover destroys local knowledge, forcing coworkers to train replacements. Unreliable shifts can create absences that look like lack of commitment. A plan that minimizes payroll on paper may make the store less capable at the exact moments when customers arrive. Good managers have to make choices among such costs. They cannot make those choices honestly while half of them remain invisible.

There is a similar problem in health care, where the categories look different but the missing costs have a familiar shape. A clinic records an appointment, and later records that the patient arrived, was late, or failed to appear. Those are useful administrative facts. An office needs to know which appointments took place. The trouble begins when a label is treated as an explanation.

At Zuckerberg San Francisco General Hospital and Trauma Center, researchers followed people through an outpatient surgical clinic serving patients with substantial medical and socioeconomic needs. They timed ninety-six visits and interviewed forty-three patients about their experience. The average visit lasted almost ninety-six minutes, with more than two-thirds of the time spent waiting. About a quarter of visits stretched beyond two hours.

The waiting-room clock was only one of the clocks involved. Some patients traveled considerable distances. Some depended on public transit or a ride from somebody else. Others struggled to leave work or caregiving responsibilities. Drivers faced a one-hour street-parking limit around a clinic where the average visit was longer than an hour. Leaving to move the car could mean missing the call to be seen; staying risked a fine. The researchers documented the stress of these choices as part of the appointment experience.

A patient who arrives late may appear in the system as a punctuality problem. That same patient may have started traveling hours earlier. A missed appointment can reflect an access barrier, confusing communication, competing obligations, a genuine decision not to attend, or several things together. It would be no improvement to assume every patient who misses an appointment has the same sympathetic explanation.

The investigators gained information by observing journeys and asking people what happened. They did not replace medical care with kindness. They found that transport, parking, waiting, and the expense of being away from other obligations were part of getting care, even when the clinical visit itself was appropriate.

The sixty-eight-and-a-half-percent waiting share is from one surgical clinic, not a national estimate. Some waiting is necessary to keep a service safe and flexible. The reason to measure it is to find which delays can be reduced, which ones can be explained, and which arrangements transfer avoidable costs to people who have little power to negotiate.

We could turn the clinic and coffee-shop stories into a comforting contest between cold institutions and caring humans. That would miss one of their harder facts. Some people inside the institutions already care very much. A clinic coordinator may know which patients are desperate for a ride and have no budget for transportation. A store manager may know a worker's childcare problem and still be required to meet an impossible labor target. The people closest to the consequences are not always the people who can change the rules.

Understanding needs to travel toward authority, and authority needs the capacity to act.

That is part of what makes Ana Melendez's work, from the beginning of this book, valuable. She encounters costs that medical institutions tend to file separately under insurance, referrals, transport, language, benefits, and appointments. She can identify which obstacle currently prevents progress and connect somebody to an organization with the means to help. But no navigator should be expected to personally repair every policy she encounters. Once her work exposes a recurring failure, somebody with authority faces a different question: what could change so that the next person does not need exceptional help?

The same question can be asked in a restaurant without convening an empathy committee. Sit with workers and learn how schedules interact with the hours they can actually work, including whatever commitments they choose to share. Examine how often shifts change and how much warning people receive. Find out which disruptions originated in customer demand and which came from decisions within the company. Compare turnover, coverage, missed shifts, staffing costs, and customer outcomes before and after trying a different approach. If the results are mixed, investigate the mix.

There is no guarantee the arrangement wins on every measure. That is why it should be tested. A business cannot promise that listening will erase tradeoffs, and a worker should not have to disclose intimate family details to earn a reasonably stable schedule.

That boundary is easy to miss. Organizations eager to understand their people can become intrusive. Someone may need predictable evenings without wanting to tell a supervisor about a divorce, a medical condition, or who is raising their child. A patient may decline to repeat a history of hardship to every staff member who controls an appointment. Being fluent in another person's problems does not entitle you to enter every part of their life.

The more respectful question is often narrower: which constraints should this decision accommodate, and which costs have we made people absorb without noticing?

That phrasing protects against another failure of well-meaning helpers. They substitute their own version of somebody's interest for that person's account. The proposed fix looks excellent from the outside. The recipient does not want it. A manager thinks every employee wants maximum hours, while some workers want fewer but steadier hours to finish a qualification or care for family. Another worker does want more hours despite irregular shifts. The useful fact is not what a caring observer would choose. It is what the person can choose when the tradeoffs are explicit.

Business researchers have made a related point about customer service. In a widely discussed 2010 Harvard Business Review account, Matthew Dixon, Karen Freeman, and Nicholas Toman argued that companies overinvested in attempts to delight customers while neglecting repeat calls, handoffs, and unresolved requests. The research came from a commercial customer-service program, and its publicly available methodological detail is limited. It should not be treated as a law about every customer. It offers a worthwhile diagnostic: a correct resolution can be worth far more than an exquisitely worded apology for a mistake the company has not corrected.

There is a difference between the cost of serving a person and the cost of being served. Organizations rarely capture both in the same report.

That difference may become still more important as artificial intelligence enters routine work. Systems can forecast store traffic, draft a service reply, search policy, recommend a roster, predict missed appointments, or flag an overbooked clinic. These are useful capabilities. Nobody needs to preserve the days of schedules made with erasers and sticky notes as a tribute to human dignity.

The consequential issue is what the system is permitted to value.

If an optimizer minimizes paid idle time while treating employees as interchangeable blocks of availability, it may produce excellent numbers according to its instructions. It may also create childcare instability and turnover that were never included in the calculation. The omission is a choice about what counts, even when the people making it believe they are simply buying better software.

A more thoughtful system could record voluntarily supplied preferences, discourage late changes, flag back-to-back closing and opening shifts, anticipate staffing needs farther ahead, and make the cost of recurrent exceptions visible. It could tell managers that promotions or inaccurate shipments are driving disruptions routinely blamed on customers. Better models may learn to identify some of the costs that earlier systems ignored. There is no durable career strategy in hoping machines remain oblivious to human circumstances.

What must remain visible is the difference between a successful forecast and a workable arrangement. A forecast can be accurate while the schedule it produces is damaging. A service answer can be fluent while the customer remains stuck. A clinic can send a punctual appointment reminder while the patient has no workable means of arriving.

In a 2024 description of its scheduling practices, Starbucks said it was posting schedules three weeks ahead and using employees' shift preferences. The company also described forecasting transactions in fifteen-minute increments while allowing managers to adjust the results. These were Starbucks' own accounts of its processes and improvements, not an independent audit of how every worker experienced them. But they show the possibilities: sophisticated demand forecasting and the constraints of the people doing the work need not be enemies. The test is whether those constraints actually influence decisions.

In 2014 the announced changes had arrived remarkably fast. The day after Kantor's story, Starbucks told its US workers it would change the software, give managers more control, end close-then-open assignments, enforce a minimum week's notice, and seek shorter commutes for employees traveling more than an hour. A spokesman later said Navarro herself had a more predictable forty-hour schedule. That is a reported corporate account of the immediate aftermath, not a verified history of her later years. An announcement, an implemented process, and a better life are three different things.

Kantor did not stop at the announcement. She continued asking workers and managers what happened and later reported evidence of training and genuine implementation efforts, while acknowledging how difficult a change to a large scheduling system could be. That was the appropriate level of certainty. A corporate memo establishes intention; the next several schedules reveal whether the promise survives contact with work.

There is something awkward about using Navarro as a protagonist in a book about becoming useful. She did not volunteer to become a case study for management development. She was trying to earn a living, finish her education, care for Gavin, and eventually buy a car that would reduce the effort required to keep the job paying for it. The reporting mattered because those ambitions remained ordinary. The car was not a metaphor for an entrepreneurial mindset. It was transportation.

If the chapter's lesson were simply that executives should care about their employees, the story would have been exhausted long ago. The harder work is learning how an institution can be competent at its own task and persistently mistaken about its effects. Someone must carry the view from outside the spreadsheet into the room where the spreadsheet is trusted. Often that person has little authority. They are the worker explaining why a shift cannot be worked, the patient describing the route to an appointment, or the customer who has just explained the same problem for the fourth time.

Their account may be incomplete. It deserves testing rather than automatic agreement. But without it, even impeccable internal numbers can support a decision that is wrong about the world.

Navarro had saved nine hundred dollars toward a car. She still needed her next schedule in time to plan how to get to the job paying for it.
