# The Two-Minute Favor

Charlie Brown carried his paperwork in a large bag.

In 2012, when Chicago public-radio reporter Shannon Heffernan met him at the Harold Washington Library, Brown was an older man who depended on social services for housing and health care. Increasingly, the offices he dealt with were short-staffed and directing people to websites. Forms that had once been handed across a counter now lived behind usernames, menus, passwords, links, and unfamiliar fields.

Brown described himself as computer illiterate.

So he went to the library.

Once a week he met with a cyber navigator named Zach McMahon. McMahon’s formal job was to help people learn to use computers. In practice, much of his time went toward the things that had migrated onto computers: government benefits, housing information, job searches, immigration forms. The library had not become a government office, but government offices had become websites, and the people who could not navigate those websites showed up at a place where somebody might still sit beside them.

There is one detail in Heffernan’s reporting that keeps the story from turning sentimental. When Brown became frustrated and pushed the keyboard toward McMahon, McMahon pushed it back.

He would help. He would not take over.

That is a useful place to begin a chapter about favors, because most advice about helping other people is weak precisely where the boundary matters.

We are told to be generous, go the extra mile, make ourselves useful, pay it forward, be the person who says yes. These instructions sound humane because they often are. A small act of help can remove a ridiculous amount of friction from somebody else’s day. It can also become the first installment in an obligation that never ends.

The difference is not always visible when the favor begins.

Someone asks how to submit an expense report. You show them. Fine.

They ask you to submit this one because the system is confusing. Maybe still fine.

Then every month an expense report appears in your inbox because you are “so much better at this stuff.” What began as kindness has quietly become a job transfer.

The two-minute favor is valuable partly because it is small enough to reveal what happens next.

Some help ends with the person being more capable than before. Some help creates a relationship. Some reveals a broken process worth fixing for everybody. Some simply teaches another person that your time is easier to spend than theirs.

Useful people eventually learn to tell the difference.

Public libraries have been learning this distinction at institutional scale for years.

As government services moved online in the 2000s and 2010s, the migration was often described as modernization. For many citizens it was. An online form can be faster than waiting in a government office, available outside business hours, searchable, trackable, and cheaper to administer.

The benefits are real. So is the transfer of work.

A paper form can be difficult because the policy is complicated. An online form adds other requirements: a device, a connection, an email address, passwords, familiarity with browser behavior, the ability to upload documents, and enough confidence to know whether a blank screen means you made a mistake or the website did.

When agencies removed a counter, some of that burden did not disappear. It moved.

Often it moved to libraries.

A national survey of public libraries covering 2009–2010 found that 78.7 percent reported helping patrons apply for or access e-government services. Two-thirds reported being the only source of free public computer and Internet access in their communities. Those numbers are old now, but that is part of the point. The library’s role as unofficial infrastructure for digital government did not begin with the pandemic or with artificial intelligence. It grew as ordinary life acquired more logins.

The work is easy to underestimate because each individual interaction can look trivial from the outside.

Where is the form?

How do I make an email address?

Why did the document disappear?

How do I attach a résumé?

What does this error message mean?

Can I print this?

None of these questions sounds like a civic crisis. But the thing on the other side of the question may be housing, food assistance, employment, immigration status, school access, unemployment insurance, medical care, or the ability to communicate with an agency that has no convenient human alternative.

The favor is small. The consequence is not.

That gap between effort and consequence is one reason useful people are remembered. The helper experiences two minutes. The person receiving help experiences the removal of the thing that had stopped the rest of the day.

A colleague forwards the correct contact instead of saying “not my department.” A receptionist explains which entrance is actually open after 5 p.m. A mechanic points out that the strange noise is normal before somebody spends a weekend worrying about a failing transmission. A more experienced employee shows a new hire the one field in the software that everybody forgets. The action may barely register for the giver.

For the receiver, it arrives exactly where progress had stopped.

There is research suggesting that everyday prosocial behavior can produce effects beyond the immediate act, although it should not be turned into a formula for manufacturing friendship. In a randomized workplace study published in 2018, researchers assigned employees to give acts of kindness to specific coworkers, receive those acts, or continue as controls. Both givers and receivers reported some gains in well-being, and receivers later performed more prosocial acts themselves than controls. The study was small—111 employees in one Spanish workplace—and it measured a designed intervention rather than the unruly variety of real working life. Still, the result is consistent with a familiar observation: help can propagate.

The important word is can.

Nothing about a favor guarantees trust. People can help for manipulative reasons. Recipients can resent unsolicited rescue. A favor can create debt rather than warmth. An employee who constantly helps everyone may be avoiding their own work. A manager can praise “team players” while building a system that survives only because certain people donate invisible labor.

The book would become dangerous if it treated every yes as a deposit in a future account.

Real generosity is not a networking trick.

Amanda Perry’s work at the Salt Lake City Public Library is useful here because the institution around the favor changed as the need became clearer.

Perry had worked for the library for two decades, beginning as a library aide and later becoming an associate librarian. She was involved with the library’s digital-inclusion work and ran a creative lab at a branch in the city’s Marmalade District. When the COVID-19 pandemic closed or restricted physical access to libraries, a problem that had long been manageable in person became harder very quickly.

People who relied on the library for Internet access, devices, and basic digital help could no longer simply walk in and ask.

Salt Lake City Public Library received a $411,084 grant from the Institute of Museum and Library Services in 2020 to develop a Digital Navigators Program with the National Digital Inclusion Alliance and Urban Libraries Council. The goal was not merely to answer isolated computer questions. The program was designed around one-to-one support by phone, with personalized assistance for connectivity, devices, and digital skills. In some cases, staff brought help to people who could not easily reach the library.

Perry became one of those Digital Navigators.

Her description of the work is revealing because it begins with local knowledge. Before helping, she advised understanding community demographics, connectivity, available resources, and who was likely to be excluded. That is a different posture from simply waiting for a patron to say, “My laptop is broken.” It asks what problem the person is trying to solve and what else may be attached to it.

The difference resembles patient navigation from Chapter 1. In both cases, a system has many individual services that make sense from inside their own boundaries. The difficulty appears when a person has to cross between them.

The useful worker often becomes a translator at the crossing.

Digital navigation eventually became a broader model. The National Digital Inclusion Alliance now defines digital navigators as trusted guides who provide individualized help with connectivity, devices, digital skills, and online services. Importantly, the model includes follow-up. The navigator is not simply a help desk that closes the ticket once the password is reset. The work can continue until the person reaches the actual goal.

That makes it more powerful. It also makes the boundary problem more serious.

If one person is trusted enough to help with a phone, why not the benefits application? If the benefits application, why not the housing problem behind it? If the housing problem, why not the legal issue? If the navigator is patient and competent, there is almost no natural end to the chain of needs a person may bring into the room.

Good helping systems therefore need limits as much as they need generosity.

The National Digital Inclusion Alliance’s current resources include material on boundaries, confidentiality, service design, training, and fair compensation. That is not bureaucratic decoration. It is what happens when a community learns that a recurring favor is actually a form of work.

The first person who helps can improvise. The tenth needs a process. The thousandth needs an institution.

This is how small acts become systems without losing the human quality that made them useful in the first place.

The evolution matters because organizations are very good at confusing voluntary help with free capacity.

Management researchers use the term organizational citizenship behavior for useful actions that fall outside narrow task requirements: helping a coworker, volunteering, protecting the organization, sharing information, making life easier for the team. These behaviors are generally treated as good because they often are good. Workplaces could barely function if every employee refused to lift a finger beyond the exact wording of a job description.

But citizenship has a dark side when it becomes compulsory.

Mark Bolino and colleagues studied what they called citizenship fatigue: the feeling of being worn out or on edge from repeatedly engaging in extra-role behavior. In a multiwave study of 273 employees, the relationship between citizenship behavior and fatigue depended on the surrounding environment. Fatigue was more likely when organizational support was low and when people felt pressure to perform extra-role work. Employees who became fatigued were less likely to keep helping later.

This is a useful correction to the moral language organizations sometimes use around generosity.

A workplace cannot demand permanent favors and still call them favors.

Once everybody knows that Jane always fixes the printer, the printer is Jane’s job unless the organization does something else. Once Marcus always covers the closing shift because he is reliable, reliability has become the mechanism by which his evenings are consumed. Once the only bilingual employee is expected to interpret every difficult conversation, a valuable skill has become unpaid infrastructure.

The issue is not whether these people should ever help. It is whether the system learns from the help.

The best small favor contains information.

It tells you where people get stuck.

If one person cannot find the form, help the person. If fifty people cannot find the form, move the form.

If one new employee does not understand the workflow, explain it. If every new employee asks the same question, change onboarding.

If one customer cannot interpret the invoice, walk them through it. If the same line confuses customers every week, rewrite the invoice.

A useful person notices when repetition changes the nature of the problem.

This is one reason the two-minute favor should not become a religion of individual kindness. Individual kindness can conceal institutional stupidity.

The librarian who patiently explains the same government website every day may be doing admirable work. The fact that the explanation is repeatedly necessary is still evidence about the website, the agency, the policy, or the access model. Praise for the librarian should not relieve the institution upstream of the obligation to improve.

Charlie Brown’s bag of papers was not proof that he lacked discipline. It was evidence that several systems expected him to manage their complexity at once.

McMahon’s decision to push the keyboard back toward him is therefore more interesting than simply filling out the form would have been. The immediate task might have gone faster if McMahon typed. But speed was not the only objective. The cyber navigator was trying to increase Brown’s ability to operate without him.

That is a form of respect.

Helping can become demeaning when it quietly communicates, “You cannot do this.” The competent helper has to judge how much to carry and how much to leave with the other person. Too little help and the obstacle remains. Too much and the recipient becomes a passenger in their own problem.

This is especially important when the helper has more education, technical comfort, institutional status, money, or social confidence. The difference in capability can tempt a person to seize control because it feels efficient.

Efficiency can be rude.

Anybody who has watched a fast typist snatch a keyboard from a slower one has seen this in miniature. The problem gets solved while the person disappears.

Good help preserves agency where it can.

There is evidence for this beyond etiquette. In two workplace studies published in 2024, employees who were offered help they did not want reported more frustration of autonomy and competence. In a two-wave study, autonomy frustration was then associated with more after-work rumination and poorer psychological detachment. The studies do not show that unsolicited help inevitably causes harm, and other research in this chapter shows that receiving help can reduce strain. They do show why the recipient’s judgment belongs in the definition of useful. A favor can solve the task and still take something away.

Sometimes that means doing the task together. Sometimes it means writing down the steps. Sometimes it means making the introduction and leaving the conversation. Sometimes the person really does need you to carry the thing because they are exhausted, sick, grieving, out of time, or dealing with a system that has become absurd. Human beings are not training exercises. There is no virtue in making someone struggle through every obstacle for educational value.

Judgment is the work.

This is why a favor can build trust out of proportion to its duration. Trust is not measuring the minutes. It is learning something about what happens when you are briefly vulnerable in front of another person.

Do they make you feel stupid?

Do they disappear halfway through?

Do they take over?

Do they do what they said they would do?

Do they respect the limit of what they know?

Do they point you toward someone better qualified when the problem leaves their territory?

Small interactions answer these questions cheaply.

That makes favors important in careers for a reason that has little to do with traditional networking. People gradually build models of one another. They remember who closes loops, who explains things without performing superiority, who sends the useful link, who notices when somebody is stranded at the edge of a process.

Months later, when the stakes are higher, those memories matter.

The relationship between a small favor and a future opportunity is not contractual. Help somebody with a printer and they do not owe you a promotion. That kind of accounting poisons the thing that made the favor valuable.

What accumulates is evidence.

You become evidence that a certain kind of problem gets easier when you are involved.

That can create access. People include you earlier. They ask your opinion. They introduce you to someone. They trust you with a messier assignment. They tell you about an opportunity before it becomes public. None of this is guaranteed, and much of it happens without either side calculating the exchange.

Reputation is often less dramatic than ambition makes it sound.

It may begin with the person who answered the email.

There is a technology question hiding inside this chapter too.

As software becomes better at explaining forms, resetting accounts, translating language, drafting messages, and guiding users through procedures, some two-minute favors will become one-minute interactions with a machine. This is good. Nobody needs to preserve bad password-reset experiences in order to protect human dignity.

But digital navigation offers a warning against assuming that information is the whole problem.

A person may know exactly which benefit exists and still lack a device. They may have the device and no affordable connection. They may have the connection and struggle with literacy, language, disability access, identity verification, fear of fraud, or the experience of having been bounced between institutions so many times that they no longer trust the next instruction.

A chatbot can answer a question. A navigator is responsible for noticing when the answer did not restore progress.

The difference may shrink as systems improve. It should. But the book’s larger argument survives: usefulness lives near the gap between an available answer and an achieved result.

In 2026, the National Digital Inclusion Alliance published an evaluation of a multi-year Digital Navigator Corps operating through eighteen organizations in rural and Tribal communities across fifteen states. The program reported serving more than 6,500 people through more than 9,200 sessions and distributing more than 4,100 devices. Those are program outputs, not proof that every participant achieved the intended long-term outcome, and the evaluation came from the organization running the initiative. Still, the scale makes one point clearly enough: the need was not a collection of isolated favors anymore.

It had become infrastructure.

This is the mature path for useful work.

Help the person in front of you when you reasonably can. Notice what the request is teaching you. If the same need keeps returning, build something better than your own availability.

A note. A template. A clearer instruction. A referral list. An office hour. A training session. A process. A funded role. A different piece of software. A rule that says what you will and will not do.

The highest compliment to a generous person is not that everybody depends on them forever.

It is that some of the things they once had to do as favors no longer require a favor at all.
