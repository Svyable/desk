# Be Early, Not Loud

Long before Tonya Hicks owned an electrical contracting company, she had a habit of reorganizing the crew.

She was not the foreman. She was not the lead electrician. She had no title that authorized her to assign anybody anywhere. In an interview years later, she remembered looking around a job and telling one worker that another task suited him better, then moving somebody else to where she thought the work would go faster. The men did not follow because she had won a popularity contest. Hicks’s explanation was simpler: she was good at the trade, and the plan usually made sense.

That distinction matters.

A great deal of bad career advice treats initiative as a personality trait you display. Speak up. Raise your hand. Volunteer. Be visible. Take ownership. These can all be useful behaviors, but they can also become theater. A person can volunteer for everything and still leave everyone else cleaning up after them. Another can say very little, notice that the crew will run out of material after lunch, and solve the problem before it stops the job.

Hicks’s early advantage was not that she was louder than the men around her. By her own account, she had to earn their respect over and over. Her advantage was that she had begun to see the work as a sequence rather than as a list of assignments.

She had not planned on becoming an electrician. Hicks went to Central State University on a mathematics scholarship and imagined a career using math in government intelligence work. She has said that a professor discouraged that idea and suggested teaching instead. She did not want to teach. After her sophomore year she worked on an industrial construction site and watched electricians using algebra to calculate loads and bends. The work looked like mathematics with consequences. She decided to learn it.

The route she found was a five-year apprenticeship with the International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers. It combined full-time work with classes several nights a week. Hicks says she became the first woman to reach journeyman wireman status in IBEW Local 917 in Meridian, Mississippi, and the first Black woman to do so in the state. Her account of those years is not a sentimental one. She has described traveling long distances for work because local employers would not give her enough hours, then driving back for required classes. She also says she was graduated several months ahead of her class so that she would not appear in the graduation photograph.

Those details are her recollection, not a complete institutional history of the local, and the distinction should stay visible. What can be checked is the larger context. Women remain rare in the occupation. In 2025, Bureau of Labor Statistics annual averages put women at 3.5 percent of employed electricians in the United States. The number is higher than it was when Hicks entered the trade, but not high enough to make her description of being conspicuous on a job site difficult to imagine.

There is another detail in her story that is more important for this chapter than the barrier-breaking firsts. Hicks says she was never formally placed in charge. Still, crews began accepting her judgment about how the day should go.

That is a different kind of leadership from the one most organizations know how to photograph.

A job site makes the difference unusually easy to see because physical work exposes bad sequencing quickly. The schedule may say that conduit goes in this week, but that tells you very little about whether the work can actually start on Tuesday morning. Is the material there? Has another trade finished the section you need? Is the lift available? Has the drawing changed? Is access blocked? Has the inspection happened? Does the worker assigned to a difficult bend have the right experience? Is the circuit safe to work on? The answer to one of those questions can turn an eight-hour plan into twenty minutes of actual progress and seven hours of expensive improvisation.

Construction researchers have a useful phrase for this: making work ready.

In the Last Planner System, a production-planning approach used in construction, lookahead planning sits between the master schedule and the weekly work plan. The master schedule can say what should happen. Lookahead planning asks what has to be true for that work to become executable. Researchers studying the system describe the work in terms of identifying constraints early and removing them before the crew is expected to perform the task. One metric is literally called Tasks Made Ready.

The phrase is almost comically plain, which is part of its strength. A surprising amount of competent work consists of turning what should happen into what can happen.

This is what experienced operators do all day, often without naming it. A kitchen manager checks whether the prep cook has enough onions before dinner service rather than discovering the shortage after the tickets begin printing. A nurse looks ahead to whether a patient being discharged actually has transportation and medication access, not merely whether the discharge order exists. A field technician notices that the replacement part is correct but the mounting hardware is not. A project manager asks whether the person whose approval is needed will be in the country next week. The task itself may be obvious. The constraints around it are where the day is usually won or lost.

Researchers Farook Hamzeh and colleagues have studied lookahead planning in construction by separating work that is anticipated from work that is made ready. Their findings are more technical than any individual worker needs to memorize, but the practical point is useful: reliable execution depends heavily on identifying constraints before the planned start date. A weekly plan can look disciplined while still being fragile if the team has not done the earlier work of making tasks executable.

That helps explain why certain people seem to be permanently one move ahead without appearing frantic. They are not predicting the future. They have learned which conditions the future depends on.

The difference is easy to miss because many workplaces reward visible completion more readily than invisible preparation. If a report is submitted at 4:57 p.m., the submission is visible. The person who discovered three days earlier that the required data source would be unavailable on Friday may receive no special credit. If a delivery arrives on time, nobody applauds the employee who confirmed the loading dock hours on Monday. If an event runs smoothly, guests rarely know who checked the power supply, dietary restrictions, spare batteries, extension cords, weather plan, access list, and vendor arrival windows.

Preparation disappears into the absence of trouble.

This creates a strange career problem. The more effective someone becomes at preventing small failures, the harder it can be for outsiders to see what they are doing. The loud employee can point to the emergency he solved. The quiet employee may have prevented five emergencies that never acquired names.

The answer is not to manufacture crises so people can watch you fix them. It is to become more deliberate about understanding the work you are preparing.

Organizational researchers Michael Frese and Doris Fay used the term personal initiative for work behavior that is self-starting, future-oriented, and persistent in overcoming barriers. Their work is useful because it does not reduce initiative to enthusiasm. They describe it through goals, information gathering, planning, and feedback. The sequence begins before action. A person who rushes into a problem without enough information may be energetic, but energy and initiative are not synonyms.

Later research on proactivity at work makes the same point in a different way. Adam Grant and Susan Ashford argued that proactive behavior is anticipatory and change-oriented, but that it is shaped by the situation and by the person’s judgment about whether action is appropriate. This is important because many workplaces have absorbed the language of proactivity without absorbing its conditions. Employees are told to act like owners while being denied the information, authority, time, or psychological safety that would let them exercise ownership intelligently.

Initiative without context is guesswork.

Hicks had context because she knew the craft. Her informal direction of other electricians came after years of training and work, not before she had learned which end of the tool to hold. In her telling, the men followed because they thought her plan would get the job done. That is not a small qualification. Competence changed how the same behavior was interpreted.

Imagine a first-week apprentice ordering experienced electricians around because a career book advised her to demonstrate leadership. The result would not be admirable initiative. It would be evidence that she had confused confidence with judgment.

There are good reasons for hierarchy in skilled work. Electrical work makes the point more sharply than most office jobs because a bad improvisation can injure somebody. OSHA rules require that only qualified people work on energized electrical parts, and electrical safety procedures deliberately constrain who may act, under what conditions, and with what protections. Nobody should read “be proactive” as permission to freelance around live equipment.

Useful people learn the boundary between removing a constraint and creating a hazard.

That boundary exists outside the trades too. A junior employee can prepare a draft contract clause; that does not mean they should send it to the customer without legal review. A medical assistant can notice that a patient has misunderstood instructions; that does not authorize a diagnosis. An analyst can identify an anomaly; that does not mean changing the production database is a sensible first response. Acting early is valuable only when the action belongs to your level of knowledge and authority.

Sometimes the right proactive move is to escalate early.

This is less glamorous than the mythology of the self-directed employee. It is also more useful. A person who recognizes a problem at 10 a.m. and gets it in front of the right decision-maker may save a week. A person who spends three days trying to solve a problem they do not have the authority or expertise to solve may be working very hard while making the situation worse.

Hicks’s story contains another warning about initiative: organizations do not evaluate proactive behavior in a social vacuum.

In a 2021 study published in Personnel Psychology, researchers Melody Zhang, Kenneth Law, and Lin Wang examined employees who took charge relative to their teammates. Those employees were more likely to be seen as competent and to emerge as informal leaders. They were also more likely to be seen as less warm and to receive less support from coworkers, especially where the team climate did not welcome initiative.

This is one of those findings that feels familiar before it feels fair. The person who sees a better way is not automatically the person everyone wants to hear from. Taking charge changes the work, but it also changes status relationships. A suggestion that appears operational to the person making it can feel like criticism to the person whose process is being changed.

The lesson is not to keep quiet. It is to understand that the technical question and the social question arrive together.

Can this problem be solved? Who already owns it? What does that person know that I do not? Will my intervention reduce their burden or merely announce that I noticed their mistake? Am I proposing a better sequence, or am I demonstrating that I want to be seen proposing one?

These questions matter for everybody, but they do not cost everybody equally. Hicks entered crews where, by her account, she was often the only woman and where she experienced both racial and sex discrimination. A person already treated as an outsider has less room to be casually wrong and may pay a steeper social price for behavior that reads as confidence from someone else. Her story therefore should not be flattened into the pleasant claim that competence always wins. She says she had to re-earn respect on each job. That is not meritocracy working smoothly. It is a person becoming unusually good inside a system that repeatedly made her prove she belonged.

The distinction matters because this book is about usefulness, not obedience to unfair systems. Sometimes becoming better at the work creates freedom. Sometimes it merely makes an unjust arrangement more tolerable. The goal is to learn enough from competence that you gain choices, not to romanticize the amount of friction some people must absorb before those choices appear.

There is a revealing moment in Hicks’s account of how she eventually started her company.

She was on a job doing what she says she often did: helping direct the work without holding the supervisory title. A man on the crew finally told her that if she was going to tell everyone what to do, she might as well own the company. Hicks took the suggestion more seriously than he may have expected. By the end of the day, she had decided to leave and start a business.

The origin story is almost too tidy, and it would be a mistake to use it as evidence that informal leadership inevitably becomes entrepreneurship. Most competent crew members do not start firms. Most should not. Hicks herself has said she knew nothing about owning a business at the time. She bought a Gateway computer, made business cards with clip art, set up a desk in the sunroom of her apartment, and began trying to find customers.

Those details are useful precisely because they make the transition look less inevitable. She had trade skill. She had the habit of coordinating work. She did not yet have a business model delivered by destiny.

What she did have was a widening field of view.

First she learned to do electrical work. Then she learned to see how several electricians could be deployed across a job. Later she learned to sell work, hire people, manage clients, and operate a company. In her own description of running Power Solutions, she eventually stopped doing most of the field work herself and concentrated on getting business and hiring people with deeper expertise where needed. The skill did not disappear. It moved up a level.

That is a pattern worth noticing because career growth is often described as accumulating harder tasks. Sometimes it is better understood as increasing the distance over which you can see consequences.

An apprentice sees the bend in front of her. A journeyman sees the run. A foreman sees the crew. A contractor sees labor, material, inspections, customer expectations, payment timing, supply chains, and the next project that has to replace this one. The job changes because the horizon changes.

You can practice this without waiting for a promotion.

Suppose somebody asks you to prepare a presentation for Thursday. The reactive interpretation is straightforward: make the slides. A more useful interpretation starts with the event the slides belong to. Who is in the room? What decision is supposed to come out of it? Which number is likely to be challenged? Does legal need to review a claim? Is the executive presenting from the version you are editing? Does the client expect the file the night before? Is there an appendix somebody always asks for? The answers may change what you work on first.

None of this requires theatrics. In fact, the best anticipation often produces a quieter day.

The useful employee does not need to announce, “I am taking ownership of this.” They send the finance question on Tuesday because waiting until Wednesday night would put the meeting at risk. They book the room before the calendar fills. They discover that the spreadsheet uses last quarter’s definition and resolve the discrepancy before it becomes a debate in front of twenty people. They ask the presenter which argument matters before polishing forty slides that may be deleted.

The result is not heroism. It is fewer preventable surprises.

This is also where automation becomes more interesting than the usual question of whether a machine can complete a task.

Researchers are already building systems that automate parts of construction lookahead planning. One 2022 study combined linked data and reinforcement learning to generate conflict-free lookahead schedules more quickly than conventional manual methods. This is useful work. A planning system can inspect codified constraints across many activities faster than a human can hold all of them in memory.

But another line of construction research points directly at the limitation. Schedules contain explicit dependencies; real projects also depend on implicit knowledge that is often transferred verbally. A drawing may say one thing while the crew knows that access will be blocked by another trade. A sequence may look legal in a planning system while an experienced worker knows that the chosen order creates rework. A supplier may technically be on time while everyone on site knows that “on time” at that vendor means the afternoon after you needed it.

Software becomes very good at anticipating once the relevant constraint has been represented. Human usefulness often begins one step earlier, when somebody realizes that the constraint exists.

That is a durable way to think about AI at work. The interesting divide is not human task versus machine task. It is visible condition versus hidden condition, codified rule versus local context, generated plan versus responsibility for whether the plan survives contact with reality.

A model can produce the schedule. Somebody still has to notice that the key person will be on vacation, the permit has not cleared, the loading dock cannot fit the truck, the customer’s definition changed, or the data being fed into the plan describes a world that ended last Tuesday.

This does not guarantee humans permanent superiority. More context will become machine-readable. More sensors, workflows, messages, calendars, inventories, and approvals will be connected. Systems will identify constraints that people currently discover through experience. That is good. Much of the avoidable scrambling in modern work deserves to disappear.

The human advantage, if there is one, will not come from protecting drudgery. It will come from continuing to widen the context that the system can use and accepting responsibility for the exceptions that remain.

There is a practical way to develop this habit.

When you finish a task, resist the impulse to stop at the boundary of the request. Look one handoff farther.

Where does this go next? What will the next person need in order to use it? What could make them send it back? What decision are they actually trying to make? Which missing input would turn tomorrow into a delay?

Then look one step backward.

What assumption did you depend on? Who supplied the information? Is it still current? What would you want to know earlier next time?

This is not a checklist to recite at every desk. It is a way of training attention until dependencies become easier to see.

The temptation is to overdo it. Once a person becomes known for anticipating problems, everybody begins bringing problems to them. The employee who checks one vendor issue can quietly become the unofficial procurement department. The analyst who fixes one broken dataset can inherit every spreadsheet nobody else wants to own. The nurse who remembers every family detail can become the emotional backup system for an understaffed unit.

Anticipation creates leverage, but leverage attracts load.

Chapter 1 dealt with work that falls between roles. This chapter adds another complication: the person who reliably catches what is about to fall can become the reason the organization never builds a better net.

So being early also means being early about your own limits. If the same constraint appears every week, stop treating it as a surprise. Document it. Change the handoff. Ask for the authority that matches the responsibility. Train someone else. Put the recurring issue into the system. If none of that is allowed, at least name the pattern clearly enough that your competence does not hide the cost forever.

Hicks eventually built that kind of leverage into an institution of her own. Power Solutions dates its founding to 2000. She later created Women Do Everything to help more women enter occupations where they remain badly underrepresented. Her career has grown far beyond the day when she was informally telling a crew how to divide the work.

Still, the small scene explains something about why the larger story became possible.

She learned the trade deeply enough to see past her own assignment. She could look at several people, several tasks, and the shape of the day, then make a judgment about what should happen next. The title came later. The company came later. The reputation came later.

The useful part happened first.
