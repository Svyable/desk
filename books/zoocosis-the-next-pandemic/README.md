# Zoocosis: The Next Pandemic

**Sven Hardy Benson**

## Working thesis

A lion can pace the same strip of concrete until the path becomes a groove. A bear can sway. A primate can pluck itself bare. Animal-welfare science calls these and related patterns stereotypic or abnormal repetitive behaviors, not proof that an animal has simply "gone insane." Their causes are heterogeneous: frustrated motivation, impoverished environments, developmental history, habit, stress, loss of control, and sometimes altered neural function all matter.

*Zoocosis* begins with an uncomfortable question: **what would the human version look like?**

Humans did not stop being animals when we invented climate control, cubicles, supermarkets, suburbs, smartphones, algorithmic feeds, cars, elevators, delivery apps, fluorescent light, endless entertainment, infinite pornography, global status markets, remote work, and an economy capable of putting a variable-reward machine in every pocket. We changed the enclosure faster than genes could change the animal.

This book uses **zoocosis** as Sven Hardy Benson's deliberately provocative cultural term, not as a clinical diagnosis. It describes the possibility that some widespread forms of modern distress and self-defeating behavior are not best understood only as defects inside individual people. They may also be intelligible responses by a highly social, mobile, daylight-calibrated, status-sensitive, novelty-seeking primate living inside environments that superstimulate some ancient drives, starve others, monetize attention, flatten movement, fragment belonging, erase natural stopping points, and make comparison effectively infinite.

The thesis is not that modernity is a mistake or that prehistoric life was healthy, peaceful, or desirable. Civilization has reduced many forms of violence, mortality, hunger, exposure, drudgery, and isolation. Humans are unusually plastic niche constructors; culture is part of our biology, not its opposite. The harder claim is narrower: **an environment can be materially comfortable and still be behaviorally hostile to the animal living inside it.**

The "next pandemic" in the title is therefore not a pathogen. It is a metaphor for a diffuse epidemic of cultural and environmental mismatch: anxiety without a predator, loneliness in crowds, compulsive checking without information scarcity, status panic without a stable village, exhaustion without physical exertion, appetite without seasons, stimulation without satisfaction, convenience without agency, and entertainment that can continue long after pleasure has stopped.

## Reader promise

A book about the zoo we built without noticing the bars.

The opening image is not a metaphor pasted on at the end. The manuscript will begin in animal welfare science: what captive stereotypies are, what they are not, when environmental enrichment reduces them, why some persist after conditions improve, and why "crazy zoo animal" is too crude a description. Only then does the book turn the glass around.

Each chapter asks a concrete environmental question. What happens to circadian biology when noon and midnight become visually similar? What happens to status systems when the comparison group expands from dozens of people to billions? What happens to appetite when scarcity-calibrated reward systems meet engineered abundance? What happens to social mammals when belonging becomes optional, mobile, asynchronous, and partly parasocial? What happens to attention when other institutions can buy, measure, and optimize its capture? What happens to agency when friction disappears faster than purpose appears?

The commercial hook is simple enough to say at dinner and difficult enough to spend a book proving:

> We keep asking what is wrong with people. *Zoocosis* asks what kind of enclosure would make a normal animal behave this way.

That line is a hypothesis, not an acquittal of individual responsibility and not a universal explanation for mental illness. The book has to earn every causal step.

## Evidence discipline

1. **Established / well supported:** captive-animal stereotypies and abnormal repetitive behaviors exist; environmental conditions, behavioral opportunity, control, developmental history, husbandry, enrichment, and species-specific needs can affect their expression; human sleep, circadian timing, physical activity, social connection, work conditions, food environments, and other environmental factors can affect health and wellbeing.
2. **Credible but incomplete:** evolutionary-mismatch accounts of particular modern problems; causal effects of specific digital environments, status exposure, urban form, social fragmentation, persuasive design, or reduced contact with nature; analogies between human compulsive behavior and captive-animal stereotypy where mechanisms have not been demonstrated to be the same.
3. **Disputed / not established:** claims that "modern life" causes a single mental-health epidemic; claims that smartphones, cities, processed food, pornography, social media, medication, or any other single factor explains contemporary distress; claims that zoo animals and humans share one pathology; simplistic dopamine explanations; romantic claims that ancestral humans were psychologically well adapted or broadly happier.
4. **Author synthesis / open conjecture:** **Zoocosis** as a useful umbrella for cultural-environmental mismatch; the claim that societies should evaluate environments partly by the behaviors they reliably evoke from ordinary humans; a proposed Zoocosis Index measuring environmental pressures rather than diagnosing people.

## Proposed 20-chapter architecture

1. **Pacing the Glass** — begin with the lion, bear, elephant, giraffe, primate, and the scientific argument over stereotypic behavior. What does repetitive behavior actually tell us about an environment?
2. **Animal in a Human Cage** — Homo sapiens as an animal and civilization as a constructed behavioral ecology. The cage does not need bars if it can shape default behavior.
3. **Mismatch** — the useful core of evolutionary mismatch and its greatest danger: turning every modern annoyance into an ancestral just-so story.
4. **Supernormal** — Tinbergen's old insight meets engineered abundance. Foods, images, sexual cues, novelty, outrage, jackpots, metrics, and notifications can exaggerate signals that once arrived in bounded doses.
5. **Infinite Status** — a primate status system evolved for finite communities encounters global wealth visibility, celebrity, follower counts, professional rankings, dating markets, and continuous comparison.
6. **No Tribe Big Enough** — loneliness, community fragmentation, kin distance, declining associational life, parasociality, and the difference between contact and belonging.
7. **A Sun We Replaced** — daylight, darkness, sleep, shift work, electric light, screens, and the strange fact that modern humans can live for weeks without seeing dawn.
8. **Bodies Without Miles** — chairs, cars, elevators, delivery, desk work, exercise as a scheduled simulation of movement, and the limits of blaming sedentary life for everything.
9. **Food Without Seasons** — appetite systems inside engineered abundance: hyperpalatability, portion size, convenience, marketing, cheap calories, and why obesity cannot be reduced to weak will or one nutrient.
10. **Attention Farm** — the industrialization of interruption. Variable reinforcement, feeds, autoplay, notifications, advertising markets, and the evidence boundary around "addiction" language.
11. **Work Without Endpoints** — inboxes that refill, invisible output, remote availability, effort-reward imbalance, low control, burnout, and labor whose natural stopping cues have disappeared.
12. **Children in the Enclosure** — indoor childhood, risk, play, school, phones, social development, surveillance, sleep, and why current evidence on youth technology requires more care than culture-war certainty.
13. **Sex in the Marketplace** — dating platforms, pornography, partner abundance as interface, fertility decline, loneliness, assortative mating, and the danger of deterministic evolutionary psychology.
14. **Crowded and Alone** — cities can produce both extraordinary connection and extraordinary isolation. Housing, third places, commute time, noise, green space, public realm, and social trust.
15. **Comfort That Removes Agency** — when convenience removes competence, friction, feedback, movement, and small acts of self-efficacy; and when convenience genuinely liberates rather than diminishes.
16. **Chemical Quiet** — psychiatric medication, pain relief, stimulants, alcohol, cannabis, sedatives, and the temptation to treat environmental arguments as anti-treatment arguments. They are not. Symptoms deserve treatment even when environments also deserve indictment.
17. **Enrichment** — what animal welfare science can responsibly contribute: choice, control, complexity, novelty, social opportunity, foraging/problem solving, movement, refuge, and species-appropriate behavior. Where the analogy breaks is as important as where it works.
18. **Rewilding the Human** — not caves, loincloths, paleo mythology, or abandoning medicine. Designing modern environments around light, movement, attention, agency, community, green space, physical competence, and real stopping points.
19. **Zoocosis Index** — a speculative environmental dashboard: sleep/light integrity, movement opportunity, social connection, autonomy/control, attention capture, green/nature contact, work strain, status exposure, child independence, and other measurable pressures. It scores enclosures, not people.
20. **Open the Gate** — civilization is the human superpower. The argument is not to escape it but to become conscious designers of the behavioral habitat we have already built.

## Case and reporting spine

The manuscript should move repeatedly between animal welfare and human environments without pretending equivalence. Candidate reporting objects include zoo stereotypies and enrichment programs; laboratory evidence on controllability and stress; natural experiments involving daylight and shift work; walkability and active transport; social isolation and mortality literature; third-place decline; work-control and effort-reward studies; food-environment research; gambling-machine and variable-reward design; platform notification and feed experiments; youth-development evidence; urban green-space interventions; and communities that have deliberately redesigned streets, schools, workplaces, or social institutions.

The strongest scenes will be physical. Stand where the lion paces. Watch what happens when enrichment arrives. Sit in the windowless office at 4 p.m. Walk the six-lane arterial to a grocery store a quarter-mile away. Visit the school where children are never outside alone. Enter the casino designed without clocks. Compare those environments with places that alter the defaults and then ask whether behavior changes.

## Strongest competing explanations

The book must not smuggle cultural pessimism in as biology. Several rival explanations could account for apparent modern distress:

- improved recognition, reporting, diagnostic access, and reduced stigma can raise measured prevalence without an equivalent rise in underlying disease;
- inequality, trauma, housing insecurity, discrimination, war, infectious disease, chronic illness, and economic precarity may explain outcomes better than broad "mismatch" narratives;
- different technologies have different effects, effects can be small and heterogeneous, and digital communication can relieve isolation as well as create it;
- humans are extraordinarily culturally plastic and may adapt to novel environments far more effectively than a simple Stone-Age-brain story allows;
- modern environments provide enormous protective benefits that historical nostalgia routinely erases.

If these explanations outperform the Zoocosis frame, the book should say so.

## Defeat conditions

The central thesis weakens if environmental variables such as sleep/light disruption, low movement opportunity, weak social connection, low control, chronic attention capture, status exposure, or degraded public environments explain little after conventional socioeconomic and clinical factors are controlled; if environmental redesign produces no meaningful behavioral or health improvement; if captive-animal research proves too mechanistically remote to illuminate human environmental design; or if the supposed "pandemic" mostly disappears after correcting for measurement and diagnostic changes.

A good book must be able to lose its argument.

## Book boundary with *Zoonosis*

**Zoonosis: Nature's Deadliest Diseases** owns pathogens, reservoirs, spillover, bridge hosts, farms, markets, epidemic amplification, One Health, and literal pandemic prevention.

**Zoocosis: The Next Pandemic** owns the human enclosure: culture, technology, architecture, work, light, movement, food, attention, status, belonging, agency, and behavioral design.

The titles rhyme because the books ask parallel questions about environments, not because they are the same book.
