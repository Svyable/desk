# Plates, Press, and Paper

The one-dollar bill at the Smithsonian is fake.

That is what makes it useful.

It is dated February 16, 1807, and modeled on a note of the Union Bank of Boston. The design promises payment to the bearer on demand. It carries the names a banknote is supposed to carry. It looks, at first encounter, like a small unit in an expanding American financial system.

Across the face of the surviving note, somebody later stamped the answer twice: COUNTERFEIT.

The man associated with making it was Stephen Burroughs, a counterfeiter whose career had begun before the Constitution and carried across the border into Canada. Burroughs had passed debased coin, escaped confinement repeatedly, published a memoir that helped turn him into a kind of antihero, and learned a commercial fact that should have been obvious to every early American banker: the more kinds of legitimate money there were, the more room existed for illegitimate money to hide among them.

The United States in 1807 did not have a uniform federal paper currency. It had a monetary ecosystem.

Coins circulated. Foreign coins circulated. State-chartered banks issued notes. The First Bank of the United States issued notes. Local banks appeared in growing numbers. A merchant might see paper from institutions in other towns or states and have to decide what discount, if any, to apply. A traveler could carry a note that was trustworthy near the issuing bank and less welcome as distance increased. Designs varied because issuers varied. Financial information traveled more slowly than the paper itself.

To a counterfeiter, heterogeneity was camouflage.

This is easy to underestimate from a world in which a dollar bill is not supposed to reveal which commercial bank printed it. Modern Americans can be annoyed when a card terminal rejects a payment, but the basic unit of account is standardized. In the early nineteenth century the person behind a shop counter had to perform more of the monetary system’s verification personally.

Who issued this?

Does that bank exist?

Is it solvent?

Is the note genuine?

How far away is redemption?

What is it worth here?

Those are different questions. The paper could pass one and fail another.

Burroughs exploited the authenticity question. Weak banks and distant banks complicated the value question. Together they created a market in which a false note from a real institution might look more reassuring than a genuine note from an institution the recipient did not know.

That inversion is the central scam of 1806–1815.

The individual counterfeiter matters less than the environment that made counterfeiting an industry. America had begun to privatize one of the visible surfaces of money. A banknote carried the credibility of a bank, and bank credibility was local knowledge trying to become portable.

Portable trust always has a translation problem.

Near the bank, a note could be presented for payment. Far away, the holder depended on intermediaries, published note reporters, correspondent relationships, reputation, and accumulated experience. The value of information therefore changed with geography. The farther the note traveled from the institution whose promise it represented, the more expensive verification became.

Distance acted like a transaction fee on truth.

The banking system had already experienced counterfeiting before Burroughs’s 1807 note. A National Monetary Commission history preserved by the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis records that counterfeit notes of the First Bank of the United States had become alarmingly common by 1794, especially five-dollar bills. The Bank of North America and the Bank of the United States jointly offered a large reward and sent officials toward Winchester, Virginia, where counterfeiters were believed to be operating. Congress made counterfeiting notes of the national bank a federal felony in 1798.

Lawmaking did not remove the economics.

A law can increase the expected cost of being caught. It cannot make a design impossible to copy or make every recipient an expert. Counterfeiters responded to the same variables legitimate issuers did: printing quality, distribution, reputation, denominations, and the probability that a note would return quickly to someone capable of recognizing it.

Small notes were particularly useful because people needed them for ordinary exchange. A one-dollar transaction does not invite the same scrutiny as a fortune. The fake could move through many hands before reaching an expert.

The Smithsonian’s Burroughs note makes this visible. It imitates a real bank rather than inventing an entirely fictional institution. That choice matters. The easiest confidence to steal is confidence someone else has already built.

A fake bank with a ridiculous name has to create credibility from nothing. A counterfeit Union Bank note arrives wearing borrowed credibility. The recipient’s prior knowledge of the real institution works for the attacker.

This is brand impersonation two centuries before phishing.

The modern digital version is familiar. A fraudulent email does not create a new delivery company; it copies the logo of the company you already use. A fake login page does not explain why you should trust it; it imitates the bank whose site you intended to visit. A caller spoofs the number of a government agency. A scam token borrows a ticker or name that resembles a known project. The attack works by shortening the distance between recognition and consent.

Burroughs did the same with engraving and paper.

The analogy should not be stretched too far. Early banknotes were physical bearer instruments with very different legal and technical properties from digital credentials. But the psychological mechanism is durable: recognition substitutes for verification until something gives the holder a reason to slow down.

The early note economy created another problem that digital systems know well. False positives are expensive too.

If merchants become so worried about counterfeits that they reject unfamiliar genuine notes, commerce suffers. If a bank redesigns notes too often, holders may distrust older genuine issues. If every transaction requires elaborate authentication, the payment system loses the convenience that made paper useful. Security has to be good enough without making ordinary use impossible.

The solution therefore becomes a moving compromise. Issuers add complexity. Counterfeiters learn it. Merchants acquire guides. Counterfeiters improve. Authorities circulate warnings. Designs change. Professional detectors emerge. Reputation networks grow.

The system learns because the fake exists.

Burroughs’s life is sometimes told as comic Americana, the adventures of a clever rogue playing tricks on institutions no more respectable than he was. His own memoir encouraged the pose. There is something seductive about the counterfeiter as folk hero, particularly in a banking system where real institutions failed and note holders could be ruined without anybody having forged a thing.

That ambiguity deserves to survive.

If a bank issues more promises than it can redeem, the note is authentic but the promise may still fail. If a counterfeiter prints a beautiful imitation of a solvent bank’s note, the promise is false even if the note circulates at full value for weeks. The holder cares about both, but the law treats them differently because one failure can arise from business risk or mismanagement while the other begins with intentional falsification.

Scam history becomes unserious when every loss is collapsed into the same moral category.

The period around the War of 1812 demonstrates why. The expiration of the First Bank’s charter in 1811 removed a national institution that had helped impose some discipline on state banks. Wartime finance then intensified pressure on the system. Banks expanded in number and lending. In 1814, outside New England, many suspended specie payments — meaning holders could no longer reliably turn paper bank liabilities into gold or silver on demand.

A suspension did not make every banknote counterfeit. The note could be genuine and the bank real. Yet the practical experience of the holder changed. The promise printed on paper was no longer immediately performable.

This is the difference between fraud and fragility.

The distinction will matter for the entire book. A fragile institution can collapse without anyone committing fraud. A fraud can exploit fragility. Sometimes executives cross from one to the other as they conceal losses, fabricate assets, or make promises they know cannot be kept. The historian’s job is to identify the crossing rather than assume it.

The 1806–1815 chapter earns its place because the American payment system forced ordinary people to live near that boundary. They held private notes whose authenticity, credit quality, and redemption value all required judgment.

Information businesses emerged around the uncertainty. Banknote reporters and counterfeit detectors published descriptions and quoted discounts. Merchants kept track of failed institutions and altered notes. Cashiers learned printing details. The marketplace created a secondary layer whose product was not money but information about money.

That is an important institutional development. When a trust system becomes too complex for ordinary users to evaluate directly, an assurance industry appears.

Auditors will later do this for corporate accounts. Credit-rating agencies will do it for debt. App stores will do it for software. Certificate authorities will do it for websites. Identity vendors will do it for users. Exchanges will do it for listed securities. Review platforms will do it for sellers. Each intermediary compresses a difficult verification problem into a simpler signal.

Each intermediary can then become the next attack surface.

Early banknote detectors were imperfect. Information could be stale. A bank might fail after a guide was printed. Counterfeiters could alter genuine notes to higher denominations. Unscrupulous people could circulate notes far from the issuing bank precisely because information weakened with distance.

The system was trying to solve a latency problem with paper.

This is why the story of Stephen Burroughs is larger than Stephen Burroughs. He did not invent decentralized banknote issuance. He did not create the communication limits of a geographically expanding country. He did not make banks fail. He found a place where the speed of the instrument exceeded the speed of verification.

That is one of the most reliable habitats for a scam.

A new instrument can often travel faster than the norms, databases, laws, and professional practices required to authenticate it. The first years of an innovation therefore contain a peculiar opportunity. Users know enough to recognize the thing but not enough to recognize every failure mode.

In 1807 an American knew what a banknote was.

That did not mean he knew this banknote.

The problem intensified as the country expanded westward. A note issued in Boston or Philadelphia could enter a place where few people had direct experience with the bank. New banks could exploit the same distance deliberately by putting notes into circulation far from home, making redemption inconvenient. The next chapter will move from the counterfeiter’s plate to the bank’s own balance sheet, because the most destabilizing paper did not always come from a criminal press.

Some of it came from chartered institutions printing perfectly genuine notes and arranging the system so that holders would have difficulty asking for the money behind them.

The mechanism acquired several names over the nineteenth century. “Wildcat banking” would become the most famous, though historians argue about how broadly the label should be used and how fraudulent the typical free bank actually was. In 1820 a contemporary observer described an extreme practice with a more tactile name: the saddle-bag bank. Its notes were carried around the countryside in saddlebags and exchanged for the obligations of landowners.

The bank no longer had to counterfeit another institution’s promise.

It could exploit its own.

Before reaching that system, it is worth looking once more at the counterfeit note in the museum.

The stamps identifying it as false were added because somebody eventually knew. That is the comforting part of a preserved counterfeit: history has already solved the puzzle for us. The object now sits under controlled conditions, described by experts, cataloged with provenance. Nobody viewing it has to decide whether to accept it for dinner.

Its first holders did not have the label.

That is the position a useful history of scams should try to recover. Not the moment after the collapse, when the newspaper headline supplies the answer, but the moment before, when the object is plausible and the verification system incomplete.

The fake note looks like money because money has taught you what money looks like.

The next step in the confidence game is more difficult.

What happens when the note is real?
