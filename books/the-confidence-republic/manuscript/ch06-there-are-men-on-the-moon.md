# There Are Men on the Moon

On August 25, 1835, readers of *The Sun* in New York were told that one of the world’s most respected astronomers had found life on the moon.

Not microbes.

Not a suggestion buried in a technical paper.

Animals.

Forests.

Beavers that walked upright.

Unicorn-like creatures.

And, eventually, winged humanoids.

The newspaper was real. The astronomer was real. The expedition was real. The telescope was not. The discoveries were not. The journal from which the reports were supposedly reprinted was no longer publishing.

The story worked because almost everything around the lie had already been made plausible by truth.

Sir John Herschel really had traveled to the Cape of Good Hope to make astronomical observations. His father, William Herschel, had speculated about inhabited worlds and reported what he thought might be lunar volcanic activity. Telescopes were improving. Scientific societies and journals were carrying observations across oceans. Newspapers were discovering that an urban mass audience would pay a penny for astonishing information about places they would never see themselves.

Then Richard Adams Locke, a writer for *The Sun*, assembled those true pieces into a false instrument of witness.

For this book, that makes the Great Moon Hoax more important than its bat-men.

The decade’s scam is not merely a silly story that fooled some nineteenth-century readers. It is a demonstration of what happens when information becomes cheap enough to scale faster than the reader’s ability to verify its chain of custody.

The penny press was doing something economically radical. Older newspapers had often been relatively expensive, partisan, commercial, or aimed at narrower readerships. Papers like *The Sun* sold for a penny and sought circulation among a broad urban public. The business model rewarded attention. Street sales mattered. Novelty mattered. Crime, spectacle, human interest, and astonishing claims could pull readers who had no subscription relationship with the publisher.

That did not make the penny press fraudulent. It made news more democratic.

It also changed what a lie could become.

A rumor told in a tavern might reach twenty people. A fabricated scientific report printed in a mass newspaper could reach thousands in the morning and be copied by other papers before the original reader had any practical way to ask the astronomer in South Africa whether he had, in fact, found bipedal beavers.

The verification path was slower than the publication path.

That gap is the trust surface.

The six-part series carried the grand title “Great Astronomical Discoveries Lately Made by Sir John Herschel.” It claimed to have been reprinted from a supplement to the *Edinburgh Journal of Science*. That attribution supplied the architecture of seriousness: named expert, named journal, distant observatory, technical apparatus, scientific prose.

A reader did not have to believe *The Sun* alone.

The reader was invited to believe a chain.

Herschel saw it.

A scientific reporter recorded it.

A respected journal printed it.

The New York newspaper reproduced it.

The chain was invented, but it looked like the chain by which real knowledge increasingly arrived.

This is an important transition from the first five chapters. Paper money, deeds, charters, and banknotes asked people to trust documents because those documents represented value or authority. The Great Moon Hoax asked people to trust a document because it represented witness.

The newspaper was becoming an instrument for seeing at a distance.

Once that role exists, counterfeit witness becomes valuable.

Locke’s articles exploited more than the name Herschel. They imitated the texture of scientific specificity. The fictional telescope was described through technical details. Lunar geography was narrated with observational confidence. The creatures accumulated gradually rather than appearing all at once. Early installments offered enough strange but not yet absurd detail to acclimate the reader. By the time winged humanlike beings appeared, the series had already built an internal world.

A good con rarely asks for the impossible first.

It walks the victim there.

The Library of Congress preserves an 1835 print associated with the hoax showing the lunar menagerie: fantastical animals, including human-bat creatures, presented as if copied from scientific sketches. The image matters because it added another layer of apparent mediation. Readers were no longer merely hearing that someone had seen something. They could see a visual representation of what the observer supposedly saw.

A picture is not evidence of the underlying event.

It is evidence that somebody made a picture.

That distinction seems obvious after photography, film, television, Photoshop, and synthetic media. It was already relevant in 1835. Illustration extended the false witness into a second medium.

The Smithsonian’s later cataloging of the hoax notes that the articles pretended to derive from a scientific supplement and were subsequently collected and republished. The Library of Congress describes the series as six articles written by Locke under the invented identity of a reporter called Dr. Andrew Grant. The historical details matter because the hoax’s credibility depended on the existence of a professional role between reader and astronomer.

The reader did not need to know Herschel personally.

The reader needed to know what an astronomical report looked like.

This is how trust scales. We stop verifying people and begin verifying formats, institutions, titles, conventions, and reputations.

That is usually progress.

No functioning society can require every reader to reproduce every observation. You do not rebuild a telescope before reading an astronomy article. You do not rerun a clinical trial before taking medicine. You do not inspect a warehouse before accepting an audited inventory figure. You rely on layered institutions that make direct verification unnecessary most of the time.

The scammer asks which layer the public has learned to accept as a substitute for direct evidence.

In 1835, the scientific-news form was becoming one of those layers.

There is an important complication. Locke appears to have understood the series at least partly as satire. Later accounts describe his target as the extravagant “plurality of worlds” speculation associated with writers such as the Reverend Thomas Dick. The Library of Congress notes that the story might, under a different reception, have been remembered as a great moon satire rather than a great moon hoax.

That ambiguity should not be cleaned up for narrative convenience.

Satire can be written in a form meant to be recognized as satire. It can also be written so plausibly that many readers accept it as fact. Nineteenth-century conventions around hoax, humbug, satire, and newspaper entertainment were not identical to modern journalistic ethics. A reader in 1835 did not inhabit a media culture with today’s labels separating news, opinion, sponsored content, parody, and fiction.

The chapter therefore does not depend on proving that Locke woke up intending to defraud every reader.

It depends on what the publication system demonstrated.

The story traveled as factual news.

Other newspapers reprinted it.

Readers debated it.

Illustrations circulated.

The fame of *The Sun* increased.

The supposed scientific chain was false.

Those facts are sufficient to expose the mechanism.

The hoax also reveals how credibility can be borrowed from a person who has no opportunity to participate in the transaction. Herschel was in South Africa. His reputation became collateral in New York without his consent.

That pattern will recur constantly.

Cassie Chadwick will borrow Andrew Carnegie’s name.

Financial promoters will borrow respected directors.

Television producers will borrow the authority of the camera.

Corporate frauds will borrow auditors.

Phishing attacks will borrow banks and government agencies.

Synthetic media will borrow faces and voices.

The person whose credibility is being spent may be entirely absent.

This creates an asymmetry. Reputation takes years to build and seconds to copy.

The more famous the expert, the more useful the expert becomes to someone who can impersonate a connection to him.

That was already true before electronic communication. The hoax needed only print.

The Great Moon Hoax also benefited from a peculiar characteristic of scientific progress: genuine discovery expands the range of claims people are willing to consider.

When technology is static, an extraordinary claim can be rejected because the supposed instrument does not exist. When instruments are visibly improving, yesterday’s impossibility becomes tomorrow’s headline. Steamships cross oceans faster. Railroads accelerate travel. telegraphy is on the horizon. Astronomers are building larger telescopes. The public learns that experts routinely discover things that would have sounded absurd a generation earlier.

Progress increases the option value of plausibility.

This can make periods of rapid innovation unusually fertile for fraud.

The liar does not need the audience to be scientifically ignorant. He can exploit the audience’s correct understanding that science surprises people.

The moon series did this elegantly. Herschel really was a major astronomer doing serious work with advanced instruments. The false report therefore attached itself to the true meta-story: observational science was expanding what humanity could know.

The same structure will appear in wireless, aviation, biotechnology, computing, and artificial intelligence. The underlying field is genuinely moving. Investors, consumers, and journalists know enough to understand that a breakthrough is possible but not enough to independently evaluate whether this particular breakthrough is real.

The true sentence creates room for the lie.

The newspaper’s economic incentive mattered too, though it should be handled carefully. Popular retellings sometimes assert precise circulation jumps or claim that the hoax single-handedly made *The Sun* the largest newspaper in the world. Those numbers vary across later accounts and are difficult to isolate from the paper’s broader rise.

The safer claim is also the more useful one: the series attracted enormous attention and was repeatedly reprinted. The attention itself demonstrated that astonishing claims could be monetized through circulation.

That is a new kind of confidence game.

In the banknote chapters, the scammer wants the victim to accept the false paper once.

In the media chapter, the publisher may benefit every time the claim is repeated, argued about, mocked, defended, or purchased.

Belief is no longer the only valuable outcome.

Attention can be enough.

This distinction will become central to later media systems. A deceptive headline can be profitable even if some readers distrust it. A television stunt can attract viewers because they suspect manipulation. A social-media rumor can travel because people are outraged at its falsity. A conspiracy can create community through argument about evidence. The information market can reward claims that generate response rather than claims that survive verification.

Barnum, in the next chapter, will make an industry out of that ambiguity.

The moon hoax arrived before his American Museum became the definitive factory of American humbug, but the logic is already visible. The audience is not always buying truth.

Sometimes it is buying the experience of deciding.

That makes regulation and ethics more difficult. If a showman says, in effect, “Come see for yourself,” does the presence of doubt cure the deception? If a newspaper prints satire in the form of scientific reportage and readers believe it, who bears responsibility for the misunderstanding? If an advertisement contains the literal truth but is designed to create a false overall impression, is disclosure enough?

These questions do not belong only to modern consumer protection. They are old problems of scalable persuasion.

The Great Moon Hoax marks the point in this book when verification moves beyond the object.

A counterfeit bill can be examined.

A corrupt land grant can be traced.

A banknote can be presented for redemption.

But a claim about the moon cannot be resolved by the ordinary reader through physical inspection. The evidence is necessarily mediated. The reader needs institutions that certify not merely whether the paper is genuine but whether the reporting process behind the paper occurred.

Modern journalism develops norms for this: named sources, corrections, editorial independence, attribution, documentary evidence, conflicts disclosure, professional reputation, and competition among outlets. Science develops peer review, reproducibility, institutional affiliation, data sharing, and method sections. None is perfect. Together they reduce the number of claims a reader has to verify from scratch.

In 1835, those systems were less mature and mass circulation was accelerating.

The result was a six-day lunar civilization.

The hoax was exposed. Herschel’s reputation survived. The moon remained uninhabited by bat-men, at least according to every observation since.

But the important invention did not disappear.

Americans had learned that a newspaper could make a distant event feel witnessed.

They would not give that up because one story was false.

They would buy more newspapers.

They would demand more news.

They would become dependent on mediated evidence for politics, science, war, crime, finance, and everything else happening beyond the end of the street.

The solution to the hoax was not to abandon mass information.

It was to build norms that made mass information worth trusting.

Those norms would always be incomplete because attention is valuable and ambiguity sells.

P. T. Barnum understood that before almost anyone.

Seven years after the moon filled with impossible animals, he began advertising a creature closer to home.

It had the upper body of something like a small primate and the tail of a fish.

Some experts said it was artificial.

Others were said to believe.

Barnum’s most profitable move was not to settle the question.

It was to sell admission to the argument.
