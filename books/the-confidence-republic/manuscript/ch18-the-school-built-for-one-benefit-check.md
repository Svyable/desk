# The School Built for One Benefit Check

The GI Bill worked.

That fact is essential.

Millions of veterans used it to study, train, buy homes, and build postwar lives. The education provisions expanded access to colleges and vocational training on a scale the country had never attempted.

The money was real.

The students were real.

The educational opportunity was real.

So were the schools built mainly to capture the benefit.

After the Servicemen’s Readjustment Act of 1944 created generous education and training support, a private market rushed to serve veterans. Much of that market delivered useful instruction. Some of it did not.

New proprietary schools appeared quickly. Courses multiplied. Programs learned that an eligible veteran arrived with a federal payer behind him. Congress and the Veterans Administration began receiving evidence of schools offering weak, inflated, unnecessary, or sometimes essentially worthless training while collecting government money tied to enrollment.

By 1950 congressional investigators were describing a substantial number of new schools as fly-by-night operations. A 1951 House investigation into GI schools assembled hundreds of pages of testimony about the abuse surface. The government’s problem was not that education had suddenly become fake.

It was that eligibility had become a credibility signal.

If the government would pay, the school looked approved.

If the school looked approved, the veteran could reasonably infer that somebody had determined the course was worth buying.

That inference was stronger than the actual verification system could support.

This is the trust surface of 1946–1955.

A federal benefit does two things at once.

It subsidizes demand.

It certifies a market.

The first effect is explicit.

The second can be accidental.

When government money follows a person into a private institution, the public naturally asks why the institution is eligible to receive it. The answer may be narrow: it satisfied statutory and administrative requirements.

The customer often hears something broader:

this is a good school.

That gap can be monetized.

The chapter title uses one imaginary school because the real abuse was diffuse. There was no single postwar institution whose name became synonymous with the problem in the way Ponzi’s did. That is a weakness as narrative history and a strength as institutional evidence.

The scam was a market design.

A school could be created because the payer existed.

It did not need enough ordinary customers willing to spend their own unrestricted money on the training.

It needed enough eligible veterans.

This distinction matters.

A normal private school has at least one crude market test.

Someone chooses to pay the price.

That does not prove quality. Consumers can be mistaken, education is difficult to evaluate before purchase, and schools can exploit information asymmetry even without subsidies.

But a third-party payer changes the signal.

The student chooses.

The government pays.

The school receives.

Those three roles can create a triangle in which no one participant feels the full price of a bad decision.

The student may reasonably think the government has screened the school.

The government may reasonably think the veteran’s choice is evidence of value.

The school points to enrollment as proof of demand.

Each party reads another party’s action as verification.

The loop can sustain a weak provider.

This is the same recursive trust problem Cassie Chadwick exploited in banking.

Other people’s confidence becomes evidence for confidence.

The postwar training market gave the recursion a public subsidy.

The government’s eventual response is one of the most interesting verification mechanisms in the book.

Congress developed what became known as the 85/15 rule.

The logic was simple.

If a training program is genuinely valuable, at least some people who do not arrive with government-paid tuition should be willing to choose it.

The current Department of Veterans Affairs describes the rule as a safeguard created in response to predatory school abuses after the original GI Bill. In broad terms, a program cannot fill essentially every seat with students whose costs are being supported by VA or the school itself; some portion must be self-supported.

That is a market test embedded inside a benefit program.

It does not ask a federal official to determine whether every lesson is good.

It asks for evidence that customers outside the subsidy channel will pay.

That is elegant because it recognizes the limits of administrative knowledge.

The government cannot inspect every classroom every day.

It can require an external signal.

Fifteen percent is not a sacred number.

The principle is the interesting part.

Verification can use an independent constituency.

A company that claims customers should have customers who are not related parties.

A product that claims market demand should have buyers who are not being paid to buy it.

A research result should survive another laboratory.

A marketplace should contain participants who are not controlled by the marketplace operator.

A school funded by a benefit should attract at least some people who would choose it without that benefit.

The independent group acts like the external confirmation in an audit.

Reality enters from outside the closed loop.

The postwar school abuses also expose a recurring problem with outcome timing.

Education is a delayed product.

You pay now.

You discover value later.

A bad meal can reveal itself before you leave the restaurant.

A bad training program may consume months of benefits before the veteran discovers that the credential has little labor-market value.

That delay increases the importance of ex ante verification.

But ex ante educational quality is difficult to measure.

Facilities can be inspected.

Teacher qualifications can be checked.

Curricula can be reviewed.

Placement claims can be audited.

None of those measures perfectly answers the question the student cares about:

will this training make my life better?

Fraud enters the measurement gap.

A school can own equipment it rarely uses.

It can employ an instructor who appears on paperwork more often than in the classroom.

It can advertise jobs that few graduates obtain.

It can stretch a simple skill into a long program because the payer reimburses time.

It can charge the supported student more than an ordinary customer would willingly pay.

It can optimize against the eligibility rule rather than the educational outcome.

This is Goodhart’s law before the term became fashionable.

When a proxy becomes a target, institutions learn to produce the proxy.

Government says approved school.

The operator builds whatever satisfies approval.

Government says enrolled veteran.

The operator recruits veterans.

Government pays for course length.

The operator lengthens the course.

Government measures completion.

The operator makes completion easy.

The metric is not useless.

It is incomplete.

The distinction between subsidy and endorsement is therefore crucial.

A benefit program may intend to expand choice without certifying every choice as wise.

But the recipient experiences the institution through one interface.

Eligible.

That status carries more semantic weight than the statute may intend.

Modern government programs face the same problem.

Medicare provider numbers.

Federal student-aid eligibility.

Approved contractors.

Licensed childcare.

Certified minority-owned businesses.

Registered investment advisers.

App-store approval.

Verified accounts.

Every badge begins as a bounded statement.

Users turn it into a general statement of trust.

Fraudsters live in the expansion.

This chapter also needs another boundary.

The original GI Bill’s educational history cannot be reduced to predatory schools.

The program transformed American higher education and mobility for millions. It also operated through a society structured by racial discrimination. Black veterans often faced segregated institutions, discriminatory local administration, housing barriers, and unequal access to the opportunities the statute nominally offered.

Those injustices are not the same mechanism as fly-by-night school fraud.

They belong in the historical frame because a trust system can fail in more than one direction.

One participant may be admitted into a market that is too weakly screened.

Another may be wrongly excluded from the best part of the market altogether.

Better verification does not solve unequal access by itself.

Institutional design has to answer both questions.

Who gets in?

What are they getting into?

The abuses of the late 1940s and early 1950s forced Congress to tighten standards around schools and training programs. Existing institutions were not automatically perfect, and new institutions were not automatically fraudulent. The policy challenge was to suppress organizations created primarily around benefit capture without freezing the educational market into whatever already existed before the war.

That tradeoff appears throughout regulation.

Require too little evidence and opportunists flood the subsidy.

Require too much history and capital and legitimate new entrants cannot compete.

The goal is not maximum friction.

It is discriminating friction.

A two-year operating-history requirement, a market-share test, accreditation, outcome reporting, price parity, and inspection each test different failure modes.

No single rule establishes quality.

Together they make it harder to build a school for one benefit check.

This case therefore advances the book’s central argument in an important way.

The scam no longer needs to forge the verifier.

It can satisfy the verifier’s rule while violating the purpose behind the rule.

That is specification gaming in institutional form.

The school asks:

What must be true for the government to pay?

Not:

What must be true for the veteran to receive a useful education?

Those questions overlap.

They are not identical.

Whenever an institution converts a broad human goal into eligibility criteria, a new market appears around the criteria.

Tax codes create tax planning.

Procurement rules create bid strategies.

Insurance contracts create billing strategies.

Search rankings create optimization industries.

School accountability creates test preparation.

AI benchmarks create benchmark optimization.

Some of that adaptation is productive.

Some is gaming.

The difference lies in whether satisfying the metric also satisfies the purpose.

The GI Bill school problem is therefore not ancient history.

It is an early large-scale example of public money creating a measurable interface and private actors learning to optimize against it.

The government responded by demanding evidence from outside the subsidy loop.

That is a durable design move.

But the next chapter will show a different problem.

What happens when the product is not a school or a financial statement but reality itself?

Television will place a contest in front of millions of people.

The camera will be genuine.

The contestants will be genuine.

The questions will be genuine.

The answers will already be known.
