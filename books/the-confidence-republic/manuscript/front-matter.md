## Author’s Note

A country can survive people lying to one another. It is harder to survive when nobody can tell which pieces of paper are real.

That was an American problem before there was a United States in the modern sense of the phrase. The Continental Congress could declare independence in 1776, authorize armies, appoint diplomats, and ask thirteen states to behave as one political project. What it could not do was reach into the pocket of a farmer in Pennsylvania, a merchant in Connecticut, or a soldier outside New York and make a piece of paper worth what was printed on it. That required confidence.

Confidence is usually described as a feeling. In an economy it is closer to infrastructure. You take a dollar because you believe the next person will take it. You buy land because you believe the deed describes a claim the law will defend. You deposit money because you believe the balance on the screen is an obligation rather than a suggestion. You buy a stock because you believe the financial statements refer to a company that more or less exists as described. You sign a contract with a person you have never met because courts, banks, auditors, databases, identity systems, reputations, and a long chain of institutions stand behind the marks on the page.

Modern life depends on an extraordinary amount of behavior like this. Most of it works. That is why the failures are so revealing.

A successful scam does not merely find a gullible person. The important ones borrow something society has already decided to trust. A counterfeiter borrows the state’s printing. A land promoter borrows the authority of a deed. A fraudulent corporation borrows the legal shell of incorporation. A rigged television show borrows the camera’s claim to be showing you what happened. An accounting fraud borrows the auditor. A Ponzi scheme borrows the appearance of investment performance. A crypto exchange borrows the interface of custody: the number on the screen looks like money held for you because every other financial interface has trained you to read it that way.

The con is parasitic on the confidence.

That observation is the organizing idea of this book. The United States is often told through wars, presidents, elections, inventions, migrations, booms, crashes, court cases, and social movements. All are legitimate ways to cut through 250 years. This book uses a stranger chronology: one scam for every decade of the American experiment, selected not because it was always the largest theft or the most famous swindler, but because it exposed something the country had newly learned to trust.

The structure is literal. Start in 1776 and count ten years at a time. The first chapter covers 1776 through 1785. The second covers 1786 through 1795. Continue that way and twenty-five chapters arrive at the end of 2025. The semiquincentennial year, 2026, sits just outside the sequence. That makes it a useful place to look back before asking what comes next.

This choice avoids a small trick that historical books often perform without admitting it. “The 1920s” feels like a natural object because a calendar gives it a name. It is not. A decade is simply ten years, and the ten-year blocks that matter to a country born in 1776 do not begin on years ending in zero. America’s twenty-fifth decade in this book is 2016 through 2025, not “the 2020s.” The awkwardness is useful. It prevents the chapter selections from collapsing into a familiar list of period clichés.

The word *scam* also needs discipline. It is broad in ordinary speech and dangerous in historical writing. People call taxes a scam, wars a scam, insurance a scam, higher education a scam, cryptocurrency a scam, Wall Street a scam, politics a scam, and sometimes anything they bought at the wrong price. That is not how the word is used here.

Every selected case has to contain a meaningful element of deception, falsification, hidden self-dealing, rigging, corrupt inducement, misappropriation, or knowingly false representation. A speculative bubble is not automatically a scam. A bank failure is not automatically fraud. A bad product is not necessarily deceptive. A policy can be disastrous without being dishonest. A company can collapse after executives make optimistic mistakes without those mistakes becoming a con. Where a case ended in a criminal conviction, the book says so. Where it involved bribery or a documented hoax but not a modern fraud conviction, the book says that instead. Where the legal category remained contested, the uncertainty stays on the page.

That standard removes some tempting stories. It also makes the remaining ones more interesting.

The second rule is that “most important” does not mean “biggest nominal loss.” Comparing a fraud measured in 1795 land claims with one measured in twenty-first-century customer deposits is mostly an exercise in false precision. The more useful question is what the episode taught Americans to verify. Did it expose a new attack surface in a system of trust? Did it travel through a technology or institution that was becoming important? Did it change law, regulation, auditing, journalism, consumer behavior, or the design of the system itself? Does the mechanism still help explain why a later fraud worked?

Those questions produce a book in which the criminal is sometimes less important than the interface.

Charles Ponzi matters, but the more durable story is that a plausible claim about an unfamiliar cross-border financial instrument could be converted into a machine for taking in new money. Bernard Madoff matters, but so do the statements, custodial assumptions, professional reputation, feeder networks, and regulatory failures that made an impossible performance record look boringly respectable. Sam Bankman-Fried matters, but the deeper chapter is about what a customer thinks the word *balance* means when an exchange displays one.

The older stories work the same way. The Yazoo land fraud of 1795 was not merely a tale of greedy speculators. It exposed the terrifying possibility that the public authority responsible for making title legible could itself be purchased. Aaron Burr’s Manhattan Company is not treated here as a criminal fraud; it was something more legally slippery and historically useful, a water charter containing a provision that permitted a banking business its public justification had helped make politically possible. The point is not to drag a modern criminal code backward. It is to watch Americans discover that a corporate charter could contain one business in its title and another in its economics.

There is also an asymmetry worth preserving from the start. Scams are evidence that trust failed in a particular place. They are not evidence that trust itself is foolish.

The easiest lesson to take from a book like this would be adolescent: everybody is lying, institutions are fake, only suckers believe anything. American history does not support that conclusion. The reason counterfeit money, forged deeds, fake inventory, rigged broadcasts, fabricated insurance policies, false financial statements, and misappropriated customer balances are useful to a criminal is that authentic versions of those things coordinate real activity every day. A counterfeit bill has no market without a real bill. A forged audit matters because an honest audit can reduce the cost of trust. A fake exchange balance matters because most financial institutions actually do treat customer liabilities as liabilities.

The confidence game is therefore not separate from the confidence republic. It lives inside it.

The pattern that emerges across 250 years is less cynical and more demanding. Every time America made trust more portable, fraud found the new portability. Every time the country made verification more scalable, someone learned how to borrow the verifier. The response was rarely to abandon trust. More often it was to build another layer: new note designs, bank supervision, securities disclosure, independent confirmation of receivables, broadcast rules, deposit guarantees, custody requirements, criminal statutes, data controls, professional standards. Those layers were imperfect. Some failed spectacularly. Then the next scandal arrived and tested them again.

This is one reason the book begins with money. During the Revolution, Continental currency did not collapse simply because British agents and Loyalists counterfeited it. Congress printed vast quantities of paper into an economy damaged by war and possessed no ordinary national taxing power capable of supporting confidence in the issue. Inflation had several causes, and counterfeiting was one of them. Pretending otherwise would turn a good opening story into bad economic history.

But the counterfeiting mattered. In 1777 George Washington forwarded an advertisement from British-held New York offering counterfeit congressional notes cheaply and boasting that they were difficult to distinguish from genuine bills. American officials arrested people carrying counterfeit currency, circulated descriptions of false notes, and worried openly about the enemy using fake money as another weapon of war. The problem was not only that prices were rising. The more intimate problem was that a person holding a bill had to inspect the object in his own hand and ask a question a nation cannot afford to leave permanently unanswered: is this ours?

That question keeps changing form.

Is this deed real?

Is this company doing what its charter says?

Is this mine worth what the assay says?

Did the contestant know the answer?

Does this warehouse contain the inventory on the receipt?

Did these policyholders exist?

Did this company earn the money it reported?

Is the money on this screen still there?

The technology improves. The question survives.

A final note concerns people. Fraud history attracts mythology. Swindlers are good copy: charming, audacious, shameless, inventive, sometimes funny at the safe distance of a century. Victims are often flattened into a mass described as greedy, naïve, or stupid. That is usually too easy. The useful question is not why some uniquely foolish person believed something ridiculous. It is what made the proposition plausible at the time it was offered. What fact was true? What institution lent credibility? What did the victim have to know, and what could the victim not realistically verify? Which safeguards existed only on paper? Which respected people were already participating?

A con that requires its victims to be idiots is not a very interesting con. The important scams recruit ordinary intelligence against incomplete information.

They also begin, more often than the caricature suggests, with a true sentence.

Paper money really did finance a revolution. Georgia really did possess enormous western land claims. New York really did need clean water. Wireless communication really was transforming the world. International postal reply coupons really did exist. Television quiz contestants really could know astonishing things. Computerized records really could make an insurer more efficient. Home ownership really could create wealth. Enron really did contain valuable businesses. Crypto really can move assets without the operating hours and settlement structure of a traditional bank.

The lie works better when it can stand beside a truth.

Across the next twenty-five chapters, that will be the recurring scene: a new mechanism becomes useful enough that Americans begin to rely on it, someone discovers what the mechanism asks users to assume, and the assumption becomes an opening.

The scandal arrives later.
