# The Exchange That Spent the Deposits

The balance was on the screen.

That did not mean the asset was where the customer thought it was.

FTX grew into one of the world’s largest cryptocurrency exchanges by making a technically unusual asset class feel operationally ordinary. Customers opened accounts. They deposited dollars, stablecoins, bitcoin, ether, and other assets. They traded through an interface. Their balances updated. Withdrawals worked—until they did not.

The exchange looked like a place where assets were held so customers could trade them.

The criminal case established something fundamentally different about billions of dollars of customer funds.

In November 2023, a federal jury convicted FTX founder Samuel Bankman-Fried on seven counts including wire fraud and conspiracies to commit wire fraud, securities fraud, commodities fraud, and money laundering. In March 2024 he was sentenced to twenty-five years in prison. The Justice Department said the scheme misappropriated billions of dollars of FTX customer funds and concealed the misuse from customers, investors, and lenders.

The legal posture is unusually clear.

This is not a chapter built around an indictment.

It is not a chapter built around a failed company and an inference of guilt.

It is built around convictions after trial and a sentencing record.

The trust surface is custody.

Custody sounds simple because physical possession is intuitive.

If I give a coat to a coat check, I expect the coat to remain available for me.

If I put cash in a safe-deposit box, I expect the contents not to fund the bank’s trading desk.

Financial custody is more abstract.

Assets may be represented in databases, omnibus wallets, bank accounts, ledgers, settlement systems, or cryptographic keys. The customer rarely knows which exact wallet or account corresponds to the balance on the screen.

The interface performs the translation.

It says:

You have 2 bitcoin.

You have $10,000.

You can withdraw this amount.

The customer experiences ownership as a number.

That creates the final trust surface in this 250-year chronology.

The record itself becomes the object.

A Continental note represented a claim on a political issuer.

A private banknote represented a redeemable promise.

An audit represented the existence of corporate assets.

An insurance database represented policies tied to real people.

An investment statement represented securities supposedly held or traded.

An exchange balance represents assets supposedly available to the customer.

Each innovation makes ownership and coordination easier by replacing constant physical inspection with a trusted representation.

FTX showed how much can go wrong when the representation and the custody arrangement diverge.

The Justice Department’s sentencing record states that Bankman-Fried repeatedly told customers, investors, and the public that customer deposits were safe, held in custody for customers, kept separate from company assets, and would not be used by FTX. It also states that he represented Alameda Research, the affiliated trading firm he founded, as not having privileged access or special treatment on the exchange.

The jury convicted him after prosecutors showed a different reality.

Customer funds were used for investments, political contributions, personal expenditures, and to repay Alameda’s loans. Alameda received special privileges inside FTX’s systems.

The customer-facing balance could remain visible while the economic resources associated with customer deposits were being used elsewhere.

That is the systems-era confidence game in its purest form.

The system says the asset is there.

The customer acts as though the asset is there.

The institution uses the asset somewhere else.

The representation survives until too many people ask to convert the record back into possession at the same time.

That is what happened in November 2022.

Questions about Alameda’s finances accelerated withdrawals from FTX. The exchange experienced a run. The gap between displayed claims and available assets became impossible to bridge. FTX filed for bankruptcy.

The speed was striking because digital finance compresses time.

A nineteenth-century bank run required people to travel to a bank.

A twentieth-century fund might face redemption requests by phone, fax, or intermediary.

A crypto exchange can face global withdrawal demands continuously through software.

Confidence can disappear at network speed.

That has a paradoxical effect.

Digital systems can make custody look more transparent because balances update instantly.

They can also make institutional fragility resolve faster because everyone can act on doubt at once.

The interface gives confidence and accelerates its reversal.

FTX is therefore not a chapter about cryptocurrency being inherently fraudulent.

That claim would fail the book’s own selection test.

Cryptographic assets can be held honestly or dishonestly. Exchanges can maintain customer assets or misuse them. Blockchains can provide transparent transaction records while the corporate entities interacting with them remain opaque. Self-custody can reduce reliance on an intermediary while creating different risks around key loss, theft, coercion, and operational error.

The fraud here is specific.

A custodial intermediary made representations about customer assets and affiliated-party privileges that the criminal case established were false.

That specificity matters because frontier technologies attract two symmetrical mistakes.

Believers treat criticism of one company as an attack on the entire technology.

Critics treat one fraud as proof that the technology itself has no legitimate use.

Both positions collapse layers that need to remain separate.

Bitcoin is not FTX.

A blockchain is not an exchange.

An exchange balance is not a blockchain balance.

A token is not custody.

Custody is not solvency.

Solvency is not governance.

Governance is not proof that a particular transaction was authorized.

The book has spent twenty-five chapters learning to separate those propositions.

That is the central habit of fraud resistance.

Ask exactly what the evidence proves.

A banknote can be authentic and still lose value.

A charter can be genuine and still hide a permission.

A newspaper can be real and publish a fabricated report.

A railroad can be transformative and contain self-dealing.

A technology can work and its stock can still be sold fraudulently.

A lottery can pay real prizes and operate inside a corrupt political machine.

A bank branch can be federally insured while a security sold there is not.

A company can have audited statements and materially misrepresent performance.

An investment manager can pay withdrawals without having made the reported trades.

A crypto exchange can process real transactions while misusing customer funds.

Truth does not arrive in bundles.

One true property does not certify the whole system.

FTX also returns the book to the problem of related parties.

Crédit Mobilier showed a railroad and its contractor appearing as separate entities while insiders occupied both sides of the economic relationship.

FTX and Alameda were legally separate companies with intertwined ownership, management, systems, funding, and risk.

The criminal case made Alameda’s privileged treatment central to the deception.

Again, the question is not whether related parties are automatically illegitimate.

They are everywhere in business.

The question is whether the relationship is disclosed, governed, and constrained in a way consistent with the promises made to everyone else.

Affiliation becomes dangerous when the public interface says one rule while the related party operates under another.

Rules are trust infrastructure.

An exchange tells participants they trade inside a common system.

If one actor can carry a negative balance others cannot, access credit others cannot, withdraw under different constraints, or bypass risk controls, then the market visible to ordinary users is not the market actually operating.

The institutional counterfeit is equal treatment.

This connects FTX to the quiz-show scandal.

The audience sees a competition governed by rules.

Behind the interface, one participant has information or privileges unavailable to the others.

The display can remain accurate about what happened on screen while concealing the conditions that made the outcome possible.

Modern systems make this kind of asymmetry easier to hide because permissions live in code and configuration.

A special privilege may be one database flag.

One API role.

One risk exemption.

One line in a configuration file.

One administrator override.

The system can treat two parties differently without any visible sign in the user interface.

That is why governance of privileged access is one of the defining control problems of the digital age.

Who can override the limit?

Who can see the override?

Who approves it?

Is the exception logged?

Does the board understand it?

Does the auditor test it?

Can customers discover that it exists?

Does the risk system apply equally to affiliates?

The technological sophistication of the platform can distract from these ordinary governance questions.

That distraction is another version of Electric Sugar.

The black box looks advanced.

The important fact may be simple.

Someone with power exempted himself from the rule.

FTX’s collapse also demonstrates the difference between proof of reserves and proof of solvency.

After the bankruptcy, crypto markets intensified efforts to provide cryptographic evidence that exchanges held assets. Proof-of-reserves systems can improve transparency about particular holdings at particular times.

But an asset snapshot is not a complete balance sheet.

What liabilities exist?

Are assets pledged elsewhere?

Do related parties have claims?

Who controls the keys?

What happens between snapshots?

Are customer liabilities included completely?

Can the institution borrow the assets temporarily for the proof?

Again, a bounded assurance can become a halo if users infer too much.

The lesson of 250 years is not to reject verification technologies.

It is to read them literally.

What does this proof establish?

What does it not establish?

Who generated it?

Who can contradict it?

What happens when the claim is converted back into the underlying thing?

That final question is redemption again.

The book ends where it began.

In 1776, Americans accepted paper that promised value from a revolutionary government whose fiscal capacity was still being built. Counterfeiters attacked the authenticity of the paper, while inflation and weak taxation attacked the value of authentic notes.

In 2022, customers looked at digital balances promising assets from a global exchange. The interface could be authentic while the custody reality behind the balance was false.

Two and a half centuries of institutional innovation separate the Continental note from the FTX account screen.

The confidence problem survives.

Can the representation be converted into the thing it says it represents?

Can the promise be redeemed?

Can an independent party verify the claim?

Who absorbs the loss if the promise fails?

Who benefits from the opacity?

Who is allowed to override the rules?

What is the shortest path from the user interface back to reality?

America’s history is not a story of learning never to trust.

That would be a story of institutional failure.

The country grew by trusting more.

Paper money let strangers transact.

Corporate forms pooled capital.

Banks moved savings through the economy.

Newspapers and broadcast media scaled witness.

Railroads and communications networks coordinated distance.

Auditors, regulators, insurers, databases, and markets let millions of people rely on facts they could not personally inspect.

Digital systems pushed that delegation even further.

The price of scale is that confidence becomes an asset worth stealing.

Every layer that says “you no longer need to check this yourself” creates a question:

who checks the checker?

The answer is never one final institution.

It is a structure of overlapping evidence, independent incentives, bounded assurances, visible conflicts, reversible claims, and the permanent possibility of challenge.

A confidence republic is not a society in which everyone believes.

It is a society in which belief can travel farther than personal knowledge without becoming untethered from reality.

That tether is the real infrastructure.

And every generation has to rebuild it.
