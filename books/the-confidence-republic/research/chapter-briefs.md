# Chapter Briefs and Selection Tests

This is the working evidence map for *The Confidence Republic*. A chapter slot is not protected merely because it appears in the book README. Each candidate must survive the book’s four gates: documented deception, a legible trust surface, meaningful institutional consequence, and a stronger claim to the decade than its runner-ups.

## 1 — 1776–1785 — The Money That Could Not Prove Itself

**Primary:** Counterfeit Continental currency during the Revolution.  
**Trust surface:** National paper money before a mature national fiscal state.  
**Why it earns the decade:** The new government needed strangers to accept a portable political promise. British-linked counterfeiting and domestic counterfeiting attacked the authenticity of that promise while inflation and weak fiscal capacity attacked its value. The case establishes the book’s first distinction: authentic versus valuable are separate questions.  
**Runner-ups:** Land frauds involving soldiers’ bounty claims; wartime procurement fraud.  
**What would make this selection wrong?** Evidence that the British counterfeiting story is too folkloric to support a substantial chapter, or that the episode cannot be separated cleanly from the much larger monetary causes of Continental depreciation.  
**Evidence status:** Strong. Library of Congress, Founders Online, Smithsonian, and congressional-era records support the core facts. Drafted.

## 2 — 1786–1795 — Thirty-Five Million Acres for Half a Million Dollars

**Primary:** The Yazoo land fraud.  
**Trust surface:** State-created land title and the portability of property claims.  
**Why it earns the decade:** The document was genuine; the public process behind it was corrupted. The scandal exposes a harder verification problem than forgery: how later buyers should treat a formally valid property right created by bribed public authority. Its afterlife reaches *Fletcher v. Peck* and the constitutional treatment of contracts.  
**Runner-ups:** Early land-company speculation and fraudulent western claims; post-Revolution securities manipulation.  
**What would make this selection wrong?** Evidence that the bribery was less central to the transaction than later partisan retellings suggest, or that a competing fraud better explains the transformation of property claims into tradable paper.  
**Evidence status:** Strong. New Georgia Encyclopedia and court history provide a stable starting record. Drafted.

## 3 — 1796–1805 — The Water Company That Wanted to Be a Bank

**Primary:** Aaron Burr’s Manhattan Company charter.  
**Trust surface:** The corporate charter as a public statement of legal purpose and permission.  
**Why it earns the decade:** New York needed water; Burr’s coalition wanted banking power. A surplus-capital provision let a water company become a bank. The company did build a water system, so the chapter is not a fake-company story. It is about the distance between the public rationale used to win a charter and the economically decisive permission embedded in the document.  
**Runner-ups:** Early securities promotions; land-title and banking schemes carried over from the 1790s.  
**What would make this selection wrong?** Evidence that legislators broadly understood and deliberately endorsed the banking authority, leaving too little deception to justify inclusion under the book’s scam definition. In that case the episode may remain an institutional vignette but lose the decade slot.  
**Evidence status:** Good but legally bounded. The Museum of the City of New York and banking histories establish the charter structure. Drafted with an explicit warning that this was not a modern criminal-fraud conviction.

## 4 — 1806–1815 — Plates, Press, and Paper

**Primary:** Stephen Burroughs and the counterfeit private-banknote economy.  
**Trust surface:** A heterogeneous field of state and private banknotes whose authenticity and value had to be judged locally.  
**Why it earns the decade:** A surviving 1807 counterfeit Union Bank of Boston note makes the mechanism physical. A fake no longer had to imitate the national revolutionary issue; it could borrow the brand of one bank inside a proliferating note economy. The case introduces assurance markets such as counterfeit detectors and banknote reporters.  
**Runner-ups:** Counterfeiting the First Bank of the United States; wartime financial fraud.  
**What would make this selection wrong?** Evidence that Burroughs is too biographically colorful but institutionally minor, or that First Bank counterfeiting provides the stronger system-level case.  
**Evidence status:** Strong for the artifact and broader counterfeiting problem. Smithsonian and National Monetary Commission material support the chapter. Drafted.

## 5 — 1816–1825 — The Bank at the End of the Road

**Primary:** The “saddle-bag bank” and deliberate distant circulation of redeemable banknotes.  
**Trust surface:** Redemption: a genuine bank liability whose practical value depends on the holder’s ability to present it.  
**Why it earns the decade:** The scam is not the Panic of 1819 and not ordinary bank failure. It is the use of geography as friction: push notes far from the issuer so redemption becomes costly and slow. The decade shows that a promise can be genuine while access to the promised value is engineered to be inconvenient.  
**Runner-ups:** Insider abuse at early state banks; fraudulent western land-and-credit schemes; counterfeit notes.  
**What would make this selection wrong?** Evidence that “saddle-bag bank” was primarily polemical language describing aggressive but ordinary banking rather than a sufficiently deceptive practice. If so, replace with a better documented note-pushing or bank-fraud case.  
**Evidence status:** Good, with careful language. National Monetary Commission banking history documents the term and practice; FDIC and Federal Reserve histories bound the larger Panic. Drafted.

## 6 — 1826–1835 — There Are Men on the Moon

**Primary:** The Great Moon Hoax, published by *The Sun* in 1835.  
**Trust surface:** Cheap mass newspapers borrowing the authority of scientific witness.  
**Why it earns the decade:** The series claimed that the astronomer John Herschel’s telescope had revealed extraordinary lunar life. The newspaper was real; the scientific report was fabricated. The case captures a new scale of mediated witness: readers could know things they had not seen because a publication claimed that an expert had.  
**Runner-ups:** Patent-medicine advertising; land and bank promotions; religious and archaeological impostures.  
**What would make this selection wrong?** Evidence that contemporary readers overwhelmingly treated the series as obvious satire or entertainment rather than reportage, weakening the claim that newspaper authority carried the deception.  
**Evidence status:** Strong institutional secondary record through Smithsonian; chapter still needs a close read of contemporaneous issues before drafting.

## 7 — 1836–1845 — The Mermaid Upstairs

**Primary:** P. T. Barnum’s Feejee Mermaid exhibition.  
**Trust surface:** Paid spectacle, promotional journalism, museums, and the ambiguity between entertainment and factual display.  
**Why it earns the decade:** Barnum learned that publicity could make authenticity itself the commodity. The fabricated or assembled object mattered, but the more durable mechanism was the promotional system that made an impossible specimen worth seeing precisely because people argued over whether it was real.  
**Runner-ups:** Patent medicines; banknote fraud during the free-banking era; land promotions.  
**What would make this selection wrong?** Evidence that the exhibition was understood so widely as humbug or theatrical entertainment that calling it a defining scam imposes modern expectations on nineteenth-century show culture.  
**Evidence status:** Moderate. Strong museum/history sources exist; primary advertising and reception evidence still needed.

## 8 — 1846–1855 — Have You Confidence in Me?

**Primary:** William Thompson, arrested in New York in 1849, and the birth of the phrase “confidence man.”  
**Trust surface:** Urban anonymity and interpersonal recognition among strangers.  
**Why it earns the decade:** Thompson’s reported method was almost comically pure: approach a respectable stranger, behave as if acquainted, then ask whether the stranger had enough “confidence” to entrust him with a watch. The *New York Herald* story of July 8, 1849 appears to be the first printed use of “confidence man.” For a book about a confidence republic, the decade contains the moment Americans gave the mechanism a name.  
**Runner-ups:** Gold Rush mine salting, rigged scales, spurious assaying and mining-device promotions.  
**What would make this selection wrong?** Evidence that the Thompson story was materially embellished by the Herald or that an earlier printed use substantially changes the linguistic claim. The chapter can survive without “first ever,” but not without a reliable core incident.  
**Evidence status:** Strong enough for architecture. Cambridge PMLA scholarship and later historical references trace the July 8, 1849 Herald usage. Gold Rush fraud remains an unusually strong runner-up and may supply the decade’s wider economic context.

## 9 — 1856–1865 — Shoddy

**Primary:** Civil War procurement fraud and the “shoddy” contractor scandals.  
**Trust surface:** A federal procurement machine expanding faster than inspection capacity.  
**Why it earns the decade:** War transformed government purchasing in scale and speed. Documented contractors supplied inferior clothing, equipment, food, animals, and other goods while inspectors and Congress struggled to distinguish urgency, incompetence, profiteering, and fraud. The term “shoddy” itself migrated from a material into a moral description of wartime profiteering.  
**Runner-ups:** Counterfeit greenbacks; bounty fraud; war-related securities and supply schemes.  
**What would make this selection wrong?** Evidence that the famous shoddy stories are dominated by generalized wartime quality complaints rather than provable deception. The chapter must use specific documented contracts or prosecutions, not folklore.  
**Evidence status:** Good institutional starting point through the National Archives; needs specific primary cases before drafting.

## 10 — 1866–1875 — The Railroad Built Itself Twice

**Primary:** Crédit Mobilier of America and Union Pacific insider self-dealing.  
**Trust surface:** Federally supported railroad finance, construction contracting, and public-company governance.  
**Why it earns the decade:** Union Pacific insiders used the construction company to contract with themselves at generous prices, capturing value created by federal support and the railroad’s capital structure. Discounted shares placed with public officials turned a corporate-governance scheme into a national political scandal.  
**Runner-ups:** Boss Tweed and municipal contract fraud; Black Friday gold-market manipulation.  
**What would make this selection wrong?** A stronger case that Tweed’s public contracting better exposes the decade’s trust infrastructure, or evidence that the Crédit Mobilier story is being oversimplified into “the railroad was fake” rather than insider extraction from a real buildout.  
**Evidence status:** Strong. U.S. House Historian provides a reliable institutional record. The chapter must preserve that the transcontinental railroad was real and transformative.

## 11 — 1876–1885 — The Machine Behind the Locked Door

**Primary:** The Electric Sugar Refining Company scheme, whose fraudulent technology story began in the mid-1880s and was exposed later.  
**Trust surface:** Patent-age technological expertise and investment in machinery outsiders could not inspect.  
**Why it earns the decade:** Investors were told that a secret electrical process could refine sugar at extraordinary cost advantage. Demonstrations concealed the mundane source of the refined sugar. The supposed machine weaponized a familiar asymmetry in frontier technology: the inventor claims that secrecy is necessary precisely where investors need independent verification.  
**Runner-ups:** Mining-stock fraud; patent-company promotions; Grant & Ward, whose collapse in 1884 revealed false representations around elite financial reputation.  
**What would make this selection wrong?** If the core deception cannot be dated securely inside 1876–1885, or if Grant & Ward offers a better documented same-window mechanism. Because exposure of Electric Sugar came later, the chapter must be explicit about which deceptive acts occurred before 1886.  
**Evidence status:** Moderate. PBS/American Experience provides a strong narrative secondary source; primary corporate, press, and court records still needed.

## 12 — 1886–1895 — A Ticket to Nowhere

**Primary:** The Louisiana State Lottery political machine.  
**Trust surface:** Nationwide mail distribution, monopoly privilege, celebrity credibility, and state political protection.  
**Why it earns the decade:** The lottery sold real tickets and paid real prizes; the scam lane is therefore not “fake lottery.” It is the documented corruption and political influence used to protect a highly profitable national operation, plus the rental of respected public names to reassure buyers at a distance. Federal anti-lottery laws and postal restrictions make the institutional consequence unusually legible.  
**Runner-ups:** Electric Sugar exposure; fraudulent building-and-loan promotions; mining and railroad securities fraud.  
**What would make this selection wrong?** Evidence that corruption claims relied more on partisan accusation than documentary proof, or that the scheme’s legal lottery operations overwhelm the deception component required by the book.  
**Evidence status:** Good. U.S. House history supplies a strong entry point; chapter needs congressional and postal primary records.

## 13 — 1896–1905 — Carnegie’s Secret Daughter

**Primary:** Cassie Chadwick.  
**Trust surface:** Elite reputation, bank credit, and the assumption that private wealth can be verified socially rather than directly.  
**Why it earns the decade:** Chadwick persuaded lenders that she was Andrew Carnegie’s illegitimate daughter and used forged instruments and elite social signals to borrow enormous sums. The most valuable asset in the scheme was a billionaire’s name. Banks that could have attempted independent confirmation often preferred the implication of privileged knowledge.  
**Runner-ups:** Daniel D. Miller’s “520 percent” postal investment scheme; get-rich-quick mail fraud; municipal corruption.  
**What would make this selection wrong?** Evidence that the Carnegie-daughter story was more a later journalistic simplification than the operative basis of lender behavior, or that another case better explains the rise of impersonal credit verification.  
**Evidence status:** Strong narrative and court-history base; Smithsonian coverage is useful, but the chapter should add trial and contemporary banking records.

## 14 — 1906–1915 — Wireless Riches

**Primary:** United Wireless Telegraph Company stock promotion and the mail-fraud prosecution of its leadership.  
**Trust surface:** A genuinely transformative communications technology sold through stock promotion to investors who could not distinguish operational progress from promotional claims.  
**Why it earns the decade:** Wireless was real. United Wireless also operated real stations. That makes the fraud more revealing than an imaginary invention: genuine technological change can lend credibility to false claims about who will capture the economics.  
**Runner-ups:** The United States Motor Company and other promotion failures; mining securities; bucket-shop frauds.  
**What would make this selection wrong?** Failure to secure primary federal prosecution records showing exactly which representations were fraudulent, or evidence that the case’s surviving popular account overstates the stock fraud relative to genuine operations.  
**Evidence status:** Needs primary-source close read before drafting. This is the least secure primary on the current 25-chapter slate and should be treated as replaceable.

## 15 — 1916–1925 — Forty-Five Days

**Primary:** Charles Ponzi’s Securities Exchange Company, 1920.  
**Trust surface:** Mass investment solicitation, financial novelty, newspaper attention, and a plausible cross-border arbitrage story.  
**Why it earns the decade:** International postal reply coupons were real. The claimed returns were not generated at the scale required. New investor money funded earlier payments. The scheme became so culturally durable that Ponzi’s name now describes a mechanism much older than he was.  
**Runner-ups:** Blue-sky securities promotions; oil-stock frauds; postwar land booms.  
**What would make this selection wrong?** Almost nothing on decade dominance, but the chapter could still fail if it retells the celebrity biography without explaining why the true underlying instrument made the impossible return story plausible.  
**Evidence status:** Strong. Smithsonian National Postal Museum, postal-inspection history, court and contemporaneous records.

## 16 — 1926–1935 — The Estate That Did Not Exist

**Primary:** Oscar Hartzell’s Sir Francis Drake inheritance scheme.  
**Trust surface:** Postal solicitation, genealogy, legal claims, and the authority of an imagined transatlantic estate proceeding.  
**Why it earns the decade:** Hartzell persuaded contributors that they were financing a legal effort to recover Drake’s supposed estate and would share in enormous proceeds. The scheme converted distance from the alleged legal process into credibility: victims could not easily verify an estate said to be entangled in English institutions.  
**Runner-ups:** Samuel Insull-related securities promotion is historically important but must not be mislabeled as criminal fraud where acquittals matter; Florida land frauds; bucket-shop and oil promotions.  
**What would make this selection wrong?** Weak official documentation of the exact representations or a stronger case showing that Depression-era securities deception better shaped verification institutions.  
**Evidence status:** Moderate. Contemporary reporting and British parliamentary material support the nonexistence of the estate; U.S. court and postal records should be added before drafting.

## 17 — 1936–1945 — The Inventory Nobody Counted

**Primary:** McKesson & Robbins, exposed in 1938.  
**Trust surface:** Independent audit as a scalable substitute for investors personally verifying corporate assets.  
**Why it earns the decade:** The company’s accounts contained fictitious inventory and receivables. The scandal exposed audit procedures that could validate records without physically observing inventory or independently confirming receivables. The resulting shift in auditing practice makes this one of the clearest examples in the entire book of a scam attacking the verifier and changing the verification standard.  
**Runner-ups:** Securities boiler rooms and wartime contracting fraud.  
**What would make this selection wrong?** Only a major correction to the standard accounting-history account linking the scandal to auditing procedure.  
**Evidence status:** Strong. SEC-era material and PCAOB historical discussion provide high-quality institutional evidence.

## 18 — 1946–1955 — The School Built for One Benefit Check

**Primary:** Fly-by-night and predatory schools exploiting the original GI Bill education market.  
**Trust surface:** Federal benefit eligibility and government payment as a credibility signal in a newly subsidized private education market.  
**Why it earns the decade:** The GI Bill produced enormous genuine educational opportunity. Its payment architecture also attracted schools organized around capturing benefits rather than delivering comparable value. Congress responded with restrictions including what became the 85/15 rule, intended to make some non-government demand a test of market legitimacy.  
**Runner-ups:** Home-improvement fraud aimed at returning veterans; postwar franchise and installment-credit schemes.  
**What would make this selection wrong?** Evidence that the abuses are too diffuse to support one coherent narrative case, or that the 85/15 causal history is more complicated than current institutional summaries imply.  
**Evidence status:** Good policy history; needs one or two deeply sourced school cases to keep the chapter narrative rather than abstract.

## 19 — 1956–1965 — The Answer Before the Question

**Primary:** The television quiz-show scandals.  
**Trust surface:** Broadcast television’s claim to show a real contest happening before the viewer.  
**Why it earns the decade:** Producers secretly supplied answers or otherwise rigged outcomes while presenting the contests as authentic. The deception did not counterfeit the broadcast; it manipulated reality behind a genuine camera. Congressional hearings, participant testimony, and later law regulating deceptive quiz-show practices provide a clean institutional arc.  
**Runner-ups:** Payola in radio; land-sales promotions; direct-mail sweepstakes.  
**What would make this selection wrong?** A stronger case that payola more directly transformed disclosure rules and the economics of mass attention, or evidence that the quiz shows were widely understood as staged entertainment before exposure.  
**Evidence status:** Strong. U.S. House historical material and congressional hearings.

## 20 — 1966–1975 — The People Who Never Bought Life Insurance

**Primary:** Equity Funding Corporation of America.  
**Trust surface:** Computerized insurance records, reinsurance, accounting, and management information systems.  
**Why it earns the decade:** Employees fabricated insurance policies and related financial data at scale, turning the computer from a verification aid into an amplifier of invented reality. Reinsurers and investors encountered records that looked systematic precisely because the system itself had been fed fiction.  
**Runner-ups:** Penn Central disclosure failures; National Student Marketing; commodities and land fraud.  
**What would make this selection wrong?** Evidence that the “computerized fraud” framing exaggerates technology’s causal role relative to ordinary management conspiracy and accounting falsification.  
**Evidence status:** Strong legal/regulatory base. SEC enforcement records exist; detailed chapter work should also use trial and industry records.

## 21 — 1976–1985 — The Money Never Touched a Teller

**Primary:** Stanley Rifkin’s 1978 fraudulent wire transfer from Security Pacific National Bank.  
**Trust surface:** Electronic authorization codes and bank records turning money movement into authenticated system instructions.  
**Why it earns the decade:** Rifkin reportedly learned the bank’s transfer code, impersonated an authorized caller, and caused millions to be wired without stealing physical cash. The event is a clean transition from forging an object to forging authorization inside a system.  
**Runner-ups:** Equity Funding’s tail; E. F. Hutton check-kiting; early computer and telecommunications fraud; commodities schemes.  
**What would make this selection wrong?** If the episode is better classified as a spectacular theft with too little broader confidence consequence, or if E. F. Hutton provides a stronger institution-scale mechanism for electronic float and bank trust.  
**Evidence status:** Moderate. Strong contemporaneous journalism exists; chapter needs court and bank-security records before drafting.

## 22 — 1986–1995 — The Government Will Make You Whole

**Primary:** Charles Keating, Lincoln Savings & Loan, and the fraud/corruption lane inside the savings-and-loan crisis.  
**Trust surface:** Deposit insurance, thrift regulation, political access, and the assumption that an insured institution is synonymous with a safe investment relationship.  
**Why it earns the decade:** Lincoln sold uninsured securities through branches where customers could plausibly associate the products with federally insured deposits. The institution’s failure became one of the costliest of the S&L crisis, while political pressure around regulators produced the Keating Five scandal. The chapter must separate Lincoln’s adjudicated conduct from the macroeconomic and regulatory causes of the broader S&L crisis.  
**Runner-ups:** Drexel/Milken securities offenses; televangelist fundraising fraud; boiler-room penny stocks.  
**What would make this selection wrong?** If the chapter cannot cleanly separate criminal/civil wrongdoing at Lincoln from the nonfraud causes of the S&L crisis, or if another case better captures the decade without relying on systemic conflation.  
**Evidence status:** Strong institutional history through FDIC and court records, with careful legal-status work required.

## 23 — 1996–2005 — The Company That Beat Its Own Numbers

**Primary:** Enron.  
**Trust surface:** Public-company financial statements, analyst culture, complex structured finance, governance, and gatekeepers.  
**Why it earns the decade:** Enron contained real businesses and real innovation, which made false presentations of financial condition more credible. The fraud exploited the institutions meant to translate a complex corporation into numbers ordinary shareholders could rely on. Its collapse helped produce Sarbanes-Oxley and transformed governance and auditing practice.  
**Runner-ups:** WorldCom, whose accounting fraud was in some respects simpler and larger by accounting adjustment; Adelphia; dot-com stock promotions.  
**What would make this selection wrong?** A persuasive case that WorldCom better represents the decade’s trust failure because its accounting deception and institutional consequences were clearer, while Enron’s complexity makes it too easy to substitute mythology for mechanism.  
**Evidence status:** Very strong. DOJ, SEC, congressional, bankruptcy, court, and accounting records.

## 24 — 2006–2015 — The Statement That Never Lost Money

**Primary:** Bernard L. Madoff Investment Securities’ investment-advisory Ponzi scheme.  
**Trust surface:** Brokerage reputation, investment statements, feeder funds, professional social proof, and the assumption that custody and trading existed as reported.  
**Why it earns the decade:** The reported returns looked implausibly smooth in retrospect but were socially normalized by Madoff’s status and the surrounding network. The scandal exposed gaps in independent custody, due diligence, and regulatory follow-through.  
**Runner-ups:** Mortgage-origination fraud and securities misrepresentation during the financial crisis; Allen Stanford; online-ad and affinity fraud.  
**What would make this selection wrong?** A defensible decision to make mortgage fraud the decade’s primary because it had larger systemic consequences. The difficulty is that “the mortgage crisis” was not one scam and cannot be flattened into a single criminal mechanism.  
**Evidence status:** Very strong. SEC, DOJ, SIPC/trustee, inspector-general, court, and congressional records.

## 25 — 2016–2025 — The Exchange That Spent the Deposits

**Primary:** FTX and the adjudicated fraud led by Sam Bankman-Fried.  
**Trust surface:** Crypto-exchange custody, digital balances, affiliated trading firms, and the assumption that an interface showing customer assets corresponds to segregated or available assets behind it.  
**Why it earns the decade:** A customer did not need to misunderstand blockchain cryptography for the scheme to work. The trust failure sat in a familiar custodial relationship presented through a new asset class. The conviction and sentencing record establish misappropriation and false representations with unusual clarity.  
**Runner-ups:** Theranos; OneCoin; pandemic-relief fraud; online romance/investment scam compounds; Celsius and other crypto failures where legal categories differ.  
**What would make this selection wrong?** A stronger editorial case for Theranos as the decade’s defining trust failure in expert medicine and venture-capital validation, or evidence that FTX’s institutional consequence remains too unsettled to judge so soon after collapse.  
**Evidence status:** Very strong legal record. DOJ conviction and sentencing materials provide the core; bankruptcy and regulatory records can supply custody mechanics.

## Epilogue — 2026 — The Next Con Begins With a True Sentence

The epilogue is not a “scam of 2026.” It asks what the twenty-five selections have in common without pretending to predict the next fraud. The working claim is that the best confidence games rarely begin with pure invention. They begin with a genuine innovation, institution, relationship, or social truth and counterfeit the inference that people have learned to draw from it.

The epilogue should test contemporary trust surfaces without accusing unnamed future actors: synthetic media, agent authorization, identity proofs, prediction markets, AI-generated expertise, real-time payment rails, tokenized assets, and machine-to-machine commerce. The final question is institutional rather than paranoid: which facts will people soon delegate to systems they no longer inspect personally, and what evidence should those systems preserve so ordinary trust remains rational?
