# Loch Ness eDNA survey audit

## Editorial question

Chapter 1 is strongest when the 2018 Loch Ness campaign is not treated as a magic bottle that either found or disproved a monster. What did the actual survey design make detectable, and which popular conclusions outran that design?

## What the project record supports

The University of Otago's 5 September 2019 results account says the team took about 250 water samples around the loch, through its centre, and into its depths; captured, extracted and sequenced DNA; and compared the sequences with global databases. Neil Gemmell reported no evidence in the sequence data for a creature remotely related to a plesiosaur, no shark DNA, no catfish DNA, no sturgeon evidence, and abundant eel DNA at almost every sampling location. He also stated explicitly that the data did **not** reveal eel size.

Source: https://www.otago.ac.nz/news/newsroom/first-edna-study-of-loch-ness-points-to-something-fishy

The participating Loch Ness Project preserves useful field-design detail that the publicity summaries flatten. It records shoreline, surface, mid-water and deep-water sampling at Loch Ness plus three other lochs, with deep samples at 50 m intervals down to 200 m in the north and south basins. Its retrospective project page also records an important negative-control lesson: some known vertebrate predators of loch fish—seals, otters, mergansers and cormorants—were not detected, which it attributes to low frequency or transience. The same page notes that a rare transient sturgeon could evade a one-time collection and that eel eDNA cannot distinguish ordinary eels from an exceptional giant individual of the same species.

Source: https://www.lochnessproject.org/FIELDWORKGROUNDTRUTH/eDNA%20LOCH%20NESS/eDNA%20LOCHNESS_index.html

A contemporaneous project account reports 259 water samples rather than the rounded 250 used in later publicity and says sampling included depths beyond 200 m. Until the originating sample manifest/data release is recovered, manuscript prose should use `about 250` or explicitly explain the 250/259 discrepancy rather than manufacture precision.

Source: https://www.modernaustralian.com/news/2019-using-environmental-dna-to-survey-life-in-loch-ness

## What this changes in the argument

The survey contains its own lesson about absence. It recovered abundant eel signal across the loch while apparently missing some vertebrates independently known to use the system. That is more valuable to this book than a clean `nothing extraordinary was found` ending because it supplies a positive and negative control inside the same expedition.

The inference should therefore be hypothesis-specific:

- A **resident, abundant population** of a taxon distinguishable by the metabarcoding workflow owes a much larger detection burden than a rare transient animal.
- A **rare visitor** can remain compatible with nondetection when occupancy during sampling is low.
- A **giant individual belonging to an ordinary detected species** can be invisible to taxonomic eDNA even when that species' DNA is abundant, because the barcode identifies lineage rather than body length.
- A **hypothetical lineage absent from or poorly represented in the reference database** creates a different identification problem from simple nondetection.

This is the denominator the chapter currently asks for in the abstract. The Loch Ness survey itself can begin to supply it.

## Publication holds

Do not say the campaign `ruled out large animals` as a general statement. The project reported no evidence for several proposed taxonomic categories under its sampling and identification design; that is narrower.

Do not say `no plesiosaur DNA` as though a validated plesiosaur barcode existed. The defensible wording is Gemmell's lineage-level claim: no sequence evidence remotely related to the proposed reptilian category was found in their data. Before stronger wording, recover the primer regions, taxonomic assignment pipeline and reference set.

Do not use eel read abundance as evidence for giant body size. The project leader explicitly said the data do not reveal size.

Do not treat the survey's failure to detect some known transient/low-frequency vertebrates as proof that eDNA is weak. Treat those misses as calibration evidence: detectability depends on residency, abundance, shedding, transport, assay and sampling timing.

Do not quote an exact 250-sample denominator without acknowledging that a contemporaneous project account reports 259. Recover the sample manifest before making exact quantitative coverage claims.

## Strongest counterargument

This audit may weaken the proposed book-level `biological residue burden` more than it strengthens it. If known vertebrates can be missed in an intensive survey, cryptid advocates can reasonably argue that one or a few transient animals might also escape molecular detection. The manuscript should concede that point rather than hide it.

But the concession cuts in a specific direction: it protects **rare/transient** hypotheses much more than it protects a **resident breeding population of large animals**. The book must quantify that difference instead of letting `eDNA can miss animals` become an all-purpose escape hatch. If later occupancy/shedding modeling cannot show a meaningful separation between those hypotheses, the residue-burden framework should be narrowed or cut.

## Best eventual prose move

The most engaging version of the chapter does not make the bottle a verdict. Make the survey argue with itself. It found eel DNA nearly everywhere, failed to register some animals known to pass through the system, and could not tell an ordinary eel from an extraordinary one by body size. One expedition therefore demonstrates detection, nondetection and non-identifiability at once. That is a better opening mechanism than believer-versus-skeptic symmetry because the instrument itself supplies the complication.
