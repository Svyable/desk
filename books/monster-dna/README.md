# MONSTER DNA

## What the water remembers after the creature is gone

**Sven Hardy Benson**

## Thesis

Cryptozoology has usually been an argument about sightings. Environmental DNA changes the object of the argument. A large aquatic animal does not merely have to appear in front of a camera; it must eat, excrete, shed cells, reproduce, die, and leave molecules moving through an ecosystem. The collision between monster folklore and modern eDNA surveillance creates a much harder question: **when does failure to detect a biological trace become evidence that the animal is not there?**

This is not a book claiming that Loch Ness contains a giant eel, nor that eDNA has disproved every unknown animal. It is a book about turning absence into a measurement without pretending absence is simple.

## Governing rule

**A sighting is not a specimen. A DNA detection is not an organism. A nondetection is not an extinction certificate.**

## Distinct contribution

The book uses serious cryptozoological claims as a stress test for a technology already used by conservation agencies to find rare, invasive, secretive and difficult-to-capture species. The interesting collision is not believer versus skeptic. It is between two kinds of invisibility: an animal too elusive to see and DNA too patchy, degraded, transported or taxonomically unassignable to interpret cleanly.

## Evidence classes

1. **Verified / documented.** Organisms shed genetic material into water, soil and air. eDNA is operationally used for conservation and invasive-species surveillance. Its detection probability depends on shedding, transport, degradation, sampling design, extraction, assay choice and reference databases.
2. **Credible but incomplete.** Some historical animal reports preserve useful morphology, location, seasonality or behavior; some unknown sequences in metabarcoding datasets may represent incomplete reference libraries or poorly characterized taxa. Neither class identifies a monster by itself.
3. **Disputed / unconfirmed.** A breeding population of large unknown vertebrates in Loch Ness; a surviving plesiosaur; giant eels large enough to explain the classic Nessie phenotype; cryptid identity inferred from ambiguous photographs, sonar traces or witness testimony without recoverable biological material.
4. **Speculative synthesis.** Mature eDNA ecology may allow us to assign a quantitative **biological residue burden** to cryptozoological claims: the larger, more abundant, more resident and more metabolically active the proposed population, the harder it becomes for repeated well-designed molecular surveys to miss it.

## Strongest countermodel

There may be no special epistemology here at all. eDNA is simply another ecological survey method with familiar detection errors. Famous monster claims may be heterogeneous folklore built from wakes, logs, known animals, expectation and hoaxes; molecular sampling may add little beyond making the null case more quantitative. If the book cannot show that cryptid-scale hypotheses create distinctive sampling and inference problems, it should collapse into an essay rather than an 18-chapter book.

## Chapter progression

1. **A Bottle of Loch Ness** — the monster hunt becomes a sampling problem.
2. **Everything Sheds** — mucus, skin, feces, gametes, blood and the biological tax of existing.
3. **The Water Moves** — transport breaks the naïve map from molecule to animal.
4. **DNA Dies** — degradation creates both useful recency and false absence.
5. **How Rare Is Rare?** — occupancy, detection probability and repeated sampling.
6. **The Reference Library Problem** — a sequence can be real while its name is missing.
7. **The Contamination Animal** — boats, birds, laboratories and DNA arriving without its source organism.
8. **Nessie Gets Sampled** — reconstruct the Loch Ness campaign, what it could exclude, and what it could not.
9. **The Eel** — abundance is not body size; a plausible category is not a giant specimen.
10. **Pythons Without Sightings** — operational eDNA detection of secretive invasive animals as a positive control.
11. **Carp Before Capture** — when agencies act on molecules before bodies.
12. **A Monster Needs a Population** — energetics, reproduction, biomass and residue burden.
13. **One Animal Is Harder** — transient visitors, vagrants and the asymmetry between a population claim and a single-animal claim.
14. **Old DNA, New Ghosts** — sedimentary DNA and the temporal ambiguity of preserved traces.
15. **Air Is a Sample** — airborne eDNA and the expanding surveillance volume.
16. **Unknown Does Not Mean New** — unclassified reads, database gaps and the seduction of residual categories.
17. **The Negative Expedition** — design a prospective cryptid survey with preregistered thresholds.
18. **What Would Change My Mind?** — specimen, sequence, chain of custody, replication, and the dignity of a real defeat condition.
