# Who Chooses the Axes

Every frontier begins with a constitutional decision.

Somebody decides what counts.

The axes may be expected return and risk. Cost and speed. Emissions and reliability. Income and leisure. Accuracy and false positives. Housing units and neighborhood preservation. Security and convenience.

The graph feels neutral after the labels are printed.

The labels are not neutral.

Choosing objectives defines what the system is allowed to call improvement. Choosing constraints defines what the system is forbidden to trade. Choosing the population defines whose outcomes matter. Choosing the time horizon defines which future costs belong to the present decision.

Before there is optimization, there is governance.

This is obvious in public policy. A transportation project can be evaluated by construction cost and travel time. Add crash risk, emissions, access for people without cars, neighborhood disruption, reliability, land use, and maintenance. The preferred project may change.

No analysis can include every consequence at full resolution. The act of deciding which consequences are material is already a public choice.

The same is true inside companies, though the politics are easier to hide.

A chief executive decides that revenue growth and operating margin are the central objectives. A regulator adds safety. Customers add reliability. Employees add workload and compensation. Investors add capital return. A board adds risk limits. A community adds environmental effects.

The company's actual frontier is produced by all of them, whether management acknowledges that or not.

Power determines which axes arrive as preferences and which arrive as constraints.

A customer with many alternatives can make service quality binding through exit. A powerful buyer can make supplier price a hard constraint. A regulator can convert safety from a management preference into law. Workers with scarce skills can force schedule or compensation into the trade. A weak stakeholder may carry a cost for years before it reaches the decision process.

This is why frontier analysis can be political without becoming partisan.

It asks who has the power to make their objective count.

That question is relevant even when everyone is acting legally and rationally.

Suppose a company chooses a lower-cost production method that increases noise for nearby residents within legal limits. The company may be on its private cost-output frontier. Residents experience a different frontier between industrial activity and quality of life. The legal system determines how much of the resident objective enters the firm's feasible set.

Regulation is one way societies redraw private frontiers.

Taxes, liability, property rights, standards, disclosure rules, labor law, zoning, and environmental limits all change what firms and individuals can treat as external.

This does not mean more regulation always improves the social frontier. A rule can be expensive, badly targeted, captured by incumbents, outdated, or harmful on another objective. The point is structural. Law changes the axes and constraints that private optimization faces.

Markets do the same through prices.

When a resource becomes expensive, conservation enters the frontier more strongly. When pollution is free to the emitter, it can remain off the private chart. When insurance prices risk, behavior changes. When prices fail to capture a social cost, the private frontier can diverge from the social one.

Economics has developed extensive theory around externalities, public goods, property rights, and welfare. The frontier view is a way to make the institutional choice visible rather than pretend efficiency exists before the rules.

There is no market frontier without a market design.

Contracts decide which risks are transferred. Liability rules decide who pays after failure. Bankruptcy rules decide how losses are distributed. Accounting rules decide when costs appear. Antitrust rules shape feasible competitive behavior. Standards decide what forms of interoperability are available.

Institutions are part of the feasible set.

This can be seen in small settings too.

A household deciding how to divide paid work and care is not choosing from a natural frontier produced only by personal preference. Childcare cost, school hours, parental leave, work flexibility, taxes, elder-care support, and social expectations shape the options. Change those institutions and different combinations become feasible.

Calling the outcome a preference without examining the constraints can confuse adaptation with desire.

People often choose among the options available. Repeated choice does not prove the feasible set was fair or complete.

This matters in organizations because leaders can mistake revealed behavior for endorsement. Employees keep working late, so perhaps they prefer flexibility over formal staffing. Customers tolerate a confusing process, so perhaps they do not care enough to change. Suppliers accept long payment terms, so perhaps the terms are efficient.

Sometimes people accept a point because the alternatives are worse.

Revealed preference is information about choice under constraint. It is not a referendum on the constraint.

Who chooses the axes is also a question of expertise.

Experts know things decision makers do not. Engineers know failure modes. Clinicians know clinical consequences. Economists know incentive effects. Operators know process constraints. Lawyers know obligations. Communities know local impacts. Users know experience.

A good frontier process needs enough expertise to identify material objectives and feasible options.

But expertise does not automatically decide the weights.

An engineer can estimate how much a safety measure reduces risk and what it costs. Whether the remaining risk is socially acceptable may involve law, ethics, and public legitimacy. A physician can explain treatment probabilities and side effects. The patient may value outcomes differently. An economist can estimate efficiency effects. Elected institutions may choose a distribution the model would not select on aggregate welfare alone.

The boundary between fact and preference is not perfectly clean, but preserving the distinction helps prevent authority from expanding by vocabulary.

The analyst should own the model and expose its assumptions.

The legitimate decision maker should own the value judgment.

In companies, legitimacy often comes from delegated authority rather than democracy. The board, chief executive, or accountable executive has the right to choose among efficient options within law and policy. Even there, quality improves when the affected functions and stakeholders can surface hidden axes before the choice.

Consultation is not the same as veto.

This distinction matters because an attempt to include every stakeholder in every decision can paralyze the organization. Governance itself has a frontier between participation, expertise, speed, accountability, and consistency.

A two-way-door product change does not need a constitutional convention. A plant closure does not deserve the same process as a font change.

The consequence should shape the participation burden.

This is the same risk-sensitive governance principle from the organizational chapters, now applied to axis selection.

For low-consequence reversible decisions, the decision owner can choose a compact model. For high-consequence irreversible decisions with distributed harms, a wider process is justified because omitted axes are more dangerous and legitimacy matters more.

Public systems often use formal procedures for this reason: notice, comment, environmental review, cost-benefit analysis, legislative debate, judicial review, hearings, and elections. These procedures can become slow and bureaucratic. They also exist because the public frontier cannot responsibly be defined by one analyst.

Speed is an objective. Voice is another.

The correct frontier point depends on the decision.

There is another problem once axes are chosen: units.

How do you compare lives saved, dollars spent, minutes of travel, emissions, dignity, biodiversity, privacy, jobs, and regional equality? Some frameworks monetize outcomes. Others use multicriteria analysis. Some impose hard constraints. Some leave parts of the comparison to political judgment.

Every method has tradeoffs.

Monetization can create a common unit and force explicit assumptions. It can also imply exchangeability where people believe thresholds or rights should bind. Multi-criteria scoring can preserve several dimensions and hide preference inside weights. Qualitative judgment can preserve nuance and reduce reproducibility.

The absence of a perfect aggregation method does not erase the need to decide.

It makes transparency more important.

A decision should be able to answer, "Which objective would have to matter more or less for us to choose differently?"

That question reveals the moral load-bearing beams.

If a project is preferred only because one distributional cost receives a very low weight, say so. If a safety regulation is justified because risk above a threshold is treated as unacceptable regardless of cost, say so. If a company chooses a lower-margin option because customer trust is a binding strategic objective, say so.

The value judgment becomes reviewable.

This is especially important when models use composite scores. A score compresses the axes and can make political choices look technical. The weights may have been selected by experts, executives, surveys, algorithms, or inherited convention. Once multiplied and summed, their origin disappears.

A composite score is a preference machine.

It should come with a label explaining whose preferences it contains.

Algorithmic decision systems do not escape this. A model optimizing click-through, fraud detection, credit risk, hiring predictions, routing, content ranking, or medical triage is operating on objectives chosen by people and data shaped by institutions.

The loss function is governance written in mathematics.

Optimizing it can improve performance and amplify whatever it omits.

A fraud model can reduce losses and increase false declines. A recommendation system can increase engagement and narrow exposure. A credit model can improve repayment prediction and distribute access unevenly. A hiring model can improve consistency and inherit patterns from historical data.

There is no neutral objective because every objective defines which error matters.

This is one reason model evaluation needs more than accuracy. Different error types impose different costs on different people. Calibration, false positive rates, false negative rates, subgroup performance, interpretability, latency, privacy, and operational consequences can all become relevant axes.

Adding axes is not enough by itself. Someone still chooses the acceptable tradeoff.

That choice should not be hidden inside a technical parameter nobody outside the team knows exists.

The same issue appears in personal optimization tools. Fitness trackers, budgeting apps, productivity systems, and recommendation engines make some dimensions visible and others quiet. Steps are counted. Deep conversation is not. Spending categories are counted. Generosity, anxiety, and the option value of cash are harder. Tasks completed are counted. Whether the tasks mattered is not.

What gets measured becomes an axis of attention.

A person should sometimes ask who chose the dashboard.

The answer may be a company whose objective differs from the user's. A social platform wants engagement. The user may want connection and then to leave. A workplace tool wants activity data. The employee may want meaningful output. A fitness product wants daily use. The user may want a durable habit requiring less dependence on the product.

The user's frontier and the provider's frontier can diverge.

Good product design tries to align them. Regulation, competition, reputation, and user agency can help when they do not.

Who chooses the axes is therefore also a market-design question.

There is a deeper philosophical limit here. Some values may resist placement on an efficiency frontier because the language of trade itself feels wrong. Rights, dignity, consent, and sacred values can be treated as constraints rather than objectives with continuous weights.

That does not remove conflict. Constraints can collide. Rights can conflict. Resources remain finite.

But the representation matters.

If a value should not be traded below a threshold, do not put it into a weighted average that permits the model to compensate for violating it with enough gain elsewhere.

Make the boundary visible.

This is one of the strongest uses of frontier thinking. It lets us say, "Within these nonnegotiable constraints, here is the efficient set."

Optimization occurs inside a constitution.

That constitution can be a safety standard, a rights framework, a law, a professional ethic, a company principle, a family commitment, or a personal boundary.

It limits some efficiency in order to protect values the decision maker refuses to price at the margin.

The constraint can be revised, but revision happens at a different level of authority.

This distinction prevents every rule from being reopened whenever a local optimization dislikes it.

A hospital does not renegotiate patient confidentiality for every workflow improvement. An airline does not let a scheduling optimizer waive safety requirements to gain utilization. A company does not ask each product team to invent its own financial-control policy.

The constitution reduces decision cost by removing regions of the feasible set.

Good constraints can move the practical frontier outward by creating trust and standardization. Bad constraints can trap the system inside an old world.

Governance needs a way to tell the difference.

That requires periodic review at the level capable of changing the rule. What failure was this constraint designed to prevent? Is that failure still material? Has technology changed the tradeoff? Has the rule created larger harms? Is there a less costly way to protect the same value?

The mechanism matters more than the age of the rule.

New is not efficient. Old is not wise by default.

The final test of axis choice is accountability.

After the decision, can the decision maker explain what was optimized, what was constrained, what was omitted, who bore the costs, and what evidence would cause the model to change?

If not, the frontier may be sophisticated and ungoverned.

The graph begins before the first data point.

It begins with the decision about whose world is allowed onto the page.

Representation creates another frontier before the model begins. A stakeholder can be physically present in a process and still be poorly represented if the process requires expertise, time, language, legal resources, or confidence the stakeholder does not have. Open participation is not the same as equal ability to place an axis on the chart.

This is why institutions use representatives, advocates, ombudsmen, public-interest groups, unions, professional bodies, and procedural rights. These mechanisms can become bureaucratic or captured. Their purpose is to reduce the gap between being affected and being legible to the decision.

Representation itself has an efficiency cost.

A process that hears every possible claimant individually may never finish. A process that hears only organized actors can systematically miss diffuse interests. Future people, nonusers, small suppliers, and people who would enter a market if rules changed are especially difficult because they are absent from the room by construction.

The frontier therefore needs a representation rule proportionate to consequence.

Who is affected materially? Who can already protect their own interest through price, contract, or exit? Who cannot? Which absent group would predictably experience a cost large enough to change the choice?

The goal is not universal veto power. It is to stop low bargaining power from being mistaken for low value.

Compensation is one way to handle a frontier with winners and losers. If a project creates large aggregate gains and concentrated losses, the analysis may show that compensation is possible. The political and institutional question is whether it will actually happen.

Potential compensation and paid compensation are different points.

This distinction matters because economists and managers can casually treat a compensable loss as solved. A factory closure can raise total productivity while imposing severe local costs. A land-use change can create regional gains and property-level losses. A product migration can improve the system while imposing switching costs on a minority of customers.

If compensation is part of the argument, put the compensation mechanism inside the feasible set. Who pays, who qualifies, when is payment made, how is loss measured, and what transaction cost does the mechanism create?

Otherwise the compensation is a hypothetical axis that exists only long enough to make the aggregate result look cleaner.

There is a parallel problem with consent. A person may accept a trade voluntarily and still face weak alternatives. Voluntary exchange is meaningful information about preference under constraint. It does not tell us that the surrounding constraint deserves to remain unchanged.

This distinction prevents two opposite mistakes. One is to dismiss every accepted bargain as coercion because alternatives were imperfect. The other is to treat acceptance as proof that no institutional improvement is possible.

The frontier can respect choice and still ask whether a wider feasible set would produce better choices.

Constitutions also need jurisdiction. A value can be protected at the wrong level. A local team may impose a security rule whose system-wide cost is large. A national rule may override useful local variation. A company principle may be interpreted by every manager differently.

Who can add an axis? Who can turn an objective into a constraint? Who can waive it? Who can revise it?

Those are decision-right questions around the model itself.

A good governance system separates ordinary optimization from constitutional change. The product team can optimize inside privacy constraints. The privacy constraint is revised through a different process with broader authority. A city agency can optimize a route inside statutory accessibility rules. The legislature or court, not the route planner, changes the legal floor.

This separation protects both speed and legitimacy.

If every local choice can reopen first principles, nothing stabilizes. If no higher-level process can revise the principles, obsolete constraints accumulate.

The constitution needs a revision frontier of its own.

The final practical rule is to annotate the axes with provenance. For each material objective or constraint, record where it came from: law, contract, customer evidence, safety standard, executive preference, public mandate, inferred proxy, or historical convention.

Provenance changes how a disagreement should be resolved.

A legal constraint requires legal interpretation or legal change. A customer preference calls for evidence. An executive preference can be owned as judgment. A historical convention deserves a mechanism check. A proxy deserves validation against the outcome it represents.

The graph becomes more honest when every axis carries the answer to one small question.

Who gave this line authority?
