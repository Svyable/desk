# The Enough Line

The efficient frontier has an uncomfortable property.

It does not tell you when to stop wanting more.

If more expected return is better, the frontier can keep inviting you toward more risk. If lower latency is better, the frontier can keep inviting you toward more cost. If more income is better, it can keep inviting you toward more work. If more safety is better, it can keep inviting you toward more expense, delay, restriction, or redundancy.

The frontier shows the best available tradeoffs. It does not contain a natural command to stop moving.

That stop has to come from somewhere else.

I call that boundary the enough line. It is not a mathematical theorem. It is a decision discipline: define a level beyond which the next unit of an objective no longer deserves automatic pursuit, especially when the marginal price is rising.

Enough does not mean mediocre.

It can be extremely demanding. A commercial aircraft does not aim for "enough safety" in the casual sense. A medical device, payment network, or nuclear plant may require extraordinary reliability. The point is that even high-reliability systems operate through standards, tolerances, and acceptable risk criteria rather than a literal demand for infinite safety at any cost.

Every real system stops somewhere.

The choice is whether the stopping rule is explicit.

Without one, optimization tends to inherit the incentives of the strongest metric. A company with a growth target keeps buying growth after the cheap opportunities are gone. A product team keeps adding capability after the product already satisfies the core use. A person keeps converting time into income after the money has stopped changing daily life very much. A government keeps adding safeguards after a risk has been reduced to the point where the next safeguard costs more than competing protections elsewhere.

The problem is not ambition. The problem is directionless marginalism.

More remains better by definition, so the system has no internal reason to stop.

Economics usually handles this through diminishing marginal utility, opportunity cost, and budget constraints. Real decision makers handle it through thresholds, aspirations, policies, habits, and sometimes exhaustion. Herbert Simon's notion of satisficing captured something important about bounded decision makers: search can stop when an option crosses a satisfactory threshold rather than continuing until an unknowable global optimum is found.

The enough line brings that logic into frontier thinking.

Suppose a company is designing a customer support promise. It could aim for an average first response in one business day, four hours, one hour, fifteen minutes, or one minute. Faster is generally attractive. But each step has a different operational cost. A one-minute promise may require staffing for peaks, extensive automation, global coverage, or idle capacity. If customers do not value the difference between one minute and fifteen minutes enough to justify those resources, the fastest point may be efficient and still be a bad choice.

An enough line might say that responses within fifteen minutes satisfy the product promise. Once that threshold is reliably met, extra capacity should compete with other uses of capital and attention rather than receiving automatic priority.

The threshold changes the shape of management.

Below it, response time is a problem. Above it, response time becomes one objective among others.

This is especially useful for reliability. Engineers often speak in terms of service levels because reliability has a steep marginal cost near the edge. The difference between a system that is usually available and one designed for extremely high availability can require redundant components, fault isolation, careful operations, and significant expense. The appropriate target depends on the consequence of failure.

A family photo app and a cardiac monitoring system should not share an enough line.

The point is contextual sufficiency.

This can sound dangerously subjective. What prevents a company from setting a low enough line to excuse poor quality? Nothing, if the line is arbitrary and private. A credible threshold needs a basis: customer need, law, engineering standard, contract, mission, evidence about harm, or explicit strategic choice.

An enough line should be defendable to the people who bear the consequence.

That makes it different from lowering the bar after missing a target.

It is chosen before the marginal chase, then revised when evidence changes.

Personal finance offers a simple example. A person can pursue higher expected return indefinitely by accepting more risk, leverage, concentration, or illiquidity. The right choice depends on goals, time horizon, liabilities, and tolerance for loss. If the portfolio already has a high probability of funding the person's actual obligations and desired life, the marginal value of more expected wealth may be lower than the marginal cost of additional risk.

The enough line can be a funded goal rather than maximum wealth.

That shift sounds obvious, yet many financial decisions are made against relative performance, status, or fear of missing out rather than the liabilities the portfolio exists to meet. The frontier becomes a racetrack because no finish line was defined.

Careers produce the same problem.

Compensation is measurable, visible, and socially comparable. Autonomy, schedule control, health, meaning, and relationship quality are harder to compare. A person can keep moving along an income frontier because each higher number is legible even after additional income buys little that the person can name.

An enough line does not require declaring money unimportant. It asks for a number or condition after which another dollar must compete honestly with what it costs.

The same applies to prestige.

There is always another title, audience, award, invitation, house, neighborhood, credential, or institution. Status goods are partly positional, which means the frontier can move as the reference group moves. If the objective is to be ahead, enough is structurally unavailable.

A positional objective has no natural saturation point.

That makes it a powerful engine and a poor master.

Organizations can fall into positional frontiers too. Market share, rankings, benchmark performance, response times, feature counts, and headcount can become relative competitions detached from customer value. A company adds features because competitors have them. A university adds programs because peers have them. A city pursues a trophy project because another city built one. The marginal unit serves comparison rather than mission.

An enough line reconnects the objective to purpose.

What level is sufficient for the job this system exists to do?

That question can be radical in environments trained to maximize.

It is also difficult because underoptimization has real costs. Stop too early and you tolerate avoidable waste or inferior outcomes. A company that declares product quality "good enough" before customers agree can lose the market. A public agency that declares service adequate can institutionalize neglect. A person who uses enough as an excuse for fear can avoid growth that would matter.

Enough needs an audit.

Is the threshold based on evidence or convenience? Does it satisfy the affected stakeholder or only the provider? Has the environment changed? Does the threshold conceal unequal outcomes? Are we calling an interior point sufficient because moving feels hard?

The enough line should stop marginal pursuit after a credible requirement is met. It should not protect domination.

This is why the frontier comes first.

If another available option improves an important objective without worsening anything material, enough should not be used to defend the worse option. Why pay for waste? The enough line becomes relevant when further improvement requires sacrifice.

It separates adequacy from maximality.

This is useful in design.

A product can be overdesigned. A building can carry finishes, systems, and tolerances that add cost without meaningful benefit to its actual use. Software can be generalized for hypothetical futures that never arrive. A report can be polished long after the reader's decision would be unchanged. A kitchen can be equipped for dishes nobody cooks.

Perfection is sometimes a frontier point with a terrible exchange rate.

The enough line protects resources for other objectives.

A team that decides a report needs to be accurate, clear, and delivered by Wednesday can stop formatting at the point where additional polish no longer changes use. The saved time can improve another analysis. A product team that defines a latency target based on user perception can stop optimizing once the threshold is consistently met and spend on reliability or capability. A household that decides what level of housing satisfies its actual needs can compare the next increment of space with savings, travel, flexibility, or a shorter commute.

Enough creates option value elsewhere.

There is an important connection to budgets. A budget is not only a constraint. It can be a forced enough line. It says one objective cannot consume every resource because resources must be allocated across a portfolio of needs.

This is why organizations sometimes perform better under constraint. A hard limit forces prioritization. Features are cut. Processes are simplified. Teams find cheaper ways to satisfy the core requirement. The constraint can reveal interior waste.

But romanticizing constraint is dangerous. Some budgets are simply inadequate. A hospital, school, maintenance program, or safety function can be underfunded below the threshold where its mission is feasible. Constraint does not automatically produce creativity. It can produce degradation.

The enough line therefore belongs on both sides of allocation.

Define a floor below which the mission is not acceptably served. Define a point above which additional resources face a higher burden of proof.

Between them is a region where judgment operates.

This resembles the idea of aspiration levels in decision analysis and satisficing, but its practical use here is broader. It keeps the frontier from turning every objective into an infinite appetite.

Consider climate and energy policy. Societies care about cost, reliability, emissions, land use, energy security, local pollution, industrial capacity, and speed of deployment. There is no single two-axis frontier that resolves those objectives. Yet enough lines appear everywhere as standards and targets: reliability requirements, emissions goals, affordability limits, safety standards, deployment timelines.

The quality of the decision depends partly on whether those thresholds reflect the actual mission and whether they can be updated when technology changes.

If clean generation becomes cheaper, a previous affordability tradeoff may weaken. If reliability risks become clearer, the minimum reserve requirement may change. If a technology's environmental burden is larger than expected, the axis set may expand.

Enough is provisional because frontiers move.

A threshold that was ambitious under an old technology can become wastefully low under a new one. A response time of two days might once have been excellent and later become unacceptable. A building efficiency standard can become cheap to exceed after materials improve. A data transfer speed that once defined premium service can become ordinary.

The enough line should not fossilize yesterday's frontier.

This creates an interesting asymmetry. We should be conservative about moving an enough line downward when the people who bear the cost have little power. We should be curious about moving it upward when new capabilities make improvement cheap.

The line is a governance choice, not a monument.

It can also protect against metric gaming because it removes incentives to overoptimize a measure after the mission is satisfied.

If a call center must answer within a service window but receives no extra reward for shaving a few seconds below that window, managers have less reason to rush conversations merely to improve an already sufficient metric. If a delivery promise is two days and customer value does not improve materially at one day, the network does not have to contort itself for a vanity ranking. If a safety process has defined performance requirements, teams can focus on meeting the requirement robustly rather than maximizing a proxy that may not map to actual safety.

Targets can create gaming too. A hard threshold can lead people to cluster performance just above the line and ignore cases just below it. The design still needs judgment.

Enough is not a magic number.

It is a refusal to let "more" remain unexamined.

That refusal matters most when the marginal exchange rate becomes steep.

A person can spend increasingly large amounts of time trying to eliminate the final uncertainty from a decision. A company can spend increasingly large amounts trying to forecast a market that remains inherently uncertain. A writer can revise a sentence for another hour. A planner can delay action to collect one more study. A security team can add another control that reduces a tiny risk while creating significant friction.

At some point the search for a better decision becomes the costliest part of the decision.

The enough line can apply to information itself.

How much certainty is enough to act?

This is not permission for recklessness. High-consequence decisions deserve more evidence. Reversible low-consequence decisions deserve less. The point is that information has a marginal value and a marginal cost. Waiting can be costly. Analysis can consume opportunity. Data can become stale while it is collected.

A decision process needs a stopping rule.

This is where enough meets uncertainty and reversibility. If a decision is easy to reverse, you can often act with less information and learn. If a decision is irreversible and catastrophic failure is possible, the evidence threshold should be higher. There is no single standard detached from consequence.

The enough line is therefore not softness. It can be strict where failure matters and relaxed where experimentation is cheap.

It is a way to allocate seriousness.

The modern optimization culture sometimes treats any unmaximized metric as a problem waiting for a project. Faster is better. More personalized is better. More automated is better. More data is better. More utilization is better. More engagement is better.

Each statement may be true near one part of the curve.

None should be granted infinity.

The frontier gives us the tradeoff. The enough line gives us permission to stop paying after the job is done.

That permission is valuable because resources saved at one frontier reappear on another. Time not spent polishing can become rest. Capital not spent shaving seconds from delivery can become resilience. Attention not spent maximizing status can become curiosity. Organizational energy not spent chasing a saturated metric can become experimentation.

Enough is not the opposite of ambition.

It is how ambition avoids eating its own budget.

Enough is easier to govern as a band than as a single point. A single threshold creates the temptation to manage exactly to the number. A band can distinguish unacceptable, sufficient, and exceptional performance.

Below the floor, improvement receives priority because the mission is not being met. Inside the sufficient band, tradeoffs with other objectives become legitimate. Above the upper edge, additional improvement requires a specific reason because the marginal resources probably belong elsewhere.

The band makes room for noise.

A service with a fifteen-minute target should not reorganize itself every time one week averages sixteen. A system operating near a hard safety threshold, by contrast, may need a buffer because measurement and conditions vary. Enough should be defined with the uncertainty of the metric in mind.

This prevents threshold fetishism.

The point of enough is not to turn one optimization problem into a new target game. It is to create a stopping rule robust enough that ordinary variance does not trigger constant reallocation.

Enough can also differ by segment. A hospital may need different response requirements for different clinical urgency. A cloud provider can offer different reliability tiers. A logistics company may promise different delivery speeds by service level. A household may require much more certainty for housing than for vacation plans.

One universal threshold is attractive administratively and often wrong substantively.

Tiered enough lines can preserve resources by matching service intensity to consequence.

They can also create unfairness when the lower tier is imposed on people with less power rather than chosen or justified by need. A two-tier system is not efficient merely because it is segmented. The question remains who gets which line and why.

This is especially important in public systems. A school, court, hospital, or transit network may be tempted to define "acceptable" service in aggregate while a particular group consistently sits below it. The average crosses the enough line and the distribution does not.

Sufficiency has to be checked at the level where the mission is owed.

The enough line also changes competition. In markets, an objective that has crossed sufficiency for customers may still matter competitively if a rival uses it as a signal. A camera can be good enough for ordinary use while specification races continue. A delivery network can be fast enough for customer need while firms compete on speed because the number is easy to advertise. A fund can be adequately diversified while clients chase a recent return ranking.

Competitive pressure can push systems beyond user sufficiency.

Sometimes that race produces innovation that later matters. Sometimes it consumes resources in positional competition. The distinction is whether the extra unit creates durable value or merely preserves relative status.

This is why an enough line is ultimately a portfolio-allocation concept. Stopping on one dimension is not inactivity. It releases scarce resources for another dimension whose marginal return is higher.

A company that stops shaving milliseconds can invest in accessibility. A household that decides the current house is enough can invest in financial freedom. A city that stops widening one road can improve transit reliability elsewhere. A researcher who stops polishing a settled method can explore another question.

Enough is the point where the opportunity cost becomes more interesting than the improvement.

There is also courage in declaring enough because many institutions reward visible maximization and do not reward the opportunity preserved by stopping. An executive can point to market share gained, not to complexity avoided. A professional can point to the promotion accepted, not to the family schedule preserved. A product team can demo the feature built, not the maintenance burden never created.

The gains from enough are often counterfactual.

That makes the discipline vulnerable unless the released resource is deliberately reallocated. "We are done optimizing this" should be followed by "therefore this is what we will do instead."

Otherwise enough can become a polite name for drift.

A good enough line has a mission basis, a measurement tolerance, a review condition, and a competing use for the resources it saves.

That is much more demanding than saying good enough.

It is a decision about where ambition should move next.
