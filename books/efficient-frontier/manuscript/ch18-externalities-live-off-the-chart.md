# Externalities Live Off the Chart

The easiest way to improve your frontier is to make somebody else carry the cost.

The sentence is unfair to many genuine improvements and accurate about enough systems to deserve suspicion.

A factory lowers private cost by discharging pollution into a river. A commuter saves time by driving in a way that adds congestion and risk to others. A company improves cash conversion by extending payment terms to suppliers. A digital platform keeps prices low by shifting fraud, moderation, or data costs elsewhere. A city restricts new housing and preserves incumbent property values while increasing costs for people trying to enter.

From one boundary, the choice is efficient.

From another, the cost has moved.

Economics calls many of these effects externalities: costs or benefits imposed on people who are not fully reflected in the transaction. The literature is far richer than this chapter can summarize. The frontier idea contributes a simple visual discipline. Before accepting an outward move, widen the boundary until the displaced cost has a chance to reappear.

The system boundary is an argument about responsibility.

A company's accounting boundary is legally important. It is not a law of physics. Emissions cross it. Traffic crosses it. Workers carry stress home. Suppliers finance terms. Customers dispose of packaging. Governments repair public infrastructure used by private activity.

Some of those effects are appropriately left outside the company's decision because they are tiny, already priced, or governed elsewhere. Some are central enough that excluding them produces counterfeit efficiency.

The hard problem is not discovering that externalities exist.

It is deciding which ones matter enough to bring onto the chart and how.

Prices do some of this work. If a company must pay for waste disposal, land, energy, insurance, congestion charges, emissions permits, or liability, some external cost becomes internal. Contracts can do the same. Regulation can impose standards. Courts can assign damages. Reputation can make customers punish behavior. Worker bargaining can price undesirable conditions.

Institutions move costs across boundaries.

This is why policy can change private frontiers without changing technology.

A carbon price, safety rule, zoning change, disclosure requirement, or liability standard changes the set of economically attractive actions. A practice that looked cheap becomes expensive once part of its social cost enters the decision. Another technology or process becomes relatively more attractive.

The policy does not necessarily reduce the whole social frontier. It can move private choices closer to a broader one.

That is the theory. The practice is messier.

Regulation has administrative cost. Prices can be set badly. Measurement can be weak. Firms can lobby for rules that protect incumbents. Compliance can favor large organizations that can absorb fixed cost. A policy aimed at one externality can create another.

Internalization is itself a frontier problem.

How much measurement, enforcement, friction, and rigidity are justified by the harm being priced?

This is where ideological arguments often outrun mechanism. One side says the market will solve it. Another says regulation will solve it. The frontier asks what institutional arrangement makes the cost visible to the actor best positioned to change it, at an enforcement cost worth paying.

Sometimes the answer is a price.

Sometimes a rule.

Sometimes liability.

Sometimes property rights.

Sometimes information and choice.

Sometimes public provision.

The correct instrument depends on the shape of the externality.

Pollution illustrates why. If harm is roughly related to the amount emitted and emissions can be measured, pricing can create a continuous incentive to reduce. If a substance is dangerous above a threshold or monitoring is difficult, a standard or ban may be more appropriate. If local concentration matters, a uniform price may not reflect geography. If the harm is irreversible, waiting for price signals can be too weak.

The frontier of policy instruments depends on measurement, enforceability, distribution, and uncertainty.

Externalities also have time horizons.

Climate change is an obvious case because present emissions alter future conditions. Infrastructure undermaintenance creates future users. Public debt shifts some financing burden through time. Antibiotic overuse can contribute to resistance that affects others later. Depletion of groundwater or soil can create costs after current production has been sold.

Future people are the ultimate weak stakeholder in many decisions because they cannot bargain today.

Discounting becomes central.

How much less should a future cost count than a present one? Finance, economics, public policy, and ethics offer different answers depending on the context. A positive discount rate can reflect time preference, opportunity cost, growth, risk, and other considerations. But the choice of rate can determine whether long-lived harms appear trivial or dominant.

The discount rate is an axis-weighting device across time.

It should not be treated as a neutral formatting choice.

A project with benefits now and costs in fifty years can look attractive under one discount rate and unattractive under another. The analysis should show that sensitivity when it matters.

Again, the frontier does not settle the ethical question. It identifies where the ethical choice enters.

Externalities can also be positive.

Education can benefit people beyond the student through productivity, civic participation, or spillovers. Vaccination can reduce transmission risk to others. Research can produce knowledge that others use. Open standards can make complementary innovation easier. A well-maintained property can improve a street. A dense transit network can become more useful as participation grows.

Private actors may underinvest when they cannot capture enough of the social gain.

The private frontier can sit inside the social frontier because a benefit is off the private chart.

This is the mirror image of pollution.

Subsidies, public funding, prizes, intellectual-property rules, procurement, and cooperative institutions are attempts to move more of the social benefit into the actor's feasible set.

They can overshoot or create rent seeking. The mechanism matters.

The frontier perspective is agnostic about the instrument and insistent about the missing value.

Network effects create especially complicated boundaries because one user's choice changes the value for others. A communication network becomes more useful when more relevant people join. A marketplace gains liquidity with participation. A standard becomes more valuable when more products support it.

The private and social frontier can move together during growth.

Then congestion, spam, fraud, moderation, monopoly power, or lock-in can create new externalities. The same network that generated positive spillovers begins generating negative ones.

The frontier bends as scale changes.

This is why policies that encourage early adoption can become inappropriate after market power emerges. A subsidy designed to build a network may be wasteful after the network is self-sustaining. Light regulation that encouraged experimentation may fail once the platform becomes infrastructure.

Institutional design should notice lifecycle frontiers.

Housing provides another example where boundaries collide.

A homeowner may value neighborhood stability, low traffic, sunlight, parking, school quality, and property value. A prospective resident may value access to jobs and housing. A city may value tax base, affordability, environmental outcomes, infrastructure capacity, and political consent.

Restrictions on new housing can protect some local objectives and impose regional costs through higher prices or longer commutes. Unrestricted development can add supply and impose local congestion, infrastructure burdens, or loss of amenities.

The argument is not resolved by calling one side efficient.

The axes belong to different people.

A better analysis identifies the external costs and benefits, asks which are priced or compensated, and distinguishes real constraints from distributive preferences.

If additional residents create infrastructure costs, those costs can potentially be priced through fees or taxes. If incumbents enjoy scarcity gains from blocking entry, that is a distributional effect rather than a pure efficiency benefit. If development creates environmental harm, mitigation may change the frontier.

The policy question becomes more specific once the effects are separated.

Externalities hide inside companies as well.

A platform team makes a change that simplifies its service and forces twenty product teams to migrate. The platform's maintenance frontier improves. The company's total migration cost rises. A sales team offers custom terms that increase its revenue and create exceptions for billing, support, and legal. A finance team centralizes a process and makes local decisions slower.

These are internal externalities.

Transfer pricing, shared metrics, governance, and architecture are ways organizations attempt to internalize them.

The same logic explains why internal platforms can be powerful when designed well. They absorb recurring cross-team complexity into a shared capability. A compliance platform can make a legal constraint cheap to satisfy. A data platform can reduce duplication. A common identity system can improve security and user experience together.

The platform moves the company frontier if its shared benefit exceeds its coordination and centralization cost.

Bad platforms become tax authorities with APIs.

They impose standardization, migration, and process while optimizing their own elegance.

The externality has simply moved to the product teams.

This is a reminder that system boundaries should be tested after organizational design, not assumed from it.

Personal choices also create externalities inside relationships.

One person's flexible schedule can make another person's schedule less flexible if caregiving, household work, or coordination shifts. One person's career move can change family geography. One person's consumption can create shared debt. One person's preference for spontaneity can impose planning cost on others.

These are not market failures in the formal sense. They share the structural feature that one person's frontier point changes another person's feasible set.

Relationships require joint frontiers.

A household that treats each adult as an independent optimizer can create a system where unpaid work disappears from both spreadsheets. The total burden still exists and often lands unevenly.

Naming the shared system changes the decision.

This is especially important when the costs are hard to monetize. Care, emotional labor, schedule predictability, and administrative work are real even when no market price is paid.

The frontier is not limited to things with invoices.

That principle can be abused. If every subjective inconvenience becomes an externality demanding compensation, ordinary social life becomes impossible. People affect each other. The relevant question is materiality, reciprocity, and consent.

Which effects are large enough, persistent enough, or asymmetrical enough that leaving them off the chart predictably distorts choice?

There is no universal threshold.

Institutions create rules so every interaction does not require bespoke negotiation. Noise ordinances, traffic laws, contracts, norms, property rules, workplace policies, and family agreements all predefine some boundaries.

The rules are imperfect and cheaper than permanent renegotiation.

This brings externalities back to the enough line. We internalize costs only to a point because measurement and enforcement themselves consume resources. A city cannot meter every sidewalk interaction. A company cannot allocate every minute of shared infrastructure precisely. A household should not invoice every favor.

Good governance tolerates small unpriced spillovers to avoid larger transaction costs.

The frontier includes the cost of perfect accounting.

This is a useful antidote to moralized optimization. The existence of an externality does not prove that a formal intervention improves welfare. The intervention has its own frontier.

The practical discipline is narrower.

When a choice appears to improve several objectives at once, trace the boundary.

Did another team inherit work?

Did another party take risk?

Did a future budget receive the bill?

Did an ecosystem absorb waste?

Did a public system provide an input without being priced?

Did a household member pick up the coordination?

Did a customer give up privacy, time, or switching freedom?

If the answer is yes, the next question is whether that transfer is legitimate, compensated, reciprocal, regulated, or simply hidden.

Sometimes the transfer is the point. Insurance transfers risk. Contracts allocate obligations. Taxes fund public goods. Families share burdens. Firms specialize. A good system moves costs to the party best able to manage them.

Externality is not a synonym for injustice.

It is a clue that the frontier depends on a boundary.

The most important frontier may therefore be the one you get after asking a childish question the spreadsheet dislikes.

Where did the cost go?

If the answer is nowhere, you may have discovered real progress.

If the answer is someone else, later, the graph was never finished.
