# Research Brief

## Thesis under test

*Efficient Frontier* argues that a concept associated most visibly with portfolio selection can serve as a disciplined language for consequential tradeoffs far beyond investing, provided the book does not confuse mathematical efficiency with goodness, preference, legitimacy, or progress.

The strong version of the claim — that every important decision can be reduced to an efficient-frontier calculation — is false and would make the book worse. The version worth testing is narrower:

> When several objectives conflict, it is useful to distinguish three different jobs: eliminating dominated choices, choosing among nondominated choices, and changing the feasible set itself. The distinction becomes more useful, not less, when the model also exposes omitted objectives, uncertainty, path dependence, reversibility, distribution, and externalities.

The manuscript's smallest formulation is: **Waste lives inside the frontier. Values live on it. Invention moves it.**

That line is an interpretive synthesis. It is not presented as a theorem from Markowitz, Pareto, multiobjective optimization, robust optimization, decision theory, or any other source.

The book should survive serious counterexamples. A claimed frontier move that only transfers cost to another stakeholder is not a move of the broader frontier. A supposedly efficient choice that becomes dominated once a material objective is restored is not efficient under the relevant problem. A weighted score that hides a hard constraint is not a neutral technical answer. A robust choice that is robust only to a conveniently narrow uncertainty set should be described as fragile. A learning investment that does not measurably change capability is cost, not tuition.

## Established theory versus manuscript synthesis

The following concepts are established bodies of theory and must be attributed as such:

- mean-variance portfolio selection and the efficient set;
- Pareto dominance and Pareto optimality in multiobjective optimization;
- scalarization and preference articulation;
- bounded rationality and satisficing;
- risk versus deeper uncertainty;
- real options, irreversibility, and the value of waiting for information;
- robust optimization and robust portfolio selection;
- learning and experience curves;
- externalities, transaction costs, and institutional responses to social cost.

The following labels are manuscript synthesis and should **not** be written as if they were standard technical terminology:

- **frontier debt** — a present improvement financed by consuming a future stock of capability, condition, trust, resilience, or other capacity;
- **the enough line** — a decision discipline for stopping marginal pursuit once a credible sufficiency region is reached;
- **the counterfeit frontier** — an apparently improved frontier produced by a narrowed boundary, manipulated baseline, omitted cost, subsidy, selection effect, proxy failure, false feasibility, or similar accounting move;
- **the option value wedge** — the difference between a static comparison and a path comparison once future branches and reversibility are priced;
- **frontier foreclosure** — the case where borrowing against the future destroys or makes costly the path back to a wider feasible set;
- **strategy as feasible-set design** — the book's strategic framing for building capabilities, permissions, assets, relationships, and constraints that change which choices become reachable.

These phrases are useful only if the underlying mechanisms remain distinguishable from the names.

## Core empirical and conceptual tests

1. **Dominance test:** Is the allegedly inferior option actually dominated after transition cost, uncertainty, time horizon, and all material objectives are included?
2. **Preference test:** When nondominated options remain, is the recommendation driven by stated preference or constraint information, or is preference being disguised inside a score?
3. **Frontier-movement test:** Did technology, learning, process, bargaining, institutional design, or another mechanism actually expand the feasible set, or was a cost merely displaced?
4. **Boundary test:** Which stakeholders, dates, risks, and system effects sit outside the model, and would restoring them reverse the result?
5. **Uncertainty test:** Does the recommendation survive plausible parameter or model error, and which assumption is load-bearing?
6. **Reversibility test:** Which future branches are preserved or destroyed, and is their option value material relative to carrying cost?
7. **Debt test:** What stock is consumed to create today's apparent improvement, how is that stock observed, and what constitutes repayment?
8. **Enough test:** Is a sufficiency threshold grounded in mission, safety, customer need, law, or evidence, or is it an excuse to protect an interior point?
9. **Learning test:** What measurable capability should improve with experience, at what horizon, and what evidence would show that the learning claim failed?
10. **Governance test:** Who has authority to define objectives, constraints, and weights; who bears the consequences; and is that authority legitimate for the decision?
11. **Externality test:** Where did the cost go, and can the receiving party avoid it, price it, bargain over it, recover from it, or be represented before it occurs?
12. **Durability test:** For public and organizational policy, can the selected point actually be administered and sustained long enough for the expected mechanism to work?

## Counter-evidence the manuscript must preserve

- Some simultaneous improvements really are free lunches relative to the previous point because waste, rework, information failure, or a removable bottleneck existed.
- Some tradeoffs are genuine under current physics, budgets, rights, or capacity; refusing to admit them is not creativity.
- Weighted scoring and scalarization can be legitimate preference tools when the weights are owned explicitly and hard constraints are represented correctly.
- Optionality can be overbought. Commitment can create learning, scale, coordination, trust, and depth that flexible systems never earn.
- Robustness can become overconservatism if the uncertainty set includes every imaginable failure rather than plausible ones.
- Standardization can improve robustness through interchangeability and create fragility through monoculture; neither diversity nor uniformity is universally superior.
- Learning curves are not automatic. Repetition without feedback can entrench bad practice.
- Externalities do not imply that formal regulation is automatically superior. Measurement, bargaining, enforcement, information, and administrative costs matter.
- Aggregate gains do not settle distribution, compensation, rights, legitimacy, or transition.
- Public policy that is theoretically efficient can fail because administration, compliance, political durability, or behavioral response makes the modeled point infeasible.

## Evidence lanes

### I. Portfolio selection and efficient sets

Use Markowitz's 1952 paper as the foundation for the finance-specific opening: portfolios should be evaluated as combinations, expected return and variance create a tradeoff, and an efficient set can be distinguished from inferior combinations. Do not project later terminology backward into the paper.

### II. Pareto fronts and multiobjective optimization

Use standard multiobjective sources to define dominance, nondominated/Pareto-optimal points, trade-off surfaces, scalarization, and the fact that multiple efficient points normally remain. These sources support the distinction between identifying an efficient set and articulating preference; they do not decide which preference is morally or politically legitimate.

### III. Bounded rationality and sufficiency

Use Simon to ground the observation that real decision makers search under cognitive and informational limits and may use aspiration levels or satisficing rather than global optimization. The **enough line** remains the manuscript's broader decision device, not Simon's term.

### IV. Uncertainty and robustness

Use Knight for the historical distinction between measurable risk and deeper uncertainty, Goldfarb–Iyengar for robust portfolio sensitivity to statistical/modeling errors, and broader robust-optimization sources for uncertainty-set reasoning. Preserve the limitation that robustness is always conditional on the uncertainty set and objective chosen.

### V. Reversibility and option value

Use Dixit–Pindyck for irreversible investment under uncertainty and the value of waiting for information. The manuscript generalizes the intuition to staging, exit, scale-up, modularity, career trials, and policy review without pretending those are one formal option-pricing problem.

### VI. Learning and experience

Use Wright as a historical anchor for learning/experience effects in aircraft production and later learning-curve literature where needed. Do not assume every task follows a power law or that time automatically creates learning.

### VII. Metrics and proxies

Use Goodhart cautiously as a historical reference for target/measure problems. Avoid attributing the popular one-line slogan as though every later formulation appears verbatim in his original monetary-policy discussion. The chapter's concern is broader: optimizing in proxy space can move the metric frontier while the outcome frontier stands still.

### VIII. Externalities and institutions

Use Coase and the wider economics of externalities to ground the role of system boundaries, transaction costs, bargaining, liability, pricing, and institutional design. Do not flatten Coase into the slogan that bargaining always solves externalities; transaction costs and institutional comparison are central to the useful reading.

## Falsification protocol

For every major example or case added during later revisions, create a note with five fields:

1. **Claim:** the concrete factual or causal statement used in the prose.
2. **Source:** a primary, authoritative, or high-quality secondary source that directly supports the claim.
3. **Boundary:** which objectives, stakeholders, and time horizon the claim assumes.
4. **What would make this false:** a concrete observation or alternative explanation that would reverse or materially weaken the claim.
5. **Disposition:** keep, qualify, replace, or remove.

The purpose is not to make the book unfalsifiable by qualification. It is to make the argument easier to kill where it is weak.

## Freshness discipline

Foundational mathematical and economic sources are intentionally historical. Time-sensitive examples are not. Before a publication-candidate pass, recheck any named company, technology, regulation, market practice, public program, or contemporary institutional claim against sources current at the time of revision.

Do not refresh a stable theorem merely to make the bibliography look recent. Do refresh claims whose truth can change.

## Source discipline

- Prefer original papers, publisher records, books from authoritative publishers, government or regulator material, and primary institutional sources.
- Keep URLs and DOI identifiers in the source registry so claims can be rechecked without relying on memory.
- Do not invent quotations, scene details, numerical examples presented as historical fact, or attributions.
- Distinguish illustrative hypothetical numbers in the prose from observed statistics.
- Treat the manuscript's coined vocabulary as synthesis, not evidence.
- If a source supports only an analogy or adjacent mechanism, say so.
- If a source contains assumptions that do not transfer to the book's broader use, preserve the limitation.
