# Group Psychosis Therapy

*How to Think Like a Wolf in a World of Sheep*

| | |
|---|---|
| **Author** | Sven Hardy Benson |
| **Status** | Drafting |
| **Chapters** | 3 of 20 drafted |

The working thesis is that the most dangerous property of a crowd is not that it is usually wrong. It is that a crowd can change the cost of seeing clearly.

Human beings are social learners. We copy because copying works. We trust other people because no individual can independently verify the bridge, the medicine, the weather model, the bank balance, the election count, the aircraft maintenance log, and the thousand invisible systems required to cross an ordinary Tuesday. Civilization is coordinated trust.

The trouble begins when the social signal stops being evidence and becomes a substitute for evidence.

*Group Psychosis Therapy* is a deliberately provocative title, not a clinical diagnosis. Clinical psychosis is a serious medical phenomenon involving disruptions in perception and contact with reality. This book uses "group psychosis" as a metaphor for something different: situations in which social incentives, identity, repetition, silence, authority, and distorted perceptions of consensus pull groups away from reliable reality-testing.

The wolf/sheep language is also a metaphor, and the book refuses the cheap version of it. A wolf is not a person who automatically chooses the unpopular opinion. Reflexive contrarianism is another form of dependence: the crowd still decides what you think; you merely multiply its answer by minus one. The wolf is the person who can preserve independent observation long enough to ask what the evidence would look like if nobody were watching.

That is the therapy.

The book follows conformity experiments, pluralistic ignorance, preference falsification, identity-protective belief, online norm distortion, misinformation, authority, expert herding, rumor cascades, moral contagion, and the fragile engineering of collective intelligence. It ends with practical reality-testing protocols for individuals, teams, institutions, and online communities.

This is distinct from *Deceptatron*, which investigates adaptive propaganda systems; *Algorithmic Influence*, which follows people trying to influence machine intermediaries; and *Public Pressure*, which focuses on accountability inside powerful institutions. This book is about the mind under social load.
