# Research brief — Group Psychosis Therapy

## Working title

**Group Psychosis Therapy: How to Think Like a Wolf in a World of Sheep**

## Core claim

The book argues that collective failures of reality-testing often emerge not because most people become irrational at once, but because social systems alter what evidence is visible, what disagreement costs, what people believe other people believe, and which motivations—accuracy, belonging, status, safety, identity, outrage—are rewarded at the moment of judgment.

The key variable is not crowd size. It is the integrity of the information process.

The constructive thesis is that independent thought can be engineered. Individuals and institutions can preserve private estimates, separate evidence from social reaction, lower the cost of dissent, distinguish independent sources from copied ones, use probabilistic forecasts, reward error correction, and design networks that combine information without immediately homogenizing it.

## Terminology discipline

"Group psychosis" is a rhetorical metaphor, not a psychiatric diagnosis.

Clinical psychosis involves disruptions in thought and perception and may include delusions or hallucinations. Do not imply that political disagreement, misinformation, ideological commitment, conformity, crowd behavior, or polarization is literally psychosis.

Never diagnose a population or named group.

"Wolf" and "sheep" are also metaphors. The book must explicitly reject the notion that minority opinion is inherently superior. Reflexive contrarianism is treated as dependence on the crowd with the sign reversed.

## Adjacent-book boundaries

- **Deceptatron** asks how adaptive propaganda systems model and move individuals. This book asks what happens inside the individual's and group's reality-testing process under social pressure.
- **Algorithmic Influence** examines people optimizing themselves for machine intermediaries. This book treats algorithms mainly as distorters or amplifiers of perceived social norms.
- **Public Pressure** focuses on legitimacy, coercion, accountability, and institutions. This book focuses on conformity, private belief, perceived consensus, and epistemic independence.
- Do not turn this into a generic misinformation book. Misinformation is one domain in a broader theory of social reality-testing.

## Chapter progression

### 1. The Line on the Page

Open with Asch because the task is concrete and the conflict is visible: private perception versus public consensus.

Reject the "people are sheep" simplification. Human social learning is often rational and necessary. Define the core distinction between treating the group as evidence and treating the group as permission.

End with the first practice: make an independent pass before receiving social information.

### 2. Reality Has a Dress Code

Introduce pluralistic ignorance, preference falsification, spirals of silence, and the gap between public performance and private belief.

Frame false consensus as a measurement failure: people infer private belief from behavior produced under incentives.

Explicitly separate the title's metaphor from clinical psychosis using NIMH's definition.

### 3. The Crowd in Your Pocket

Move into social-media norm distortion, identity, misinformation, accuracy motivation, and online sampling.

Preserve counterevidence: most people can often distinguish true from false material when asked; groups can be wise; social influence can improve collective judgments under some network structures.

Introduce counterfeit independence: contrarian communities can reproduce the same conformity structure they oppose.

### 4. Belonging Before Belief

How identity changes the utility function around belief. Separate identity effects, motivated reasoning, heuristic trust, and ordinary source selection.

Include current disputes over identity-protective cognition rather than presenting one theory as settled.

### 5. The Cost of Saying I Don't Know

Uncertainty as a socially risky signal. Why confident people can dominate group impressions even when calibration is poor.

Develop "certainty markets": environments that reward confidence independently of accuracy.

### 6. Certainty Is a Team Sport

How repeated claims, synchronized language, confidence, and mutual reinforcement can create subjective certainty.

Distinguish repetition effects from proof of broad persuasion.

### 7. When Experts Herd

Professional incentives, publication pressure, reputational cascades, shared models, and correlated error.

Do not imply experts are generally unreliable. The point is that expertise becomes fragile when supposedly independent experts share data, priors, methods, incentives, or status networks.

### 8. Panic Has a Supply Chain

Rumor propagation under uncertainty; emergencies; scarcity; ambiguous threats; institutional information vacuums.

Avoid sensational historical analogies unless supported by primary or high-quality secondary sources.

### 9. Rumor Markets

Treat rumors as products competing for attention, status, identity fit, and explanatory compression.

Explore why some false claims die while others become socially useful.

### 10. Moral Contagion

Outrage, moral-emotional language, punishment, audience effects, and reputational signaling.

Avoid reducing moral conviction to performance; distinguish genuine belief from amplified expression.

### 11. The False Majority

Deep dive into pluralistic ignorance, preference falsification, visible minorities, bot amplification, polls, comment sections, and biased samples.

### 12. Authority Without Orders

How hierarchy shapes what subordinates perceive as sayable even without explicit commands.

Use organizational and experimental evidence carefully. Avoid cartoon retellings of classic obedience research.

### 13. The Contrarian Trap

The minority can herd too. Conspiracy communities, anti-establishment status, and "do your own research" as an identity performance.

Build the principle: truth has no popularity requirement.

### 14. Wolves Hunt in Packs

Collective intelligence, wisdom-of-crowds conditions, decentralized networks, diversity of error, and structured information aggregation.

This is the strongest counterargument chapter against the subtitle's simplistic reading.

### 15. Build a Private Mind

Independent first-pass protocols, private forecasting, note-taking before discussion, delayed exposure to comments, source provenance, and calibrated confidence.

### 16. Friction Rituals

Practical tools that make impulsive conformity harder: accuracy prompts, pre-mortems, red teams, anonymous estimates, senior-speaks-last, double-crux, adversarial collaboration.

Treat intervention evidence as mixed and domain-specific.

### 17. Exit the Audience

Why being observed changes cognition. Performance, posting, audience capture, professional brands, and the ability to think without publishing.

### 18. Choose Your Herds

No one is independent of all social systems. The realistic task is portfolio design: groups with different incentives, sources, error processes, and norms.

### 19. Reality Testing for Groups

An institutional protocol: independent observations, provenance, dissent channels, forecast records, decision logs, postmortems, and protected reversal.

### 20. Think Like a Wolf

Return to Asch. The goal is not permanent dissent but the ability to keep seeing the line before the room tells you what it is.

## Strongest competing explanations

1. **Conformity is usually adaptive social learning.** Other people often know things the individual does not; copying can be rational under uncertainty.
2. **Consensus often reflects genuine shared evidence rather than pressure.** Agreement is not proof of herding.
3. **Polarized or false beliefs may reflect information environments, trust differences, or priors rather than identity-protective motivation.**
4. **Crowds can be wise even when members interact.** Network structure and task difficulty can allow social learning to improve accuracy.
5. **Brief anti-misinformation interventions have mixed effects.** Recognizing manipulation does not automatically improve truth discernment.
6. **Contrarianism can be as socially driven as conformity.** Minority status is not evidence of independence.
7. **Apparent pluralistic ignorance can be overclaimed if researchers infer private belief poorly or sample nonrepresentative groups.**

The manuscript should narrow whenever these explanations fit the evidence better.

## Falsification notes

The thesis weakens if:

- conformity effects vanish or become trivial in ecologically realistic settings;
- measured norm misperceptions mostly reflect accurate reading of population attitudes;
- social-media samples track offline norms closely enough that "funhouse mirror" effects are minor in consequential domains;
- identity-congruent misinformation effects fail to replicate outside narrow experimental tasks;
- independent precommitment and dissent-protection procedures do not improve group calibration or decision quality;
- greater informational diversity reliably harms accuracy more than correlated error does;
- social influence tends to improve, rather than degrade, group judgment across the relevant task classes;
- intervention effects depend so strongly on context that the proposed practical protocols do not generalize.

## Evidence discipline

- Use current systematic reviews where available.
- Preserve sample sizes and experimental contexts; do not generalize a headline-rating task into a theory of civilization without intermediate evidence.
- Separate belief, expression, sharing, and behavior.
- Separate normative influence ("I want to fit in") from informational influence ("others may know better") when the evidence permits.
- Mark classic findings that face replication or interpretation disputes.
- Do not medicalize ideological disagreement.
- Do not infer manipulation when selection, incentives, or ordinary social learning can explain the outcome.
- Do not use "the algorithm" as a unitary actor; specify ranking, recommendation, engagement, sampling, or network effects.
- Give collective-intelligence evidence enough space to change the book's argument.
