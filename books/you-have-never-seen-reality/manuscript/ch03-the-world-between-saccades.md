# Chapter 3 — The World Between Saccades

Your eyes are moving while you read this.

Not drifting poetically.

Jumping.

A saccade is a rapid eye movement that shifts the fovea from one target to another. Human eyes make them constantly during ordinary viewing. They are among the fastest movements the body produces.

If visual experience were a frame-by-frame display of retinal input, the world should lurch every time the eyes do.

It does not.

The letters stay put.

The room stays put.

Your coffee does not streak sideways because your gaze moved from the cup to the screen.

You experience a stable world through a moving sensor.

That stability is one of the quiet miracles of perception.

It is also evidence that seeing is an active construction.

## The camera comparison fails

We reach for the camera metaphor because eyes contain lenses and images form on a light-sensitive surface.

The resemblance is useful until it becomes misleading.

A camera records from one optical position at a time. A human observer does not simply record. The eyes move to sample information. Head and body move. Attention selects. Memory contributes. The visual system integrates across time and uses signals associated with movement itself.

Seeing is closer to interrogation than photography.

The organism asks the world questions with movement.

Look here.

Now there.

Resolve that edge.

Check the face.

Sample the sign.

Return to the road.

The scene you experience is tied to this sensorimotor loop.

That is why a perfectly stationary eye would not simply give you a more stable version of normal vision. Vision evolved around movement.

## The missing smear

During a saccade, the retinal image moves rapidly.

Why do you not see a smear?

Part of the answer is that visual sensitivity is reduced around saccades.

The phrase **saccadic suppression** covers a family of effects, not one simple shutter mechanism. Some suppression begins before movement; some depends on the visual consequences of the movement; different visual pathways and features can be affected differently. The exact neural mechanisms remain an active research topic.

A 2024 human neurophysiology study reported suppression of processing for both luminance and color signals in early visual cortex during saccade execution, contributing to a long-running debate about how broad saccadic suppression is.

The important point is not that the brain “turns vision off.”

It does not.

The important point is that sensory processing is modulated around self-generated movement in ways that help prevent retinal disruption from becoming phenomenal chaos.

The organism does not merely receive the visual effects of action.

It predicts and manages them.

## You also blink into darkness

Blink.

There.

For a fraction of a second, your eyelids blocked incoming light.

Did the world go black?

Usually, no.

You may notice the blink if you attend to it, but the visual environment generally feels continuous. Reviews of blink perception emphasize that blinks drastically attenuate visual input while normally failing to intrude into awareness as disruptive darkness.

Again, the nervous system is not showing you the sensor history.

It is showing you the world as behaviorally continuous across a self-generated interruption.

The distinction between world continuity and sensory continuity matters.

The world may indeed have stayed where it was while your eyes closed.

But your experience of continuous presence cannot be explained by continuous retinal input because the retinal input was interrupted.

The perceptual system preserves a model of stability across missing data.

## Stability is a hypothesis that usually wins

There is a simple prior that works almost embarrassingly well:

the world probably did not teleport during the eye movement.

A coffee mug that occupied the left side of the table 200 milliseconds ago probably remains there after your gaze lands on the window.

A face does not usually exchange identities during a blink.

Walls usually persist.

Words stay on the page.

The environment contains enormous temporal regularity.

A perceptual system can exploit that regularity.

This is where predictive-processing language becomes useful, but we need to keep it disciplined.

“Prediction” does not have to mean a little homunculus guessing the future. It can mean that neural processing incorporates prior structure and expectations so that sensory signals are interpreted in light of what is likely to have generated them.

Current predictive-processing frameworks range from relatively specific predictive-coding models to broader active-inference proposals. Reviews emphasize that the empirical support is mixed and that some findings described as predictive coding can also be explained by feedforward or alternative mechanisms.

Good.

A theory should be allowed to have competitors.

We do not need predictive processing to be universally correct for the central fact to stand:

vision uses context, prior information and action-related signals to maintain useful stability under constantly changing input.

## What is the object doing while you look away?

Now we reach a philosophical trap.

If the brain maintains a stable representation across saccades and blinks, does that mean objects exist only when represented?

No.

That is Berkeley in a hurry.

The perceptual fact is compatible with straightforward realism. The coffee mug remains on the table while your eyes move. Perception is stable because the world is stable.

But notice the direction of explanation.

The stability you experience is not itself proof that you continuously sensed the object.

The object may persist independently.

Your experience of persistence is constructed from intermittent sampling plus an extremely reliable environmental regularity.

This is how indirect realism can be powerful without becoming skepticism.

The organism need not hallucinate the mug into existence.

It needs to maintain a representation of a mug that remains useful while sensory access fluctuates.

## The world is not stored at full resolution

There is another common intuition: perhaps the brain simply builds a detailed internal panorama and updates it with each eye movement.

This is seductive because experience feels panoramic.

Evidence from change blindness and visual memory complicates the picture. People can miss substantial changes to scenes, especially when a visual interruption masks the motion transient that would normally attract attention.

The visual system does not appear to maintain a photographic, uniformly detailed internal copy of every visible feature.

Instead, the experienced richness of the scene can coexist with surprisingly limited explicit retention of unattended detail.

This is one of the strangest findings in perception science.

We feel as though the detail is there because the world itself is available for resampling.

Your environment can function as its own external memory.

If you need to know the exact shape of the lamp, look at it again.

If you need the word at the top of the page, move your eyes.

The world is part of the information loop.

Perception can therefore feel richer than the internally stored representation because the organism is embedded in an accessible environment.

This idea cuts both ways.

It supports realism: the external world matters precisely because the brain can repeatedly sample it.

It also weakens the idea that conscious experience is a literal internal photograph.

The richness is relational.

## Active vision

A stationary camera waits.

An organism acts.

Modern sensory-motor theories emphasize that perception and action are deeply coupled. A 2024 perspective in *Nature Neuroscience* proposed an active predictive-coding architecture in which cortical areas estimate sensory states together with actions and their consequences. Whether that particular architecture wins is less important here than the empirical direction of travel: vision is increasingly difficult to describe as a one-way feed from world to eye to brain.

Eye movements are not noise added to perception.

They are part of perception.

You decide where high-resolution information will arrive next.

The decision can be automatic, task-driven, salience-driven, memory-guided or socially guided.

The eye is a sensor on a motor system.

The motor system is inside the inference loop.

What you see depends partly on what you do.

## This is where virtual reality becomes philosophically useful

Virtual-reality systems exploit the sensorimotor structure of perception.

Turn your head and the scene updates.

Move your hand and the controller moves.

Approach an object and its visual geometry changes.

If the latency, tracking and cues are good enough, the rendered environment can acquire presence: the feeling of being in a place rather than looking at an image.

Presence is philosophically interesting because it demonstrates that perceptual reality is not determined by visual pixels alone.

It depends on lawful coupling between action and sensory consequence.

A world feels stable when it behaves correctly under interrogation.

This suggests a more powerful definition of interface than “pretty picture.”

An interface is a **rule-governed action-perception relationship**.

The icon matters because clicking it does something.

The virtual object matters because moving around it changes the image coherently.

The perceived mug matters because reaching toward it produces predictable tactile consequences.

Reality, for an organism, is deeply tied to counterfactual reliability.

What would happen if I looked again?

What would happen if I moved closer?

What would happen if I touched it?

The answer keeps coming back coherent.

## The realist has a point

This is where a realist can smile.

Of course the world is real, the realist says. That is why all these predictions work.

Exactly.

Any interface theory that cannot explain the stubborn coherence of sensorimotor contingencies is useless.

A fantasy can be vivid.

A world resists.

It constrains action.

You cannot simply decide that a closed door is open.

You cannot choose a different gravitational acceleration by expecting one.

You cannot make the table disappear by blinking.

The environment pushes back.

So the book's argument cannot be that perception invents reality.

The stronger claim is that the form in which reality becomes available to an organism is shaped by what the organism can sense and do.

The resistance is real.

The representation is constructed.

Both are true.

## The important invariants

Now we can sharpen the question from the previous chapter.

What does perception preserve?

Not pixel-by-pixel resemblance.

Not continuous sensor history.

Not complete detail.

Perhaps what matters is invariance.

The mug remains graspable across eye movements.

Its identity survives changing retinal projections.

Its edges move lawfully when you change viewpoint.

Its distance predicts how far your arm must extend.

Its apparent size changes with perspective while physical size remains inferable.

These stable relationships may be more important than whether experience resembles the substrate in some absolute sense.

This is where structural realism begins to look formidable.

Maybe perception is an interface, but the interface preserves real structure.

Maybe spacetime, objects and causality are not photographs of fundamental reality, yet their relations correspond to something objective enough for science.

That possibility is not a compromise designed to make everyone happy.

It may be the actual answer.

## Between the jumps

The deepest error would be to imagine that you somehow see the world during the pauses between saccades and merely endure distortions during the movements.

The movements are part of the seeing.

The pauses are part of the seeing.

The predictions are part of the seeing.

The memory is part of the seeing.

The world is part of the seeing.

Visual experience is not located at one step in the chain.

It is the result of a loop.

This makes the phrase “you have never seen reality” less nihilistic than it sounds.

Of course you have been in contact with reality.

You have navigated it.

Touched it.

Been injured by it.

Loved people inside it.

Built instruments that detect things no naked human sense could reveal.

The claim is narrower and stranger:

contact does not require resemblance.

A blind person's cane can reveal a doorway without becoming a doorway.

A radar return can reveal an aircraft without looking like an aircraft.

A retinal image can guide action in three-dimensional space without being three-dimensional.

A percept can be real, useful and constrained while remaining a transformation.

The world between saccades is not missing.

Your access to it is intermittent.

The stability you experience is an achievement built from that intermittence.

And once again, the mechanism disappears inside the result.

Your eyes jump.

The world stays still.

The interface wins.
