# Chronology

**1963** — Limited Test Ban Treaty prohibits nuclear tests in the atmosphere, outer space, and under water, sharpening the need for reliable national technical verification.

**1960s–1970s** — Vela satellites and later generations carry nuclear-detection instrumentation including optical bhangmeters; the system accumulates a strong record against known atmospheric nuclear tests.

**22 September 1979, 00:53 GMT** — Vela 6911 records the anomalous double-pulse optical event over the broad southern Atlantic/Indian Ocean region.

**22–25 September 1979** — U.S. agencies begin nuclear-event assessment and collection. Allied consultations begin quickly; a September 25 State Department memorandum records plans to inform close allies and requests regarding Australian seismic coverage.

**Late September–October 1979** — Atmospheric/radiological collection, weather reconstruction, geophysical review, and intelligence work continue. Public attribution is withheld.

**10 October 1979** — State Department INR official Ross Cowey records a synopsis of a CIA-sponsored review by Harold Agnew, Richard Garwin, and Stephen Lukasik. The review judged the Vela signals consistent with an atmospheric nuclear explosion while noting unusual self-consistency problems between the two bhangmeters.

**25 October 1979** — White House Situation Room log summarizes collection activity and the difficulty of obtaining decisive radioactive debris.

**26 October 1979** — Gerard C. Smith briefs Congress. According to the declassified record, he says the United States has no evidence assigning responsibility to South Africa and an equal amount assigning responsibility to Israel: none at that stage.

**Circa 1 November 1979** — Nuclear Explosion Panel charter calls for investigation of natural phenomena, technical failure, and any additional data that could verify the event.

**8–9 November 1979** — White House science staff reports that the Ruina panel is leaning toward a non-nuclear interpretation; a meteoroid-related mechanism is considered possible. The same reporting notes interest in an Arecibo ionospheric disturbance while emphasizing that the connection is less well understood than optical nuclear phenomenology.

**9 November 1979** — Separate NORAD false missile-warning event occurs. It is not evidence about Vela but later provides a useful contrast in how redundant warning channels can rapidly falsify a large attack indication.

**19 November 1979** — State Department/OES “Nite Note” records ongoing sheep-thyroid sampling and uncertainty around radiological indicators.

**26 November 1979** — Los Alamos International Technology Office report ITO-79-155 discusses the event, including bhangmeter and Arecibo material; later declassified version contains significant redactions on proliferation/actor issues.

**December 1979** — DCI interagency memorandum NI IIM 79-10028 examines possible actors under an instructed assumption that the event was a nuclear detonation. This analytical structure is important: actor analysis does not independently prove the initial assumption.

**Late 1979–1980** — White House/Ruina scientific review concludes a non-nuclear event is more probable. Other intelligence and technical officials continue to dissent; later declassified records indicate CIA assessed the probability of a nuclear test at greater than 90 percent at one point.

**1 December 1980** — NSC intelligence-coordination memorandum reports abnormal iodine-131 in thyroids of sheep slaughtered near Melbourne in the October 1979 timeframe and explicitly notes that DIA is investigating industrial/pharmaceutical alternatives.

**1981** — Los Alamos publishes *Evaluation of Some Geophysical Events on 22 September 1979* (LA-8672), a key technical source requiring full page-level reconstruction in the expansion pass.

**Late 1980s onward** — Officials, scientists, journalists, and historians continue to dispute the event and actor attribution. Some retrospective accounts favor a joint Israeli–South African test; public evidence remains incomplete.

**1990s** — South Africa discloses and dismantles its nuclear-weapons arsenal in connection with its accession to the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty as a non-nuclear-weapon state. This establishes historical capability but does not independently resolve the 1979 event.

**2000s–2020s** — FOIA, mandatory declassification review, archival research, and National Security Archive releases expose additional internal disagreement and differently redacted versions of key documents.

**2016** — National Security Archive publishes a major Vela document collection emphasizing the CIA-sponsored panel, hydroacoustic controversy, policy debate, and unresolved actor questions.

**2019** — National Security Archive's fortieth-anniversary collection adds Carter Library material, including reporting of a CIA “90% plus” nuclear-test assessment and additional records on White House handling.

**2026 drafting posture** — No new public primary evidence checked in this pass conclusively closes the event or establishes a specific actor. The manuscript therefore preserves the physical-event question and actor-attribution question as separately unresolved.
