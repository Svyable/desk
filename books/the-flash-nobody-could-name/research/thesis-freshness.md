# Thesis freshness

Selected thesis: **the 1979 Vela incident is best treated as a sensor-forensics and institutional-epistemology problem, not as a solved secret-test story.** The book asks how a high-quality nuclear-detection system produced an observation that remained unresolved once optical phenomenology, hydroacoustics, radionuclide searches, ionospheric data, actor intelligence, classification, and policy thresholds collided.

## Reader promise

By the end, the reader should understand why “Vela detected a nuclear test,” “Vela recorded a nuclear-like optical signal,” “a nuclear test probably occurred,” and “Israel and South Africa jointly conducted the test” are four different claims with four different evidentiary burdens.

## Strongest competing explanation

A rare non-nuclear event local to the spacecraft, plausibly involving a meteoroid impact and reflected/scattered sunlight, produced the anomalous double-pulse record; apparent corroborating evidence was non-unique, weakly localized, delayed, or discovered retrospectively after the nuclear hypothesis was already salient.

## Defeat condition

The book's central synthesis should be narrowed or abandoned if a complete reconstruction shows that one hypothesis dominates the optical record and all independent channels by a large margin, leaving no meaningful unresolved classification problem. Specific actor-attribution claims remain subject to a separate evidentiary gate even if the physical event is conclusively classified as nuclear.
