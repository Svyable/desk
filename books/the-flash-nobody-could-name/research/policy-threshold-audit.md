# Policy-threshold and negative-evidence audit

This note is a bounded research pass for *At 00:53*. It does not resolve Event 747. It tightens a more interesting claim in Chapter 1: institutions did not merely disagree about the same evidence; they had to decide what level of uncertainty was sufficient for different actions.

## Primary-record findings

### The first scientific judgment was narrower than attribution

Ross Cowey's 10 October 1979 memorandum records the CIA-sponsored Agnew/Garwin/Lukasik panel's conclusion that the Vela signals were "consistent with detection of a nuclear explosion in the atmosphere." The same synopsis records the troublesome channel mismatch, unusual nighttime timing, low inferred yield, and the panel's request for additional evidence including debris, air sampling, and activity in the area.

Source: National Security Archive, Ross E. Cowey memorandum, 10 October 1979: https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/document/22322-10-memorandum-files-ross-e-cowey

**Use:** Keep this as an event-classification judgment. It does not establish an actor.

### Policy officials were explicitly thinking in thresholds, not certainty

A State Department options paper dated 10 October is unusually revealing. Lewis Macfarlane considered what Washington should do under different evidentiary states and noted that even a 15–30 percent possibility of technical malfunction would materially affect the policy problem. That is direct documentary support for the book's idea that the cost of error changes the decision threshold.

Source: National Security Archive, Lewis R. Macfarlane, "Next Steps-Possible Scenarios," 10 October 1979: https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/document/22321-09-paper-prepared-lewis-r-macfarlane-deputy

**Use:** This is stronger than a generalized claim about diplomats having different "loss functions." The archive contains an actual policy official pricing residual malfunction probability.

### Early attribution evidence was explicitly absent

On 26 October, arms-control official Gerard C. Smith told the House Foreign Affairs Committee that the United States had no evidence that South Africa was responsible and an equal amount that Israel was responsible: none. This is a useful contemporaneous brake on later narratives that let a nuclear classification automatically inherit an actor.

Source: National Security Archive, Gerard C. Smith memorandum, 26 October 1979: https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/document/22327-15-gerard-c-smith-secretary-congressional

**Use:** Put this close to the first actor-attribution discussion. Later intelligence may change the ledger; the manuscript should show when it does rather than back-projecting later suspicions into September/October 1979.

### Negative corroboration existed and should not disappear

A 22 October INR memorandum records a British report that acoustic evidence from Rarotonga had produced negative results. This does not settle the event—the station geometry, sensitivity, propagation conditions, source assumptions, and meaning of "negative" all need reconstruction—but it is disconfirming evidence that belongs in the same ledger as later hydroacoustic claims.

Source: National Security Archive, William G. Bowdler memorandum for the record, 22 October 1979: https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/document/22324-12-memorandum-record-william-g

**Publication hold:** Do not write "hydroacoustics corroborated the test" until the manuscript reconciles the negative Rarotonga report with later positive hydroacoustic interpretations. Identify whether they concern different stations, frequency bands, processing, source locations, or subsequently recovered data.

### The Ruina process was designed to test both a natural event and technical failure

The panel charter called for investigation of both a natural phenomenon and a false alarm produced by technical failure, plus review of any other data that could verify the signal. By November, White House staff reported the panel leaning skeptical because the trace differed from previous nuclear signals and lacked corroboration, while still calling the Arecibo disturbance the best lead then available.

Sources:
- National Security Archive, "Nuclear Explosion Panel: Charter," circa 1 November 1979: https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/document/22326-14-nuclear-explosion-panel-charter-n-d-circa-1
- National Security Archive, White House Science and Technology evening report, 9 November 1979: https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/document/19604-national-security-archive-doc-3-white-house

**Use:** Avoid narrating Ruina as simply "the skeptical panel." Its assigned job was adversarial model comparison.

### The institutional disagreement itself changed policy posture

By 9 January 1980 a mini-SCC discussion recorded the consensus as, in effect, not knowing what happened and proceeding in policy terms accordingly. On 23 January, Jerry Oplinger recorded Intelligence Community dissatisfaction with the panel's treatment of the Vela and Arecibo evidence and concern that the report could appear politically motivated if controversial issues were not freshly aired. Frank Press separately recommended leaving open the possibility that future corroborating evidence could emerge.

Sources:
- National Security Archive, Henry Owen/Jerry Oplinger memorandum, 9 January 1980: https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/document/19605-national-security-archive-doc-4-henry-owen-jerry
- National Security Archive, Jerry Oplinger memorandum, 23 January 1980: https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/document/22345-33-jerry-oplinger-henry-owen-south-atlantic
- National Security Archive, Frank Press memorandum, 17 January 1980: https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/document/22344-32-frank-press-henry-owen-and-gerard-smith

## Strongest counterargument to the book's institutional thesis

"Different loss functions" is plausible but can become an all-purpose explanation for disagreement. The record may instead show ordinary technical disagreement: experts assigning different likelihoods to the same waveform, hardware condition, and corroborating observations because the physical models were genuinely underdetermined. Political stakes do not prove political distortion, and institutional role does not prove a predictable bias.

The manuscript should therefore distinguish three propositions:

1. officials faced different consequences for false positive and false negative judgments;
2. those consequences affected policy thresholds or requests for further evidence;
3. those consequences altered scientific judgments about what physically happened.

The records above directly support (1) and, especially in Macfarlane's options paper, (2). They do **not** by themselves establish (3). Any claim that politics changed the technical conclusion needs evidence of that causal step.

## Narrative opportunity

Chapter 1 currently explains the institutional point abstractly. The Macfarlane memorandum can make it concrete. An official in October 1979 was already asking what policy should follow if the chance of malfunction were not zero but merely 15–30 percent. That is a better hinge than a generic paragraph about laboratories, diplomats, and intelligence services having different incentives because it puts a number—however provisional—on the uncomfortable part of the decision.

The sequence can then tighten:

- a detector produced a nuclear-like signal;
- the first panel called it consistent with a nuclear explosion but asked for corroboration;
- negative acoustic evidence also entered the file;
- policy planners explicitly cared about residual malfunction probability;
- early public/congressional attribution evidence for Israel or South Africa was still zero;
- later evidence and later suspicions therefore have to earn their way into the chronology rather than arriving from hindsight.

## Required reconciliation for later chapters

Before publication, build a dated corroboration ledger with columns for observation time, collection mode (routine vs event-triggered), analyst awareness of Event 747, source/station geometry, raw-data availability, original interpretation, later reinterpretation, and independence from the Vela alert. Include negative observations, not only positive candidates.

That ledger should make it possible for the strongest nuclear and non-nuclear models to lose on the same terms.
