# What Secrecy Does to Error Bars

Secrecy does not merely hide conclusions. It changes the ecology in which conclusions are tested.

The strongest argument for classification in a case like Vela is straightforward. A nuclear-detection network is valuable partly because a potential violator does not know its exact sensitivity, coverage, calibration, and failure modes. Publish every threshold and blind spot and the monitoring system becomes easier to evade. Protecting sources and methods is not bureaucratic superstition. It can be part of the sensor itself.

The cost appears later.

An open scientific anomaly can be attacked by people who do not share the institution's assumptions. Data can be reprocessed. Alternative models can be published. Replications can be attempted. A classified anomaly can receive rigorous internal review, but the number of independent critics is structurally smaller. People who can see the data may also share training, mission, organizational culture, or the same hidden intelligence about likely actors.

This does not make classified science bad science. It makes independence harder to measure.

The Vela record illustrates another problem: declassification is not the inverse of classification. It does not restore the original information environment. Records emerge at different times, under different Freedom of Information Act requests, with different redactions. A 2004 release may contain a passage missing from a later release. A summary may be declassified while the technical annex remains withheld. One agency can release a fact another still treats as sensitive.

Historians then reconstruct the event from a record shaped by decades of administrative decisions unrelated to the original uncertainty.

That produces what might be called asymmetric survivorship. A dramatic sentence in a White House memo survives and circulates widely. A calibration table does not. A senior official's opinion is quotable. The waveform processing notes that informed it may remain buried. The public debate therefore becomes more rhetorical as the most technical evidence becomes least accessible.

Secrecy also distorts reputational stakes. An analyst who dissents internally may know the disagreement will never be publicly examined. A panel may write for senior decision-makers rather than for an adversarial scientific audience. Years later, declassification can expose a confident judgment without the informal uncertainty that surrounded it. The document looks cleaner than the room was.

The Vela case has another layer: the event itself concerned alleged secrecy by other states. The United States was using secret instruments to determine whether another country had conducted a secret test, while trying not to reveal what the instruments could see. Epistemology and counterintelligence were fused.

This creates a recurring problem in national-security science. The more consequential the evidence, the harder it can be to expose to the process most likely to correct it. UAP investigations, weapons intelligence, cyber attribution, biological threat analysis, and signals intelligence all encounter versions of this. Outsiders complain that key evidence is hidden. Insiders reply that revealing it would destroy the collection method. Both can be right.

The solution is not radical transparency in every case. Some data should remain protected. The solution is to design institutions that can generate genuine adversarial review inside classification boundaries and later release enough methodology that historical and scientific claims can be audited without disclosing live capabilities.

Vela shows what happens when that second step remains incomplete for decades.

The public debate then starts treating classification itself as evidence. Believers say the missing files must contain proof. Skeptics say the absence of public proof means there was none. Both positions are logically weak. A classified document can contain decisive evidence, ordinary duplication, or another ambiguous memo.

The correct stance toward missing evidence is disciplined ignorance.

That is unsatisfying because hidden archives invite imagination. But the alternative is worse: letting redaction marks function as blank checks for whichever story the reader already prefers.

Error bars are usually drawn around measurements. In intelligence, some of the error belongs around the archive.
