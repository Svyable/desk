# Own the Range

A deep shelf turns flexibility into a practical question.

The object is not heavy. The room is not dangerous. Nothing about the task looks athletic. You squat, kneel, or fold far enough to reach the back, and suddenly a joint angle that never matters at a desk becomes the whole problem. If the range is available, the task remains ordinary. If it is not, the body invents another route.

This is what mobility is for.

The word has become so broad in fitness culture that it can mean stretching, joint circles, soft-tissue work, warm-ups, yoga, gymnastics, deep squats, corrective exercise, or nearly any slow movement performed with an expression of concentration. That breadth makes the term attractive and occasionally useless.

A more practical definition is enough for this book: mobility is usable movement range.

That definition contains two ideas that are often separated.

First, a position has to be reachable.

Second, the body has to be able to do something there.

A passive stretch can show that a joint or group of joints permits a certain position under external assistance. An active movement asks the person to reach or control part of that range using their own muscles. A loaded movement asks more: produce or resist force there.

The useful body cares about all three, but the third is where range becomes part of work.

Consider ankle dorsiflexion, the ability of the shin to move forward over the foot. Adequate ankle range can make deep squatting, stair descent, kneeling, and some running and jumping tasks easier to organize. A person may increase passive ankle range with stretching. That can be valuable. But if they cannot control the range when the knee moves forward under bodyweight, the new position has not yet become fully useful.

Strength can help convert access into ownership.

This is one reason the old war between “strength people” and “flexibility people” has become increasingly difficult to defend. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses have found that resistance training performed through meaningful ranges can improve joint range of motion in healthy adults. Some analyses have found gains comparable to stretch training, although study quality, populations, protocols, and the joints measured vary substantially.

The implication is not that stretching has become obsolete.

Stretching works for increasing range of motion. Reviews covering dozens of studies have consistently found that repeated stretch training can produce lasting increases. The more interesting implication is that flexibility and strength are not opposite adaptations. A muscle can become stronger while the movement repertoire expands.

This should change how we imagine mobility training.

If a person wants to become more comfortable in a deep split squat, one route is to stretch the positions that limit it. Another is to perform a supported split squat through the largest comfortable range, gradually reduce assistance, and add load as control improves. The strength exercise becomes mobility practice because it repeatedly asks the tissues to tolerate and produce force in the expanded position.

The body learns that the range is not merely a place it can visit. It is a place where work happens.

This matters because flexibility without strength can create false confidence.

Someone may be able to pull a joint into a large passive range and assume that the same range will be stable under speed, load, or surprise. The assumption does not always hold. A dancer, gymnast, martial artist, or climber who uses extreme range athletically does not simply possess flexibility. They have spent years developing strength, coordination, and tolerance in those positions.

The useful adult rarely needs extreme range.

They need enough.

Enough ankle movement to navigate stairs and uneven ground. Enough hip motion to squat, hinge, step, kneel, rotate, and get onto the floor. Enough shoulder motion to reach overhead, behind, and across the body for the tasks their life actually contains. Enough thoracic and trunk movement to turn and reach without requiring every rotation to come from one joint.

The exact amount varies.

This is why universal mobility screens are both useful and dangerous. A screen can reveal that a particular position is difficult, painful, asymmetric, or unavailable. That information can guide training or clinical assessment. Trouble begins when the screen is treated as proof that every body must satisfy one geometric ideal.

Human anatomy varies.

Hip socket orientation varies. Femur shape varies. Limb lengths vary. Previous injuries change movement. Sports create adaptations. Age changes tissues. Some people naturally possess more joint laxity than others. A squat stance that feels roomy for one person can feel mechanically blocked for another even when neither is injured.

The useful question is not “do you have perfect mobility?”

It is “what ranges does your life ask you to control?”

This reframes stiffness.

Stiffness is usually described as an enemy, but biological stiffness is also useful. Tendons store elastic energy partly because they resist deformation. The trunk becomes stiffer temporarily to transmit force. A sprinter needs the leg to behave like a spring during very brief ground contacts. A joint that had no resistance at all would be useless.

The problem is not stiffness in the abstract. It is stiffness that prevents a desired movement, creates compensations that become costly, or reflects a condition needing attention.

This distinction matters because many people spend years chasing sensations of looseness rather than capacities.

Stretching can feel good. Joint circles can reduce the sense of being rusty after sitting. A warm-up can make motion feel easier. Those are legitimate reasons to do them. But a transient feeling of increased range is not always the same as a lasting adaptation, and lasting passive range is not always the same as usable range.

If the goal is function, eventually the function should appear.

Want to squat lower? Practice squatting lower with support and appropriate loading.

Want to reach overhead comfortably? Train tolerated overhead positions, not only stretches adjacent to them.

Want to kneel and stand? Practice transitions to and from kneeling.

Want to take longer hiking steps over rocks? Build the hip, ankle, and balance capacity that the actual step requires.

This sounds almost tautological because specificity keeps dragging fitness back toward common sense.

But mobility culture can drift away from tasks because range is easy to fetishize. A photograph of a deep stretch looks like proof. A dramatic joint angle seems more advanced than a modest one. The result can be the same status problem that affects strength: a measurement becomes the definition of capability.

More is not universally better.

Hypermobility makes this clear. Some people have unusually large joint ranges due to generalized laxity or specific conditions. Their challenge may not be acquiring more range but controlling what already exists. For such people, strength, proprioception, pacing, and medical guidance can matter more than aggressive stretching. A universal command to “get more mobile” can be exactly backward.

The useful body seeks options, not maximum angles.

Options include being able to make a movement smaller when necessary.

This point is often overlooked. Mobility is valuable partly because it gives the body alternative shapes. If one route is blocked by furniture, terrain, or an object, another may work. But usefulness also means not needing the deepest possible range for every task. A person who can comfortably squat low can still choose a hip hinge to lift a box because the shape of the box favors it. A person with excellent shoulder range can still use a step stool rather than reaching awkwardly overhead under load.

Capacity expands choices. Judgment chooses among them.

Warm-ups offer another place to make this distinction practical.

A warm-up should prepare the body for the work that follows. That may include raising temperature, rehearsing the movement, increasing range temporarily, practicing technique, and progressively introducing load or speed. It does not need to become a separate forty-minute discipline unless the person has a particular reason.

For many training sessions, the most specific warm-up is a lighter version of the task.

Before a heavy squat, squat with less load. Before throwing, throw gently. Before sprinting, run progressively faster. Before climbing, climb easy routes and expose fingers gradually. General movement can help, but preparation should eventually resemble the demand.

This is another way mobility becomes embedded rather than appended.

If the training program routinely uses comfortable full ranges, the person receives repeated exposure without needing to reserve flexibility for a separate ceremony. A split squat can train legs and hips through range. A pulldown or overhead press can train shoulder movement through a chosen arc. A calf raise can strengthen the ankle at different muscle lengths. A controlled Romanian deadlift can load the posterior chain in a lengthened position.

Resistance becomes a teacher at the edge.

That edge deserves respect.

Loaded stretching and long-muscle-length resistance training have attracted research interest because training muscles at longer lengths can influence hypertrophy, strength, and range. But the fashionable interpretation—load everything as deeply as possible—is too simple. Deep ranges increase mechanical demands, and joint tolerance varies. A person should not force depth through pain because a study found average benefits in a controlled population.

The position has to be earned at the dose being used.

This is the mobility version of power training's rule about speed. Control first enough to practice. Then progressively add demand.

There is no contradiction in reducing range temporarily when load rises.

A person may squat deeply with bodyweight but use a slightly shallower range under a heavier load while building strength. Another may use a box or support to make a deep position accessible without fear. Over time, the loaded range can expand.

Training is negotiation, not purity.

Mobility also changes under fatigue.

A range that feels easy at the beginning of a hike may become less controlled after hours. A tired person may shorten steps or avoid deeper positions. Strength loss within the session can make the same joint angle feel less stable. This is another reason reserve matters: the range you “have” in a fresh assessment may not be the range you can use safely after a long day.

Athletes train this reality through repetition. A wrestler must access low positions late in a match. A climber must lift a foot high when the forearms are already tired. A goalkeeper reaches and rotates after repeated accelerations. A weightlifter must preserve enough mobility under a heavy load for the competition positions to remain available.

General fitness can be less demanding, but the principle survives. Useful range should occasionally appear inside real tasks, not only in an isolated pre-workout test.

The floor is one excellent place to practice.

Sitting in different positions changes hip demands. Half kneeling exposes the hips and ankles differently. Squatting toward the floor integrates ankles, knees, hips, trunk, and balance. Reaching while seated or kneeling adds rotation. Moving between these shapes turns flexibility into transition skill.

None of the positions needs to be held as a moral ideal.

The floor is simply a cheap laboratory with many joint angles.

Mobility work can also be a form of maintenance because it asks a question before life asks it more abruptly. Does reaching overhead feel different on one side? Has ankle range changed after an injury? Is kneeling uncomfortable? Does a particular position produce pain rather than stretch? These observations can guide modification or evaluation before the issue becomes attached to a heavier task.

Again, observation is not diagnosis.

Pain is complex, and range restrictions can come from many causes. Persistent pain, traumatic loss of motion, neurological symptoms, swelling, locking, instability, or major changes after injury can require clinical assessment. No amount of generalized mobility advice can identify what is happening in an individual joint.

The useful-body philosophy is especially suspicious of pretending every problem is trainable by will.

Some are medical problems.

Some are equipment problems.

Some are simply anatomy.

Some are trainable.

Good judgment lies partly in learning the difference.

For the trainable ones, mobility does not need mystery. Choose a range that matters. Enter it under conditions you can control. Repeat it enough that the position becomes familiar. Add muscular work so the range is not passive territory only. Progress depth, load, duration, or complexity gradually. Keep what transfers to the life or sport you want.

You do not need to become more flexible everywhere.

You need to keep enough doors open that a low shelf, high reach, long step, floor position, awkward seat, or sudden need to turn does not reveal an entire missing section of the map.

Range of motion is geography.

Strength is one way of claiming the land.
