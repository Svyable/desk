# The Things We Carry

A grocery bag has terrible ergonomics.

The load is concentrated below the hand. The handle is often thin. The contents shift. Milk and canned food migrate toward the bottom, bread occupies volume without adding useful counterweight, and some unseen packer has placed the eggs precisely where a swinging bottle can find them. If the trip from car to kitchen is long enough, the bag begins as a leg problem, becomes a hand problem, and eventually turns into a negotiation about whether pride is worth crushed produce.

This is one of the oldest physical problems in human life: move a load while moving yourself.

Carrying is so ordinary that exercise programs often treat it as a novelty. Pick up dumbbells and walk. Hold a kettlebell on one side and walk. Carry a sandbag. These movements are filed under “functional training,” as if locomoting under load were a clever modern variation on strength rather than a task older than gyms, roads, and probably most things we would recognize as furniture.

For soldiers, porters, parents, farmers, builders, travelers, nurses, warehouse workers, hikers, and anyone whose shopping cart stops some distance from home, carrying is not metaphorical. The object must travel and the body becomes part of the transport system.

That changes everything.

A deadlift asks whether you can produce enough force to raise an external load. A carry asks whether you can continue organizing posture, grip, breathing, and locomotion after the load is already off the ground. The demand repeats with every step. The floor moves beneath you. One leg supports while the other swings. The load may pull downward, forward, backward, or to one side. Hands fatigue. Trunk muscles prevent unwanted motion. Feet negotiate terrain. Breathing competes with bracing when the load becomes large.

Carrying is strength that has somewhere to be.

Military science has studied this problem for obvious reasons. Soldiers have always had to move equipment, but the amount and arrangement of that equipment changes performance in measurable ways. Reviews of load carriage have found what experience already suggests: energy cost rises as load, speed, grade, and terrain demands rise, and where the load sits relative to the body matters. A pack that keeps mass close to the body's center is generally more economical than weight held far away.

This is why backpack design is not merely about comfort. Load placement changes mechanics.

The same principle can be discovered during a house move. A forty-pound box held against the torso feels very different from the same box held several inches forward because the hands cannot get around it. The mass is unchanged. The torque around the trunk and hips is not. A child held close is different from a child leaning away to point at something. A suitcase rolling smoothly is transport; the same suitcase carried up stairs becomes loaded locomotion.

Awkwardness is a multiplier.

This is one reason absolute weight can be a poor predictor of whether a real object will feel manageable. Handles, dimensions, center of mass, movement inside the object, available grip, path, and whether the load obstructs vision all change the problem.

Gyms usually remove these variables to make strength easier to train. Carrying is where we deliberately put some of them back.

The simplest loaded carry—weight in each hand, walk forward—creates a useful hybrid demand. The legs repeat low-level force. The hands maintain grip. The shoulders support the load. The trunk resists excessive side bending. The cardiovascular cost rises because the moving body is heavier. Nothing about the exercise is magical, but it integrates several capacities without requiring high skill.

The integration is why carries can reveal weak links quickly.

Someone with strong legs may stop because the hands open. Someone with strong hands may lose posture. Someone with excellent aerobic fitness may be surprised by how quickly a heavy load drives breathing. Someone who can lift a large weight once may find that carrying a much smaller weight for sixty seconds creates a different kind of fatigue.

The body cannot outsource any of these failures. The load only cares whether the system continues.

This makes carrying an especially good example of the reserve principle. Most daily loads should not require maximal grip, maximal trunk stiffness, or maximal leg strength. Training can make them cheaper by raising multiple ceilings at once.

But carrying also teaches an important limit: more load is not always more useful.

Soldier load-carriage research exists partly because heavy loads can reduce mobility and increase physiological and musculoskeletal strain. The same basic tradeoff applies outside military settings. There is a point at which a training carry stops being a broad strength-endurance exercise and becomes an awkward test of how long tissues can tolerate compression, grip strain, or altered gait.

The useful-body goal is not to become good at carrying the heaviest possible objects for no reason. It is to make likely loads affordable and to have some reserve for unlikely ones.

That distinction should influence how carries are progressed.

Distance can rise. Load can rise. The object can become less symmetrical. Terrain can change. A person can carry in one hand, at the shoulder, in front, or in a backpack. Each variation changes the demand. Because carrying has so many variables, progression works best when not all of them become harder at once.

A heavier load on flat ground is one problem. The same heavier load on stairs with one hand occupied is another. Training can eventually include complexity, but adaptation is easier to read when one variable moves at a time.

This is exactly the logic behind occupational lifting guidance. The revised NIOSH lifting equation, used to assess risk in repetitive workplace lifting, does not treat a load's mass as the whole problem. It considers factors such as horizontal distance, vertical location, travel distance, asymmetry, frequency, and coupling—the quality of the hand-to-object interface. Workplaces care about these details because “fifty pounds” is not a complete description of a lifting task.

Neither is it a complete description of a carry.

Coupling is a wonderfully unglamorous word for something your hands understand immediately. A sturdy suitcase handle creates better coupling than a slippery cardboard edge. A thick rope is different from a thin plastic strap. Gloves can improve or worsen the connection depending on the material. Sweat changes friction. The same muscles may be strong enough, yet poor contact makes the task insecure.

This is why useful grip training should not be reduced to squeezing as hard as possible.

Grip has forms. Crushing grip closes the hand around an object. Support grip maintains a hold over time. Pinch grip relies more on the fingers and thumb against one another. Open-hand gripping appears when an object is too thick to close around fully. Carries naturally train support grip, often in positions where the rest of the body is moving.

The hand is not merely a clamp. It is also a sensor.

When a load begins to slip, small changes in pressure and skin deformation help trigger grip adjustments. Skilled carrying is partly automatic. You tighten just enough when the bag swings. You change finger position before the handle becomes intolerable. You shift a box against the body without setting it down.

This coordination is another reason carrying real objects occasionally adds something that perfectly shaped implements cannot.

A dumbbell teaches the capacity cleanly. A sandbag, suitcase, water jug, or awkward household object asks the capacity to cooperate with a more complicated interface. Both are useful. The first is easier to progress. The second is closer to the uncertainty of life.

The mistake would be to make every carry awkward in the name of realism.

Realism is not automatically a training virtue. If you want stronger support grip and trunk endurance, a stable handle may be exactly what allows enough repeatable work. If you want practice managing irregular objects, then irregularity becomes the point. Training should know which problem it is posing.

Backpacks deserve their own discussion because they solve the hand problem by transferring load to the torso.

A well-fitted pack allows hands to remain free and can keep the mass close to the body. This is why humans have invented countless carrying technologies—baskets, yokes, shoulder poles, tumplines, panniers, slings, carts, packs, suitcases with wheels. The history of carrying is largely the history of trying to move mass without wasting as much human effort.

Technology changes fitness demands, but it also reveals them.

Rolling luggage is superior to hand-carrying luggage across a flat airport floor. Choosing wheels is not cheating. The useful body is not obligated to reject mechanical advantage. Usefulness includes knowing when a tool should do the work.

Then the escalator breaks.

The wheels become dead weight on the stairs and the luggage returns the job to you.

This is why training for usefulness is less about simulating primitive hardship than maintaining options when convenience disappears. Carrying capacity is an option.

Parents understand this intuitively because children create progressive overload without consulting a training plan.

A newborn is light enough to hold for long periods, though the duration and sleep deprivation make the task more complicated. Months pass. The child becomes heavier but still expects to be carried. The load moves, arches, reaches, sleeps, and occasionally becomes completely limp at the worst possible moment. The parent adapts partly through repetition, often developing specific endurance in the arms, shoulders, and trunk.

There is nothing ideal about this progression. Parents get aches and injuries too. But it illustrates how biological adaptation can be embedded in life when demand increases gradually.

Modern training tries to make that gradual increase deliberate.

The same logic applies to hiking with a pack. Someone who can walk comfortably without load may assume that adding twenty or thirty pounds changes only the strength requirement. In reality, it changes energy cost, foot loading, heat, balance, posture, and the local work of tissues over thousands of steps. Research on load-carriage training has generally found that specificity matters: if the goal is to improve loaded marching performance, some training under load is useful alongside general strength and aerobic conditioning.

Again, the heart can be ready before the feet and shoulders are.

A useful program therefore treats carrying as exposure, not punishment.

Start with a load that allows normal walking mechanics and confident handling. Carry it far enough to create a training effect without turning posture into a slow collapse. Add load or distance gradually. Use symmetrical carries for straightforward strength-endurance work and asymmetrical carries when resisting lateral or rotational forces is part of the goal. Use backpacks when backpacking is the goal. Practice stairs if stairs are relevant.

Then stop before grip failure turns the final meters into a dropping contest.

There is a macho tradition around carries because the exercises look like work. Farmers' walks, yoke carries, stones, sandbags, and loaded drags have strongman roots, and strongman is a sport built partly around making transport brutally difficult. The sport is excellent at revealing what extreme carrying can look like. General training should borrow the movement family without borrowing the requirement for extremity.

This is true of nearly every sport in this book. Elite practice can show us a capacity in high resolution. It does not automatically provide the right dose for everyone else.

Carrying also gives us one of the clearest examples of why bodyweight-relative strength matters in daily life but does not settle everything.

Larger people often possess more absolute strength because muscle cross-sectional area scales with size, but they also have more body mass to move. Smaller people may be relatively strong while still facing an external object whose absolute mass does not scale down for them. A forty-pound suitcase is the same suitcase whether its owner weighs 120 pounds or 220.

This is why absolute and relative strength both have uses. Relative strength helps with moving your own body. Absolute strength matters when the world hands everyone the same object.

Carrying combines them.

You move your body plus the load.

That combination is one reason loaded locomotion can become a powerful conditioning tool. As the load rises, the metabolic cost of walking increases without requiring running speed. Hills amplify the effect further. For some trainees, this can be a useful way to train work capacity. For others—especially those with joint, foot, or back issues—the added load may be a poor bargain. The fact that an exercise is “functional” does not exempt it from individual constraints.

There is no universal useful carry weight.

The relevant test is the life around the person.

Can you carry the objects you routinely need to carry without the task consuming your full capacity? Can you carry with one hand while keeping the other available? Can you manage a load on stairs? Can you hold something close when its shape prevents a perfect grip? Can you carry for several minutes without posture degrading into a problem? Can you put the object down under control?

The last question matters more than it sounds.

People train lifts and forget deposits. Life requires both. A box does not teleport away at the end of the carry. It must be placed on the ground, shelf, counter, rack, or vehicle. Fatigue makes the final lowering more difficult precisely when attention tends to relax because the destination has been reached.

Useful carrying includes the whole trip.

This can be trained with almost comic simplicity. Pick up a manageable object. Walk. Change direction. Stop. Put it down well. Rest. Repeat.

Somewhere inside that plain sequence are grip strength, leg strength, aerobic cost, posture, footwork, perception, pacing, and judgment.

The grocery bag was a fitness test after all.

It was simply too ordinary to get a scoreboard.
