# Research Brief — The Useful Body

## Purpose

This book argues for athleticism as broad, transferable physical capability rather than specialization around a single metric, physique, exercise modality, or sport. The research trail therefore has two jobs: support concrete physiological and training claims, and prevent the word “useful” from becoming a license for confident but untestable advice.

The manuscript is narrative nonfiction, not a clinical guideline. It does not diagnose disease, prescribe rehabilitation, or promise injury prevention. Where the evidence is population-specific, short-term, heterogeneous, observational, or tied to a particular sport, the chapter should say so or avoid generalizing beyond it.

## Evidence hierarchy

Prefer, in order:

1. Systematic reviews, meta-analyses, consensus statements, and major public-health guidelines.
2. Randomized controlled trials when a specific intervention is central to a claim.
3. Prospective cohort studies for long-term associations that cannot reasonably be randomized.
4. Biomechanical and physiological studies for mechanism and task demands.
5. Narrative reviews for orientation when they clearly distinguish established evidence from hypothesis.

Avoid treating elite-athlete findings as universal. Avoid treating a biomarker, laboratory test, or association with mortality as a direct measure of “usefulness.”

## Chapter 1 — The Fitness Test You Did Not Know You Were Taking

Sources:

- U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, *Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans, 2nd edition*. The guidelines distinguish aerobic and muscle-strengthening activity and emphasize that health benefit is not reducible to one exercise modality. https://health.gov/our-work/nutrition-physical-activity/physical-activity-guidelines/current-guidelines
- Lang J.J. et al. “Cardiorespiratory fitness is a strong and consistent predictor of morbidity and mortality among adults: an overview of meta-analyses representing over 20.9 million observations from 199 unique cohort studies.” *British Journal of Sports Medicine* (2024). PMID 38599681. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38599681/
- Currier B.S. et al. “Resistance training prescription for muscle strength and hypertrophy in healthy adults: a systematic review and Bayesian network meta-analysis.” *British Journal of Sports Medicine* (2023). PMID 37414459. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37414459/

### Falsification note

No single test captures general physical usefulness. Cardiorespiratory fitness, strength, power, mobility, balance, skill, and task familiarity are separable constructs. Strong associations between a fitness measure and health outcomes do not make that measure a complete proxy for real-world capability.

## Chapter 2 — Strength Is a Relationship With Gravity

Sources:

- Currier B.S. et al. (2023), systematic review and network meta-analysis of resistance-training prescriptions in healthy adults. All analyzed resistance-training prescriptions improved strength relative to non-exercise controls, with heavier-load prescriptions ranking highly for maximal strength. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37414459/
- Lopez P. et al. “Resistance Training Load Effects on Muscle Hypertrophy and Strength Gain: Systematic Review and Network Meta-analysis.” *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise* (2021). PMID 33433148. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33433148/
- Borde R. et al. “Dose-Response Relationships of Resistance Training in Healthy Old Adults: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis.” *Sports Medicine* (2015). PMID 26420238. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26420238/

### Falsification note

Maximal gym strength is task-specific and does not automatically transfer to awkward objects, unstable surfaces, unfamiliar grips, or skilled tasks. More strength also has diminishing practical returns once common tasks sit comfortably below a person’s available capacity. The chapter treats strength as reserve, not as a universal ranking of bodies.

## Chapter 3 — Power Before You Need It

Sources:

- Byrne C. et al. “Ageing, Muscle Power and Physical Function: A Systematic Review and Implications for Pragmatic Training Interventions.” *Sports Medicine* (2016). PMID 26893098. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26893098/
- Straight C.R. et al. “High-velocity resistance training as a tool to improve functional performance and muscle power in older adults.” Review (2022). PMID 34656749. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34656749/
- Swinton P.A. et al. “Dose-Response Modelling of Resistance Exercise Across Outcome Domains in Strength and Conditioning: A Meta-analysis.” *Sports Medicine* (2024). PMID 38652410. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38652410/

### Falsification note

Power is not interchangeable with strength, and evidence about power training varies by population, protocol, test, and outcome. Fast movement increases coordination and loading demands; it should not be introduced as though every person can safely perform maximal jumps, sprints, or ballistic lifts.

## Chapter 4 — The Long Human Day

Sources:

- Lang J.J. et al. (2024), overview of meta-analyses on cardiorespiratory fitness and morbidity/mortality. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38599681/
- U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, *Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans, 2nd edition*. https://health.gov/our-work/nutrition-physical-activity/physical-activity-guidelines/current-guidelines
- CDC, “Older Adult Activity: An Overview.” Current U.S. guidance includes aerobic activity, muscle strengthening, and balance work for adults 65 and older. https://www.cdc.gov/physical-activity-basics/guidelines/older-adults.html

### Falsification note

Endurance is specific to mode, intensity, duration, environment, fueling, heat, altitude, and training history. A high laboratory fitness value does not guarantee comfort during a long hike, manual-work day, or unfamiliar sport. The chapter uses aerobic capacity as one component of reserve, not a complete measure of stamina.

## Chapter 5 — The Things We Carry

Sources:

- Yang F. et al. “Effects of anterior load carriage on gait parameters: A systematic review with meta-analysis.” *Applied Ergonomics* (2022). PMID 34547577. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34547577/
- “A systematic review of the physiological and biomechanical differences between males and females in response to load carriage during walking activities.” (2023). PMID 37625283. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37625283/

### Falsification note

Load carriage research often studies military, occupational, or laboratory tasks and cannot be mapped directly onto household carrying. Object shape, grip, load position, footwear, terrain, distance, sex, body size, and fatigue alter the task. The chapter therefore avoids a universal “safe” percentage of bodyweight for carrying.

## Chapter 6 — The Floor Is Still Part of the World

Sources:

- Brito L.B.B. et al. “Ability to sit and rise from the floor as a predictor of all-cause mortality.” *European Journal of Preventive Cardiology* (2014). PMID 23242910. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23242910/
- Araújo C.G.S. et al. “Sitting-rising test scores predict natural and cardiovascular causes of deaths in middle-aged and older men and women.” (2025). PMID 40569873. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40569873/

### Falsification note

The sitting-rising test is an observational prognostic marker influenced by age, strength, mobility, balance, body dimensions, health status, and test familiarity. An association with mortality does not mean floor transitions themselves cause longevity. The chapter uses floor competence as a practical skill, not as a longevity score.

## Chapter 7 — Balance Is a Stream of Corrections

Sources:

- Mansfield A. et al. “The Effect of Reactive Balance Training on Falls in Daily Life: An Updated Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis.” (2023). PMID 37651698. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37651698/
- Sharma S. et al. “Perturbation-Based Balance Training Reduces Falls and Fall Injuries in Older People: Insights on Mechanisms and Training Parameters From a Systematic Review.” (2026). PMID 42391766. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42391766/

### Falsification note

Reactive-balance training has evidence in older adults and people with balance impairment, but it can also produce adverse events and requires appropriate progression and supervision. Standing still on one leg, laboratory postural-sway measures, and the ability to recover from an unexpected trip are related but not identical skills.

## Chapter 8 — The Ground Begins at the Foot

Sources:

- Jaffri A.H. et al. “Evidence for Intrinsic Foot Muscle Training in Improving Foot Function: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis.” *Journal of Athletic Training* (2023). PMID 35724360. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35724360/
- Willemse L. et al. “The effect of interventions anticipated to improve plantar intrinsic foot muscle strength on fall-related dynamic function in adults: a systematic review.” (2022). PMID 35057831. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35057831/
- Xu J. et al. “The Effects of Minimalist Shoes on Plantar Intrinsic Foot Muscle Size and Strength: A Systematic Review.” (2023). PMID 35878616. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35878616/

### Falsification note

Foot-strengthening evidence is heterogeneous, and improvements in intrinsic foot-muscle measures do not prove that barefoot or minimalist footwear prevents injury. Footwear needs are individual and task-dependent. The chapter should not romanticize barefoot movement or imply that supportive footwear makes feet weak by default.

## Chapter 9 — Pulling Yourself Through Space

Sources:

- Saul D. et al. literature on physiological demands of climbing is used only for the broad point that climbing integrates grip, upper-body work, lower-body positioning, and skill; specific training claims should be sourced to climbing-specific research rather than generalized from resistance training.
- Currier B.S. et al. (2023) provides the resistance-training evidence base for general pulling strength. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37414459/

### Falsification note

Climbing performance is highly skill-specific. Grip strength, pulling strength, body composition, route-reading, technique, fear management, finger-tendon tolerance, and experience all matter. A pull-up is not a proxy for climbing competence, and climbing should not be presented as a universally necessary capacity.

## Chapter 10 — The Fastest Thing Your Body Can Do

Sources:

- Seroyer S.T. et al. “The Kinetic Chain Revisited: New Concepts on Throwing Mechanics and Injury.” (2016). PMID 26972269. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26972269/
- Fleisig G.S. et al. “Biomechanics of overhand throwing with implications for injuries.” (1996). PMID 8784962. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8784962/
- Swinton P.A. et al. (2024), resistance-exercise dose-response meta-analysis including power outcomes. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38652410/

### Falsification note

Throwing is not merely arm strength. Overhead throwing is a skilled kinetic-chain action, and repetitive high-intensity throwing creates substantial shoulder and elbow demands. Casual throwing, medicine-ball work, baseball pitching, and track-and-field throws should not be collapsed into one exposure.

## Chapter 11 — Own the Range

Sources:

- Favro F. et al. “The Influence of Resistance Training on Joint Flexibility in Healthy Adults: A Systematic Review, Meta-analysis, and Meta-regression.” (2025). PMID 39787531. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39787531/
- Schoenfeld B.J. et al. “Effects of range of motion on resistance training adaptations: A systematic review and meta-analysis.” (2021). PMID 34170576. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34170576/

### Falsification note

More range of motion is not automatically better. Joint structure, sport demands, injury history, pain, neurological factors, and task constraints matter. The chapter distinguishes possessing usable range from chasing maximum flexibility and avoids claiming a universal ideal posture or joint angle.

## Chapter 12 — The Body Learns the Problem

Sources:

- Hall K.G. and Magill R.A. “Variability of Practice and Contextual Interference in Motor Skill Learning.” *Journal of Motor Behavior* (1995). PMID 12529226. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12529226/
- “High contextual interference improves retention in motor learning: systematic review and meta-analysis.” (2024). PMID 38987617. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38987617/
- “The effect of contextual interference on transfer in motor learning — a systematic review and meta-analysis.” (2024). PMID 39205981. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39205981/

### Falsification note

Random or variable practice is not always superior during acquisition, and applied transfer effects are less consistent than laboratory retention results can make them sound. Novices may benefit from stability before variability. The chapter treats variation as a tool for learning, not a command to make every session chaotic.

## Chapter 13 — Play Is Training With an Opponent

Sources:

- Motor-learning reviews listed for Chapter 12 support the narrower claim that variable practice and changing task demands can alter retention and transfer.
- Team-sport and neuromuscular-training literature supports the idea that sport practice combines decision-making with acceleration, deceleration, balance, and coordination, but the manuscript avoids claiming that informal play is a standardized dose of any one fitness quality.

### Falsification note

Play can be low intensity, high risk, socially exclusive, repetitive, or poorly matched to a participant’s ability. It is not automatically superior to structured practice. The useful property is that many games couple perception to action under uncertainty.

## Chapter 14 — Fatigue Is a Changing Problem

Sources:

- Enoka R.M. and Duchateau J. research on neuromuscular fatigue provides the conceptual basis for fatigue as task-dependent rather than a single substance or sensation.
- Wilson J.M. et al. “Concurrent training: a meta-analysis examining interference of aerobic and resistance exercises.” (2012). PMID 22002517. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22002517/

### Falsification note

Fatigue can refer to performance decline, perceived effort, sleepiness, central or peripheral mechanisms, substrate depletion, heat strain, or accumulated training stress. The chapter avoids using the word as though all forms share one cause. Interference effects in concurrent training depend on training status, mode, dose, recovery, and outcomes.

## Chapter 15 — The Work Happens After the Work

Sources:

- Dobrosielski D.A. et al. “The Association Between Poor Sleep and the Incidence of Sport and Physical Training-Related Injuries in Adult Athletic Populations: A Systematic Review.” *Sports Medicine* (2021). PMID 33560506. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33560506/
- Public-health physical-activity guidance emphasizes progressive activity while recognizing health conditions and individual ability. https://health.gov/our-work/nutrition-physical-activity/physical-activity-guidelines/current-guidelines

### Falsification note

Recovery cannot be reduced to sleep alone, and much athlete recovery research is observational or population-specific. Soreness is not a direct measure of adaptation. The chapter avoids claiming that a specific sleep duration, supplement, cold exposure, or recovery gadget guarantees performance or prevents injury.

## Chapter 16 — The Limit Is Not an Enemy

Sources:

- Hübscher M. et al. “Neuromuscular training for sports injury prevention: a systematic review.” (2010). PMID 19952811. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19952811/
- Jiang T. et al. “The effects of integrative neuromuscular training on injury risk and athletic performance in athletes: a systematic review and meta-analysis.” (2026). PMID 41645324. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41645324/

### Falsification note

Exercise can reduce the incidence of some injuries in some populations; it cannot make a body injury-proof. Injury is multifactorial and affected by exposure, previous injury, contact, equipment, environment, workload, anatomy, chance, and many other factors. Persistent or severe symptoms can require qualified clinical assessment rather than more training.

## Chapter 17 — No One Is Athletic Alone

Sources:

- Team-sport research on concurrent demands and neuromuscular training illustrates that performance emerges from interacting physical and perceptual tasks rather than isolated capacities.
- Kang J. et al. “Effects of Concurrent Strength and HIIT-Based Endurance Training on Physical Fitness in Trained Team Sports Players: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis.” (2022). PMID 36429528. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36429528/

### Falsification note

Team coordination cannot be inferred from physical conditioning. Communication, shared models, tactics, trust, expertise, role clarity, and practice history can dominate outcomes. This chapter uses athletics as an example of distributed action, not evidence that every group problem resembles sport.

## Chapter 18 — The Margin Narrows

Sources:

- Byrne C. et al. (2016) on aging, muscle power, and physical function. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26893098/
- Borde R. et al. (2015) on resistance training in healthy older adults. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26420238/
- CDC, current physical-activity guidance for adults 65 and older. https://www.cdc.gov/physical-activity-basics/guidelines/older-adults.html
- Review of motor performance and aging (2025). PMID 41016071. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41016071/

### Falsification note

Aging trajectories vary enormously. Population averages do not predict one person’s future, and declines in performance reflect interactions among biology, disease, injury, activity, environment, medication, social conditions, and opportunity. Training can preserve and improve capacity but does not halt aging.

## Chapter 19 — Train for the Version You Did Not Plan

Sources:

- Reactive-balance meta-analyses cited in Chapter 7 support the idea that practicing recovery responses can alter some real-world fall outcomes in selected populations. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37651698/
- Motor-learning contextual-interference meta-analyses cited in Chapter 12 support a qualified role for variability in retention and transfer. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38987617/
- Eckardt N. and Rosenblatt N.J. “Healthy aging does not impair lower extremity motor flexibility while walking across an uneven surface.” (2018). PMID 30248484. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30248484/

### Falsification note

Training with uncertainty cannot prepare a person for every emergency or environment. Randomness is not specificity. Some high-consequence tasks are better addressed with equipment, instruction, engineering controls, rehearsals, or avoidance than with generalized athletic capacity.

## Chapter 20 — A Week You Can Live In

Sources:

- Currier B.S. et al. (2023), resistance-training network meta-analysis. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37414459/
- Wilson J.M. et al. (2012), concurrent-training meta-analysis. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22002517/
- U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, *Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans, 2nd edition*. https://health.gov/our-work/nutrition-physical-activity/physical-activity-guidelines/current-guidelines
- CDC older-adult activity guidance, including aerobic, strengthening, and balance activity. https://www.cdc.gov/physical-activity-basics/guidelines/older-adults.html

### Falsification note

The example week is not a medical prescription or an optimized program for every objective. Competitive athletes, people with disabilities, people with physically demanding jobs, pregnant people, people recovering from injury, and people with chronic health conditions may need very different organization and professional guidance. Adherence is important, but “the plan you can stick to” is not enough if the plan is mismatched to the goal or unsafe for the individual.

## Cross-book claim rules

- Do not convert cohort associations into causal promises.
- Do not use “longevity” as shorthand for a single physical test.
- Do not imply that training prevents all falls or injuries.
- Do not assign universal safe loads, weekly volumes, heart-rate zones, ranges of motion, or body-composition targets.
- Distinguish general-population guidance from competitive-sport preparation.
- Treat pain, disability, and chronic disease as areas where general training advice may need adaptation rather than as character tests.
- Preserve evidence of heterogeneity. A useful body is not one standardized body.
