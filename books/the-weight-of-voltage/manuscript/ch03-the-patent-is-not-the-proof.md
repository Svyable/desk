# The Patent Is Not the Proof

Brown’s patents are among the strongest primary documents in the story and among the easiest to misuse.

That combination makes them perfect evidence.

U.S. Patent 2,949,550, issued in 1960, describes electrokinetic apparatus in which electrical potential produces force between a structure and the surrounding medium. U.S. Patent 3,187,206, issued in 1965, claims shaped electrical fields can produce thrust. The drawings show the sort of asymmetry that still defines hobby lifters: a small high-field electrode and a larger collector, separated by a dielectric region.

What exactly does the patent prove?

It proves Brown put those claims on paper by those dates. It proves the United States Patent Office granted claims around those devices. It helps reconstruct geometry, terminology and the inventor’s theory of operation. It establishes that Brown’s work was not invented decades later by internet folklore.

It does not prove antigravity.

This sounds obvious until you notice how often the patent number itself is used as a physics credential. “Patented” is allowed to do the rhetorical work that a force curve, vacuum run or independently replicated measurement ought to do.

The distinction matters especially in fringe technology because patents sit at an unusual intersection of seriousness and incompleteness. They are more formal than anecdotes. They contain engineering detail. They can be searched, dated and compared. But their job is to establish intellectual-property claims, not to determine whether nature obeys the inventor’s preferred explanation.

Brown’s 1965 patent is especially interesting because its language broadens the mechanism. It says shaped electrical fields can propel a device and discusses forces in dielectric fluid media. That wording can be read two ways. A conventional reader notices that dielectric fluid is exactly where electrohydrodynamic momentum exchange belongs. A Brown advocate notices that the patent asserts more than an ordinary ion blower and imagines the medium as incidental.

The experiment has to decide.

There is a second reason to dwell on the patent. Brown was thinking in terms of field geometry long before modern hobby lifters made the effect visually familiar. He recognized that thin electrodes concentrate field strength, that electrode asymmetry matters, that dielectric properties affect behavior and that force changes with applied potential. Those are legitimate engineering observations even if gravity never enters the apparatus.

This is how frontier claims become historically slippery. A wrong mechanism can coexist with useful apparatus.

The steam engine worked before thermodynamics. Static-electric machines did things before field theory. Early aviation included bad aerodynamic theories attached to machines that still flew. So “Brown misidentified ion wind” cannot be the end of the story unless the only question is whether Brown discovered antigravity.

It is not the only question.

The deeper question is what kind of inventor Brown was at the boundary between poorly characterized electrohydrodynamics and imagined electrogravity. Did he see a real force and overinterpret it? Did some configurations produce residuals that deserved better testing? Did later myth amplify ambiguous laboratory behavior into a suppressed aerospace revolution? Which parts of his program survive modern instrumentation?

The patents give us a map for asking.

A modern replication should not copy the old rhetoric. It should copy the hardware variables. Electrode radius. Gap. Voltage. Current. Gas species. Pressure. Humidity. Dielectric constant. Surface finish. Polarity. Frequency if pulsed. Mechanical mounting. Thrust vector. Thermal load. Leakage path. Corona onset.

Then remove the environment one piece at a time.

If thrust scales with leakage current and gas density, the apparatus is telling you about charged matter. If the signal collapses as pressure falls, that is useful. If it survives into high vacuum, reverses exactly with electric geometry, remains when cable forces are nulled and appears on independent balances, that is useful too.

The patent becomes a starting geometry rather than a conclusion.

There is a strange fairness in this approach. It takes Brown more seriously than the believers who quote him and the skeptics who laugh at him.

To take an inventor seriously is to let the apparatus lose.

Brown’s legacy has often been protected from failure by moving the claim into inaccessible territory: the right dielectric was missing, the voltage was too low, the secret version used a better material, the effect was classified, the geometry was misunderstood. That is how a research program turns into folklore. Every null becomes evidence that the real device exists somewhere else.

A patent does not deserve that kind of protection.

It deserves a balance, a vacuum chamber and a technician who is slightly annoyed by the whole thing.

That is the proper honor.
