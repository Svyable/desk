# Reaction Mass in the Room

There is a child’s version of reactionless propulsion: a sealed box moves without throwing anything out.

There is an adult version: define the system boundary.

That boundary is where half the history of fringe propulsion goes wrong.

A Brown lifter in air appears self-contained if the observer thinks the device ends at the frame. It is not self-contained. The electric field extends into surrounding gas. Ions created near the emitter move through that gas and collide with neutral molecules. The room participates in the engine.

Extend the system boundary to include the air and momentum conservation looks ordinary again.

The same bookkeeping problem recurs in more sophisticated experiments. A magnetic device can push on Earth’s field or nearby steel. A microwave cavity can push through thermal deformation, cable forces or emitted radiation. A high-voltage balance can push electrostatically on its chamber. A vibrating apparatus can couple into a support structure. A vacuum device can expel adsorbed gas too slowly to look like a rocket and still make thrust.

“Nothing came out” is an observation with poor eyesight.

Electrogravitics raises the stakes because the claimed new interaction is itself a field. If Brown were right in the strongest sense, perhaps the device would exchange momentum with a gravitational field, distant matter or some vacuum structure. A field-propulsion advocate can therefore answer the conservation objection by widening the system boundary again.

In principle, that is not absurd.

Electromagnetic radiation carries momentum. Gravitational waves carry energy and momentum. A vehicle can exchange momentum with an external field or body without carrying propellant in the ordinary rocket sense. Solar sails are propellantless onboard because photons from the Sun do the exchanging. Electrodynamic tethers push on a planetary magnetic environment. “No onboard propellant” is not the same as “no momentum exchange.”

This is why conservation laws do not automatically disprove every field-propulsion concept.

They do demand accounting.

A useful claim must say what the apparatus couples to strongly enough to generate the measured force. If the answer is gravity, estimate the coupling. Gravity is famously weak. Ordinary electric fields do not produce observable gravitational forces anywhere near lifter thrust. General relativity allows electromagnetic energy to gravitate, but the effect at laboratory energy densities is fantastically small.

Calling a measured millinewton “electrogravity” therefore creates an enormous quantitative obligation.

Where did that coupling strength come from?

This is one reason Brown’s interpretation is so difficult to reconcile with established physics. The problem is not merely that Einstein forgot a capacitor term. Electromagnetic stress-energy is already in the theory. The predicted gravitational influence of laboratory fields is tiny. A useful Brown coupling would need either a new interaction or a radically enhanced regime.

Again: new physics is allowed. But it needs its own ledger.

What symmetry is broken? What variable controls the coupling? Does the force violate Lorentz invariance? Is momentum transferred to an external field? Does the effect depend on orientation relative to gravity? Does it alter inertial mass, passive gravitational mass or neither? Are there radiation products? Can the reverse process generate measurable electrical energy from acceleration?

Brown himself explored generator and transducer concepts in related patents, which is exactly where reciprocity becomes interesting. If electrical configuration couples strongly to mechanical/gravitational degrees of freedom, the inverse effect should often exist in some form.

A one-way miracle is suspicious.

The best modern electrogravitics experiment would therefore measure more than thrust. It would look for the reciprocal footprint.

A machine claiming a new momentum channel is not just a better rocket. It is a new term in the bookkeeping of the world.

The room has to balance.
