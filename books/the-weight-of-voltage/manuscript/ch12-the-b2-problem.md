# The B-2 Problem

Every fringe technology eventually acquires a machine that supposedly proves the secret version exists.

For electrogravitics, that machine is often the B-2 Spirit.

The argument usually runs from appearance and coincidence. The B-2 is a flying wing with unusual leading edges, enormous electrical systems, classified subsystems and a development history wrapped in secrecy. Some writers have proposed that high-voltage electrostatic charging contributes to drag reduction, propulsion or gravity control. Brown’s disc concepts are then placed upstream as the hidden ancestor.

This is a perfect claim to practice evidence discipline on because it mixes several true facts with a very large inference.

The B-2 is real. Much of its detailed technology was classified. Plasma and electrostatic flow-control concepts have been researched in aerospace. Brown did build asymmetric high-voltage devices. None of those facts demonstrates that the B-2 uses electrogravitic lift.

A military aircraft is also an adversarial object for reverse engineering by silhouette. Features can have mundane functions that are themselves classified. Radar cross-section control drives shapes that look aerodynamically peculiar. Edge treatments, conductive structures and electrical systems can exist for stealth, lightning protection, sensors, de-icing or electromagnetic compatibility.

The stronger question is not whether an electrogravitic B-2 is imaginable.

It is what observable consequences the technology would have.

If electrogravity supplied meaningful lift, engine-out performance and energy accounting should look unusual. If it reduced effective weight, takeoff data, structural loading, landing behavior or payload relationships might contain anomalies. If high voltage were required at extreme levels, insulation, corona control, maintenance hazards and power architecture would leave fingerprints. If the effect depended on atmosphere, altitude scaling would matter. If it persisted in vacuum, the technology would be more valuable to spacecraft than to a subsonic bomber.

The public B-2 performance story does not require such a mechanism.

That does not prove no classified electrostatic system exists aboard it. It means the exotic claim has not earned necessity.

This distinction—possible versus required—is one of the most important in suppressed-technology research.

Secrecy creates room for possibilities. Evidence narrows them.

The B-2 also shows how a fringe narrative can become self-sealing. If public performance appears conventional, that is because the secret technology is hidden. If a feature looks unusual, that is evidence of the secret technology. If engineers deny it, classification explains the denial. Every outcome points in the same direction.

A theory that predicts every observation predicts nothing.

The Brown question deserves better than aircraft pareidolia.

If electrogravitics exists at useful scale, we should find it first where the physics is easiest to isolate: on a thrust balance, in a vacuum chamber, across reproducible materials and geometries.

Do not use a black airplane to rescue a weak laboratory signal.

Use the laboratory to earn the airplane.
