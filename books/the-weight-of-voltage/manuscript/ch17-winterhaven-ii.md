# Winterhaven II

If I had to spend one million dollars on Townsend Brown, I would not build a flying disc.

I would build three boring rooms.

Room one would hold a high-vacuum chamber with two independent thrust balances: a torsion pendulum and a flexure balance. Room two would hold an atmospheric-pressure rig capable of controlled gas composition, humidity and pressure. Room three would be metrology: high-voltage calibration, current probes, optical interferometry, thermal imaging, vibration characterization and the electronics needed to synchronize everything.

Then I would build families of devices instead of a hero prototype.

Asymmetric wire-to-foil geometry. Symmetric controls. Brown-style solid dielectric capacitors. Matched thermal dummies. Swappable dielectric inserts spanning large permittivity ranges. DC and pulsed variants, but only after the DC artifacts are understood.

Every device would receive an identity before testing. The analyst would not know which configuration was in the chamber during the first pass.

Pressure would be swept, not sampled at “air” and “vacuum.” That curve matters. If thrust follows gas-mediated expectations through corona and transitional regimes, we learn something. If a residual turns on below a particular pressure, we learn something else. Gas species would change ion mobility. Mechanical orientation would change. The chamber would rotate relative to gravity. Wiring would be rerouted between runs to expose cable coupling. Grounded-wall geometry would change to expose electrostatic reaction forces.

The raw data would be public.

Not because crowds are wiser than laboratories, but because fringe claims need adversaries. Somebody should be rewarded for finding the forgotten cable force.

The pre-registration would name three outcomes.

Outcome A: force is fully accounted for by electrohydrodynamic, electrostatic, magnetic, thermal and mechanical mechanisms within uncertainty. Brown’s strong electrogravity claim loses again.

Outcome B: a small residual survives but lacks stable scaling or cross-lab replication. Interesting instrument problem. No propulsion claim.

Outcome C: a reproducible residual survives controls, appears in independent apparatus and follows a preregistered scaling law inconsistent with modeled conventional forces. Publish immediately, invite hostile replication and stop calling it antigravity until the coupling is characterized.

That is the short version.

The real experiment would begin months before high voltage.

The first object under test would be the laboratory.

A precision thrust stand does not measure force in the abstract. It measures the laboratory’s capacity to impersonate force. Pumps vibrate. Buildings breathe with temperature. Elevators move mass. Trucks pass. HVAC cycles. Vacuum hoses stiffen and relax. Optical tables creep. People lean on floors. The electrical grid carries switching noise. Earth tides alter local gravity by tiny amounts. Atmospheric pressure loads walls and structures. A torsion fiber remembers yesterday.

None of this is mystical. That is the point.

Before a Brown device enters the chamber, Winterhaven II would run empty balances for days. The apparatus would learn its own weather. Temperature, pressure, humidity, floor acceleration, acoustic noise, magnetic field and pump state would be recorded against balance output. Known calibration forces would be injected at irregular times. Analysts would have to recover them blind.

If the instrument cannot find a force we deliberately hid in the data, it has no business discovering a new one.

The two balances should fail differently.

A torsion pendulum is exquisitely sensitive to horizontal force or torque, but it has its own fiber dynamics and electrostatic vulnerabilities. A flexure balance has different stiffness, damping and thermal behavior. A signal that appears at the same commanded voltage on both is more interesting than a signal that lives on one mechanical architecture. A third laboratory should use a different design again: perhaps a beam balance or another force sensor with a different set of systematic errors.

Replication means changing the nuisance landscape.

This is the weakness of experiments that duplicate apparatus too faithfully. If three laboratories buy the same balance, use the same feedthrough, route the same cable and copy the same control software, they can replicate an artifact. Independence is not a social property. It is an engineering property.

The device family would be manufactured as a matrix.

One axis: electrode asymmetry.

Another: dielectric permittivity.

Another: electrode spacing.

Another: active area.

Another: polarity.

Another: pressure.

Another: gas species.

Another: voltage waveform.

One cannot sweep the whole combinatorial space at once. That is why theory has to risk something. Brown’s strongest historical interpretation must nominate scaling relationships before the data arrive. Does force scale with voltage squared? With stored electrostatic energy? With capacitance? With permittivity? With field gradient? With the product of some material property and field? Does polarity matter? Does orientation relative to Earth’s gravitational field matter?

Write the prediction down.

The conventional models get predictions too.

For atmospheric electrohydrodynamics, force should track ion production, mobility, geometry and gas conditions. Replace air with gases of different ion transport properties and the atmospheric force should change. Lower pressure and the discharge regime should evolve. Current and optical emission should carry information about the momentum-transfer process.

For simple electrostatic boundary forces, moving grounded surfaces should matter. Change chamber-wall distance. Add guard electrodes. Alter shielding. A device whose “thrust” changes when a grounded plate is moved nearby is giving away part of its mechanism.

For thermal effects, time is a diagnostic. Apply the same dissipated power to a dummy that reproduces heating without reproducing the claimed field geometry. Watch thermal images. Measure structural temperature. Use runs long enough for equilibrium and runs short enough to isolate transients. If the force follows heat instead of field configuration, the experiment has answered cheaply.

For magnetic coupling, rotate the apparatus relative to ambient field, add nonmagnetic structural replacements, map the local field and reproduce the measured current waveform through a dummy conductor. High voltage does not mean low electromagnetic complexity. A few microamps in the wrong loop can still matter when the target force is tiny.

For photon recoil, calculate the ceiling. If the device radiates electromagnetic energy directionally, momentum leaves with it. Radiation pressure is ordinary propulsion. It is usually far too small to explain grand Brown claims, but a precision residual does not get to ignore it merely because it is small.

The hardest conventional category may be electrostatic interaction with the test environment because it is simultaneously obvious and invisible.

The chamber is a conductor. The balance frame is material. Wiring has capacitance to ground. A charged or polarized device induces charge elsewhere. Those reaction forces can close through structures the experimenter mentally excludes from the “device.” This is why the system boundary must be drawn around the entire electrical apparatus, not the object we wish were a spacecraft.

Talley’s vacuum work encountered chamber-wall effects. Winterhaven II should promote that nuisance into a primary test. Build an adjustable grounded shell around the test article. Change its radius while preserving everything else. Repeat with different shapes. A real anomalous body force that is claimed to couple to gravity should not track the geometry of a nearby grounded can with the enthusiasm of an electrostatic interaction.

Then change gravity orientation.

This is one of Brown’s most consequential historical ambiguities. If the claimed effect is truly gravitational rather than merely propulsive, does the force depend on orientation relative to the local gravitational field? A device can be mounted so the proposed force vector points up, down or horizontally while electrode geometry remains fixed relative to the balance. If the same device produces a body-fixed thrust independent of gravity direction, that is evidence for some propulsion effect but not obviously for “weight control.” If it changes with gravitational orientation, the control burden gets much harder—and much more interesting.

The experiment should use Earth as a variable carefully.

Do not start with eclipses, lunar phases or folklore. Start with rotation.

If an effect survives, later tests can ask whether local gravitational potential, latitude or altitude matters. But those are second-generation experiments. The first generation is about whether a force exists at all.

Device fabrication needs its own blind.

A surprisingly common failure mode in fringe experiments is that the inventor knows which device is supposed to work and handles it differently, intentionally or not. Winterhaven II would include active and sham units that are externally identical. Some shams would contain dielectric material but altered electrode topology. Others would reproduce capacitance while suppressing the proposed asymmetry. Some would deliberately include known tiny force mechanisms as positive controls.

The laboratory operator would know the safety state but not the hypothesis state.

High-voltage safety cannot be blinded.

Interpretation can.

The data format matters too. Every sensor should share a synchronized clock. Voltage, current, chamber pressure, residual gas channels, temperatures, balance displacement, magnetic field, accelerometers, acoustic sensors, pump state and optical detectors need to be alignable at the event level. If a force transient occurs seventeen milliseconds after a current spike and every current spike has a cousin in the balance, that pattern should not require somebody’s memory of the run.

Store the ugly data.

Do not keep only averaged thrust.

Discharge spikes, failed runs, arcs, aborted voltage ramps and zero shifts are where the apparatus explains itself. A clean final graph created after nuisance events were “obviously” removed is one of the ways extraordinary claims become permanent. The exclusion rule should be written before the run where possible, and every excluded segment should remain inspectable.

The pressure campaign would proceed in stages.

Atmospheric tests first, because the force is large enough to instrument richly. Map force, current and gas motion. Use schlieren or other appropriate flow visualization if available. Confirm that the apparatus can reproduce ordinary ionic thrust and that the force stand agrees with an independent momentum estimate within a tolerable error budget.

Then reduce pressure through the discharge regimes slowly enough to see the transition rather than leap over it. Repeat with different gases. Map when visible plasma changes. Record breakdown thresholds. If the force collapses with available gas momentum, Brown’s familiar atmospheric effect becomes even better characterized.

Only then spend the expensive time in high vacuum.

In high vacuum the device must operate without breakdown at a preregistered voltage range. A run that arcs at the exact moment a force appears is not a positive result. It is an electrical-breakdown result unless the force persists independently afterward.

This sounds severe because it is.

A new force deserves a severe childhood.

Suppose a residual appears.

The first response is not a press release. It is sabotage.

Swap polarity. Rotate the device. Change its position in the chamber. Move grounded walls. Replace the dielectric with a matched dummy. Reroute cables. Change the balance. Repeat on another day. Hand the active/sham coding to somebody outside the propulsion group. Send devices to a second laboratory without telling them which result the first lab saw.

If the signal dies, the experiment worked.

A null after a seductive anomaly is not wasted money. It is the value of the apparatus.

If the signal survives, quantify the conventional force budget. Every known candidate gets an upper bound. Not “we don’t think it’s thermal.” How many newtons can thermal drift produce under measured conditions? Not “the vacuum is too good for ion wind.” Given the measured residual gas and current, what momentum transfer remains possible? Not “the cables were shielded.” What force was measured when the electrical waveform was applied to the cable system with a sham load?

The word anomalous should appear only after the budget closes badly.

Then replicate.

The replication criterion should be statistical and physical. A second lab does not need the identical numerical force if the apparatus scales differently, but the result should obey the preregistered relation within uncertainty. If the proposed law says force follows a combination of field energy and dielectric property, changing those variables should move the signal the predicted way.

If every laboratory needs its own unexplained correction factor, the law is not traveling.

At that point the experiment would have earned the right to become stranger.

A confirmed residual could be tested for equivalence-principle behavior. Does it act on nearby neutral masses? Does the device’s passive gravitational mass change? Does inertial response change? Is the force reciprocal? Can momentum be located in fields outside the immediate apparatus? Does an isolated system center of mass behave conventionally?

Those questions separate propulsion from gravity.

A device can generate thrust through an overlooked electromagnetic momentum channel and still be scientifically interesting. A true modification of gravitational or inertial behavior would need additional observables beyond motion of the energized device.

This is where Brown’s legacy becomes experimentally useful rather than culturally decorative.

He gives us a hypothesis with enough historical specificity to build a hostile apparatus around it.

The budget need not be enormous. One million dollars is not a serious aerospace development program. It is enough, perhaps, for several good instruments, careful fabrication, staff time and replication grants if the scope stays narrow. The goal is not propulsion performance. The goal is evidentiary closure.

The project should publish nulls.

This needs to be contractual. Every completed configuration enters the public table whether it “worked” or not. Extraordinary-technology research is uniquely vulnerable to survivor bias because failed devices disappear while one suggestive trace gets copied for decades. A public null matrix is infrastructure.

The final report would not use a binary title like *Antigravity Proven* or *Antigravity Debunked*.

It would report a parameter space.

At these pressures, voltages, geometries, materials and sensitivities, no residual above this force was observed after this conventional budget. Or: a residual of this magnitude followed this scaling law in these independent systems and remains unexplained by the enumerated mechanisms.

Either sentence advances the subject.

The pre-registration would still name the three broad outcomes.

Outcome A: force is fully accounted for by electrohydrodynamic, electrostatic, magnetic, thermal and mechanical mechanisms within uncertainty. Brown’s strong electrogravity claim loses again.

Outcome B: a small residual survives but lacks stable scaling or cross-lab replication. Interesting instrument problem. No propulsion claim.

Outcome C: a reproducible residual survives controls, appears in independent apparatus and follows a preregistered scaling law inconsistent with modeled conventional forces. Publish immediately, invite hostile replication and stop calling it antigravity until the coupling is characterized.

This would be expensive by tabletop standards and cheap by aerospace standards.

It would also be historically clean.

Winterhaven’s original purpose was to perform critical experiments that could confirm or deny an electrodynamic-gravitational relationship. Seventy-four years later, the best tribute is not to assume the confirmation happened in secret.

Run the denial hard enough that confirmation would mean something.
