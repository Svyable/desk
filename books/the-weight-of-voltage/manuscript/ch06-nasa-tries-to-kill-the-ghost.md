# NASA Tries to Kill the Ghost

NASA’s Breakthrough Propulsion Physics Project had a peculiar mandate: look at ideas that sounded like science fiction without allowing science fiction standards of evidence.

From 1996 to 2002, the program examined propellantless propulsion, warp drives, wormholes, gravity coupling, vacuum fluctuation energy and other concepts far outside ordinary mission planning. Marc Millis, who managed the effort at Glenn Research Center, later summarized the work in a 2004 technical memorandum with a tone that should be mandatory reading in every frontier laboratory.

The project did not ask whether an idea was respectable. It asked whether there was a small experiment capable of killing or advancing it.

Brown’s descendants were among the casualties.

The report’s section on Biefeld–Brown devices and variants is unusually direct. It notes the long history of claims around high-voltage asymmetric capacitors, including “lifters” and alleged electrostatic antigravity. Then it says rigorous experimental tests attribute the observed thrust to ion wind. Vacuum work, including tests sponsored outside the core project, did not indicate new propulsion physics. The conclusion: these devices were not viable breakthrough-physics candidates.

That paragraph is devastating if your claim is that ordinary Brown-style lifters already demonstrate antigravity.

It is less devastating if your claim is that high-voltage field propulsion remains an interesting engineering family or that some different, tightly specified effect could still exist.

This is what a good negative result does. It narrows the territory instead of setting fire to the map.

NASA’s larger program is useful because Brown advocates sometimes treat institutional interest as evidence of hidden success. Here we have institutional interest with the opposite outcome. NASA looked. NASA funded people to look. NASA published a report saying several seductive concepts did not survive.

Interest is not endorsement.

That sounds trivial until the acronym changes. The same discipline applies to Air Force studies, DIA reports, contractor white papers, intelligence requirements and classified programs. Government agencies investigate possibilities for reasons that include threat assessment, technological hedging and uncertainty reduction. A document can be fascinating precisely because the agency did not know whether the claim was true.

The Breakthrough Propulsion Physics work also showed why fringe research is difficult to manage. When the payoff of success is enormous, almost any tiny prior probability can justify a cheap test. If a million-dollar experiment had one chance in a million of revealing a practical gravity-control mechanism, the expected strategic value could still be absurdly high. That does not make the mechanism likely.

This distinction between *worth checking* and *probably true* is routinely destroyed in retellings.

Brown’s work belongs in that gap.

Aerospace organizations were right to check unusual coupling claims. The mid-century revolution in electronics, nuclear physics and materials made overconfidence dangerous. NASA was right decades later to revisit purported breakthrough propulsion with better instrumentation. Researchers today would be right to test a claimed vacuum residual if the experiment is cheap, clean and pre-registered.

None of that requires pretending earlier nulls did not happen.

Millis’s methodology contains another lesson. He emphasized incremental questions. Not “build a starship.” Not even “prove gravity control.” Measure the suspicious force. Determine its scaling. Identify conventional couplings. Characterize the boundary conditions. A field propulsion concept earns complexity one verified anomaly at a time.

This is almost the opposite of the culture that grew around electrogravitics, where the narrative tends to expand outward: Brown’s disks lead to military interest; military interest leads to classified development; classified development leads to rumored bombers; rumored bombers lead to UFO propulsion; UFO propulsion leads to a hidden post-Newtonian civilization.

The chain can be entertaining while containing no new force measurement after the first link.

NASA worked the other direction.

Reduce the claim until it fits on a balance.

The more fringe the implication, the more ordinary the apparatus should become. Voltage meter. Current probe. pressure gauge. thrust stand. control article. error bars.

If the universe contains an electrical handle on gravity, it will not be insulted by careful bookkeeping.

The ghost should survive being killed.
