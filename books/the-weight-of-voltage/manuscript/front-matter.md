# Front Matter

# The Weight of Voltage

*Townsend Brown, Electrogravitics, and the Force That Would Not Behave*

Sven Hardy Benson

There is a particular kind of fringe subject I trust more than the others.

It has hardware.

Not a photograph of hardware. Not a retired man remembering hardware behind a fence. A machine you can build badly on a kitchen table and better in a laboratory.

Townsend Brown’s asymmetric high-voltage devices belong to that category. They move. The public evidence says the dramatic atmospheric thrust is electrohydrodynamic—charged particles pushing ordinary gas—not gravity control. That should have killed the romance. Instead it made the case more interesting.

Because Brown left behind something rare: a disputed interpretation wrapped around a reproducible physical effect, a 1950s aerospace paper trail, patents, a proposed military research program, later vacuum tests and enough surviving measurement to ask exactly where the extraordinary claim fails.

This book is about that boundary.

It does not claim Brown discovered antigravity. It does not claim every aerospace mention was a classified program. It also refuses the equally lazy conclusion that because ion wind explains lifters, every question Brown asked was stupid.

The point is to follow the force all the way down.

Evidence classes used throughout:

1. **Documented:** patents, reports, published experiments, official technical assessments.
2. **Credible but incomplete:** historical accounts or measurements that are real enough to investigate but do not establish the strong mechanism.
3. **Disputed:** antigravity interpretations, hidden deployment claims, aircraft applications without independent physical confirmation.
4. **Speculative synthesis:** experiments and implications proposed here.

A patent proves a patent. A proposal proves a proposal. A thrust proves a thrust. Gravity control has to earn its own sentence.
