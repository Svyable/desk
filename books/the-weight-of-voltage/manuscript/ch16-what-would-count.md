# What Would Count

A serious fringe book owes the reader a defeat condition.

Here is mine for Brown-style electrogravity.

A modern experiment would need to demonstrate a force that persists in a pressure regime where gas momentum transfer is quantitatively inadequate; survives reversal and dummy controls; cannot be explained by electrostatic interaction with the chamber, magnetic forces, photon pressure, outgassing, thermal gradients, vibration or discharge impulses; reproduces on independent balances; and follows a scaling law tied to a physically specified variable such as stored field energy, dielectric response or orientation relative to an external gravitational parameter.

One clean experiment would not prove a practical drive.

It would earn the phrase *anomalous force*.

Then the work becomes more demanding. Replicate at another institution. Alter scale. Change materials. Search for reciprocal effects. Test whether momentum is transferred to an external environment. Measure any orientation dependence. Ask whether the force violates Lorentz symmetry or equivalence-principle constraints already tested to exquisite precision elsewhere.

The reason for this staircase is not academic conservatism.

It is that extraordinary propulsion claims couple into some of the best-tested physics humans possess.

If a tabletop capacitor produces a large gravitational interaction, precision experiments in electromagnetism and gravity have somehow missed a nearby phenomenon. That can happen only if the effect occupies a narrow regime, is masked by assumptions or depends on a variable those experiments did not probe.

Specify the regime.

Do not say “high voltage.” High voltage is ordinary.

Do not say “asymmetry.” Asymmetry is ordinary.

Do not say “secret dielectric.” Give a measurable property.

Do not say “vacuum energy” unless the model predicts a force.

This is how an exotic claim becomes a research program instead of a mood.

Brown’s work deserves this standard because it came close enough to reality to be testable. The worst fate for his legacy would be endless protection from the experiment that could finally make it interesting again.
