# Back Matter

*Magnetecture* is a synthesis, not a declaration that a new fundamental force or universally recognized engineering discipline has been discovered.

I use *magnetecture* to describe a design posture in which magnetic material state, field geometry, actuation, sensing, feedback and application intent are treated as one system. The individual sciences and technologies discussed here have independent histories and literatures: electromagnetism, permanent magnets, electrical machines, superconductivity, spintronics, altermagnetism, skyrmions, magnonics, multiferroics, magnetocaloric materials, quantum magnetometry, magnetic soft robotics, magnetic bearings and medical magnetic robotics among them.

The novelty claim is deliberately narrow. I have not found prior use of *magnetecture* as the name for this cross-scale systems synthesis during the research pass for this edition. That is not proof that nobody has ever used the word somewhere, and it is not a priority claim over the underlying science. If earlier relevant usage surfaces, the useful question will remain whether the synthesis helps engineers and readers see a common architecture across otherwise separate magnetic technologies.

This book was written while several of its scientific subjects were moving quickly. Altermagnetism is a particularly obvious example. The category was formalized recently, experimental evidence and material assignments are developing rapidly, and scientific debate remains active. Claims in the manuscript are therefore tied in the research trail to dated reviews and primary papers rather than presented as timeless final classifications.

The same discipline applies to technologies closer to translation. A magnetic microrobot moving in a laboratory channel is not a clinical device. A room-temperature skyrmion is not a commercial memory. A high-temperature superconducting tape is not a fusion power plant. A material with a large magnetocaloric response is not a complete refrigerator. A quantum sensor with exceptional field sensitivity is not automatically a practical medical instrument. Each chapter tries to keep the enabling result separate from the system required to make it useful.

The research files beside this manuscript contain the working evidence policy, chapter-by-chapter falsification notes, source notes and a source ledger. Those files are part of the Desk process rather than part of the narrative. They exist so a future revision can identify which claims are stable background, which depend on fast-moving research, and which are explicitly my own engineering extrapolations.

The central thesis should be allowed to fail. If magnetic technologies continue to mature only as isolated bespoke devices, with little reusable architecture across material state, field design, sensing and control, then *magnetecture* may remain a useful metaphor rather than an emerging engineering layer. If remote magnetic actuation remains too limited by distance and field infrastructure, if spin-based computing cannot produce system-level advantages after conversion costs are included, if reprogrammable magnetic matter proves too fragile, or if sensing and control remain too expensive to close the loop, the platform claim should narrow accordingly.

The opposite failure would also matter. It is possible to become so cautious about new materials and prototypes that we miss a genuine architectural transition. The history of technology contains effects that looked specialized until manufacturing, control and interfaces matured around them. A full assessment therefore has to track not only record material properties but the surrounding layers that can turn those properties into reusable capability.

That is why this book focuses less on the strongest magnet than on the field layer around it.

Magnetism has always connected matter across space. What is becoming more interesting is our ability to design that connection, observe it, alter it and place software in the loop.

If that capability grows, the important object will not be the magnet.

It will be the architecture of the field.
