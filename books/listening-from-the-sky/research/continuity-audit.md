# LISTENING FROM THE SKY — continuity audit

## Editorial problem

The opening has a strong and defensible object: **moving the receiver can change what is observable**. It currently risks attaching that object to a broader historical claim that the evidence does not support. The gap that modern balloon work closes is a gap in **in-situ stratospheric receiving**, not a seventy-year absence of atmospheric infrasound science or nuclear-test monitoring.

That distinction should govern the next manuscript pass. It makes the book harder to sensationalize and gives the historical middle something real to discover.

## Authoritative-source check

### MOGUL really was an atmospheric acoustic detection program

The Air Force's documentary reconstruction says Maurice Ewing proposed an atmospheric analogue of the ocean sound channel and that MOGUL initially pursued three linked requirements: a long-range low-frequency microphone for explosions/missiles, telemetry, and an upper-atmosphere platform. It also points to Sandstone acoustic-detection work in 1948.

Source:
- Department of the Air Force historical report, *The Roswell Report: Fact versus Fiction in the New Mexico Desert*, attachment 32 / Project MOGUL discussion: https://www.dafhistory.af.mil/Portals/16/documents/AFD-101201-038.pdf

This supports the historical starting point. It does **not** by itself establish MOGUL's detection denominator, operational success rate, or equivalence between Ewing's proposed channel and modern AtmoSOFAR.

### Atmospheric infrasound did not disappear with MOGUL

CTBTO's institutional history says infrasound was used by both the United States and Soviet Union in the 1950s and 1960s to monitor atmospheric nuclear tests. Interest declined after the 1963 Partial Test Ban Treaty pushed testing underground, then revived during CTBT negotiations in the 1990s.

Sources:
- CTBTO, “Infrasound Technology Workshop 2014”: https://www.ctbto.org/news-and-events/news/infrasound-technology-workshop-2014
- CTBTO, “Listening without sound — CTBTO engineers refine a unique technique” (2010): https://www.ctbto.org/news-and-events/news/listening-without-sound-ctbto-engineers-refine-unique-technique

CTBTO's 25th-anniversary technical history goes further. It identifies post-1963 experimental work including Donn and Shaw (1967), Rind et al. (1973), the Palisades/Lamont-Doherty network, the Swedish Infrasound Network, French networks, and later large-explosion work. That is incompatible with a simple `MOGUL → scientific silence → rediscovery` chronology.

Source:
- CTBTO 25th-anniversary technical booklet: https://www.ctbto.org/sites/default/files/2024-07/20240618-CTBTO%2025th%20Anniversary%20booklet%20Final%20LRes.pdf

A CTBTO conference record also reports Soviet infrasound stations beginning in 1954, a 25-station network by 1991, and a Talgar microbarograph installed in 1962 that recorded atmospheric nuclear tests. This is not a peer-reviewed history of the entire Soviet network, so the underlying archival/technical sources should be recovered before using the station counts in narrative prose. But it is strong counterevidence to any U.S.-centric claim that the field itself vanished.

Source:
- CTBTO Science & Technology 2015, Sokolova, “Acoustic Waves from Atmospheric Nuclear Explosions Recorded by Infrasound and Seismic Stations of Kazakhstan”: https://conferences.ctbto.org/event/15/contributions/2615/

### Ground-based infrasound is now mature nuclear-monitoring infrastructure

The present CTBT International Monitoring System is explicitly multimodal. When complete it includes 60 infrasound stations, alongside 170 seismic stations, 11 hydroacoustic stations, 80 radionuclide stations and 16 radionuclide laboratories. CTBTO says the infrasound network is intended to detect atmospheric nuclear explosions and can complement seismic monitoring of shallow underground events. Its current IMS map says roughly 90% of all 337 facilities are operating.

Sources:
- CTBTO, “Infrasound monitoring”: https://www.ctbto.org/our-work/monitoring-technologies/infrasound-monitoring
- CTBTO, “IMS Map” (current map states locations as approved May 2026): https://www.ctbto.org/our-work/ims-map

This matters editorially because Chapter 1 currently lets “seismology won” do too much work. Seismology became crucial for underground testing, but modern verification did not choose one sensor and discard atmospheric acoustics. It built a system in which seismic, hydroacoustic, infrasound and radionuclide measurements answer different parts of the detection/discrimination problem.

## The narrower historical gap the book can earn

The promising discontinuity is not `infrasound disappeared`. It is closer to:

> **Direct in-situ stratospheric infrasound measurement remained sparse even while ground-based infrasound matured into a serious monitoring technology.**

That is a stranger and better story. It creates two simultaneous histories rather than a false dark age:

1. **Ground infrasound:** atmospheric nuclear-test monitoring → post-PTBT contraction and scientific applications → CTBT-era revival → global IMS infrastructure.
2. **Balloon / in-situ stratospheric infrasound:** MOGUL and scattered early experiments → sparse published record → modern lightweight digital balloon sensing → direct observations of the stratospheric soundscape and elevated ducts.

The manuscript should prove the second chronology before calling it a hiatus. Bowman & Lees's claim about sparse reported in-situ stratospheric work is an important lead, not permission to generalize from `few microphones aloft` to `little infrasound science`.

## Strongest counterargument

The most damaging countermodel to the current framing is also the most useful one: **classification may explain little or none of the later balloon gap.**

A receiver in the stratosphere is operationally inconvenient. Balloons drift, are difficult to recover or hold on station, carry limited power and telemetry, and historically competed against increasingly capable fixed networks. After atmospheric nuclear testing declined, a ground array could be maintained continuously while a balloon platform offered episodic coverage and awkward geometry. Modern GPS, digital storage, miniature pressure sensors, satellite communications and long-duration balloon systems may explain the revival without invoking lost classified knowledge.

The book should therefore not ask merely whether MOGUL was secret. It should compare balloon infrasound against other **nonclassified but platform-constrained** atmospheric measurements. If similar fields also went dormant until instrumentation became cheap and autonomous, `measurement hiatus` is mainly an engineering/economics story.

A second counterargument runs in the other direction. The survival and later global institutionalization of ground infrasound means MOGUL's underlying strategic premise was not simply rejected. Long-range atmospheric acoustics became useful without requiring the receiver to remain aloft. That weakens a dramatic “the world forgot” story but strengthens a subtler one about **which part of an idea survives technological selection**.

## Manuscript implications

Hold or revise these formulations unless the primary record earns them:

- **Subtitle:** `The Secret Nuclear Sensor That Science Took Seventy Years to Verify` currently collapses atmospheric infrasound, Ewing's proposed channel, MOGUL hardware and modern balloon sensing into one “sensor.” The continuity record makes “seventy years to verify” too broad. A future title pass should attach the delay specifically to **listening from within the stratosphere / elevated acoustic duct**, not to nuclear infrasound detection generally.
- **“Seismology won.”** Treat this as one technology-selection result, not the disappearance of infrasound. The CTBT system is explicitly multimodal.
- **“The technology went quiet.”** If retained, name which technology: balloon-borne in-situ receiving, not atmospheric infrasound as a field.
- **“classification-shaped measurement hiatus.”** Keep as a hypothesis until the public continuity record and matched nonclassified cases are reconstructed. Classification may have distorted publication lineage without causing the instrumentation gap.
- **“rediscovery tax.”** Do not imply that modern balloon researchers had to reinvent work that demonstrably survived in ground-infrasound literature. Identify the actual quantities or methods that were lost, inaccessible, or independently rebuilt.

## Quantitative / documentary extraction before prose revision

1. Build a dated table of U.S. and Soviet ground-infrasound nuclear-test monitoring from the 1940s through the 1970s, with station/network scale where primary sources permit.
2. Recover Ewing's `Long Range Sound Transmission in the Atmosphere` report and distinguish its predicted duct altitude/physics from later stratospheric/thermospheric propagation terminology.
3. Recover MOGUL/Sandstone detection results and denominator: source, yield, source-receiver geometry, microphone altitude, frequency band, signal/noise, successful/failed detections.
4. Build a separate table of **in-situ balloon** infrasound experiments from MOGUL through Bowman & Lees 2015. This table, not the general infrasound bibliography, is the correct denominator for a hiatus claim.
5. Reconstruct the technology-cost discontinuity: sensor mass/power, storage, telemetry, navigation, balloon duration/control and recovery burden across eras.
6. Compare the balloon record with at least one nonclassified atmospheric sensing field that faced similar platform costs.
7. For the CTBT era, distinguish detection from discrimination and attribution. A station hearing infrasound is not by itself proof that an event was nuclear.

## Counterevidence rule for the eventual chapter

The book gets stronger if it lets the mature ground network interrupt the apparent resurrection story early. A useful narrative turn is that the old idea did **not** disappear: part of it became ordinary infrastructure while the physically unusual part — putting the receiver inside the upper atmosphere — remained sparse. The reader then has a sharper question than “why did science forget?”:

**Why did one branch of the measurement architecture become a global treaty-verification network while another had to wait for cheap autonomous sensors?**

That question can be answered by documents, engineering and institutional history. It also puts the classification thesis genuinely at risk, which the Desk standard requires.
