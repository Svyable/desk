# BURN THE GAS

*When America Fracked With Nuclear Bombs*

Sven Hardy Benson

This is a book about an idea that sounds fabricated because the paperwork survived.

The United States once tried to use underground nuclear explosions as industrial tools. Not metaphorically. Not as a war plan with a civilian label pasted over it. The Atomic Energy Commission's Plowshare program seriously investigated nuclear excavation, harbors, canals, mineral recovery, underground storage, scientific experiments and stimulation of natural-gas reservoirs. At Gasbuggy in New Mexico in 1967, a nuclear explosive was detonated thousands of feet underground to fracture gas-bearing rock. Rulison followed in Colorado in 1969. Rio Blanco followed in 1973 with three devices.

The strange part is not that governments contemplated an enormous technology. Governments contemplate enormous technologies constantly. The strange part is that this one crossed the line from proposal to hole in the ground, and then encountered a boundary that secrecy, explosive yield and engineering confidence could not negotiate away: the product had to become ordinary enough to sell.

That boundary is the book.

A nuclear explosion can be contained underground while its economic consequences cannot. Gas has to be produced, sampled, processed, transported, regulated, insured, purchased and burned. A harbor has to admit ships. A canal has to connect two bodies of water. A resource-extraction technology has to create a resource somebody can use. The closer an extraordinary classified or government-sponsored technology gets to ordinary infrastructure, the more interfaces it acquires with people and systems outside its originating compartment.

That makes Plowshare unusually useful for thinking about modern claims of hidden technology. It is a documented case in which extraordinary physics, national laboratories, nuclear secrecy, private industry, geology, environmental monitoring and commercial reality all collided. It can therefore serve as a control rather than an analogy. It does not prove that any modern allegation is true or false. It tells us what a real giant program looked like when it had to touch the world.

The evidence in this book is kept in four classes. **Documented facts** are supported by official records, declassified histories, technical reports, site records or peer-reviewed work. **Credible but incomplete evidence** includes contemporary technical interpretation, reporting or testimony whose provenance is serious but whose full denominator or underlying record is incomplete. **Disputed allegations** are claims that lack independent confirmation or whose evidentiary chain is contested. **Speculative synthesis** is mine: models and deductions offered because they may be useful, not because the archive has certified them.

A patent proves that somebody filed a patent. A proposal proves that somebody proposed something. A program name proves almost nothing about achieved capability. An underground detonation proves an underground detonation. Production data, radionuclide measurements and downstream use answer different questions. Those distinctions matter here because Plowshare's history is full of the seductive slide from *possible* to *demonstrated* to *practical*.

There is also a moral hazard in the phrase *peaceful nuclear explosion*. I will not use it as an absolution. It was a programmatic and diplomatic category, and sometimes an engineering ambition. Whether a particular use was safe, economical, politically legitimate or environmentally acceptable is a separate question. The word peaceful cannot do that work.

The most important sources are public. Department of Energy and predecessor records, Office of Legacy Management site files, OSTI technical literature, contemporary scientific papers, regulatory and monitoring records, and archival material make it possible to reconstruct much of the physical story without pretending to possess classified knowledge. Where records remain missing or contradictory, the absence stays visible.

The central wager is simple. If a technology is claimed to do something large in the physical world, ask where the product goes next.
