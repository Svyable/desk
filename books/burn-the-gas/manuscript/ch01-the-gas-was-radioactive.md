# The Gas Was Radioactive

There is a particular kind of optimism that only appears when a technology has already done the impossible once.

By 1967 the impossible thing was old news. Nuclear explosives had ended cities, vaporized towers, dug craters, shaken islands, illuminated the upper atmosphere and become a unit of geopolitical grammar. The United States had laboratories full of people who knew how to make them smaller, cleaner, more predictable, more specialized. Once an object becomes an engineering object, engineers begin asking what else it can do.

One answer was natural gas.

On December 10, 1967, beneath northwestern New Mexico, the United States detonated a nuclear device in a gas-bearing formation. Project Gasbuggy was part of the Atomic Energy Commission's Plowshare program, the effort to find civilian uses for nuclear explosives. The device was buried 4,227 feet below the surface. The purpose was brutally practical: fracture rock, enlarge the effective wellbore, release gas that conventional production could not recover economically.

That sentence deserves to sit there without assistance.

They nuclear-fracked a gas reservoir.

Not in a speculative memo. Not in a futurist's drawing. Not in a patent whose claims were never reduced to practice. The hole was drilled. The explosive went down it. The device fired. A cavity formed. Rock fractured. Gas was later produced from the altered formation.

This is the point where a modern story about secret technology would usually become less useful. The explosion would become the climax. A photograph of the site, a yield, a code name, perhaps a formerly classified document: proof that the government had done something astonishing. Then the story would drift outward into what else it *might* have done.

Gasbuggy gets better when you refuse to leave the well.

Because the gas had to come back up.

The Department of Energy's later legacy documentation records radionuclides in gas samples after the test, including tritium, krypton-85, iodine-131 and argon-37. The long-lived species mattered most for what came next. Gas directly affected by the detonation included tritiated methane. During post-detonation production testing, affected gas was flared rather than entering ordinary commerce.

This was not a failure of the bomb to explode. It was not a failure of the rock to fracture. It was a collision between two definitions of success that had been allowed to occupy the same project.

The first definition belonged to the experiment: can a nuclear explosive stimulate a low-productivity gas reservoir?

The second belonged to civilization: can you sell the gas?

Those are not the same question.

A technology can work perfectly at the point where its inventors are staring and fail at the first interface they treated as somebody else's problem. Gasbuggy's great gift to the history of technology is that the interface was literal. There was a well. Material crossed it. On one side sat a nuclear cavity engineered by a national-security state. On the other sat pipelines, customers, regulators, markets, kitchens, furnaces and lungs.

The product had to cross the boundary.

Plowshare had been born from a larger dream. If nuclear explosives contained stupendous amounts of energy, perhaps treating them exclusively as weapons was itself unimaginative. The Atomic Energy Commission established the program in 1957. The menu grew quickly: excavate canals and harbors, create reservoirs, recover minerals, make underground storage cavities, stimulate oil and gas, perform scientific experiments. The ambition now reads like an alternate twentieth century whose civil engineering department was staffed by Edward Teller.

That is easy to mock from a safe historical distance. Mockery is cheap because it knows the ending.

The harder task is to recover why the proposition had engineering appeal. Chemical explosives already moved earth. Drilling and conventional stimulation were expensive. Huge volumes of low-permeability rock contained hydrocarbons that existed geologically but not economically. A buried nuclear device could deliver an immense impulse at depth without lowering a comparable mass of chemical explosive into the formation. The cavity and fracture network were not imaginary. Underground nuclear explosions really did reorganize rock on a scale ordinary well treatments could not match.

If the story ended at the fracture map, Plowshare would look considerably smarter.

But engineering does not get to choose where reality stops.

The cavity has chemistry. The explosion creates radionuclides. Gas contacts the altered zone. Some products move. Some decay. Some bind. Some remain in place. Some enter produced gas. Then an engineering demonstration becomes a radiological assay problem, a processing problem, an exposure problem and a public-acceptance problem. The unit of success changes while the machine is running.

This is why Gasbuggy belongs in a book about hidden and extraordinary technology rather than merely in a history of nuclear eccentricity. It supplies something badly missing from most arguments about secret capability: a positive control.

Here is a genuinely extraordinary government technology program. It involved nuclear secrecy, national laboratories, specialized devices, private-sector interests and technically difficult field operations. Parts of its work were not public in real time. It nevertheless left a thick physical and institutional wake because the capability it sought was not complete until it interacted with the ordinary world.

The lesson is not the childish one that secrets cannot be kept. Secrets plainly can be kept. Device design can be classified. Exact capabilities can be compartmented. Personnel can hold clearances. Cover stories can work. Records can be destroyed, delayed or remain inaccessible for decades.

The more interesting question is: **what part of the claimed capability has to leave the secret?**

For Gasbuggy, it was the gas.

For a secret aircraft, it may be heat, noise, maintenance, fuel, basing, test range activity, supply chains and the observations of people who do not know what they are observing. For a recovered foreign machine, it may be transport, metrology, specialized tooling, waste, contractor interfaces and downstream technical consequences. For an alleged exotic propulsion system, it may be power, momentum exchange, thermal management or flight behavior. For a biological control technology, it may be the receiver, dose, calibration and measurable transfer function.

The object of interest is not leakage in the gossip sense. It is **interface leakage**: the unavoidable movement of matter, energy, information, labor, money, product or consequence across the boundary of the compartment.

That proposition can be wrong. A capability used rarely, at tiny scale, inside existing infrastructure may leave almost no distinctive public trace. A recovered gram of unusual material is not Project Gasbuggy. A one-off sensor prototype is not a national pipeline technology. Classification can hide purpose extremely well, and dual-use infrastructure can make the physical footprint ambiguous. The trace burden has to be normalized to the size and claimed output of the thing.

But scale cuts both ways. The more consequential the claim, the more interfaces it tends to acquire.

Gasbuggy was followed by Project Rulison in Colorado in 1969 and Project Rio Blanco in 1973. Rio Blanco used three nuclear explosives in a vertical sequence. This was not one bizarre afternoon followed by embarrassment. Nuclear gas stimulation survived long enough to become a series. Plowshare itself lasted roughly two decades and encompassed nuclear field experiments as well as a much larger ecology of proposed, planned and nonnuclear work.

The continuation matters. Governments do not need to believe a technology is commercially ready to keep funding it. Programs survive because the next experiment may solve the last experiment's problem, because sunk infrastructure makes continuation cheaper, because institutional constituencies form, because national competition changes the value assigned to technical knowledge, because a failure can still produce data, and because the people closest to a difficult problem often see solvable subproblems where outsiders see a verdict.

This is one reason the existence of a government program proves less than either enthusiasts or skeptics want it to prove.

Enthusiasts say: they funded it, therefore there must have been something there.

Skeptics say: it sounds ridiculous, therefore serious people could not really have pursued it.

Plowshare humiliates both instincts.

Serious people pursued things that now sound ridiculous. Some of the physics worked. Some of the engineering worked. The program generated real technical knowledge. That does not mean its commercial vision worked. Government interest is evidence of government interest. A field test is evidence of what that field test demonstrated. A successful detonation is not a successful industry.

The distinction becomes even more important in anomalous research, where the mere discovery that an intelligence agency, military service or national laboratory studied a subject is often treated as a hidden peer-review system. A declassified file stamped with an agency name acquires an aura that the experiment inside it may not deserve. Plowshare gives us the opposite discipline: even when the agency unquestionably possessed the relevant extraordinary capability—nuclear explosives—we still have to ask whether the proposed application survived contact with economics, contamination, law, public acceptance and competing technology.

The bomb was real. The business model could still fail.

That sentence is more useful than it looks.

Consider what had to be true for nuclear gas stimulation to become ordinary. The explosive had to be emplaced safely at the required depth. The geology had to respond with useful permeability. The stimulated volume had to produce enough additional gas. The gas had to be recoverable. Radiological contamination had to be low enough, decay fast enough, or treatment work well enough for the product to satisfy whatever technical and regulatory limits applied. Production could not create unacceptable pathways for subsurface migration. The total operation had to compete economically with other ways of getting gas. Customers had to accept the product. The legal and political environment had to tolerate repeated nuclear detonations under resource fields.

Every arrow in that chain is a place where an impressive demonstration can die.

And then technology changed around the program. This is another problem with secret-technology mythology: it often freezes the hidden invention while allowing ordinary technology to keep moving. Conventional drilling, stimulation and reservoir engineering do not stand politely still while an exotic approach matures. An extraordinary method has to outrun the improvement curve of the boring method it hopes to replace.

The modern hydraulic-fracturing industry is not evidence that Plowshare almost succeeded. It is evidence that the economic target was real: engineers really did need better ways to create permeability and release hydrocarbons from difficult formations. But the eventual industrial solution did not require detonating nuclear devices underground.

That is a much stranger and more useful history than a simple tale of mad scientists.

The dream was directionally correct about the resource and wrong about what the winning tool would be.

There is a temptation here to turn Gasbuggy into an environmental morality play, and the archive does not need my help becoming morally complicated. The Department of Energy still maintains legacy responsibilities at Plowshare sites. Rulison and Rio Blanco require monitoring of nearby natural-gas wells and groundwater; Gasbuggy has a long monitoring history. Those facts are part of the engineering story, not an epilogue added by politics. Long-term stewardship is one of the outputs of a technology whether or not it appeared in the original performance specification.

A machine's real boundary includes its waste.

A program's real duration includes its cleanup.

A product's real cost includes the years after the people who proposed it have retired.

This is where the phrase *peaceful nuclear explosion* becomes most revealing. The adjective tried to classify purpose. Physics classified consequences separately.

There is nothing inherently dishonest about distinguishing a nuclear explosive intended for excavation from one intended to destroy a city. Intent and design requirements matter. Arms-control regimes themselves had to wrestle with the category. But peacefulness was never a material property of the device. The rock did not know the mission statement. Tritium did not know whether it belonged to energy policy or war planning.

This book will follow that mismatch.

It will go into the cavity, then back up the well. It will follow Gasbuggy, Rulison and Rio Blanco rather than treating them as three code names on a list. It will spend time with the proposed harbors and canals because excavation makes the interface problem even harder: the product of the explosion is the landscape itself. It will look at Soviet peaceful nuclear explosions because convergent state interest is evidence worth taking seriously without pretending that two governments pursuing the same idea proves the idea was wise. It will look at contamination, monitoring and economics because those are not bureaucratic details attached to the technology. They are where the technology becomes true or false as a usable system.

And eventually it will return to the subjects that made this history newly interesting: claims of deeply classified aerospace, retrieval, propulsion and anomalous technologies whose alleged physical consequences are much larger than their public evidentiary footprint.

Plowshare cannot settle those claims. Using it as a universal debunking template would be as lazy as using its secrecy to validate them. Some hidden programs are smaller. Some outputs never enter commerce. Some technologies could exploit existing facilities. Some signatures are nonspecific. Some witnesses can be telling the truth about what they saw while being wrong about what it was. Some archives can remain incomplete forever.

What Plowshare gives us is a better question than *could they keep it secret?*

Ask instead what would have to cross the boundary if the claimed capability were real.

At Gasbuggy, after the flash had happened where nobody could see it, after the rock had broken and the cavity had cooled enough for the next phase, the experiment faced that question in the most literal form possible.

They opened the well.

The answer came out as gas.
