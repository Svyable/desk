An intelligence allegation reported by journalists is not a judicial finding. An acknowledged affair does not establish espionage; a business relationship does not establish wrongdoing; a corporate export-control risk does not establish a company's participation in a smuggling conspiracy. The pages that follow distinguish company statements, securities filings, court records, public reporting, and the author's own analysis. The research ledger provides the original links. Where a claim is unresolved, the uncertainty belongs to the story.

The word *lies* in the title invites scrutiny of representations: what was said in private, what was filed in public, and what customs officials were led to believe about shipments of restricted chips. It is not a blanket accusation that Jensen Huang committed espionage or an export-control offense.

This book begins while the investigation remains open. Some later chapter titles are working hypotheses awaiting reporting. The first chapters are drafted; the remaining chapters are intentionally marked unwritten in the book's contents.

The ordinary corporate announcement arrived with a date, a ticker symbol, and three bullet points.

On November 12, 2026, Nvidia told the market that Jensen Huang had resigned as chief executive, president, and director. Colette Kress, the company's chief financial officer, would run it temporarily. Harvey Jones would become its first chairman. The statement said nothing about a completed espionage inquiry, nothing about a criminal charge against Huang, and nothing to settle the questions that had consumed the company for days.

For a technology business built around an unusually recognizable founder, this was a strange compression of a human life. Thirty-three years of decisions, products, debts, engineering arguments, near failures, extraordinary luck, and strategic discipline were reduced to the short language of succession. The SEC filing was plainer still: on November 11, the board had accepted the resignation. Those are the words the public could verify.

Four days earlier Huang had written to Nvidia's employees. In the message, published by the company, he admitted he had been unfaithful to his wife and apologized. The board announced an independent inquiry into reporting that its chief executive had been the target of a foreign intelligence effort. It also said it had no reason to believe national security or company secrets had been compromised. Between an intimate admission and an unproven national-security allegation stood the most commercially important chip company of the AI boom.

The contradiction in the public story was immediate. The machinery that trained and served increasingly powerful artificial intelligence depended on a supply chain spanning borders that governments now treated as security frontiers. The man who helped make those chips indispensable had become a subject of debate among intelligence officials, lawmakers, investors, employees, and family members. Every constituency wanted a different answer.

The trouble with a spy story is that almost every available detail seems to demand a verdict. A photograph becomes proof of access. A private relationship becomes evidence of intent. A meeting becomes a transfer. A shipment of processors becomes a covert operation. A resignation becomes an admission of whatever the observer most feared. It is possible to build a magnificent, persuasive, completely wrong story by making those substitutions one after another.

A more difficult book must live with distinctions. A board can be right to investigate and wrong to imply that harm occurred. Reporters can accurately describe what unnamed officials believe without independently proving the underlying intelligence. U.S. authorities can establish a separate chip-smuggling scheme without showing that Nvidia's founder directed it. A company can legally lobby for access to the Chinese market while its government decides that some products are too strategically sensitive to sell there.

The biography cannot be detached from the industry either. The tale of the solitary genius in a black leather jacket leaves out the programmers, researchers, manufacturing partners, customers, standards, memories, power systems, and thousands of compromises that made Nvidia valuable. Nor can the industry be detached from geography. Chips designed in California are manufactured and packaged through an international network, frequently involving Taiwan, and sold into a world where the difference between a customer and an adversary depends on which authority is drawing the line.

The most useful thing about this week in November may turn out to be that it finally made an old governance problem impossible to hide: what happens when one person's judgment becomes part of a nation's computing infrastructure?

That question outlives the affair, the inquiry, and the next earnings call. It is where the book will go.
