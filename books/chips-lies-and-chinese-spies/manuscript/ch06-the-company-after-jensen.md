# The Company After Jensen

The board's first succession announcement did not promise a reinvention. It promised continuity.

Colette Kress was already among Nvidia's most important public executives, the voice who regularly translated engineering ambition into revenue, gross margin, spending, inventory, and guidance. She had joined in 2013, long before the company became the dominant supplier for the generative-AI buildout. Nvidia now asked her to serve simultaneously as interim chief executive and chief financial officer while directors looked for a permanent leader. It was the practical answer to an emergency. A stable finance chief could preserve the reporting process, the customer contracts, and the operating rhythms while the company decided how to replace the founder.

The assignment was unusually difficult because Jensen Huang's leadership was never confined to a normal CEO portfolio. He had been a technical salesman able to converse with engineers and politicians, a product strategist who could coordinate multiple architecture generations, and the person outsiders most closely associated with the proposition that accelerated computing would become the operating system of advanced AI. His personal reputation helped recruit people and persuade customers to make extremely expensive commitments to Nvidia's platform.

Any interim leader confronting that inheritance has two tasks that can conflict. She must convince customers that existing promises remain valid. At the same time, she must demonstrate that the organization can challenge decisions without waiting for the founder's approval. The very qualities that made Nvidia coherent under Huang could become obstacles to independent judgment if employees were reluctant to question ideas associated with his success.

Reuters reported that Kress had sometimes challenged Huang over large financing commitments in the AI infrastructure ecosystem. That description is based on people familiar with the company, not a transcript of boardroom meetings. It nevertheless points to an important business problem. Nvidia earns money selling scarce compute; some of its customers and partners need enormous amounts of capital to purchase that compute. Vendor investments, customer financing, and shared infrastructure bets can help accelerate adoption. They can also create complicated economic exposure when a supplier has an interest in the financial health of its buyers.

A customer ordering a large installation may be backed by a financing arrangement that involves another part of the same AI ecosystem. The headline sales figure does not tell a reader whether the buyer's long-run revenue supports the debt and commitments used to build the capacity. This does not mean every investment is circular or improper. It means the industry must distinguish genuine downstream demand from the amount of spending made possible by investors and vendors subsidizing the buildout. Nvidia's filings and contracts, rather than the spectacle of the founder's downfall, are where that issue can be tested.

The geopolitical problems did not disappear either. The H200 licensing regime still required both American permission and Chinese acceptance. Export enforcement still needed to account for resellers and intermediaries. Taiwan remained central to high-end chip manufacturing and packaging. Competing accelerator vendors kept designing alternatives, and Chinese firms had strategic reasons to reduce dependence on American suppliers. Every delay at Nvidia created space for another ecosystem to grow. Every hurried attempt to maintain market dominance carried costs in engineering, capital, or public trust.

The governance review presented an opportunity that few healthy founder-led companies willingly take. Directors could require clearer escalation routes for conflict-of-interest concerns, stronger documentation of sensitive customer relationships, and independent review of compliance exceptions. They could also reconsider how frequently strategy and external influence depended on personal diplomacy. A policy written after a scandal is easy; a system that changes whose judgment carries weight is harder. The latter would be measurable in decisions: who can stop a deal, who verifies an end user, who challenges a forecast, and how dissent reaches directors.

There is a risk of using the affair as an explanation for every business choice Nvidia made. That would let critics replace the hard work of evaluating products and markets with a moral verdict on their inventor. A flawed executive can make a correct strategic argument. A company with an exemplary compliance program can still have a dishonest counterparty. An intelligence warning can be important even if no secret is proven stolen. It is possible for all these facts to coexist, a possibility public debate often finds unsatisfying.

By mid-November 2026 Nvidia had announced a leadership change and an outside investigation. The next earnings call, scheduled for November 17, would test its ability to communicate as an institution rather than as the extension of a single personality. Whatever the numbers showed, a 33-year organizational arrangement had ended. The board could choose another charismatic architect or a more distributed management structure. It could try to preserve Huang's long-term engineering culture while imposing procedures that made it less dependent on any one person's judgment.

The most compelling ending to this story would be an easy one: a rogue spy exposed, a genius fallen, a successor restoring order. It is also the ending that public evidence is least entitled to deliver. The actual future will be decided in product schedules, contracts, board minutes, export licenses, completed investigations, and the choices of the people who remain. The next release of a chip may tell us more about Nvidia's institutional strength than another week of speculation about its founder.

The company still had to make the chips.
