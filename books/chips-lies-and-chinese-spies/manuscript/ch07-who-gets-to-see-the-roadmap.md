# Who Gets to See the Roadmap?

On November 17, 2026, Nvidia announced a new committee of its board. The name ran to ten words: National Security and Export Control Oversight Committee. There were three directors on it, one former Goldman Sachs vice chair to lead it, and a promise to meet at least once a quarter. This was an unusually bureaucratic answer from a company that had made a global event out of one man appearing onstage with a chip.

The appointment of Suzanne Nora Johnson as chair and Stephen Neal and John Dabiri as members was not an announcement that espionage had occurred. It was a decision about who would be entitled to information and which employees could bring that information to the directors. Nvidia said its existing National Security Team and Global Trade Compliance Team would report directly to the new committee and also to interim chief executive Colette Kress on day-to-day operations. The committee could retain outside specialists in national security, law, and trade compliance. That arrangement sounded administrative. Its consequences, if the directors made use of the new authority, could be profound.

A board can possess extensive legal powers and still struggle to ask the right question. Companies are organized around what they know how to measure. A seller of advanced processors can count orders, inventory, customers, manufacturing yields, product delays, and revenue by market. It can produce meticulous reports on factories it does not own and networking equipment it manufactures with partners. None of those measurements automatically tells directors whether a customer’s ultimate users were represented accurately, whether an executive withheld a relationship from the company's lawyers, or whether a seemingly routine conversation gave an outsider insight into a confidential product plan.

The old arrangement was available for anyone with the patience to read Nvidia’s [May 2026 proxy statement](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581026000036/nvda-20260512.htm). The board then described three standing committees: Audit, Compensation, and Nominating and Corporate Governance. Audit monitored financial and information-security concerns. Nominating and Corporate Governance covered trade compliance, governmental relations, regulatory matters, complaints about the code of conduct, and the effectiveness of the company's compliance program. It had met three times during fiscal 2026, according to the proxy. The directors had not left trade compliance entirely unattended.

Another sentence in that filing became important after the scandal. Nvidia said it did not have a chief compliance officer. In the same passage, the company described its enterprise risk-management system: the internal audit team administered the program, senior leaders reviewed and managed risks in their areas, and the board received reports through its committee structure. The missing title therefore does not establish the absence of compliance professionals, much less deliberate neglect. It identifies a management design in which responsibility for compliance was distributed rather than concentrated in a C-suite role. The usefulness of that design depends on what it can escalate when the person affected is the person at the top.

The old proxy also recorded a thoughtful detail. After board evaluations in fiscal 2026, directors had chosen to focus on geopolitical and regulatory risks, government affairs, supply chains, cybersecurity, the strategic roadmap, and management development. These were precisely the categories that would become publicly entangled in November. A critic could read the list as evidence that the board saw the danger and failed to act. The document alone cannot support that judgment. It tells us what subjects directors said they intended to examine; it does not reveal which warnings they received, which inquiries they pursued, or what satisfactory answers would have looked like before the latest revelations.

The more useful clue lay in the proxy’s description of the lead independent director. Stephen Neal was empowered to work on board agendas and to advise the CEO about the quality, quantity, and timeliness of information directors received. Management prepared material for board meetings, but the lead director could ask for information to be included. This is a sober description of the oldest vulnerability in oversight: the same institution responsible for running a business is often the institution responsible for explaining to its supervisors what has gone wrong.

Directors can make rules requiring candor. Whether they receive it is a different matter. A chief executive may regard a customer relationship as commercially sensitive, an embarrassing personal matter as irrelevant, a security warning as speculative, or a compliance inquiry as something already resolved by subordinates. Any of those judgments might be made in good faith. The power of a reporting chain lies in allowing someone else to test them without needing the chief executive’s permission. The new committee was designed to create such a route for national-security and trade questions. Its existence does not show that earlier channels were broken. The records needed to answer that question were not public.

The next day-to-day problem was not confined to boardrooms. On November 17, the Justice and Commerce departments made public a chronology of their investigation into Megaspeed, the Singapore-based computing company that had become inseparable in press coverage from the story of Jensen Huang's departure. The [joint statement](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/joint-statement-department-commerce-and-department-justice-megaspeed-investigation) said Commerce began investigating the company in 2024 after monitoring detected activity it considered suspicious for possible semiconductor diversion. Investigators requested records from Nvidia in May 2025 concerning direct or indirect sales to Megaspeed. Nvidia placed the customer on a no-sell list in June 2025. Also that June, Commerce agents and Singaporean law enforcement conducted an on-site end-use inspection and interviewed Alice Huang, then Megaspeed's chief executive. Justice subsequently took the lead in a criminal investigation.

This timeline changed the shape of the reporting. It placed an export-control inquiry and a customer restriction more than a year before the public crisis of November 2026. It also showed that the government had sought relevant sales records before the personal allegations took over the headlines. A reader previously left to decide whether officials were reacting to celebrity gossip now had evidence of a more durable commercial investigation. It did not, however, prove that an intelligence agency had directed Alice Huang or that any secret passed from Nvidia to anyone else.

An official announcement of an investigation proves that the government says it investigated. The statement did not describe a filed charge against Megaspeed or identify a conviction. Its language about bringing the law to bear on criminal conduct *if* such conduct occurred preserved that uncertainty. Megaspeed denied wrongdoing and, in a separate November 15 statement reported by the Associated Press and other outlets, said Alice Huang had left the firm effective August 30, 2025. The departure date does not contradict the government's account that she was CEO during the June visit. It does narrow the period during which she could have held the executive authority the company now says ended in August. Any claim about her later role requires evidence beyond the fact of previous employment.

The 2025 no-sell decision is where the board inquiry becomes most concrete. What generated the action? Did Commerce’s request trigger it, or did Nvidia's internal checks reach an independent judgment? How was the restriction communicated to sales teams, distributors, manufacturing partners, and executives? Which entities and beneficial owners did the no-sell status cover? Were all transactions halted as planned, including indirect arrangements through resellers? What records were retained? Each question can be asked without assuming that the company made an illicit sale. Each can, in principle, be answered by contemporary documents rather than by competing memories years later.

A customer ban is useful only if the vendor can recognize the customer on the other side of the transaction. The first purchase order may give a formal company name, legal address, credit terms, and receiving location. The ultimate user may be an affiliate, a cloud customer, a contractor, or another entity renting computing capacity after delivery. A reseller may honestly believe it has a permitted buyer while someone farther downstream is hiding a prohibited destination. Screening against one set of names can miss a reorganized ownership structure. The hard part is deciding what diligence is proportionate, who is authorized to approve an exception, and what evidence should cause the transaction to stop.

For a chipmaker, that is primarily a problem about where the hardware goes. But by the time the shipment is delivered, the valuable object has entered a second kind of market. A server in Singapore can perform calculations for a customer in another country. The customer does not need to possess the GPU, know the building where it sits, or import the processor through its own customs office. It can rent computing power remotely. Whether a particular transaction is lawful depends on the controlling regulations, the equipment, the people involved, and the end use. The location on a shipping label is insufficient to describe the true user.

Commerce had been working on this gap before the scandal reached its peak. On November 16, the Bureau of Industry and Security released an [interim final rule titled *Addressing Remote Access to Advanced Semiconductors*](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2026-23529.pdf). The rule was scheduled for Federal Register publication on November 19, and the public-inspection text specified the publication date as its effective date. Its principal new provisions addressed exports, reexports, and transfers of advanced computing items intended for data centers that would provide substantial remote computing power to companies headquartered in specified countries of concern, including China. The rule also added targeted license requirements for certain advanced-semiconductor shipments to Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, and Brazil, with exceptions and reporting conditions for eligible U.S.-headquartered recipients.

The rule used a technical threshold: access exceeding twelve million units of aggregate total processing performance, or TPP, for covered arrangements. That number matters because a cloud service can divide equipment among customers and sites, changing how raw chip counts translate into useful capacity. The regulation contemplated linked data centers, ownership of ultimate customers, and uses provided indirectly for another entity. It also contained a narrow commercial-recommender-systems exclusion. None of this made every overseas cloud contract illegal. It set out conditions under which a license would be required. And its reach was not identical to a general prohibition on all cloud services provided from within the United States.

A government that regulates chips by their destination discovers that a chip can remain still while its computing power crosses a border. That sounds like a lawyer's abstraction until the practical problem comes into view: a person prohibited from importing a powerful processor may be able to rent access to an overseas server built with that processor. The goods do not move, but the capability does. The effectiveness of controls then depends on customer identity, what is actually being computed, how facilities link together, and how much a provider can know about requests originating far from the hardware.

The November rule is an independent policy response to a longstanding concern, not proof that Megaspeed violated it or that Nvidia knew of prohibited remote access. Its public language identified a class of risk. A careful investigation would compare the dates and the exact operative provisions with any transaction alleged to be unlawful. Applying a newly announced rule backward to a sale made months earlier would confuse changes in public policy with proof of historical criminal conduct. A firm can be under active investigation and still be entitled to have its actions judged under the law and facts that apply to them.

The third category of access was harder to place in a legal schedule. Even if Nvidia's chips never reached a prohibited user, a person with a personal or commercial relationship to its CEO could learn things that were not on a public specification sheet. A chip roadmap is partly a promise about future engineering. It is also a negotiation with foundries, memory suppliers, system makers, cloud providers, software developers, and capital providers. A delay in packaging capacity may alter the date customers can install an architecture. A change in interconnect design may affect what a rival chooses to build. A planned export-control response can alter which processor a supplier prioritizes and what a government expects from licensing.

These are examples of information that *could* be commercially sensitive, not statements that Jensen Huang shared any particular secret. His job made him privy to much of Nvidia's strategy. That is true of many founders and chief executives. The reporting available through November 18 did not provide a public, independently verified list of documents or conversations showing that Alice Huang obtained proprietary technical plans from him. Nor did it show a causal line from an alleged personal relationship to a product alteration or a government licensing decision. Nvidia's board initially said it had no reason to believe company secrets or national security had been compromised. That assertion is the board's stated assessment, not a completed forensic report. It must be preserved beside any suspicion about opportunity.

The distinction between the opportunity to obtain information and the actual obtaining of it is particularly vulnerable to narrative abuse. If a person met a founder in Taipei, perhaps the relationship could have created private access. If she was involved in buying compute, perhaps she understood commercially useful details about supply. If intelligence officials suspected her of acting for Beijing, perhaps some of those details might have interested a state agency. Three propositions marked *perhaps* cannot be silently multiplied into the conclusion that espionage occurred. Readers deserve stronger evidence than the connective tissue of a plausible thriller.

There is another way for influence to work, less cinematic and harder to police. Huang had publicly advocated retaining meaningful commercial access to the Chinese AI market. Nvidia's argument was that American chips and software could remain the preferred platform for Chinese developers, sustaining U.S. economic leadership and limiting the rise of incompatible domestic competitors. Washington's counterargument was that some forms of computing power could accelerate a strategic rival's capabilities regardless of which platform those users preferred. Both arguments have intelligible economic and security foundations. A Chinese official could agree with Nvidia's policy position without having planted it. Suspicion about the source of an argument cannot replace an evaluation of the argument itself.

A confidential conversation with an executive could theoretically shape market intelligence, expectations about regulatory timing, or lobbying strategy. But proof of improper influence would require a factual chain: a request or instruction, knowledge of the request, a response or decision, and a reason to conclude the action was taken for that purpose. An ordinary relationship, a dinner photograph, a personal confession, and even a reported intelligence assessment do not establish those steps. This book's unresolved question is what additional evidence the independent inquiry or future reporting can actually produce.

Nvidia was simultaneously proving how little the markets had slowed down. On the afternoon of November 17, while the new oversight committee was being announced and the government chronology was circulating, the company released results for a quarter that had ended October 25. Revenue was $115.8 billion, a 103 percent increase from a year earlier. The data-center segment alone produced $107.6 billion. Gross margin under generally accepted accounting principles was 74.8 percent. These numbers described a business of extraordinary scale at a moment when its founder was no longer running it.

The release was careful about the future. Nvidia projected fourth-quarter revenue of approximately $130 billion, with a three percent range on either side, and said the outlook did not assume data-center computing revenue from China. These were management expectations as of the release, not guarantees. The omission of Chinese compute revenue was also not evidence that every Chinese customer had been cut off or that the country had stopped using Nvidia chips. It was a forecast assumption from a company facing two governments' trade restrictions and a commercial market that could change quickly.

Harvey Jones opened the investor call, according to Reuters, by ruling out a return of Huang as chief executive and saying management would not take questions about the departure. That was an understandable attempt to contain the personnel story and deliver the financial facts investors had come to hear. It also showed the limits of an earnings call as an accountability mechanism. An investor may hear a detailed revenue forecast without learning how a prohibited customer was flagged or how a private conflict was evaluated. Quarterly financial disclosure answers one category of question well and other categories poorly. A new board committee was supposed to improve a different channel of information.

The succession did not erase the past. The May 2026 proxy had described a system in which information-security matters came to the audit committee, trade-compliance and government relations came to the nominating committee, and major risks could be elevated to the full board. The November standing committee provided a more specialized path. Whether that specialization yields better oversight will depend on matters no press release can prove: whether compliance staff may escalate a concern without retaliation, whether exceptions are documented, whether the committee receives the underlying contracts rather than only summaries, and whether directors are willing to accept commercial costs when a transaction does not pass scrutiny.

Nor should independent counsel conducting the separate investigation be confused with this standing oversight committee. One effort is examining events and conduct associated with the present crisis; the other is supposed to oversee policies and risks on an ongoing basis. A retrospective inquiry can establish what happened, assuming adequate evidence and cooperation. A standing committee can attempt to change how future decisions are taken. Both can fail. Both can be useful. They are different instruments with different standards of success.

On November 18, the House Energy and Commerce Committee chair, Brett Guthrie, sent letters to Nvidia and the Commerce Department requesting briefings by December 2. His office said the committee wanted information about export-law compliance, security controls, and the handling of advanced chip transactions. The letters were oversight requests, not proof that the committee had established violations. They moved the question of Nvidia's internal controls into another institution's calendar. Directors could no longer assume that explaining themselves to shareholders would answer every part of the public inquiry.

It is easy to define a corporate control by the page where it is written. The actual unit of protection is a decision made when the pressure runs the other way. A buyer wants a faster shipment. A favored partner claims that a license is unnecessary. A salesperson fears losing the account. A senior executive has useful information and an interest in keeping a relationship private. A staff lawyer needs a document or an answer that a colleague does not want to provide. Which of those people can insist on a stop, and will the institution back that decision when the price arrives?

The Megaspeed chronology gives the investigators one specific decision to examine: Nvidia's June 2025 decision to stop sales to that customer. The company appears in the new federal statement as a supplier that, after inquiries, took a restriction action. The public record does not yet answer how comprehensive the restriction was, whether all relevant entities were caught, or what the company knew about the possible risks before Commerce sought the records. Those unknowns do not justify inventing a finding of failure. They give the new committee and outside investigators something measurable to investigate.

A roadmap is valuable because it says what the company hopes will become possible. A compliance system is valuable because it can tell a company what it must refuse while pursuing that possibility. After November 17, Nvidia had placed those functions under more explicitly separate oversight. Its shareholders could read a revenue figure that would have seemed fantastic a few years earlier and a government statement about a no-sell list that had existed for more than a year. What connected them was a simple institutional problem: whether the people with the authority to protect the business could get the information they needed, even when that information was inconvenient to the people building it.
