# The Graphics Card That Ate the World

Jensen Huang's biography makes little sense as a tale of overnight success. The overnight success took three decades.

He was born in Taiwan in 1963 and came to the United States as a child. Biographical profiles recount the years he and his brother spent away from their parents, including a difficult period in Kentucky, before the family reunited in Oregon. Huang studied electrical engineering at Oregon State University and later earned a master's degree at Stanford. He worked at AMD and LSI Logic before starting Nvidia in 1993 with Chris Malachowsky and Curtis Priem. The company began with computer graphics, a market that was exciting enough to attract talent and uncertain enough to ruin companies that placed the wrong architectural bet.

Those names matter. A story that assigns the whole invention of Nvidia to Huang cannot explain why the engineering worked. Malachowsky and Priem were technical cofounders. Game developers and PC manufacturers helped create demand. Semiconductor manufacturing partners made the chips real. Microsoft, graphics standards, and later the extraordinary growth of the internet shaped the market. Huang's contribution was to persist through shifting markets and build an institution able to absorb the next shift before customers could see it clearly.

Nvidia's 1999 GeForce 256 became part of the firm's origin mythology as the first GPU, or graphics processing unit. The marketing label is less important than the technical direction. Graphics are full of operations that can be performed on many pixels, vertices, and fragments simultaneously. General-purpose CPUs are built for a wider variety of sequential and branching tasks. Graphics processors were designed to move a huge volume of similar calculations through parallel hardware. The specialization that made games look better would eventually make many kinds of scientific and machine-learning calculation much faster.

There was no guarantee that the second market would emerge. In the mid-2000s Nvidia made a software bet that appeared to many observers to be outside the profitable center of gaming graphics. CUDA, introduced in 2006, offered programmers a way to use GPU hardware for general-purpose computing. The name stood for Compute Unified Device Architecture; the commercial idea was harder than the acronym. A developer considering a new machine needs assurance that software will run, tools will improve, and a community will exist years later. Hardware alone cannot provide that confidence. Nvidia invested in compilers, libraries, developer relations, training, and an ecosystem of code that made its chips more useful every time another programmer chose the platform.

The first people using GPUs for scientific work did not all imagine that their choices would establish the default infrastructure for a global AI industry. Research in computer vision, simulation, physics, and statistical modeling exposed what parallel computation could accomplish. In 2012, Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever, and Geoffrey Hinton's neural-network entry in the ImageNet competition showed how powerful models trained on GPUs could outperform the prevailing image-classification approaches. That result did not create artificial intelligence, nor did it mean Nvidia alone had invented modern deep learning. It helped prove that the combination of algorithms, data, and massive parallel computation could produce practical jumps in capability.

From there, demand became circular in a productive way. Researchers needed GPUs to train bigger models. Better software encouraged researchers to use Nvidia hardware. More developers attracted customers and investment. Those customers gave Nvidia revenue and feedback to design better chips. Data-center networking, software libraries, and eventually large integrated systems reinforced the same platform. A competitor could design a fast processor and still face the formidable task of persuading institutions to move the code, people, operating practices, and support contracts around it.

The durable business advantage was an accumulation of compatibility. It is easy to admire a chip benchmark and miss the cost of changing the surrounding computer. A research lab may have model code written for CUDA, engineers who know Nvidia's profiling tools, an operations team tuned to the company's networking and memory systems, and purchases committed several quarters ahead. Switching has a price that is only partly visible in the chip invoice. Nvidia's software and systems investments helped make its hardware the default choice for a generation of AI practitioners.

There was an equally important omission in the celebrity story. Nvidia is a fabless company: it designs chips and systems but relies on foundries and manufacturing partners to make much of the physical hardware. Taiwanese semiconductor production, advanced packaging, high-bandwidth memory suppliers, specialized machines, and global logistics all sit inside the commercial achievement. A product designed in California can depend on a manufacturing sequence that crosses multiple borders before it reaches a U.S. data center. Huang's identity as a Taiwanese-born American executive carried unusual resonance, but the geography of the chip was not biography. It was the result of decades of industrial specialization.

The more valuable Nvidia became, the more unsettling that geography appeared to governments. The same accelerator that can train a medical model or improve weather prediction can also support intelligence analysis, military research, and cyber operations. These uses do not prove the motives of any particular buyer. They explain why the United States began making decisions about advanced processors that usually belong to weapons-control bureaucracies. Export restrictions follow computational performance and other technical thresholds. Product teams respond to those thresholds. Customers adjust purchases. Rivals redesign around the openings.

The founder's power grew alongside the complexity. Nvidia was no longer selling a component to a gaming enthusiast; it was negotiating compute capacity with the largest cloud providers and some of the world's most ambitious AI laboratories. Announcements became huge ecosystem events. Governments wanted factories and investment. Investors wanted to know whether the boom in large models would continue. Young companies wanted GPUs and enough capital to pay for them. Huang's command of the technical narrative could make these interdependencies sound like one straightforward revolution.

Yet the revenue followed products that were expensive to design, scarce to manufacture, and costly to integrate. Fast design cycles brought costs, too. Networking gear, memory, advanced packaging, power, cooling, and qualified manufacturing capacity all affected what Nvidia could ship. An engineering team can design a more capable accelerator and still see delivery schedules depend on a part or process it does not control. That structural fact becomes politically explosive when a government tries to redirect who can buy the accelerator.

Nvidia's own fiscal 2026 annual report describes the financial stakes with unusual clarity. Export-license requirements for its H20 product led to a $4.5 billion charge for excess inventory and purchase obligations in the first quarter of fiscal 2026. The company later reported that licensed sales to China did not resolve the problem as anticipated. It could invent products suited to restrictions, but could not decide the rules of the market into which it was trying to sell.

One reasonable defense of Huang's commercial position is that maintaining American software and hardware in Chinese data centers could preserve U.S. technological influence. If developers in China continue to build on Nvidia systems, Nvidia earns revenue to fund new research and keeps users in an American-centered platform. The competing argument is not imaginary: advanced computing can confer national-security benefits, and sales to companies in a strategic rival's ecosystem may impose costs that are difficult to recover through future restrictions. Both arguments deserve quantitative tests that are more serious than patriotic slogans.

For thirty-three years, Huang had been exceptionally good at selecting one future and organizing a company around it. His departure forced Nvidia's board to find a successor capable of managing a different task: preserving that future without allowing the company to depend on one irreplaceable individual. The enormous value of the enterprise was evidence of Huang's achievements. The difficulty of imagining it without him was evidence of a risk that success had obscured.
