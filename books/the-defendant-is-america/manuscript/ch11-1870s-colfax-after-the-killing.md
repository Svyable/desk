# Chapter 11 — 1870s: Colfax After the Killing

The courthouse at Colfax, Louisiana, was supposed to represent government.

In April 1873 it became the center of a massacre.

The Civil War had ended eight years earlier. Slavery was abolished. The Fourteenth Amendment had made national citizenship and equal protection part of the Constitution. The Fifteenth Amendment prohibited denying the vote on account of race. Congress had enacted Enforcement Acts designed to give the federal government tools against conspiracies that used violence to destroy Black political participation.

On paper, Reconstruction had built a new constitutional order.

In Grant Parish, white paramilitary power tested whether the paper had force.

A disputed local election produced competing claims to office. Black Republicans gathered at the parish courthouse in Colfax to defend the government they recognized. Armed white men attacked. The exact death toll remains uncertain, which is common in episodes where the victims' bodies were dispersed, records were poor, and the killers controlled much of the local narrative. Federal historical accounts describe more than one hundred Black men killed. Many died after surrender or after organized resistance had effectively ended.

The event was not a battle between equivalent political armies.

It became racial slaughter.

The state failed to deliver proportionate justice. The federal government prosecuted participants under Reconstruction enforcement law. That choice created one of the decisive trials of the era.

Ninety-seven men were indicted in the broader federal effort. Only a handful were brought through trial, and three were convicted on selected counts under the Enforcement Act. Even that limited result mattered. Federal prosecutors had used national law against private actors accused of terrorizing Black citizens and destroying federally protected political rights.

Then the Supreme Court dismantled the convictions.

*United States v. Cruikshank*, decided in 1876, is often taught as constitutional doctrine about state action, federalism, the First and Second Amendments, and the reach of Reconstruction enforcement power. To understand its importance, begin with the bodies at Colfax.

Doctrine answers a different question when you remember what conduct the doctrine is being asked to reach.

The legal issue was not whether the Constitution contained noble language about citizenship. It was whether the national government could punish private conspirators after local racial violence made those rights dangerous to exercise.

That is the trial of the 1870s because Reconstruction lived or died in the distance between those two propositions.

The federal Constitution had been transformed after the Civil War. Before the Fourteenth Amendment, many constitutional protections limited the federal government more directly than the states. Reconstruction constitutionalism attempted to alter the relationship between national power, state power, and individual rights. Black citizenship could not depend entirely on former Confederate states voluntarily protecting it.

Congress knew why.

The Ku Klux Klan and related white supremacist organizations used murder, assault, intimidation, economic pressure, and political terror to suppress Black voting and Republican government. The violence was not random crime in the ordinary sense. It was a method of constitutional counterrevolution.

A ballot is meaningless if casting it can get you killed and the local sheriff belongs to the side threatening you.

Federal enforcement therefore targeted private conspiracies because state failure and private violence were intertwined.

The Colfax prosecutions put that architecture before federal courts.

The defendants argued, among other things, that the indictments did not properly allege offenses within federal constitutional power. The Supreme Court agreed that the counts were defective and construed the Reconstruction Amendments and federal rights in ways that sharply limited the government's reach.

The Court emphasized that the Fourteenth Amendment prohibited state action rather than creating a general federal police power over private violence. It also treated certain rights invoked by prosecutors as rights not created by the federal Constitution in the way the indictments assumed.

As doctrine, the distinctions had antecedents and structural logic.

As history, the consequences were devastating.

A constitutional system can be formally correct about institutional boundaries and practically indifferent to who controls the boundary.

If a state genuinely protects citizens from private violence, requiring state action before federal intervention can preserve federalism without destroying liberty.

If state officials are unwilling or unable to protect Black citizens from organized racial terror, the same rule can create a jurisdictional sanctuary for violence.

The legal question “who has authority?” becomes inseparable from “who is doing nothing?”

That is why federalism cannot be evaluated without context.

The structure distributes power.

The distribution can protect liberty by preventing national domination.

It can also protect local domination by preventing national correction.

Reconstruction is the period in which both possibilities become impossible to ignore.

The Colfax trial and *Cruikshank* decision arrived as northern political commitment to Reconstruction was weakening. Economic crisis, racial prejudice, political fatigue, contested elections, and organized southern resistance were changing the national environment. Federal troops and prosecutors could not permanently substitute for local institutions without sustained political will.

Judicial narrowing therefore landed inside a broader retreat.

This is important because court decisions do not operate in isolation. The same doctrinal rule can have different effects depending on the capacity and motives of other institutions.

A narrow federal rule in a state with robust equal law enforcement may be tolerable.

A narrow federal rule in a parish dominated by people who participated in or sympathized with racial terror can be a death sentence for practical rights.

The law on the page is identical.

The institutional ecology changes its meaning.

The Colfax story also demonstrates why massacres create difficult prosecutions even when everyone knows a crime occurred.

Collective violence scrambles individual responsibility.

Dozens or hundreds of people may participate. Some plan. Some shoot. Some guard. Some pursue. Some arrive late. Witnesses may know the group but not the individual who fired a fatal shot. Victims may be dead. Local juries may be hostile. State prosecutors may decline to act.

Federal conspiracy law offers one answer because it can reach coordinated deprivation of rights without requiring proof that each defendant personally caused each death.

But broad conspiracy law also raises the danger of guilt by association.

The legal system has to distinguish organized terror from mere membership or presence.

The Colfax prosecution was attempting that difficult work under Reconstruction statutes still being interpreted.

The Supreme Court's response focused heavily on pleading and constitutional authority. Modern readers sometimes experience that as outrageous technicality after mass killing.

Technicality can be another word for legal boundary.

The question is whether the boundary protects a principle worth the human cost it imposes.

Courts cannot simply waive constitutional limits because the underlying crime is horrible. If Congress lacks power, courts are supposed to say so even in ugly cases. That is what constitutional review means.

The harder historical judgment is whether the Court understood congressional power and the Reconstruction Amendments too narrowly given the problem they were enacted to solve.

Later constitutional law would expand federal authority in important ways, but *Cruikshank* helped mark a retreat from the promise that national citizenship would come with national protection against organized private suppression.

This is where original purpose matters.

The Reconstruction Amendments were not adopted in a seminar about federalism. They were adopted after slavery and civil war, in a country where states had legally enslaved people and where white southern governments resisted Black citizenship. Any interpretation of their enforcement provisions that ignores that history risks converting the amendments into abstractions detached from their reason for existence.

At the same time, amendments are legal text, not unlimited moral mandates. Courts have to determine scope. Congress has to legislate within that scope. The debate over *Cruikshank* is not resolved by saying the justices should have cared more. It requires asking what kind of national power the postwar Constitution created to address private violence entangled with state failure.

The victims at Colfax make that interpretive question impossible to sterilize.

There is a further cruelty in the sequence.

Black citizens had participated in formal politics under constitutional amendments that promised their right to do so. They defended a courthouse because competing political factions were fighting over who legally governed. White attackers used force to decide the issue outside ordinary democratic procedure. Federal law then attempted to punish that force. The Supreme Court's decision weakened the legal response.

Violence succeeded twice.

First physically.

Then institutionally, when the system proved unable to punish it effectively.

That lesson travels far beyond Reconstruction.

Political violence is not only about the immediate victims. It sends information to everyone watching.

If a Black voter sees neighbors killed and perpetrators walk free, the message is not contained in the case file. It changes the expected cost of voting.

If a witness knows the government cannot protect witnesses, future testimony disappears.

If an official knows federal prosecutors will lose jurisdiction, local resistance becomes easier.

Impunity compounds.

This is why enforcement is part of rights.

A right that cannot survive organized retaliation is a conditional permission.

The country would spend nearly a century learning how conditional Reconstruction's promises had become.

Segregation hardened. Black disfranchisement spread. Lynching and racial terror enforced social hierarchy. Federal civil-rights enforcement receded. The Supreme Court would later approve “separate but equal” in *Plessy v. Ferguson*, our trial of the 1890s.

The path from Colfax to Plessy is not a straight line, but it is short enough to see.

Reconstruction's constitutional ambition was narrowed before segregation's legal architecture was blessed.

That sequence matters because Jim Crow did not simply appear when a court announced it. It became possible through accumulated failures of enforcement, political retreat, local violence, statutory design, and judicial doctrine.

Systems of domination are built by layers.

So are systems of liberty.

A single case rarely creates either.

The Colfax prosecution is also an example of why the “most important trial” in a decade may be one whose verdict barely survived long enough to be remembered. The three federal convictions did not become a triumphant foundation for national civil-rights enforcement. Their reversal became more consequential than the trial victory.

Failure can be historically larger than success.

That is uncomfortable for lawyers because legal education naturally celebrates precedents that endure. History should also study the routes that closed.

What if federal enforcement after Colfax had been sustained and judicially supported more robustly?

Counterfactuals cannot produce certainty. White southern resistance was deep, northern commitment was weakening, and law alone could not guarantee an interracial democracy. But the question reveals what the decision did: it altered the available tools.

Institutions operate through option sets.

A prosecutor with a statute and supportive doctrine has one set of options.

A prosecutor after *Cruikshank* has another.

A local sheriff who expects federal intervention behaves differently from one who expects jurisdictional paralysis.

Law changes incentives before the next case arrives.

That is why doctrinal narrowing can have effects larger than the defendant before the Court.

The defendants in *Cruikshank* were not constitutional theorists representing federalism as an abstract value. They were men prosecuted after one of Reconstruction's bloodiest episodes of racial violence. The constitutional ruling that freed them became part of the environment in which other men calculated the risks of political terror.

This does not mean every later act of violence was caused by the case.

It means impunity has a jurisprudence.

The Colfax case also invites a comparison with the Boston Massacre trials at the beginning of the book.

In Boston, colonial law protects unpopular British soldiers from collective vengeance by requiring individualized proof. The restraint is admirable because the local crowd wants punishment and the defendants are politically hated.

At Colfax, concern for the limits of federal criminal power operates in a setting where local institutions have failed the victims and organized private violence has destroyed political rights.

Both cases involve restraint on prosecution.

The moral result differs because the structure of power differs.

That is the difficulty of legal principles. They must be general enough to apply beyond the sympathetic case, but their effects are always specific.

A rule against collective guilt protects minorities.

A rule against federal reach can expose minorities when local power is hostile.

The Constitution contains multiple safeguards that can collide. Liberty lives in the collision, not in one slogan winning forever.

Colfax forces a final question about what counts as state action.

Private violence can be deeply political without being formally governmental. A militia without official title can enforce the social order more effectively than a statute. A mob can accomplish what officials desire without receiving a written command. Local authorities can enable violence through passivity, selective enforcement, jury manipulation, or simple refusal to investigate.

The state-action doctrine asks courts to distinguish government from private conduct.

History often shows them braided together.

Reconstruction's enemies understood that fact before doctrine fully did.

You do not need a law saying Black citizens cannot vote if terror makes voting fatal.

You do not need an official order to kill if unofficial killers know they will not be punished.

You do not need to repeal an amendment if you can make exercising it too expensive.

That is how constitutional rights can be defeated without changing the constitutional text.

The courthouse at Colfax was eventually retaken by white force.

The federal government eventually obtained a few convictions.

The Supreme Court eventually reversed them.

The Constitution still said citizenship.

It still said equal protection.

It still protected voting rights.

The words survived.

For many Black Americans in the post-Reconstruction South, the protection did not.

The massacre happened at a courthouse because control of law was the conflict.

The trial proved that control of law would remain the conflict long after the shooting stopped.
