# Chapter 7 — 1830s: Georgia Tries a Missionary

Samuel Worcester was not Cherokee.

That fact is part of why Georgia's prosecution of him became so useful.

He was a white missionary living in the Cherokee Nation. Georgia had extended its laws over Cherokee territory and adopted measures intended to weaken Cherokee self-government and make removal easier. One statute required white people living in Cherokee territory to obtain a state license and swear allegiance to Georgia. Worcester refused to accept the state's authority on those terms.

Georgia arrested him.

The state was not principally offended by a missionary's paperwork. It was making a claim about sovereignty.

If Georgia could require a state permit for Worcester to live among the Cherokee, then Georgia was acting as the government of the place. If the Cherokee Nation possessed a political status recognized by federal treaties and federal law, Georgia's claim was not merely bureaucratic. It was an invasion of another jurisdiction.

The trial therefore placed an enormous territorial argument inside the body of one defendant.

Where was Samuel Worcester, legally speaking?

Georgia said he was in Georgia.

The Cherokee Nation and the federal treaty structure said the answer was more complicated.

That is why this is the trial of the 1830s.

The decade is remembered for Indian removal and, above all, the Trail of Tears. A national history can tell that story through Andrew Jackson, the Indian Removal Act of 1830, treaties, military power, land hunger, state politics, and the forced migration of Native peoples. All of those are necessary. The Worcester prosecution adds something a policy history can miss: removal was also a fight over which government's law could reach a person standing on Cherokee land.

Georgia had powerful material incentives. White settlers wanted land. The discovery of gold intensified pressure in the region. The state rejected the proposition that the Cherokee Nation could maintain a protected political existence within Georgia's claimed boundaries. It sought to dismantle Cherokee institutions and make state law supreme.

The Cherokee Nation did not respond only with force or diplomacy. It used American legal forms.

That choice has an almost unbearable irony.

The United States had entered treaties with Native nations, recognized diplomatic relationships, and constructed a federal system in which Indian affairs were principally a national rather than state responsibility. Cherokee leaders developed a written constitution, a newspaper, schools, courts, and other institutions that white Americans often claimed were evidence of “civilization.” Yet the more legible Cherokee government became in American terms, the more threatening its claim to durable sovereignty became to people who wanted its land.

Assimilation did not cure dispossession.

Legal sophistication did not guarantee enforcement.

Worcester's case would make that second point brutally clear.

Georgia convicted him in 1831. He was sentenced to four years of hard labor in the state penitentiary. Other missionaries were prosecuted as well, but Worcester's name became attached to the Supreme Court case because he continued to challenge the conviction.

When the case reached the Supreme Court, Chief Justice John Marshall rejected Georgia's effort to impose its law in Cherokee territory. The Court described the Cherokee Nation as a distinct political community in which Georgia's laws had no force and treated the federal relationship with the Cherokee as controlling.

On paper, Worcester won.

Georgia's law had been invalid as applied to him.

The constitutional principle was significant. States could not simply erase the national government's treaty and Indian-affairs authority by announcing jurisdiction over Native territory. The Cherokee Nation possessed a legal status that was not reducible to a county waiting for state settlement.

And then came the question law hates to admit it cannot answer by itself.

Who will make the losing government obey?

The famous line often attributed to Andrew Jackson—essentially daring Marshall to enforce the decision—is too uncertain in its exact form to deserve quotation as fact. The underlying enforcement problem does not depend on the quote. Georgia did not immediately reorganize its policy around respectful compliance with the Court's recognition of Cherokee sovereignty. The federal executive did not use its enormous potential power to make *Worcester* a durable shield against removal.

Worcester was eventually released after political negotiation, but the Cherokee Nation did not receive the practical security that the legal principle seemed to promise.

Within the decade, a disputed treaty and federal policy would culminate in forced removal. Thousands of Cherokee people were driven west. Many died.

A Supreme Court victory sits in the same decade as the Trail of Tears.

That contradiction is the reason the case cannot be told as a simple triumph of judicial review.

Courts issue judgments. Governments control many of the instruments that turn judgments into reality.

Modern Americans are accustomed to imagining Supreme Court decisions as self-enforcing events. A headline announces that the Court ruled, and the sentence is written as though the world changed at that instant. Usually institutions comply because a dense network of norms, lower courts, executive officials, lawyers, budgets, and expectations translates the ruling into behavior.

When that network fails, the paper becomes visible.

*Worcester* reveals constitutional law's dependence on cooperation.

The Court had no army to send into Georgia. The judiciary's authority rested on other institutions accepting that federal judicial judgments constrained them. In ordinary disputes, that premise often works so well it disappears. In a confrontation where land, race, sovereignty, and presidential policy point the other way, the dependence becomes obvious.

This is not a weakness unique to courts.

Every institution depends on other institutions. A legislature can pass a statute that police refuse to enforce. An executive can issue an order that courts enjoin. A jury can acquit and still be followed by a mob. A constitutional amendment can promise equal citizenship while local officials and private violence make the promise dangerous to exercise.

The gap between legal declaration and practical power is one of the central themes of American trials.

Worcester's case gives that gap a geography.

The missionary was physically in Cherokee territory. Georgia insisted that meant he was within the state's sovereign reach. The Supreme Court said Georgia's assertion conflicted with the nation's recognized political status and federal law. The dispute was not about where Worcester's body was on a map. Everyone knew that. It was about which map carried legal authority.

Maps are among the most political objects in law.

A border converts conduct. Cross it and a person can move from free to enslaved, citizen to alien, lawful resident to trespasser, regulated to unregulated. In the antebellum United States, Dred and Harriet Scott's freedom litigation would turn on movement between slave and free jurisdictions. In Native sovereignty cases, the question is more fundamental: whose jurisdiction is the place itself?

Georgia wanted an answer that collapsed Cherokee territory into state territory.

The Supreme Court refused.

History then demonstrated that winning the jurisdictional argument did not guarantee possession of the land.

The prosecution also reveals a strategy governments use when confronting institutions they cannot easily abolish directly: regulate the people around them.

Georgia did not need to place the Cherokee Nation itself in a criminal dock. It could impose laws on residents, missionaries, officials, meetings, mining, and other activity. It could make daily operation harder. It could punish outsiders who recognized Cherokee authority rather than state supremacy.

This kind of pressure works because sovereignty is lived through ordinary acts.

Who grants permission to reside?

Who licenses commerce?

Who arrests?

Whose court hears the case?

Who controls land records?

Who may enter?

A government becomes real through these repeated recognitions.

Worcester's refusal was therefore more than solidarity. By declining Georgia's license, he declined to perform the state's sovereignty.

Georgia responded with prison.

The criminal sentence made the abstract dispute costly to an individual. Four years of hard labor is a serious punishment. The state was willing to use incarceration to make its jurisdictional claim concrete.

This pattern should be familiar by now.

Matthew Lyon's sedition case converted a federal theory of political order into four months of a congressman's confinement.

Worcester's case converted Georgia's theory of territorial sovereignty into a missionary's sentence.

Law is where governments make ideas physical.

That is why the quality of the underlying idea matters so much.

The case also complicates any simple account of federalism as a system in which state autonomy equals liberty. States can resist national overreach. States can also be the overreaching institution. The moral valence of federalism depends on the right and the actor.

Georgia invoked state power against Cherokee sovereignty.

Later southern states would invoke state authority against Reconstruction and federal civil-rights enforcement. The national government would sometimes be the institution capable of protecting minorities from local majorities. At other points, federal power would itself dispossess, intern, surveil, or prosecute.

“State” and “federal” are locations of power, not moral categories.

The Worcester story demonstrates why Native sovereignty does not fit comfortably inside the ordinary state-federal binary at all. Tribal nations possess political status that predates the Constitution and sits in a distinct relationship to the federal government. Reducing the story to a fight between Washington and Georgia risks repeating the erasure the case was partly about.

There were three political authorities in the room even when only one man was the criminal defendant.

The Cherokee Nation was not scenery.

That point matters because historical storytelling often turns Native peoples into the object of other governments' decisions. Land is “opened.” People are “removed.” Treaties are “made.” The passive grammar hides agency and coercion.

Cherokee leaders petitioned, negotiated, litigated, published, governed, divided over strategy, and resisted. The fact that removal ultimately occurred does not mean there was no legal or political contest. It means the contest was lost under enormous pressure.

Worcester's victory is evidence of the contest.

Its failure to prevent removal is evidence of power.

Holding both is necessary.

There is a temptation in American constitutional history to turn Supreme Court opinions into moral medals. A good result proves the Court saved the principle; a bad result proves the Court failed. *Worcester* is more sobering. A court can articulate an important principle correctly and still fail to protect the people whose political existence the principle recognizes.

Judicial virtue is not the same as institutional capacity.

The distinction becomes central after the Civil War. The Reconstruction Amendments will give the national government new constitutional tools to protect Black citizenship. Congress will enact enforcement laws. Federal prosecutors will secure convictions after racial massacres. The Supreme Court will then narrow some of those tools. In that later story, judicial restraint of federal power helps weaken protection.

In Worcester, judicial restraint of state power helps affirm protection.

The same mechanism can cut in opposite directions because constitutional structure always asks: restraint on whom, for whose benefit, under what authority?

The case is therefore a useful antidote to theory without facts.

People can argue abstractly for strong states, strong courts, strong presidents, strong federal power, or strong local control. Historical cases force the missing question.

Strong enough to do what?

Georgia wanted to extend law over the Cherokee Nation.

The Supreme Court said no.

The executive branch did not transform that no into a durable barrier against the broader removal project.

The result should make us careful whenever someone says “the courts will stop it” or “the courts have spoken” as though litigation alone exhausts constitutional responsibility.

Constitutional government is distributed responsibility.

A president has duties.

Congress has powers.

States have obligations.

Courts have judgments.

Citizens, officials, lawyers, and institutions decide whether norms become habits.

When everyone waits for one branch to save the system, the system may discover that no branch was designed to do all the saving.

Samuel Worcester eventually left the Georgia prison.

The Cherokee Nation did not remain safely on its southeastern homeland.

That asymmetry is the chapter's final fact.

The named defendant obtained relief.

The larger cause embedded in his prosecution did not.

Some legal victories are narrower than the history that made them famous.

That does not make them meaningless.

It tells us to measure them against the people still exposed after the opinion is printed.

A state tried a missionary to prove it governed Cherokee land.

The Supreme Court said the state did not.

Then America demonstrated the terrible distance between being right in court and being safe in the country.
