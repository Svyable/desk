# Prologue — Twenty-Six Rooms

The room keeps changing.

In one decade it is a colonial courtroom in Boston, where the politics outside are already hot enough to make a fair trial seem improbable. In another it is a South Carolina proceeding so compromised by slavery that the surviving record cannot be treated as neutral. Later it is a Tennessee courtroom swollen by reporters who have come to watch modernity argue with Genesis. Later still it is Los Angeles, where millions of people see evidence on television and become convinced that everyone else has misunderstood it.

The furniture changes more slowly than the country.

There is a place for the judge. A place for the accused. A rail, real or imagined, separating the people who may speak in prescribed forms from those who may only watch. Papers become exhibits. Memory becomes testimony. Story becomes theory. Injury is translated into elements. Fear is translated into self-defense. Politics is translated, imperfectly, into admissible facts. A life can be compressed into a verdict that takes seconds to read.

A trial is an unnatural thing to ask history to do.

History sprawls. Trials narrow.

A war has thousands of causes and millions of consequences. A trial wants to know whether this person did this thing under this rule. A social order can be rotten in ways that no indictment reaches. A jury receives counts. The country may be fighting over slavery, citizenship, race, religion, labor, war, poverty, presidential power, or the legitimacy of the police. The judge still has to rule on an objection.

That mismatch is why trials are so revealing.

A nation can maintain contradictory principles for years in speeches. Courtrooms are less accommodating. Eventually somebody asks a question that makes the contradiction operational.

Can a Black man who has lived on free soil sue for his freedom?

Can a state imprison a white missionary for living in the Cherokee Nation without state permission?

Can the government imprison a member of Congress for criticizing the president?

Can civilians be tried by a military commission after the president is assassinated?

Can federal law punish the men who participate in racial terror when state institutions will not?

Can a state teach one account of human origins and prohibit another?

Can the government remove a citizen from his home because of ancestry during wartime?

Does a poor defendant get a lawyer simply because the government has one?

Can the president keep evidence from a criminal prosecution of his own senior aides?

How sick must a person be before the law calls him not criminally responsible?

What does self-defense mean when the only surviving participant in an encounter testifies to the danger?

Can a former president be tried in an ordinary criminal courtroom?

The answers arrive in legal language. Their consequences do not stay there.

The strangest thing about looking at American history through trials is how often the famous answer is not the trial's answer.

We remember *Dred Scott* as a Supreme Court catastrophe. Before that, Dred and Harriet Scott spent years litigating freedom through Missouri courts and a federal jury. We remember *Worcester v. Georgia* as a statement about Cherokee sovereignty and federal supremacy. Before the Supreme Court spoke, Georgia had made Samuel Worcester a criminal. We remember *Gideon v. Wainwright* as a constitutional right. Before it was a doctrine, it was a man in a Florida courtroom asking for a lawyer and being told that state law did not entitle him to one.

Law school naturally teaches the appellate opinion. History benefits from backing up.

Back up far enough and the polished doctrine becomes a wager made by people who did not know whether they would win.

That uncertainty changes the moral temperature of a case.

John Adams's defense of British soldiers after the Boston Massacre looks inevitable only after the fact. In 1770, he had clients despised by people whose political grievances he substantially shared. The Quock Walker cases look like part of a northern march away from slavery only because we know what later generations made of them. In the 1780s, the question of whether Massachusetts's new constitutional language could be made real in an individual freedom dispute was still open enough to fight over.

The same is true in darker directions.

The prosecution of Matthew Lyon under the Sedition Act was not conducted by officials who expected to become a warning label in a civics textbook. The men who put eight anarchists on trial after the Haymarket bombing did not have to imagine a future governor concluding that the proceedings had become a grave injustice. The officials who defended the forced exclusion of Japanese Americans did not organize their case around the possibility that Korematsu's conviction would later be vacated and the Supreme Court's reasoning repudiated.

People inside history usually believe the present has more permanence than it does.

Trials preserve that confidence in unusually legible form.

The transcript records what a government was willing to argue without embarrassment. The charge to the jury records what the law was willing to ask citizens to decide. The verdict records what a particular jury could conclude from a bounded presentation of facts. The sentence records what punishment the system believed legitimate. The crowd outside records what the proceeding meant beyond doctrine. The appeal records which parts of the first answer could survive another look.

And then time cross-examines everyone.

That last stage is not legal. No clerk opens the session. No rule sets a deadline. A verdict may stand while its legitimacy collapses. A conviction may be vacated long after the prison term would have ended. A defendant may be acquitted in a criminal trial and found liable in a civil one. A case once treated as local may become national shorthand. A constitutional victory may prove nearly useless because the executive refuses to honor the principle or because violence accomplishes what law formally forbids.

This is why the book does not use “important” to mean “good.”

The Colfax prosecution is important partly because its collapse showed how a constitutional settlement could be hollowed out. Plessy's prosecution is important because a deliberately staged test case produced one of the most destructive legal permissions in American life. The trial of Emmett Till's accused killers is important because an acquittal exposed a legal system that could hear testimony about racial murder and still protect white defendants. Fred Korematsu's case is important because constitutional machinery can fail in the language of necessity.

Failure leaves records too.

The one-trial-per-decade rule creates collisions that a thematic history might avoid.

The 1820s force us into the Denmark Vesey proceedings, where even the evidentiary foundation of the alleged conspiracy remains contested by historians and where the official report was produced by the authorities responsible for prosecution. The uncertainty is not a defect to edit out. It is central to what the case reveals about slave power, secret process, fear, and the difficulty of reconstructing a proceeding when the state controlled both the tribunal and much of the surviving narrative.

The 1980s force a different kind of discomfort. John Hinckley Jr.'s not-guilty-by-reason-of-insanity verdict after the shooting of President Ronald Reagan did not become important because the public came to admire it. It became important because the reaction changed federal insanity law. The trial is a reminder that a verdict can be doctrinally defensible and politically explosive at the same time.

The 2000s present another problem. The September 11 attacks generated war, intelligence programs, military commissions, detention litigation, and decades of policy. Yet the one person convicted in a United States court in connection with the attacks, Zacarias Moussaoui, pleaded guilty. The trial came in the penalty phase. A jury then had to decide whether the government had proved the conditions necessary for death and, ultimately, whether he would die in prison or be executed. The proceeding makes visible an underexamined part of the post-9/11 story: the strain of forcing an event treated as war and catastrophe back through ordinary criminal procedure.

Then we reach the present.

The 2020s chapter cannot borrow the false calm of distance. Donald Trump's 2024 New York criminal trial produced guilty verdicts on thirty-four felony counts. By September 2026, the conviction remained in place while state and federal appellate efforts continued. Any sentence written about its “historical meaning” carries more uncertainty than a sentence written about Scopes or Gideon.

That is not a reason to omit it.

It is a reason to mark the date.

History does not become history only when everyone involved has died. One advantage of ending with a living case is that it restores the uncertainty that old cases lose in retelling. We know Aaron Burr was acquitted of treason and can discuss Chief Justice John Marshall's rulings with the tidy confidence of hindsight. We do not know the complete afterlife of the first criminal conviction of a former American president.

The open ending is instructive.

Every chapter in this book once had one.

Before the verdict, somebody waited.

Before the doctrine, somebody risked losing.

Before the symbol, there was a name.

And before America could tell itself what a case had meant, a room full of people had to decide what happened.
