# Chapter 10 — 1860s: The Conspiracy Court

The Civil War did not end when Robert E. Lee surrendered at Appomattox.

That is obvious in the broad sense. Confederate armies remained in the field, emancipation had to become a lived fact, the South was devastated, four million formerly enslaved people were entering freedom under conditions the country had barely begun to imagine, and the federal government had to decide what reunion meant.

Then, on April 14, 1865, John Wilkes Booth shot Abraham Lincoln at Ford's Theatre.

Lincoln died the next morning.

The assassination detonated inside a country that had spent four years learning to treat existential danger as ordinary. Washington was not a peacetime capital processing a homicide. It was the command center of a government that had survived rebellion, armies, spies, sabotage, guerrilla violence, prison camps, emergency measures, and hundreds of thousands of military deaths. Booth's plot included attacks on other senior officials. Secretary of State William Seward was viciously assaulted by Lewis Powell. Vice President Andrew Johnson had been assigned as another target, though George Atzerodt did not carry out an attack.

The government saw conspiracy, not merely murder.

Its next decision created the trial of the 1860s.

Eight defendants accused of involvement in the assassination conspiracy were tried not before an ordinary civilian jury but before a military commission.

Mary Surratt, Lewis Powell, David Herold, George Atzerodt, Samuel Arnold, Michael O'Laughlen, Edman Spangler, and Samuel Mudd sat before military officers in a proceeding created by executive authority. Booth himself had been killed during the manhunt. The country wanted answers quickly. The administration wanted to uncover the network behind the assassination and punish those responsible. The war had conditioned officials to use military institutions against threats to national survival.

The problem was that civilian courts in Washington were open.

Why use a military tribunal to try civilians for crimes connected to an assassination?

The government's answer rested on the character of the conspiracy. Lincoln had been commander in chief. The assassination was understood as an act connected to the rebellion and war. The accused were alleged to have participated in a broader plot against national leadership. Military prosecutors argued that the law of war and the nature of the offense justified the commission.

The defense saw something more dangerous.

If the executive could choose a military court when civilian courts were functioning, what remained of the ordinary constitutional protections of criminal trial?

That question makes the proceeding larger than the guilt of any one defendant.

The country had just fought a war to preserve constitutional government. How much of constitutional criminal procedure could the government suspend while punishing the murder of the president who had led the war?

The timing made restraint unusually difficult.

Lincoln was mourned on a national scale. Booth was hated. Rumors about the conspiracy flourished. The government did not know immediately how broad the plot had been or whether Confederate officials had directed it. Public anger made the accused easy to merge into a single moral object: the conspiracy that killed Lincoln.

Trials are supposed to reverse that merger.

They ask what each defendant did.

Mary Surratt illustrates the stakes.

She operated a boardinghouse in Washington and had family connections to a tavern in Maryland used by conspirators. Her son John Surratt was deeply involved with Booth and escaped immediate capture. The prosecution alleged that Mary Surratt had assisted the conspiracy, including through actions and communications connected to the tavern. The defense disputed the strength and interpretation of key evidence.

She was not John Wilkes Booth.

She was not Lewis Powell, who had attacked Seward.

Her criminal responsibility depended on what she knew and intentionally did.

The commission convicted her and sentenced her to death. Several members reportedly recommended clemency because of her sex and age. President Andrew Johnson did not grant it. On July 7, 1865, Mary Surratt, Lewis Powell, David Herold, and George Atzerodt were hanged.

Surratt became the first woman executed by the federal government.

That fact has helped keep her name alive, but it can also distort the central constitutional question. The issue is not whether women should receive a categorical exemption from punishment. The issue is whether a military process sufficiently tested the evidence against a civilian defendant when ordinary courts were available and the country was desperate for retribution.

The commission had procedural features that differed sharply from civilian jury trial. Military officers decided law and fact. Rules and voting requirements differed. The executive branch had created the tribunal, prosecuted the case, and retained authority over sentences. The atmosphere was inseparable from the assassination and the recent war.

None of that proves every verdict was wrong.

Lewis Powell's conduct, for example, was supported by powerful evidence of his direct attack on Seward's household. David Herold had fled with Booth. George Atzerodt was connected to the plan against Johnson. Samuel Mudd had treated Booth's injured leg after the assassination and became a subject of long-running controversy about how much he knew before and after the crime.

The constitutional problem survives even when evidence against some defendants is strong.

Procedure is not only for cases where guilt is doubtful.

That is one of the hardest ideas for a society under attack to accept.

If everybody believes a defendant is guilty, an ordinary trial can feel like theater. Why spend time on formal protections for people connected to the murder of the president? Why risk a civilian jury when the offense is intertwined with war? Why expose intelligence and witnesses? Why permit technical objections to slow punishment?

The answer is that procedure becomes most valuable when shortcutting it feels most reasonable.

A fair system does not prove its commitment to civilian justice by using civilian courts for ordinary burglary while reserving military justice for politically explosive civilians.

The explosive case is the test.

The country would receive a major constitutional lesson one year later in *Ex parte Milligan*.

Lambdin Milligan, an Indiana civilian, had been tried and sentenced by a military commission during the war even though civilian courts were open. In 1866, the Supreme Court held that military trial of a civilian in such circumstances was unconstitutional where the ordinary courts were available. The decision did not mechanically invalidate every aspect of the Lincoln conspiracy commission, whose defenders distinguished the assassination defendants as participants in a war-related offense in the national capital. But the principle cast a long shadow over the government's choice.

Milligan's case is a close alternative for the trial of the 1860s.

I chose the Lincoln conspiracy proceeding because the tension is sharper when the victim is the president and the public desire for extraordinary justice is at its highest. Milligan gives us doctrine. Surratt and her codefendants give us the emotional conditions under which doctrine becomes difficult to obey.

Emergency law is rarely created by people who think they are building tyranny.

It is created by people who think the ordinary system cannot safely handle the present danger.

Sometimes they are right that the danger is extraordinary.

The Lincoln assassination was extraordinary.

That does not answer which tribunal should try civilians.

The distinction between the existence of an emergency and the scope of emergency power will recur throughout American history. Japanese American exclusion during World War II will be justified as military necessity. Post-September 11 policy will revive debates over military commissions, detention, intelligence, and the reach of civilian courts. Presidents of both parties will claim powers unavailable in ordinary times. Courts will oscillate between deference and scrutiny.

The 1865 commission is one of the foundational American examples because it combines nearly every argument emergency government makes.

The nation has been attacked.

The offense is connected to war.

The normal system may be too slow or vulnerable.

The conspiracy may be wider than the defendants we have captured.

Intelligence has to be protected.

Speed matters.

Public confidence requires punishment.

The commander in chief possesses special authority.

Every sentence contains some truth and some risk.

The danger comes from allowing the truths to accumulate into a blank check.

The commission also shows how conspiracy law expands a trial's moral geography. A conspiracy charge does not require every participant to perform every act. That is its purpose. Coordinated crime would be impossible to prosecute if only the person who pulled the trigger could be held responsible for an agreed plan.

But conspiracy also makes association dangerous.

A boardinghouse, a conversation, a delivery, prior knowledge, assistance after an offense, and shared acquaintances can appear inside one narrative. The prosecution's task is to prove the legally required agreement and intentional participation, not merely proximity to people who committed terrible acts.

In politically charged cases, the narrative itself exerts pressure.

Once a jury or tribunal believes there was a vast plot, ambiguous conduct by a person near the plot can look more incriminating. The whole gives meaning to each part.

Sometimes correctly.

Sometimes disastrously.

Haymarket, two decades later, will demonstrate the danger of treating radical association and incendiary speech as substitutes for proof of who committed a bombing. The Vesey proceedings showed a slave regime interpreting networks and conversations through fear of insurrection. Conspiracy cases are where the government most needs inferential evidence and where inferential evidence most needs discipline.

The Lincoln commission operated before modern criminal procedure developed many of the safeguards later Americans would take for granted. That is not a reason to judge nineteenth-century actors as though they had read twentieth-century cases. It is a reason to see how doctrine grows from painful examples.

Constitutional protections are often abstract until a government crosses a line.

Then the line acquires a name.

The aftermath of the commission did not deliver one clean national verdict on its legitimacy. The executed defendants could not benefit from later doctrinal caution. Some imprisoned defendants eventually received clemency. John Surratt was later captured and tried in a civilian court; his prosecution ended without a conviction after a hung jury and legal obstacles.

That contrast is striking.

The son alleged to have been a more central conspirator received a civilian jury trial after the emergency had cooled.

The mother was executed after a military commission while the country's grief was fresh.

History cannot rerun either proceeding under identical evidence and modern rules. It can notice the difference.

Time changed the forum.

That is another recurring feature of emergency justice: temporary measures can impose permanent consequences before ordinary institutions recover their confidence.

A person imprisoned under an emergency rule can later be released.

A person executed cannot.

This asymmetry should make irreversible punishment especially sensitive to procedural uncertainty.

The commission also sat at a constitutional transition. The Civil War had enormously strengthened national power. Lincoln had suspended habeas corpus in contested circumstances, issued the Emancipation Proclamation as a war measure, expanded military administration, and confronted disloyalty across states. The Thirteenth Amendment was abolishing slavery. Reconstruction would soon require federal intervention in southern government and civil rights on a scale unimaginable before the war.

The central constitutional danger of the 1860s was not simply too much federal power or too little.

The Union needed enough national power to destroy slavery and suppress rebellion.

It also needed limits strong enough that national survival did not become a permanent justification for military rule over civilians.

Both propositions were true.

Political theories prefer one-directional warnings. History is more demanding.

If the federal government had been weaker, secession and slavery might have prevailed.

If wartime executive power had no boundary, constitutional liberty could become contingent on the president's assessment of danger.

The hard work is building a government capable of force and capable of stopping its own force.

Trials sit at that boundary.

A trial is controlled coercion. The state is preparing to take liberty or life, but it agrees to pass through evidence, rules, a decision maker, and review. The question “which court?” therefore goes to the legitimacy of the coercion itself.

The 1865 government chose soldiers to judge civilians accused in a conspiracy tied to the war.

The choice was understandable.

That does not make it harmless.

The phrase “understandable but dangerous” describes much emergency law better than moral simplification does.

Officials frequently act from genuine fear, incomplete information, and responsibility for risks the public will blame them for failing to prevent. A president who underreacts to conspiracy may be condemned for negligence. A president who overreacts can erode the system he is trying to protect.

Courts exist partly to make that trade less unilateral.

The military commission concentrated it.

The tribunal itself belonged to the executive war structure. There was less institutional distance between accusation and judgment than an ordinary civilian criminal trial would provide.

Independence is not an insult to the government's motives.

It is insurance against them.

A prosecutor can be sincere and wrong.

A president can be sincere and angry.

A military officer can be honorable and still inhabit the institutional assumptions of war.

Civilian juries can be prejudiced too. Judges can be partisan. No forum is pure. The argument for separation is probabilistic: different institutions fail differently.

That becomes important whenever the first decision maker is also the reviewer.

The Lincoln conspiracy trial is an early American case about the value of a different failure mode.

The chapter must end where the country was, not where doctrine later moved.

On July 7, four people climbed the scaffold and were executed by the federal government.

The administration believed justice had been done against participants in a plot that killed the president and attacked national leadership.

Critics then and later questioned the forum, the evidence against particular defendants, and the denial of ordinary civilian trial.

The Supreme Court would soon articulate a strong principle protecting civilian courts where they remained open.

None of that could reach backward through the rope.

A legal system learns some lessons after the person who paid for them is gone.

The 1860s leave us with a question that never becomes obsolete:

How ordinary must justice remain when the crime feels like an attack on the country itself?
