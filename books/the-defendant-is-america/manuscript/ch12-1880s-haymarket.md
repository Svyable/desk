# Chapter 12 — 1880s: Haymarket

Nobody ever proved which defendant threw the bomb.

That sentence is the center of the Haymarket trial.

It is not the whole case. A bomb did explode among police officers in Chicago on the night of May 4, 1886. People died. Police and civilians were wounded. Gunfire followed in darkness and confusion. The city had been living through intense labor conflict surrounding the campaign for an eight-hour workday. Radical anarchists openly advocated revolutionary change. Police violence against workers was real. Fear of anarchist violence was also real.

A trial can hold all of those facts and still require one more question.

What exactly did these eight men do that made them guilty of murder?

The defendants were August Spies, Albert Parsons, Adolph Fischer, George Engel, Louis Lingg, Michael Schwab, Samuel Fielden, and Oscar Neebe. They were anarchists or associated with the radical labor movement. Some had spoken or written in incendiary terms. Some were present at Haymarket Square. Some were not present when the bomb exploded.

The prosecution did not need to prove that each man personally threw the bomb. It advanced a theory of common design and responsibility: radical agitation and an alleged conspiracy to attack police made the defendants legally accountable for the killing.

The theory converted ideology into evidence of murder.

That is why Haymarket is the trial of the 1880s.

The decade was defined in part by industrial expansion, urban growth, immigration, enormous private fortunes, dangerous labor, strikes, and violent conflict over who would control the new industrial order. Workers organizing for shorter hours and higher wages confronted employers, private security forces, police, and courts. Anarchism attracted some immigrants and workers who believed ordinary politics could not deliver justice. Newspapers treated radicalism as both spectacle and menace.

Haymarket condensed all of it into one bomb.

The rally itself had been called in response to police violence the previous day near the McCormick Reaper Works, where striking workers and police clashed and workers were killed. On May 4, speakers addressed a crowd at Haymarket Square. The gathering was smaller and calmer than authorities may have feared. Rain threatened. Chicago's mayor attended for part of the event and left believing the situation had become uneventful enough to go home.

Then police moved toward the remaining crowd and ordered it to disperse.

A bomb came from the vicinity of an alley and exploded among the officers.

Gunfire followed.

The identity of the bomber has never been established with confidence.

The city nevertheless needed defendants.

That need is psychologically understandable after political violence. An unsolved attack is intolerable because the danger remains undefined. Authorities want a network, a motive, a theory that makes the event legible. The public wants names. Newspapers reward certainty. Police who lost colleagues possess both grief and investigative power.

The risk is that the state begins with the threat category and works backward toward individual guilt.

Anarchists become the suspect.

Then particular anarchists become defendants.

The trial becomes a referendum on anarchism.

The law is supposed to travel in the opposite direction.

Begin with the charged offense.

Prove the defendant's acts and mental state.

Establish the legally required connection.

Haymarket is remembered because that discipline weakened under political pressure.

The trial began in the summer of 1886 before Judge Joseph Gary. Jury selection itself became controversial. The defense argued that the process produced jurors predisposed against the defendants and radical politics. The prosecution relied heavily on speeches, writings, meetings, weapons, and evidence intended to establish a broader anarchist plan. The bomb thrower remained unidentified.

The men were convicted.

Seven received death sentences. Oscar Neebe received fifteen years in prison. Before executions were carried out, the sentences of Fielden and Schwab were commuted to life imprisonment. Louis Lingg died by suicide in jail. Spies, Parsons, Fischer, and Engel were hanged in November 1887.

The verdict did not settle whether the trial had been just.

It expanded the argument.

Supporters of the defendants saw a political prosecution in which radical beliefs substituted for proof. Many opponents saw dangerous revolutionaries whose agitation had helped produce murderous violence even if the precise hand that threw the bomb was unknown. The case became international, part labor martyrdom, part anarchist warning, part civic trauma.

Then, in 1893, Illinois governor John Peter Altgeld pardoned Neebe, Fielden, and Schwab.

Altgeld's pardon message was not a claim that political violence did not matter. It was an indictment of the fairness of the proceeding. He criticized the jury selection, the evidence, and the judge's handling of the case, concluding that the surviving defendants had not received a fair trial.

The pardon damaged Altgeld politically.

That cost matters.

Executive clemency is one of the constitutional system's strangest correction mechanisms because it allows an elected official to intervene after courts have finished. A governor or president can consider mercy, injustice, changed circumstances, proportionality, or evidence that formal appellate procedures cannot easily reach.

The power is broad because legal finality sometimes needs an escape hatch.

Broad power can also be abused.

A governor can pardon allies. A president can reward loyalty. Clemency can bypass reasoned judicial review. Yet Haymarket shows why eliminating the power would remove a way to respond when law's ordinary channels have closed around an unjust result.

Altgeld could not pardon the men already executed back to life.

Again, legal time arrives after human time.

That is one of this book's recurring tragedies.

The Haymarket proceeding is often described as an example of guilt by association. The phrase is useful but incomplete. Association can be evidence. Conspiracies exist. Groups plan violence. A person who knowingly joins a murderous plan can be responsible for outcomes the person did not physically carry out.

The legal challenge is not to pretend association means nothing.

It is to prove what the association means.

Did the defendants agree to a plan that legally encompassed the bombing?

Which defendant knew what?

What was rhetoric and what was operational instruction?

How do weapons, meetings, publications, and prior statements connect to the specific attack?

Were statements introduced because they proved the elements or because they made the defendants frightening?

These questions have modern descendants.

Terrorism prosecutions often rely on networks, communications, material support, and ideology. Gang prosecutions use association evidence. Organized-crime laws connect participants through enterprises. Online radicalization makes the boundary between speech and operational assistance harder to see. Political movements can contain people who advocate violence and people who merely share some grievances or rhetoric.

Haymarket warns against two symmetrical mistakes.

The first is to say ideas never matter to criminal responsibility.

They do when they establish intent, agreement, motive, knowledge, or solicitation under law.

The second is to say bad ideas make ambiguous evidence sufficient.

They do not.

The worse the defendant's ideology, the more important it becomes to identify the actual crime.

This principle is difficult because jurors are human. If prosecutors show that a defendant has praised violence, despised police, collected weapons, or predicted revolution, later uncertainty about a bomb may feel smaller. Character begins to answer causation.

That is precisely why evidence law distinguishes propensity from proof and why modern courts struggle over the admissibility of inflammatory material.

The Haymarket trial occurred before today's doctrinal framework, but the human problem was the same.

Once a jury learns who it thinks the defendants are, it can become harder to ask what the government proved they did.

The defendants themselves made the distinction harder. Some embraced revolutionary rhetoric rather than performing respectability for the court. Parsons had been a prominent radical organizer. Spies edited the *Arbeiter-Zeitung*. Lingg was linked to bombs and bomb-making. The movement did not present itself in language designed to reassure property owners or police.

Civil liberties are tested by people who decline to make protection easy.

Matthew Lyon was combative.

The Haymarket defendants were radical.

Later, communist defendants, civil-rights protesters, antiwar activists, and unpopular religious groups would force courts to distinguish protected belief from punishable action.

A legal system that protects only moderate dissent does not really protect dissent. It licenses a range of approved disagreement.

The violence at Haymarket prevents the case from becoming a simple free-speech parable.

Someone threw a bomb.

Police officers died.

The state had a duty to investigate and punish the responsible people.

The failure was not that authorities treated the attack seriously.

The danger was treating seriousness as permission to relax proof.

High-stakes crimes should demand more evidentiary discipline, not less, because the pressure to convict is stronger and the penalties are greater.

The death sentences make that principle concrete.

Capital punishment converts uncertainty into irreversibility. Once executed, a defendant cannot benefit from a later pardon, new evidence, or changed public understanding. The system's confidence becomes final in a physical sense even when historical confidence does not.

Haymarket's afterlife therefore belongs to the history of the death penalty as well as labor and speech.

How sure must the state be before it kills?

“Beyond a reasonable doubt” is a legal standard, not a mathematical percentage. Jurors do not receive an instrument measuring certainty. The standard works through culture, instructions, advocacy, and conscience. In a politically saturated case, the surrounding fear can alter what feels reasonable.

That is one reason jury selection matters so much.

A juror does not enter court as an empty vessel. In Chicago in 1886, potential jurors had lived through press coverage of anarchists, strikes, police conflict, and the bombing. Finding twelve people untouched by the event was impossible. The legal system instead had to decide how much prior opinion could coexist with impartial adjudication.

High-profile trials continue to face the same problem.

The O.J. Simpson case will unfold under television saturation. George Zimmerman's jurors will enter a national argument over Trayvon Martin's death. Donald Trump's Manhattan trial will occur after years in which nearly every adult voter has formed opinions about the defendant.

Impartial does not mean ignorant.

It means capable of deciding the charge from the evidence and law rather than using the trial to ratify an existing political identity.

That capacity is difficult to test in voir dire because people are imperfect judges of their own bias. A juror can sincerely promise fairness and still interpret evidence through a prior story.

The Haymarket trial became a warning that selection procedure itself can determine whether the promise is credible.

Labor history gives the case another layer.

The campaign for the eight-hour day was not synonymous with anarchism. Many workers pursued reform through unions and political action. Employers and newspapers had incentives to associate labor unrest with radical violence because the association weakened the legitimacy of worker demands. The bombing made that association easier.

Violence can damage the cause closest to it even when the movement did not authorize the violence.

Governments and employers can then treat broad categories of dissent as security threats.

This mechanism recurs across political history. A violent fringe creates justification for surveillance or suppression of a larger movement. The larger movement condemns the violence but is forced to spend political energy proving distance from it. Authorities may genuinely need to investigate networks while also benefiting from blurred boundaries.

Trials become places where the blur should be sharpened.

Who agreed to what?

Haymarket did not sharpen enough.

That judgment does not require certainty about the identity of the bomber. Historians have proposed theories. Some evidence points toward people not among the executed defendants. The unresolved identity is itself part of the case's legacy.

A city punished men for a bombing without establishing which of them, if any, threw the bomb.

The prosecution relied on a broader theory of responsibility.

The later pardon declared the process unjust.

The executions remained irreversible.

That sequence tells us more about the 1880s than a clean solved case could.

Industrial America was becoming powerful enough to generate wealth on a scale the founding generation could not imagine and conflict on a scale local institutions struggled to contain. Workers confronted long hours, dangerous conditions, wage pressure, unemployment, and employers capable of mobilizing police and private force. Radical ideologies offered explanations and promises. Cities became laboratories for both organization and repression.

The law was asked to distinguish strike from riot, speech from conspiracy, association from guilt, protest from attack.

It did so under fear.

The Haymarket case also shows how a trial can create martyrs.

A government may believe severe punishment will discredit a movement. If the proceeding appears unjust, punishment can do the opposite. The executed men became international symbols in labor and radical history. May Day traditions around the world drew meaning from the Haymarket affair.

The state won the verdict and lost control of the story.

That happens often.

The British won some legal and military confrontations before losing the American colonies.

Georgia won Worcester's imprisonment before losing the Supreme Court case and still achieving removal through politics and force.

The federal government convicted men after Colfax before losing in the Supreme Court.

Trials create records opponents can use.

The transcript is not loyal to the victor forever.

Altgeld's pardon reopened the record. Later scholarship reopened it again. The case remains contested in details, but the central procedural concern has endured because the government never overcame the basic discomfort embedded in the first sentence of this chapter.

Nobody ever proved which defendant threw the bomb.

Perhaps none did.

Perhaps one knew more than the surviving evidence can establish.

Perhaps the wider radical network included plans the jury reasonably viewed as connected.

Historical uncertainty should survive where evidence does not close it.

The legal system in 1886 closed it anyway.

Four men were hanged.

That is why Haymarket is not only a labor story or an anarchist story.

It is a story about the dangerous human desire to make the defendant as large as the event.

A bomb explodes.

A city is terrified.

An ideology is hated.

Eight men enter court.

The task of justice is to make eight men small enough to judge individually.

Haymarket made them symbols first.
