# Chapter 9 — 1850s: Dred and Harriet Scott Ask to Be Free

Dred Scott's case is famous enough to become a metaphor.

That is dangerous.

Once a case becomes shorthand for judicial failure, the people inside it disappear. “Dred Scott” becomes something a politician says to describe a wrong Supreme Court decision. The phrase can float free from Dred Scott, Harriet Scott, their children, years of litigation, multiple courts, changing defendants, lawyers, juries, and the ordinary legal proposition from which the catastrophe grew.

So begin smaller.

Dred Scott and Harriet Robinson were enslaved people whose lives had carried them into places where slavery was prohibited. Dred had been taken by an enslaver, Army surgeon John Emerson, from Missouri into Illinois and then to Fort Snelling in what was then federal territory where the Missouri Compromise prohibited slavery. Harriet was also enslaved in the region. She and Dred married at Fort Snelling and later returned to Missouri.

Their legal claim drew on a doctrine Missouri courts had previously recognized in various forms: residence in free territory could make an enslaved person free.

In 1846, Dred and Harriet Scott sued for freedom.

That date matters.

The litigation did not begin as a grand request that the Supreme Court settle the future of slavery in America. It began in state court with a family using established legal mechanisms to contest bondage.

The path was long and unstable. An early proceeding failed on an evidentiary problem. The Scotts received another trial and won in 1850. A jury concluded in their favor. If history stopped there, Dred Scott might be remembered as part of the substantial tradition of freedom suits in which enslaved people used courts to alter legal status.

History did not stop there.

The Missouri Supreme Court reversed in 1852, rejecting the Scotts' claim in a political and legal climate increasingly hostile to the older freedom doctrine. The case then moved into federal court through a new suit against John F. A. Sandford, the brother of Emerson's widow, whose name was misspelled “Sanford” in the Supreme Court report and helped give the case its familiar formal title.

In 1854, a federal jury found against Dred Scott.

That trial is the center of this chapter even though the Supreme Court opinion three years later is the event everybody knows.

Why insist on the jury trial?

Because the federal verdict reminds us that the Supreme Court did not summon Dred Scott from abstraction. A man and his family had already spent nearly a decade trying to establish freedom through ordinary legal procedure. The constitutional disaster grew out of a real lawsuit with real personal stakes.

The appellate system then made the case much larger than it needed to be.

When the Supreme Court decided *Dred Scott v. Sandford* in 1857, Chief Justice Roger B. Taney's majority opinion addressed questions that reached far beyond whether Scott had become free through his prior residence.

The Court concluded that people of African descent whose ancestors had been brought to America and enslaved were not part of the political community contemplated as citizens in the constitutional sense required for Scott to sue in federal court on diversity grounds. It also declared the Missouri Compromise's restriction on slavery in federal territories unconstitutional, reasoning that Congress lacked the asserted power to deprive slaveholders of property in that way.

The decision attempted to settle slavery's territorial conflict through constitutional law.

It made the conflict worse.

That is why this is the trial of the 1850s.

The decade was already being torn apart by the Fugitive Slave Act, resistance to slave catching, the Kansas-Nebraska Act, violence in “Bleeding Kansas,” political realignment, and the collapse of old party structures. The central national question was no longer whether slavery existed. It was whether slavery would expand, what citizenship meant across states and territories, and whether the federal constitutional order could contain the conflict.

The Court answered with an opinion so broad that it damaged the institution's claim to be merely applying law.

A narrow disposition was available. Questions of jurisdiction and case posture gave the Court routes that would not have required a sweeping constitutional ruling on congressional power over slavery in the territories. Instead, the majority tried to resolve the political crisis.

Judicial overreach often begins with a desire for finality.

The country is divided. Politics seems incapable of settlement. A court possesses authority to announce a rule. The temptation is to write broadly enough that the dispute has nowhere left to go.

But law cannot erase a political conflict by declaring one side constitutionally disabled if the losing side retains political power and rejects the legitimacy of the reasoning.

A judgment can settle a case without settling a country.

*Dred Scott* is the great American warning.

The decision enraged antislavery forces and strengthened the belief among many Republicans that national institutions were being captured by slaveholding interests. Abraham Lincoln would make the case central to his arguments against the expansion of slavery while distinguishing between respect for judgments in individual cases and acceptance of broad political doctrine as permanently settling constitutional meaning.

The decision did not cause the Civil War by itself. No serious history should give one court opinion that kind of monocausal power. The crisis had deeper roots in slavery, expansion, party collapse, sectional economics, violence, and incompatible political visions.

The case intensified those conflicts because it removed imagined compromises.

If Congress could not prohibit slavery in the territories, then one of the principal political mechanisms for containing slavery's expansion had been constitutionally attacked. If Black people could not be citizens in the relevant constitutional sense, then free Black communities in northern states encountered a national judicial statement denying the political status they lived and asserted.

The Court did not merely resolve Scott's personal claim.

It wrote a theory of the republic.

That theory was wrong in law's eventual direction and catastrophic in politics.

The Thirteenth Amendment would abolish slavery. The Fourteenth would constitutionalize national citizenship and equal protection in terms that repudiated the racial citizenship logic of *Dred Scott*. The Civil War and Reconstruction would transform the constitutional order the Taney Court had tried to fix.

The decision's reversal required constitutional amendments and war, not an ordinary appeal.

That scale of correction should make us careful about judicial finality.

Courts are powerful because somebody has to decide cases. Constitutional courts are especially powerful because their decisions can invalidate political choices and bind lower institutions. That authority is useful only if we remember that final does not mean infallible.

The legal system needs ways to live after a final court makes a profound mistake.

American constitutionalism has several.

Later courts can distinguish or overrule precedent.

Congress can legislate within remaining authority.

States can change law within constitutional limits.

Constitutional amendments can supersede decisions.

Political coalitions can transform appointments and doctrine.

The country can change the constitutional text itself.

These correction mechanisms are slow because constitutional stability is supposed to be durable.

Slow correction can be catastrophic when the error concerns human freedom.

Dred and Harriet Scott did not have the luxury of waiting for a constitutional amendment as an academic matter.

Their legal status governed their family.

That is the human cost hidden inside institutional theory.

One reason Harriet Scott is often missing from the name of the case is procedural convention and the way Dred's federal suit became the vehicle for the Supreme Court's decision. But Harriet's freedom claim mattered independently and as part of the family's strategy. Their daughters' status depended on the parents' status. Enslavement was hereditary through the maternal line under the governing law, which meant a legal determination about Harriet reached into the next generation.

Freedom suits were family litigation.

That fact changes the emotional scale.

An individual plaintiff was not only asking whether he could choose his own labor and movement. Parents were trying to prevent children from being legally owned and sold.

The law of status is the law of time.

A property dispute ends when the property transfers. A status regime reproduces itself.

That is why slavery could not be treated as a private contract between an owner and one person. The legal system created inheritance rules, enforcement procedures, interstate obligations, and territorial conflicts that made bondage a national institution even where local law prohibited it.

The Scotts' movement between jurisdictions exposed the problem.

If Missouri could treat them as enslaved after extended residence in free Illinois and free federal territory, what did freedom mean geographically?

If slavery followed a person despite crossing into a jurisdiction that prohibited it, slave law acquired extraterritorial force.

If freedom attached once a person resided on free soil, slaveholders risked losing property claims through mobility.

The conflict could not remain local in a mobile federal republic.

The Fugitive Slave Clause and federal legislation already demonstrated the interstate dimension. Slaveholders demanded national enforcement of claims to people who escaped into free states. Northerners who opposed slavery were required to confront federal law operating in their own communities.

Dred Scott turned the direction around: what happens when an enslaved person is taken by an owner into a place where slavery is unlawful?

The answer became bound up with the power of Congress over territories and the meaning of citizenship.

That expansion of issues is one reason litigation can become politically explosive. A person enters court asking for a remedy. The legal system discovers the remedy depends on a chain of larger propositions. Each proposition creates new losers.

By 1857, nearly every answer on slavery created a sectional loser.

The Supreme Court chose an answer that appeared to constitutionalize the slaveholding position across crucial parts of the dispute.

The Court also made a historical claim about the founding generation's view of Black people. That reasoning has been dismantled by historical scholarship and constitutional change, but its deeper warning remains: judges can use selective history to make contingent hierarchy look original and inevitable.

History carries unusual authority in constitutional cases because the text is old.

That authority can become a disguise for present choice.

A judge cites the past. The citation makes the ruling feel discovered rather than made. But which sources are chosen, how general practices are described, whose citizenship counts, and how contradiction is handled are all acts of judgment.

The *Dred Scott* opinion turned a racial order into constitutional history.

Later generations had to turn it back into a mistake.

There is a personal afterlife that the legal symbol often obscures. The Scotts were ultimately emancipated by private action after the Supreme Court loss. Dred Scott lived only a short time as a free man before his death in 1858. Harriet Scott lived for years afterward.

Their freedom did not come because the Supreme Court corrected itself.

That distinction matters.

A system can fail a person while history later vindicates the person's claim.

The vindication does not refund the years.

This is true of Fred Korematsu, whose conviction would be vacated decades after wartime exclusion. It is true of defendants who spend years imprisoned before exoneration. It is true of people whose civil rights become constitutional only after the law has already permitted irreversible harm.

Legal time and human time are not the same.

Courts can say “eventually” in ways bodies cannot.

The Scotts' case is also a reminder that litigants cannot always control what their case becomes.

Dred Scott sought freedom.

He did not file a lawsuit asking the Supreme Court to produce one of the most notorious race decisions in American history.

Once a case climbs through appellate courts, judges can broaden the question. Lawyers can use it strategically. Political actors can turn it into a national symbol. The plaintiff's name becomes attached to propositions the plaintiff opposed.

That is a strange form of historical possession.

Dred Scott's name is known because the Court denied him.

The legal system that treated him as property then used his name to label the decision that helped protect property in human beings.

Remembering Harriet and the years of litigation is one way to reverse the possession.

The case was theirs before it was Taney's.

It began with a family asking to be free.

That sentence is simpler than the constitutional opinion and more important.

The 1850s gave the Supreme Court an opportunity to decide one family's case within a country already nearing rupture.

The Court tried to decide the country instead.

The country refused to stay decided.
