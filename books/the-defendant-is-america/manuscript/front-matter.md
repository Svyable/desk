# The Defendant Is America

*26 Trials That Explain 250 Years of the United States*

Sven Hardy Benson

There are easier ways to write a history of the United States than to give yourself exactly one courtroom per decade.

A sensible historian can linger where the evidence is rich, move quickly where it is not, and admit that some periods contain a dozen cases that deserve a book while others resist neat selection. A lawyer can organize by doctrine: speech, race, federalism, criminal procedure, executive power. A journalist can choose the trials everybody remembers. A polemicist can choose only the verdicts that prove the point already in hand.

This book does none of those things.

It takes a harder constraint seriously: one trial for each decade from the 1770s through the 2020s. Twenty-six rooms. Twenty-six sets of litigants, defendants, lawyers, witnesses, judges, jurors, reporters, officials, families, crowds, and absences. Twenty-six moments when some part of the country had to stop arguing in the abstract and decide what it was willing to call lawful.

“Most important” is an editorial judgment, not a measurable fact. The phrase is a promise to argue for the choice, not a claim that every reasonable historian would make the same one. Each selected trial had to satisfy three demands. It had to expose a conflict larger than the litigants. Its consequences had to survive the verdict, whether in law, politics, memory, or institutional design. And it had to tell us something about the United States that a list of statutes or Supreme Court holdings could not.

That last requirement matters. The appellate opinion is often what lawyers inherit. The trial is where uncertainty still lives.

At trial, witnesses can fail. Evidence can be excluded. A lawyer can choose the wrong theory. A jury can distrust the right person or believe the wrong one. The public can turn a proceeding into theater. The government can overreach. A defendant can become a symbol without consenting to the role. A technically lawful result can become morally indefensible with time. A verdict celebrated on Friday can look different fifty years later.

Several chapters therefore follow a case beyond the room where evidence was first heard. Dred and Harriet Scott lost in a federal jury trial before the Supreme Court made the case infamous. Fred Korematsu's conviction acquired its full meaning through appellate approval and, decades later, repudiation. Clarence Gideon lost his first trial, won the constitutional principle that gave him counsel, and then returned to a Florida courtroom to be acquitted. The story of a trial can require its afterlife.

I use “trial” in the practical historical sense: a legal proceeding in which the state, a court, a jury, or another authorized tribunal had to test a consequential claim against a person, group, institution, or asserted right. That includes ordinary criminal jury trials, civil freedom suits, a military commission, and the extraordinary occasion when the Supreme Court itself tried a criminal contempt case. It does not mean that every chapter is principally about a Supreme Court argument. The center of gravity stays where facts were contested and consequences were first imposed.

The selections are not a pageant of American progress. Some proceedings corrected injustice. Some formalized it. Some did both at different stages. A trial can be important because the system worked under pressure, because it failed spectacularly, or because the failure forced a later generation to build something different.

Nor are these twenty-six chapters a ranking of human suffering. Importance is not moral worth, and historical influence is not a prize. There are decades in which a different selection would foreground women, workers, immigrants, Native nations, queer Americans, corporate power, environmental harm, or crimes that reshaped local communities without changing national doctrine. Those omissions are real. A book with one chair per decade creates empty chairs on purpose.

The point of the constraint is what it makes visible across time.

In the 1770s, revolutionaries insisted that British soldiers accused of killing colonists receive a defense. In the 1790s, a sitting congressman went to prison under a federal speech law. In the 1860s, civilians accused in the Lincoln assassination conspiracy were tried by military commission. In the 1870s, federal prosecution after the Colfax Massacre tested whether Reconstruction had enough national power to protect Black citizenship. In the 1900s, the Supreme Court put a Tennessee sheriff on trial after a lynch mob defied its order. In the 1950s, an all-white Mississippi jury acquitted the men accused of murdering Emmett Till. In the 1970s, senior officials from a president's inner circle sat before a jury in the Watergate cover-up case. In the 2020s, a former president became a criminal defendant before a Manhattan jury.

The country changes. The recurring question does not.

Who can be put in the defendant's chair, and who cannot?

Sometimes the named defendant is a soldier, a missionary, an anarchist, a Japanese American welder, a poor man accused of burglary, a presidential aide, a neighborhood-watch volunteer, or a former president. Sometimes the deeper defendant is a legal idea: slavery, segregation, sedition, emergency power, racial terror, the insanity defense, official impunity. Sometimes the institution conducting the trial deserves as much scrutiny as the person it judges.

That is the wager of the title.

America is never literally the defendant in these cases. The nation is too large, too divided, and too continuous to fit at counsel table. But a courtroom forces abstractions into choices. It asks which evidence counts, whose testimony is credible, which power is legitimate, which injury the law can see, and whether a rule survives contact with a particular human life.

For two hundred and fifty years, Americans have walked into courtrooms carrying arguments about the country they thought they lived in.

The record is messy because the country is.

That is why it is worth reading.
