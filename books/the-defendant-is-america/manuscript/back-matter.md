# Back Matter

*The Defendant Is America* is narrative history organized by a deliberately restrictive editorial device: one consequential American trial for every decade from the 1770s through the 2020s. The case selection is an argument, not a claim of objective ranking. The accompanying research files preserve source paths, close alternatives, factual cautions, and falsification notes so later editing can distinguish a memorable story from a supportable one.

The manuscript generally uses “America” and “American” in the political-historical sense appropriate to the United States while recognizing that the Americas are larger than the United States and that many Native nations predate and survive it. The book's 250-year frame begins in the decade of the Revolution and follows the legal history of the polity that became the United States.

Trials are reconstructed from public records, archival collections, court materials, government histories, and responsible secondary scholarship. Dialogue is not invented. Where a famous quotation is disputed or survives only in later recollection, the manuscript avoids presenting it as settled fact. Where the surviving record was created by interested authorities—as in the Denmark Vesey proceedings—the source problem is part of the story rather than a nuisance to be hidden.

The 2020s chapter is current through September 5, 2026. Donald Trump's New York conviction remained in place on that date while appellate litigation continued. Future editions should update the chapter's procedural afterlife without rewriting what was knowable at the time of the 2024 trial.

### A note on names

Historical sources use inconsistent names, titles, racial terms, and spellings. This manuscript uses contemporary names where they are clear and preserves older legal terminology only where needed to understand the record. Descriptions such as “enslaved person,” “Black citizen,” “Native nation,” and “Japanese American” are chosen for precision rather than to modernize the beliefs of historical actors.

### A note on verdicts

An acquittal means the prosecution did not establish criminal guilt under the governing standard. It is not a historical finding that every disputed event occurred exactly as the defense maintained. A conviction proves what the legal system treated as established at that time; later reversal, pardon, vacatur, or repudiation may change its legal force without erasing the original proceeding. Civil and criminal outcomes can also differ because the burdens, parties, and claims differ.
