# Chapter 5 — 1810s: The College That Became a Contract

The defendant was not a murderer, a soldier, a rebel, or a politician.

It was a college argument.

That sounds too small for the most important trial of the 1810s until you notice what was hiding inside it: whether a state could rewrite the legal identity of a private institution after political power changed hands, and whether the Constitution's protection of contracts reached corporate charters created before the United States existed.

Dartmouth College began under a royal charter granted in 1769. The institution outlived the king's authority that had created the charter and became part of the new state of New Hampshire. That transition raised a question the Revolution had left everywhere in American law: what happened to old legal obligations after sovereignty changed?

Some inherited arrangements were discarded because independence made them intolerable. Others survived because property, debt, and institutional continuity would become impossible if every revolution erased every legal commitment.

Dartmouth's charter was about to become a national test of the difference.

By the 1810s, the college was caught in political and religious conflict. The details involved personalities, factions, the leadership of President John Wheelock, trustees, and New Hampshire politics. The state legislature intervened in 1816 by passing laws that transformed Dartmouth College into Dartmouth University, expanded state influence, and altered the governing structure. The original trustees refused to accept the change.

William H. Woodward, who had been secretary and treasurer of the college, sided with the state-created arrangement and retained institutional property including records and the corporate seal. The trustees sued him.

A dispute over who possessed papers and a seal became a constitutional case about the nature of the American corporation.

The trustees lost in New Hampshire's courts. The state defended its authority to alter a corporation associated with public education and created under a royal charter. The trustees argued that the charter remained a protected contract and that the legislature could not simply rewrite it.

Daniel Webster, a Dartmouth alumnus, became the most famous advocate for the college when the dispute reached the Supreme Court.

The later legend of Webster's emotional courtroom performance can obscure the institutional stakes. The case mattered because the Contract Clause of the Constitution prohibited states from passing laws impairing the obligation of contracts. If a charter counted as a contract protected by that clause, the state had encountered a constitutional limit on its ability to reorganize existing private corporations.

Chief Justice John Marshall's Court held that it did.

The Dartmouth charter was treated as a contract, and New Hampshire's legislative alterations impaired it. The original trustees prevailed.

The immediate winner was a college.

The larger winner was a particular conception of private institutional continuity.

The decision became important to American economic development because corporations needed confidence that a change in state politics would not automatically permit legislatures to rewrite their foundational arrangements. Investors, charities, educational institutions, and businesses could plan around legal forms that possessed some protection from direct political alteration.

That protection is easy to celebrate if we tell the story only from the perspective of security.

Stable commitments matter. A government that can confiscate or restructure every private institution whenever a new faction wins power makes long-term cooperation difficult. Contracts allow people to coordinate across time precisely because tomorrow's preferences are not supposed to erase today's obligations.

But constitutionalizing corporate charters also moves power away from democratic revision.

That is the deeper reason Dartmouth is the trial of the 1810s.

The young republic was deciding not only who governed but what government could no longer touch after it had granted or inherited rights.

Every strong protection creates a zone of weaker public control.

The Contract Clause restrains states for a reason. The restraint can also protect arrangements that later majorities would prefer to change. The history of American corporate law is partly the history of negotiating that tension: how to provide enough stability for institutions to exist across generations without turning legal privileges into untouchable private sovereignty.

Dartmouth did not answer every later question. States adapted. Corporate charters changed. Legislatures increasingly used general incorporation laws rather than granting special charters one by one, and states learned to reserve powers to alter or repeal corporate arrangements under specified conditions. Constitutional doctrine evolved.

The case's importance lies in the architecture it revealed.

A corporation could possess a legal identity and contractual rights that survived the political coalition that created them.

The dispute looks very different from the trials around it in this book. There is no threat of execution. No one is enslaved by the judgment. There is no mob outside waiting to enforce a racial order. It would be a mistake to equate the human stakes.

Importance does not require equal suffering.

Institutions shape lives through quieter mechanisms.

A legal system that stabilizes corporate forms can influence where capital goes, how universities govern themselves, how charities hold property, and how private organizations accumulate power. The effects diffuse through decades rather than arriving as a sentence pronounced against one body.

The 1810s were a period when the United States was building that invisible infrastructure.

The War of 1812 had tested national survival. Economic development, banks, transportation projects, manufacturing, and westward expansion were changing the scale at which Americans organized. The legal meaning of corporations was not a technical side issue. It was part of deciding how a growing country would coordinate resources beyond individual lifetimes.

Dartmouth's case also exposes a feature of constitutional litigation that becomes easy to forget: great principles often enter court through a fight over control.

The trustees were not a seminar discussing private rights. The New Hampshire legislature was not writing a neutral treatise on democratic power. Both sides wanted the institution.

The state believed it could reorganize Dartmouth under a new public structure.

The trustees wanted their charter and authority preserved.

The Constitution became the language through which the losing political faction could ask another institution—the federal judiciary—to stop the state.

That pattern is fundamental to American government.

Federalism and judicial review create multiple venues for losers.

Lose in the legislature, sue in court.

Lose in state court, invoke federal constitutional limits.

Lose an election, organize for the next one.

Lose in one jurisdiction, try another if the law allows.

The system can look inefficient because it is designed to make some decisions hard to finalize.

The appeal structure is a hedge against concentrated power.

It is also a tool sophisticated and wealthy actors can use repeatedly.

Corporate litigants eventually become among the most skilled users of constitutional and statutory rights. That history makes Dartmouth a useful origin point for a question still alive in modern America: when does protecting an institution from government preserve pluralism, and when does it entrench private power?

The answer changes with the institution.

A church, newspaper, university, labor union, nonprofit, and multinational corporation may all invoke forms of autonomy, but the social meaning of that autonomy differs. Law frequently uses general categories because it cannot write a constitution for every entity. The cases at the boundaries define what the category can become.

Dartmouth helped make “corporation” a durable legal container.

It is tempting to project modern corporations backward and imagine the case as though Marshall were deciding the constitutional rights of a twenty-first-century technology company. He was not. Dartmouth was an educational and charitable institution created by charter. The corporate economy that later emerged was not sitting fully formed in the courtroom.

The better historical lesson is about path dependence.

A rule built for one institutional setting can become infrastructure for another.

Legal forms travel.

So do constitutional protections.

The same is true elsewhere in this book. A principle established for an unpopular criminal defendant becomes protection for millions of ordinary defendants. A wartime decision justified as emergency can become precedent people later try to use beyond the emergency. A civil-rights enforcement ruling can determine whether federal power reaches private violence. The original facts matter, but law outlives them.

This is why old cases are dangerous when reduced to slogans.

“Dartmouth protected contracts” is true.

It hides the struggle over state power, the royal origin of the charter, the factional politics, the institutional property, and the transformation of a local college dispute into a national rule.

The trial and appeals remind us that constitutional law is frequently a machine for converting local conflict into general constraint.

The New Hampshire legislature wanted to do one thing to one college.

The Supreme Court's answer changed what legislatures across states had to consider when dealing with chartered corporations.

That scaling effect is one reason courts attract political attention. A legislature can pass a rule for its jurisdiction. A constitutional holding can redefine the permissible range for many jurisdictions at once.

The federal judiciary thereby becomes an economic institution, even when judges do not think of themselves as economic planners.

Property and contract decisions shape the cost of political risk.

If a corporation believes its charter can be rewritten after every election, long-term investment becomes more dangerous. If a corporation believes no future legislature can meaningfully regulate it, public power becomes weaker. The legal system spends the next two centuries trying to locate the middle.

Dartmouth moved the line toward stability.

It also taught states to write charters differently.

This adaptive response matters. Constitutional decisions do not merely command obedience; they change drafting behavior. If the Constitution protects a charter from impairment, a state may reserve alteration power in the charter itself. If a court interprets a statute narrowly, a legislature can revise the statute. If a criminal rule produces an unwanted result, lawmakers can change the elements for future cases within constitutional bounds.

Law is strategic interaction across institutions.

A verdict is a move, not always the end of the game.

The case also gives us a quieter version of the theme that began with Aaron Burr: what happens when a political branch wants one outcome and a court imposes another?

In Burr's treason trial, judicial insistence on a narrow constitutional definition frustrated Jefferson's prosecution.

In Dartmouth, federal constitutional interpretation overrode New Hampshire's legislative choice.

Both cases increase judicial power in the name of limiting political power.

That can be good or bad depending on what the court protects.

The structure does not guarantee virtue.

Later Supreme Courts will use constitutional doctrine to protect slavery, weaken Reconstruction, uphold segregation, and approve wartime exclusion. Judicial independence that protects Dartmouth from legislative reorganization is the same institutional independence that can produce *Dred Scott*.

There is no constitutional design in which only the wise decision maker receives final authority.

Power comes bundled with error.

The question is how the system exposes and corrects error over time.

Dartmouth's afterlife was constructive in important ways. It strengthened confidence in private charters while states and legislatures developed new methods of corporate regulation and incorporation. The doctrine did not freeze all corporations forever. But it contributed to a legal environment in which institutions could survive political turnover.

Survival across turnover is a form of freedom.

It is also a form of power.

Universities understand this intuitively. A university that can be reorganized whenever a governor dislikes its leadership is not intellectually independent. A university that is entirely immune from public law can become unaccountable. The boundary is not solved by saying “private” or “public.” It has to be designed.

The Dartmouth litigation was an early design fight disguised as a property dispute.

That is why the physical objects in the case—the charter, records, seal—matter symbolically without needing invented drama.

Institutions exist through documents people agree to treat as authoritative. A corporate seal is only an object until law says it authenticates the acts of an entity that outlives the people holding it. A charter is only paper until courts say the commitments on it bind future governments.

The American republic was learning how much continuity to give paper.

The choice would make enormous organizations possible.

It would also give future generations of lawyers endless work deciding when continuity had become entrenchment.

The most important trials are not always the ones with the loudest crowds.

Sometimes a country's future is hiding in who gets to keep the seal.
