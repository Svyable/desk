# Chapter 26 — 2020s: The Former President Becomes a Defendant

The first thing to notice was how ordinary the room looked.

A judge.

A jury box.

Lawyers at counsel tables.

Court officers.

Reporters waiting outside.

The defendant was Donald J. Trump.

That was the extraordinary part.

He had been president of the United States. He was the leading candidate to become president again. He was surrounded by security, politics, cameras, protesters, supporters, fundraising appeals, campaign statements, constitutional arguments, and a national information system capable of turning every ruling into partisan ammunition within seconds.

Inside the courtroom, the criminal case still had to be reduced to counts, witnesses, exhibits, objections, instructions, and a verdict.

On May 30, 2024, a New York jury found Trump guilty on thirty-four felony counts of falsifying business records in the first degree.

He became the first former American president convicted of a crime.

Then he won the presidency again.

That sequence makes the trial of the 2020s unlike any other chapter in this book.

Earlier cases asked whether law would protect unpopular soldiers, free an enslaved man, punish political speech, constrain a state, survive war, provide counsel to the poor, reach presidential aides, understand mental illness, judge a celebrity, sentence a terrorist, or evaluate lethal fear.

The Trump case asked whether ordinary state criminal process could be applied to a former president while he was actively seeking to become president again—and what a verdict would mean if voters later returned him to power.

The answer remains unfinished.

That is important to say at the beginning.

The conviction still stood in late August 2026. Trump had received an unconditional discharge at sentencing in January 2025, meaning no jail, probation, or fine was imposed. He returned to the White House ten days later. He continued efforts to overturn the conviction, including arguments involving presidential immunity and attempts to move the state case into federal court. On August 28, 2026, a federal judge again rejected a removal effort. Further appellate litigation remained possible.

This chapter therefore cannot offer the neat finality hindsight gives the Boston Massacre or Gideon.

We are still inside the afterlife of the verdict.

That makes the case more useful, not less.

A country rarely knows which trial will look most important two generations later. The 2020s give us the unusual experience of watching historical significance form before appellate law has finished speaking.

The charges grew from a payment made before the 2016 election.

Adult-film performer Stormy Daniels had alleged a sexual encounter with Trump years earlier, an allegation Trump denied. During the final weeks of the 2016 campaign, Trump's then-lawyer Michael Cohen paid Daniels $130,000 under a nondisclosure arrangement. The criminal prosecution focused not on whether the alleged encounter occurred but on business records created when Cohen was reimbursed after the election.

The Manhattan District Attorney alleged that the records falsely characterized reimbursement payments as legal expenses and that the falsification was intended to conceal an unlawful effort to influence the election.

That distinction between scandal and charge was essential.

Public discussion often called the case the hush-money trial.

The indictment charged falsifying business records.

Trials become distorted when the cultural label replaces the legal elements.

The jury was not asked whether paying for silence is morally attractive.

It was not asked whether voters should have known about Daniels's allegation.

It was not asked whether Trump was a good president or a dangerous politician.

It was asked whether prosecutors proved thirty-four specified business-record felonies beyond a reasonable doubt under New York law.

The narrowing was difficult because the defendant's political identity could not be removed from the room.

Trump argued that the prosecution was politically motivated. His supporters described the case as election interference through criminal law. Prosecutors insisted they were applying ordinary state law to conduct that would be prosecuted regardless of the defendant's status.

Both claims were ultimately arguments about legitimacy as much as law.

A prosecution can be legally sufficient and still be attacked as selective.

A defendant can be politically powerful and still violate ordinary law.

The existence of political consequences does not automatically make a prosecution political in the improper sense.

Nor does the existence of legal elements automatically eliminate questions about prosecutorial discretion.

Every criminal justice system chooses which cases to charge.

The legitimacy question is whether that discretion is exercised according to lawful, defensible criteria rather than partisan desire.

The trial could not settle that question for every citizen because people's judgments about the prosecution were entangled with their judgments about Trump.

This is the central problem of prosecuting political leaders.

If the leader is not prosecuted, critics say power has created immunity.

If the leader is prosecuted, supporters say law has become a weapon against political opposition.

Either outcome can undermine trust.

The system therefore has only one durable defense: process strong enough to be inspected.

Public charges.

An identifiable statute.

A judge subject to appellate review.

A jury selected through ordinary procedures.

Evidence tested by defense counsel.

A burden of proof.

Written rulings.

A record.

Appeal.

None of those guarantees universal agreement.

They create something more modest: a structure through which disagreement can continue without making the defendant's political importance the rule of decision.

Jury selection illustrated the problem immediately.

How do you find New Yorkers with no opinions about Donald Trump?

You do not.

The legal goal is not jurors without history. It is jurors capable of setting personal political preferences aside and deciding the charged case from admitted evidence and instructions.

That is a more realistic theory of impartiality.

Americans often imagine an impartial juror as an empty vessel.

In famous trials, that person does not exist.

The juror has seen headlines, elections, speeches, social media, investigations, impeachments, lawsuits, and perhaps the defendant's own commentary on the case.

Impartiality becomes a discipline rather than an absence of knowledge.

Can you decide this case on this record?

The question sounds thin compared with the national stakes.

It is the only workable one.

The prosecution called twenty-two witnesses. Documentary evidence included checks, invoices, bank records, phone records, messages, and other business materials. Michael Cohen became the central witness because he had made the payment and later received reimbursements.

Cohen was also a difficult witness.

He had pleaded guilty to federal crimes, including offenses involving the payment. He had admitted lying in other settings. He had become one of Trump's public antagonists.

The defense attacked his credibility aggressively.

That was not a distraction from the case.

It was the case.

When prosecutors rely on a cooperating witness with a history of dishonesty, jurors must decide whether documents and other testimony corroborate enough of the account to make it reliable.

The law does not require saints as witnesses.

Criminal schemes rarely produce them.

But the less trustworthy the witness, the more important independent evidence becomes.

Watergate had faced the same problem with insiders who had motives to shift blame.

Organizational crimes are often proved through participants because outsiders do not attend the meetings.

The justice system must use compromised witnesses without becoming compromised by them.

That requires disclosure, cross-examination, corroboration, and jury skepticism.

Trump did not testify.

That fact became another demonstration of the difference between politics and criminal procedure.

Politicians are expected to answer accusations publicly. Criminal defendants possess a constitutional right not to testify, and jurors may not treat silence as evidence of guilt.

A defendant can speak endlessly outside the courthouse and remain silent inside it.

The public may find the distinction strange.

The Constitution does not.

The prosecution bears the burden.

The defendant does not have to take the stand and rescue himself.

That principle has protected obscure defendants for generations.

It protected a former president too.

The case also created repeated conflict over extrajudicial statements.

Judge Juan Merchan imposed and later modified an order restricting certain public comments after finding a need to protect the integrity of the proceeding and participants. Trump argued that the order infringed his political speech during a presidential campaign.

The tension was real.

A criminal court has authority to protect witnesses, jurors, staff, and the fairness of trial.

A leading presidential candidate has a strong First Amendment interest in speaking about a prosecution that is itself a major campaign issue.

The usual defendant can be told not to attack a witness publicly without affecting a national election.

The usual candidate is not a criminal defendant with power to direct millions of followers' attention toward people inside the case.

Old procedural tools encountered new political scale.

That is one reason the trial belongs to its decade.

The system had rules for speech and trials.

It had never applied them under these exact conditions.

The Supreme Court then changed the constitutional environment while Trump's posttrial motions were pending.

On July 1, 2024, the Court decided *Trump v. United States*, holding that presidents possess broad immunity from criminal prosecution for official acts, with absolute protection for some core constitutional functions and presumptive immunity for other official conduct.

The New York case involved conduct prosecutors characterized principally as private and campaign-related, but evidence at trial included events and communications from Trump's presidential years.

Trump argued that the new immunity doctrine required the verdict to be set aside because some admitted evidence concerned official acts.

Judge Merchan rejected that argument and later declined to vacate the conviction.

The immunity dispute illustrates how appellate law can arrive after a jury verdict and alter the framework around evidence the jury already heard.

A trial is not sealed off from constitutional change.

New precedent can reopen questions that appeared settled when testimony began.

The doctrine also sharpened the central tension of this chapter.

Presidents require some immunity because executive power cannot function if every official decision becomes a future criminal charge brought by political enemies.

Presidents cannot possess unlimited criminal immunity without creating a dangerous class of official above ordinary law.

The Constitution must protect the office without converting the officeholder into a sovereign.

That is not a problem one case can solve permanently.

The New York trial forced the problem into contact with state criminal law.

The prosecution was brought by a local elected district attorney, not the federal government.

That federalism mattered.

The president controls the executive branch of the United States. He does not control New York's prosecution system.

A state criminal case can therefore create accountability outside federal executive hierarchy.

That independence can look like a safeguard against centralized immunity.

It can also create fears that local prosecutors in politically hostile jurisdictions might target national figures.

Both concerns are structurally plausible.

Federalism distributes power.

Distributed power can protect liberty and multiply conflict.

The American design relies on courts to referee jurisdiction, immunity, removal, supremacy, and due process without pretending conflict will disappear.

Trump repeatedly sought to move the case from state court to federal court.

Federal courts rejected those efforts before trial and again after conviction. In August 2026, Judge Alvin Hellerstein concluded that the case concerned personal conduct not transformed into official presidential acts merely because some evidence touched his time in office.

The conviction therefore remained in place while further litigation continued.

The jury's May 2024 decision had been unanimous on all thirty-four counts.

That unanimity did not produce national consensus.

Nothing about Trump had produced national consensus for years.

Supporters saw the conviction as confirmation that Democratic officials would use courts to stop a political opponent they could not defeat fairly.

Critics saw it as proof that even a former president could be held accountable for ordinary financial deception.

Independent observers debated the novelty of the legal theory, the strength of appellate issues, the quality of the evidence, the sentence, and the implications for future prosecutions of political leaders.

A verdict can be legally unanimous and politically polarizing.

Those are different populations answering different questions.

The twelve jurors decided guilt under instructions.

The electorate later decided who should be president.

In November 2024 voters returned Trump to office.

The political verdict did not erase the criminal verdict.

The criminal verdict did not disqualify the electorate from choosing him.

The two decisions coexist because the Constitution does not make a New York felony conviction an automatic bar to the presidency.

That coexistence is one of the most remarkable facts in American legal history.

A man could be a convicted felon and president-elect at the same time.

The system had no simple box for the combination because no earlier president had created it.

Sentencing became the next institutional puzzle.

Ordinarily, a person convicted of thirty-four felonies appears before a judge who considers punishment under state law. Trump was days away from inauguration when sentencing finally occurred on January 10, 2025.

Judge Merchan imposed an unconditional discharge.

No incarceration.

No probation.

No fine.

The conviction remained, but the sentence imposed no continuing punitive restraint that would interfere with presidential duties.

The result satisfied almost nobody who had approached the case expecting a symbolic endpoint.

Critics of Trump could see it as too lenient.

Supporters could see any criminal judgment as illegitimate.

The sentence reflected the strange constitutional posture: a jury verdict had to be given legal effect while the defendant was about to assume the presidency.

Again the law produced a differentiated answer when politics wanted a total one.

Guilty.

No punishment beyond the judgment itself.

Appeal continues.

Presidency continues.

That is not a slogan.

It is a system trying to hold incompatible-seeming facts at once.

The case belongs beside Matthew Lyon's prosecution in the 1790s for another reason.

Lyon was a sitting congressman convicted under a law enacted by political opponents, campaigned from jail, and was reelected.

The voters' decision did not erase his conviction. His conviction did not prevent voters from returning him to office. Political judgment and legal judgment collided in the first decade of the federal republic.

More than two centuries later, the collision returned at presidential scale.

The comparison should not be pushed too far.

Lyon was convicted for political speech under a Sedition Act later condemned as a constitutional disgrace. Trump was convicted for business-record falsification under a general state statute after a jury trial, and the conviction remained under active challenge as this book was drafted.

The legal merits are different.

The structural rhyme is still useful.

Democracy does not outsource every judgment to courts.

Courts do not outsource criminal guilt to elections.

A person can win one forum and lose another.

That separation is frustrating because Americans often want one institution to settle the whole dispute.

No institution can.

The Trump trial also transformed the courthouse into a campaign location.

The defendant spoke to cameras before and after proceedings. Allies appeared outside. Opponents demonstrated nearby. Fundraising messages converted rulings into political appeals. News organizations treated each day as both trial coverage and election coverage.

Simpson had shown what happens when television turns a trial into mass culture.

Trump showed what happens when a defendant already owns a political media ecosystem capable of contesting the court's legitimacy in real time.

The shadow trial no longer merely interpreted the evidence.

It challenged the authority of the forum itself.

That is a different kind of pressure.

A court depends on compliance it cannot generate through force alone. Judges can sanction contempt, control a courtroom, and issue orders. They cannot make half a country trust them.

Legitimacy must be earned through procedure and explanation over time.

The same is true of prosecutors.

The same is true of defendants who claim persecution.

Evidence has to survive outside the slogans eventually.

That is why the written record matters.

Future historians will not have to rely only on campaign speeches. They will have the indictment, motions, transcripts, exhibits, jury instructions, verdict sheet, sentencing rulings, appellate filings, and decisions on immunity and removal.

The trial generated a documentary trail through which later generations can judge both the defendant and the prosecution.

That may be the most important safeguard in a case where present-day consensus is impossible.

Time can inspect the record.

The earlier chapters teach caution about assuming time will agree with us.

The Supreme Court thought *Dred Scott* might settle slavery.

It inflamed the crisis.

*Plessy* looked like constitutional law for generations before becoming a symbol of judicial failure.

Korematsu remained on the books long after the government apologized for the policy it upheld.

Haymarket defendants were executed before a governor denounced the proceeding.

Leo Frank was lynched after a governor commuted his sentence.

History is full of confident verdicts whose reputations reverse.

The Trump conviction may be affirmed, narrowed, vacated, or otherwise altered through legal proceedings not complete at the time of this writing.

The political meaning may change again.

A serious account cannot write the ending in advance.

What can be said now is narrower.

The United States crossed a threshold.

A former president was indicted by a state grand jury.

He appeared for trial.

He sat at the defense table.

Witnesses testified in his presence.

His lawyers cross-examined them.

A judge ruled on his objections.

Twelve jurors deliberated.

They convicted him on every count.

He remained free while seeking review.

He won the next presidential election.

A judge imposed an unconditional discharge.

He returned to the White House with the conviction still standing.

Whatever one thinks of the prosecution, those facts permanently alter the American imagination of presidential accountability.

The presidency had once appeared too politically explosive for ordinary criminal process.

Watergate ended with Nixon pardoned before trial while his aides went to prison.

Bill Clinton faced impeachment and later professional sanctions but no criminal trial.

Trump became the first to cross the courtroom threshold.

Crossing a threshold does not tell us whether every future prosecution will be justified.

It tells us the door exists.

That creates responsibilities for everyone who comes after.

Prosecutors will know a former president can be charged.

They must therefore be more careful, not less, about evidence, consistency, and political neutrality.

Presidents will know office does not necessarily erase exposure for private conduct.

They must therefore be more careful, not less, about the boundary between public authority and personal interest.

Judges will know rulings can affect elections while still needing to be legal rulings rather than political calculations.

Voters will know they may be asked to choose candidates carrying unresolved legal judgments.

The system becomes more complicated after precedent is made.

That is true even when the precedent is factual rather than doctrinal.

The most important line in the 2020s trial was not spoken in court.

It was implied by the seating chart.

The former president sat where defendants sit.

That image answered one question and opened ten others.

Could he receive a fair trial in a deeply Democratic city?

Could jurors separate politics from evidence?

Could prosecutors separate law enforcement from political consequence?

Could a judge enforce ordinary courtroom rules against a presidential candidate?

Could a conviction survive new presidential-immunity doctrine?

Could the state sentence a president-elect without interfering with federal supremacy?

Could voters return a convicted man to the presidency?

Could a president continue challenging a state conviction while exercising federal executive power?

Could the country disagree about all of it without making the courtroom irrelevant?

Those questions remain live.

This book began in 1770 with British soldiers hated by Boston receiving trials rather than revolutionary punishment.

It ends, for now, with a president loved by millions and hated by millions sitting before a jury whose legal task was smaller than the country's argument about him.

That symmetry is useful.

The rule of law is easy to praise when it produces the person you want punished and protects the person you want protected.

Its real test is whether the procedure remains recognizable when political identity makes every outcome feel existential.

Donald Trump called the prosecution illegitimate.

Prosecutors called the conduct criminal.

The jury convicted.

Voters reelected.

Judges continued to hear challenges.

No single institution got the final word on everything.

That may be the most American ending available.

The defendant was a former president.

Then the defendant became president again.

The verdict remained.

The appeal remained.

The argument remained.

Two hundred and fifty years after John Adams defended the soldiers on King Street, the country was still trying to answer the same basic question under harder conditions:

Can law judge a person without pretending it has judged the whole nation that stands behind him?
