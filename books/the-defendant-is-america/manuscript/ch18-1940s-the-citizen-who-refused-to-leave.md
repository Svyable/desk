# Chapter 18 — 1940s: The Citizen Who Would Not Leave

Fred Korematsu did not announce a constitutional theory when he stayed in California.

He wanted to stay near his girlfriend.

That ordinary motive matters because history has a habit of laundering test cases into symbols. The symbol becomes so clean that the person disappears. Korematsu was a twenty-three-year-old welder from Oakland when the United States government decided that ancestry could determine whether an American citizen had to leave his home, report to a control station, enter a detention system, and live under military exclusion.

He resisted.

The government prosecuted him.

That is why the trial of the 1940s is not Pearl Harbor, not the internment policy in the abstract, and not even the Supreme Court opinion by itself. It is the criminal case against one citizen whose refusal made the national emergency answer a small, dangerous question:

What can the government make citizenship mean when it is afraid?

The answer the country gave in the 1940s was among the worst in its constitutional history.

After Japan attacked Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941, the United States entered a war of extraordinary scale. Fear of invasion on the West Coast was not imaginary. Japan had demonstrated military reach across the Pacific. American forces had suffered a devastating surprise attack. Public officials faced uncertainty about sabotage, espionage, military vulnerability, and what might happen next.

Real danger creates the hardest civil-liberties cases because the government does not need to invent every premise.

The emergency can be genuine.

The constitutional failure can be genuine too.

President Franklin Roosevelt signed Executive Order 9066 in February 1942. The order authorized military commanders to designate areas from which people could be excluded. In practice, the policy became a system of forced removal and incarceration aimed overwhelmingly at people of Japanese ancestry on the West Coast, including American citizens.

The legal machinery was administrative before it was carceral.

Instructions.

Zones.

Curfews.

Exclusion orders.

Assembly centers.

Relocation centers.

Bureaucracy is especially useful when government wants to make coercion look procedural. A family can be removed from a home through a sequence of notices, forms, deadlines, tags, train movements, guard assignments, and regulations. Each step looks smaller than the policy it serves.

Korematsu violated an exclusion order.

He had changed his name and altered his appearance in an effort to avoid removal. He was arrested in San Leandro in May 1942. The United States prosecuted him in federal court for remaining in a military area contrary to the exclusion order. He was convicted, placed on probation, and eventually sent through the incarceration system the government had created.

The case moved upward.

By the time the Supreme Court decided *Korematsu v. United States* in December 1944, the Court was not confronting a speculative policy. Tens of thousands of people of Japanese ancestry had already been uprooted. Families had lost homes, businesses, farms, possessions, schooling, routines, and years of ordinary liberty. The question arrived after the machinery had done much of its work.

That timing is part of the story.

Courts are often slow in exactly the cases where government is fast.

An emergency order can take effect tomorrow. A constitutional challenge can take years. The state acts first and litigates later. Even a later victory may not restore the time, property, health, dignity, or relationships lost while the case moved through appeals.

Korematsu did not get the later victory in 1944.

The Supreme Court upheld his conviction.

The majority accepted exclusion as justified by military necessity rather than racial hostility. The opinion is often remembered for language acknowledging that legal restrictions curtailing the civil rights of a single racial group are immediately suspect. That sentence can sound like the seed of strict scrutiny. But in the actual case before it, the Court allowed the policy to stand.

A doctrinal warning accompanied a constitutional surrender.

Justice Hugo Black wrote for the majority. The Court insisted it was not approving hostility toward Japanese Americans as a race. It treated the exclusion order as a wartime measure connected to the risk of espionage and sabotage.

The distinction was everything.

If the policy was racial punishment, it was indefensible.

If it was military necessity, judges were reluctant to substitute their judgment for commanders during war.

The government's argument therefore depended on transforming ancestry into a security proxy while denying that the proxy was racial in the constitutionally relevant sense.

That is one of the oldest moves in emergency law.

The classification is not prejudice, officials say. It is risk management.

The burden is not punishment. It is prevention.

The action is not permanent. It is temporary.

The judiciary should not interfere because officials possess information and responsibilities courts do not.

Each proposition may contain some truth. Together they can build a structure in which constitutional protection disappears exactly when the government claims it needs the broadest discretion.

The dissents saw the danger more clearly.

Justice Frank Murphy described the exclusion as falling into the ugly abyss of racism. Justice Robert Jackson warned about what happens when courts convert military expediency into constitutional doctrine. The military can make mistakes under pressure and later change course. A judicial precedent can outlive the emergency.

Jackson's concern points to the difference between government action and judicial validation.

A commander may act unlawfully and be judged by history.

A court that blesses the action helps turn the exception into law.

That is why bad constitutional decisions can be more dangerous than bad executive decisions. They do not merely permit the present act. They teach future officials how the system can be made to permit another.

Korematsu's conviction survived the war.

The factual foundation beneath the government's position did not survive nearly as well.

Decades later, researchers and lawyers uncovered evidence that government officials had withheld, altered, or suppressed material bearing on the claim of military necessity. Intelligence assessments had not supported the sweeping assumption that Japanese Americans as a group posed the danger asserted in court. The government's presentation to the judiciary had been less candid than the stakes demanded.

In 1983, Korematsu returned to federal court through a petition for a writ of error coram nobis, an old procedural device used to attack a conviction after ordinary routes had closed.

The technical name fits the larger theme of this book.

American correction often arrives through procedural doors that sound too small for the history moving through them.

Judge Marilyn Hall Patel vacated Korematsu's conviction.

She did not erase the Supreme Court's 1944 opinion from the official reports. A district court could not do that. But the criminal judgment against the man at the center of the case was undone after evidence emerged that the government had failed in its duty of candor.

The defendant outlived the verdict.

The precedent lingered longer.

Congress later enacted the Civil Liberties Act of 1988, formally apologizing for the incarceration program and providing redress payments to surviving detainees. The statute acknowledged that the policy had been driven by racial prejudice, wartime hysteria, and a failure of political leadership.

That phrase—failure of political leadership—deserves as much attention as the racial prejudice.

Prejudice alone does not load families onto trains.

Institutions do.

A president signs.

A military command issues orders.

Congress supplies legal authority and funding.

Police arrest.

Prosecutors charge.

Judges hear cases.

The Supreme Court decides.

A bureaucracy processes the consequences.

Constitutional failure is usually collaborative.

So is constitutional repair.

Activists preserve records. Lawyers reopen files. Historians challenge official narratives. Judges revisit convictions. Congress apologizes. Presidents award honors. Schools teach what the government once defended.

The correction is real.

It is also late.

That lateness is the hardest thing about Korematsu.

The country eventually came to a broad consensus that the incarceration was unjust. But the families who lost years did not receive those years back. The people who died before apology did not hear it. The farms and businesses sold under pressure were not restored by moral clarity four decades later. Children who grew up behind fences did not retroactively grow up elsewhere.

Law is unusually good at declaring that the past was wrong.

It is much worse at giving the past back.

That is why timing belongs inside any serious theory of rights.

A constitutional protection that works only after the emergency is over is not the same protection as one that restrains government during the emergency.

The 1940s made that failure vivid.

There is another temptation in remembering the case: to treat it as an artifact of a less enlightened America.

That would be comforting and false.

The mechanism remains available whenever a government confronts fear associated with identity.

A foreign attack produces suspicion toward a domestic population.

Officials possess incomplete intelligence.

The public demands action.

Individualized assessment looks slow.

Group classification looks efficient.

The people burdened by the policy are politically weak.

Courts hesitate to challenge executive claims of security.

The program is described as temporary.

Later evidence shows the danger was overstated, the proxy was crude, or the government was less candid than it appeared.

Nothing in that sequence belongs only to 1942.

The names change.

The structure survives.

Korematsu also belongs beside the earlier chapters because he exposes a recurring contradiction in American citizenship.

Quock Walker asked whether constitutional equality could dissolve a claim of ownership.

Dred and Harriet Scott were told by the Supreme Court that Black people descended from enslaved persons could not claim national citizenship in the way their lawsuit required.

The Reconstruction Amendments rewrote that constitutional order.

By 1942 Fred Korematsu's citizenship was not disputed.

That was not enough.

The government did not need to deny that he was American. It could burden his citizenship by treating ancestry as a reason he could not remain where other citizens remained.

Citizenship on paper and citizenship in practice separated.

This is an American pattern worth naming because formal inclusion is often the beginning rather than the end of equality.

You may be a citizen and still be excluded from a train car.

A citizen and denied a lawyer.

A citizen and prevented from voting.

A citizen and moved from your home because the government distrusts your ancestry.

A citizen and treated by police, juries, schools, lenders, employers, or bureaucracies as though your claim on the country is conditional.

Trials expose the gap because the defendant forces the institution to say which version of citizenship governs the case.

Korematsu forced the government to say it plainly: citizenship did not prevent exclusion.

The Court agreed.

History did not.

In 2018, the Supreme Court finally said in another case that *Korematsu* was gravely wrong the day it was decided and had no place in law under the Constitution. The declaration came in *Trump v. Hawaii*, a case about a presidential entry restriction affecting nationals of several countries, most of them Muslim-majority. The majority rejected the analogy between the two policies while repudiating *Korematsu*.

Even that moment was contested because the Court was upholding another national-security policy challenged as discriminatory.

History rarely offers a clean ceremony of learning.

A precedent can be condemned while the argument over what the lesson requires continues in the same opinion.

That is precisely why *Korematsu* should not be reduced to a moral slogan.

The useful question is not whether modern Americans agree the incarceration was wrong.

Most do.

The useful question is what institutions should do before hindsight becomes available.

How much evidence should a government need before burdening liberty by ancestry?

How skeptical should a court be when officials invoke intelligence it cannot fully disclose?

What happens when the people affected have little political power?

Who audits the factual record presented to judges?

What remedies are fast enough to matter during an emergency?

How should courts distinguish genuine deference from abdication?

What should happen when government lawyers discover facts that weaken the government's own case?

These are not museum questions.

They are operational questions for every constitutional democracy that intends to survive fear without becoming governed by it.

The Korematsu prosecution also makes visible something the word internment can hide.

The policy was enforced through criminal law.

This was not only an administrative relocation program happening somewhere outside the courthouse. People who violated orders could be arrested, prosecuted, convicted, and punished. The criminal case gave coercive teeth to a racialized national policy.

A criminal conviction carries a special moral claim.

The state does not merely say: comply.

It says: you were wrong to refuse.

That is why vacating Korematsu's conviction mattered decades later even after incarceration had ended. The judgment embodied the government's insistence that his resistance was the legal wrong.

The 1983 order reversed that relationship.

The record had become evidence against the government.

This pattern will return in later chapters. Gideon will show a conviction becoming the vehicle for building a right to counsel. Watergate will show criminal process moving toward officials near the center of executive power. Hinckley will show the public recoiling from a verdict that follows the legal definition of insanity. The Trump trial will force the country to confront how criminal process applies to a former and future president.

Every one of these cases asks who gets to define the wrong.

Korematsu's answer changed over time.

In 1942, the government said his refusal to leave was the wrong.

In 1944, the Supreme Court upheld the conviction.

In 1983, the conviction was vacated after the government's wartime presentation came apart.

In 1988, Congress apologized for the policy.

In 2018, the Supreme Court repudiated the precedent.

The chronology looks like national learning.

It is.

It also looks like forty years of delay before the defendant's record was cleared and more than seventy before the Supreme Court formally disowned what it had done.

That is the other meaning of the chronology.

A country can correct itself.

A country can take a lifetime to do it.

Fred Korematsu wanted to stay home.

The government made staying home a crime.

The Supreme Court said the war justified the conviction.

Decades later the law returned to the file and discovered that the citizen who refused had understood something the institutions judging him had not:

Emergency does not make ancestry evidence.

Fear does not suspend citizenship.

And a verdict delivered during national panic may tell future generations less about the defendant than about the country that needed him to be guilty.
