# Chapter 21 — 1970s: The President's Men in the Dock

Richard Nixon was not sitting at the defense table.

That absence is the place to begin.

Watergate has become shorthand for presidential scandal, so completely associated with Nixon that the criminal trial at its center can disappear behind the resignation. The burglary, the tapes, the Senate hearings, the Saturday Night Massacre, the Supreme Court's subpoena decision, and the helicopter leaving the White House all compete for the same historical space.

But after the president resigned, men who had worked at the center of his government still had to answer ordinary criminal charges in an ordinary courtroom.

Former Attorney General John Mitchell.

Former White House chief of staff H. R. Haldeman.

Former domestic affairs adviser John Ehrlichman.

Former assistant attorney general Robert Mardian.

Campaign lawyer Kenneth Parkinson.

The principal Watergate cover-up trial, *United States v. Mitchell et al.*, began in federal court in Washington in 1974.

It asked a question the decade had been avoiding for two years:

Can the criminal law reach the people who help a president misuse power even if the president himself escapes trial?

The answer was yes, but not simply.

Watergate began as a burglary at the Democratic National Committee headquarters in June 1972. Five men were arrested inside the Watergate complex. Their connection to President Nixon's reelection operation soon created questions the White House treated as threats to political survival.

The crime that mattered most historically was not the break-in.

It was the response.

Money was raised to support silence. Officials discussed how to limit the FBI investigation. False stories were constructed. Grand-jury testimony became dangerous. Congressional investigations widened. The White House tapes preserved conversations participants might otherwise have remembered differently.

The cover-up became an institution.

That word is useful because conspiracy is often misunderstood as a secret meeting in which everyone agrees to one master plan. Real conspiracies can be more bureaucratic. Different actors know different facts. Some lie. Some move money. Some pressure agencies. Some destroy or conceal evidence. Some create plausible deniability. Some join late. Some leave early. The criminal law has to decide when separate acts become participation in a common unlawful project.

The Watergate prosecution turned presidential administration into evidence.

Calendars.

Meeting notes.

Telephone calls.

Campaign finances.

Grand-jury statements.

Recorded conversations.

The routine documentary exhaust of government became a map of concealment.

That transformation is one reason the case belongs in this book. Earlier trials often turned on eyewitnesses describing a street, a train car, a beating, a riot, or a murder. Watergate showed a modern state generating its own forensic record.

Power leaves paperwork.

By the 1970s, it also left tape.

The recordings changed everything because they narrowed the space in which political actors could reconstruct motives after the fact. Memories can soften. Public statements can be revised. A tape preserves tone, sequence, interruption, and the uncomfortable fact that the conversation happened at all.

The fight over the tapes produced one of the great constitutional cases of the decade, *United States v. Nixon*. The Supreme Court unanimously rejected the president's claim of absolute privilege and required production of subpoenaed recordings for the criminal proceeding.

That decision did not convict anyone.

It made conviction possible on a more honest record.

This distinction matters.

Courts often shape trials before juries ever hear a witness. Discovery rules determine what evidence surfaces. Privilege determines what remains hidden. Search-and-seizure law determines what the government may obtain. Immunity determines who may be compelled to testify. Executive privilege in Watergate was not an abstract separation-of-powers debate. It determined whether a criminal court could see evidence held by the president.

The Supreme Court said the presidency did not contain an absolute evidentiary wall.

Nixon resigned days after the decisive tapes became public.

Then President Gerald Ford pardoned him.

The pardon removed the possibility that Nixon would face federal prosecution for offenses covered by it. Ford defended the decision as necessary to end a national ordeal. Critics saw it as elite exemption from accountability.

The country has argued about the pardon ever since.

That argument can obscure what happened next.

The system did not simply stop.

The cover-up trial proceeded against senior officials.

Judge John Sirica presided. Prosecutors from the Watergate Special Prosecution Force presented evidence accumulated through one of the most intense investigations in federal history. The defense attacked witnesses, challenged interpretations, and argued that political and bureaucratic conduct had been recast as conspiracy.

On January 1, 1975, the jury returned guilty verdicts against Mitchell, Haldeman, Ehrlichman, and Mardian on major cover-up charges. Parkinson was acquitted. Mardian's conviction was later reversed on appeal and he was not retried.

Again the differentiated verdict matters.

Watergate mythology can flatten everyone around Nixon into “the president's men.” Criminal law cannot stop there. A title is not proof. Proximity is not guilt. Participation must be shown defendant by defendant.

The jury convicted several of the most powerful men in the former administration and acquitted one.

An appellate court later found enough error in Mardian's case to undo his conviction.

That is what accountability under law is supposed to look like when it is working: powerful defendants can be convicted, and convicted defendants can still win legal correction.

The goal is not maximal punishment.

The goal is ordinary law applied under extraordinary pressure.

Watergate is often praised as proof that the system worked.

The phrase is useful and incomplete.

The system worked because many parts of it nearly failed.

The burglary would have remained a burglary if reporters had stopped digging.

The investigation could have been crippled if the FBI had been successfully contained.

The special prosecutor could have become meaningless if presidential power over prosecution had no political cost.

The tapes could have remained inaccessible if executive privilege were absolute.

Congressional investigations could have become spectacle without legal consequences.

The election could have ended inquiry if victory were treated as absolution.

The pardon could have ended everything if criminal responsibility were imagined to exist only at the presidential level.

Instead, multiple institutions produced friction.

Journalists investigated.

Judges issued orders.

Grand juries subpoenaed.

Congress held hearings.

Prosecutors persisted.

The Supreme Court compelled evidence.

A president resigned.

A successor pardoned him.

A jury still decided the guilt of others.

The system worked because power was divided enough that no single actor could close every door.

That is a less comforting and more useful lesson than saying institutions automatically saved the republic.

Institutions are opportunities for people to resist concentration.

They do not resist by themselves.

The Watergate trial also changed the public meaning of obstruction.

A cover-up can sound secondary because it follows an underlying offense. In legal systems, obstruction often threatens something larger than the original crime. A burglary harms victims and violates property and political rights. A coordinated effort by senior officials to corrupt the investigation attacks the mechanism through which the state determines responsibility.

It is a crime against correction.

That is why obstruction can become more historically important than the event being concealed.

The original Watergate burglars were caught quickly.

The national crisis came from what powerful people did after the arrest.

The same pattern recurs far beyond politics.

A corporation experiences an accident. The cover-up creates the scandal.

An institution discovers abuse. Suppression creates the systemic failure.

A police department confronts misconduct. False reports turn one officer's act into institutional corruption.

A government makes a policy mistake. Concealment transforms error into deception.

Power is often judged most revealingly by how it responds after something goes wrong.

Watergate put that response on trial.

The case also marked a transformation in the presidency itself.

The modern executive branch had become enormous. Presidents commanded national-security systems, intelligence agencies, law-enforcement leadership, communications operations, patronage networks, and a professional White House staff. The potential for misuse no longer looked like a nineteenth-century president handing a favor to a friend.

The machinery was industrial.

So were the safeguards required to investigate it.

The special prosecutor became a central figure because ordinary Department of Justice hierarchy created an obvious conflict. The attorney general and other senior officials might themselves be witnesses, subjects, or defendants. A prosecution controlled entirely by presidential subordinates could not easily investigate the president's own circle with public credibility.

Independence therefore became an institutional design problem.

How independent can a prosecutor be while remaining part of the executive branch?

Who can fire that prosecutor?

Who appoints the replacement?

What happens when the person with constitutional authority over federal prosecution may also be implicated by the investigation?

Watergate did not permanently solve those questions.

Later independent-counsel statutes, special-counsel regulations, impeachment battles, and presidential investigations would revisit them.

The trial established the practical reason the questions exist.

A legal system needs some way to investigate people who control the legal system.

That is the accountability paradox.

The higher the official, the more tools he may possess to shape the institution charged with investigating him.

The system requires internal independence strong enough to survive the target's power.

The Nixon pardon sharpened the paradox further.

The Constitution gives the president broad pardon authority for federal offenses. Ford used that authority to immunize his predecessor from prosecution. The act was constitutional in the basic sense that the pardon power exists. Whether it was wise, legitimate, or corrosive was another matter.

This book repeatedly separates legality from legitimacy because the two overlap without becoming identical.

A jury can acquit and lose public confidence.

A government can win in court and be condemned by history.

A president can lawfully pardon and create the impression that office has placed a defendant beyond ordinary accountability.

Ford believed ending the Nixon ordeal served the country.

Many voters believed the pardon confirmed their suspicion that powerful people lived under different rules.

Both perceptions became part of the constitutional aftermath.

The cover-up trial mattered because it denied the broadest version of that suspicion.

Nixon was not tried.

Mitchell was.

Haldeman was.

Ehrlichman was.

The attorney general of the United States, the president's chief of staff, and one of his most important domestic advisers could be brought before a jury and convicted.

Titles did not dissolve criminal liability.

That fact was not inevitable.

Political systems throughout history have treated proximity to the ruler as a shield. Prosecutors understand which cases are career-ending. Judges understand which orders may provoke institutional confrontation. Witnesses understand what retaliation can look like. Legislatures often prefer partisan defense to neutral inquiry.

The Watergate era generated enough countervailing power to push through those incentives.

The prosecution was also a lesson in evidence management at a scale earlier courts had rarely encountered. Tape recordings had to be authenticated and contextualized. Enormous collections of documents had to be reviewed. Witnesses carried their own motives, plea agreements, and credibility problems. The defense could argue that insiders were shifting blame to save themselves.

Modern political prosecutions would inherit this complexity.

The more bureaucratic the alleged crime, the more the courtroom becomes an exercise in reconstructing organizations.

Who knew what?

When?

Who gave the instruction?

Was the instruction criminal on its face or only in context?

Did the defendant join the scheme or merely act near it?

Was a false statement deliberate or mistaken?

Did a subordinate understand the purpose?

Did a superior create distance intentionally?

Conspiracy law exists because organizations can distribute wrongdoing so that no single act contains the whole offense.

It is also dangerous for the same reason. Guilt can travel too easily through association if prosecutors are not forced to prove agreement and participation with care.

Haymarket showed the older version of that danger.

Watergate showed why conspiracy law sometimes becomes indispensable.

The difference is evidence.

Political hatred cannot substitute for proof of agreement.

Recorded discussions, coordinated acts, payments, false testimony, and efforts to obstruct an investigation can.

That is one of the deeper themes of the 250-year sequence: the same legal tool can protect order in one decade and enable injustice in another depending on how evidence and power interact.

Sedition law can punish opposition.

Conspiracy law can punish collective violence or become guilt by association.

Executive power can protect the country or conceal misconduct.

Privilege can preserve necessary confidentiality or hide evidence.

Pardons can correct injustice or look like protection for insiders.

There are few permanently good tools.

There are better and worse institutional conditions for using them.

Watergate also changed expectations about resignation.

Nixon did not resign because a criminal court convicted him.

He resigned because political support collapsed after evidence made impeachment and removal likely. The constitutional system therefore used a political remedy before the criminal system could reach the president personally.

Impeachment and prosecution are different technologies.

Impeachment protects the state from an officeholder.

Criminal prosecution determines personal legal guilt.

The country often confuses them because both use accusatory language.

Watergate clarifies the distinction.

Congress did not need to wait for a criminal conviction to decide Nixon should no longer hold office.

A criminal jury did not need Nixon in the case to decide whether his aides had obstructed justice.

Ford's pardon prevented one process from proceeding against Nixon.

It did not erase the evidence from the political process or immunize everyone else.

Multiple forms of accountability overlapped without becoming interchangeable.

That structure will matter again in the 2020s.

A president can be impeached and acquitted in the Senate, lose an election, face civil judgments, be criminally indicted, be convicted in state court, return to office, and continue appealing. None of those outcomes automatically decides the others.

Watergate taught the country the grammar for that complexity.

The language of the scandal survives everywhere.

The suffix “-gate” attaches to controversies large and trivial.

The cultural shorthand can make the original event feel inevitable.

It was not.

There was nothing inevitable about a president recording incriminating conversations.

Nothing inevitable about investigators obtaining them.

Nothing inevitable about the Supreme Court ordering production.

Nothing inevitable about Republican leaders telling a president his support had collapsed.

Nothing inevitable about senior officials being tried after the president left.

Nothing inevitable about jurors convicting men who had recently occupied offices most citizens would never enter.

The lesson is not that the American system always corrects itself.

The previous chapters have already disproved that.

The lesson is that correction becomes possible when institutions preserve enough independence to create consequences for one another.

The Watergate trial was one of those consequences.

The defendants entered court with famous names and powerful résumés.

The jury received charges, evidence, and instructions.

The former attorney general became a criminal defendant.

The former chief of staff became a criminal defendant.

The president's domestic adviser became a criminal defendant.

The republic did not collapse because powerful men were asked to answer ordinary questions under oath.

It had nearly collapsed because powerful men believed ordinary questions could be stopped.

Richard Nixon was not at the defense table.

That absence remains the unresolved part of the decade.

The presence of his aides remains the achievement.

America did not prove in Watergate that nobody is above the law.

It proved something narrower and still important:

The law can climb very high if enough institutions refuse to lower the ladder.
