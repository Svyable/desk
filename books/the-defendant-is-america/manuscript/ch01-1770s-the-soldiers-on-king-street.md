# Chapter 1 — 1770s: The Soldiers on King Street

John Adams did not need the case.

In 1770 he was thirty-four years old, ambitious, increasingly visible in Massachusetts politics, and already committed to the colonial cause. Boston was not a large city by modern standards, which meant reputation traveled quickly and political memory had a long reach. The men who needed lawyers were British soldiers accused of killing townspeople on King Street. Their uniforms represented the authority many of Adams's neighbors had come to despise. Their victims had become symbols before a jury had heard a word of evidence.

The event would soon acquire the name by which history remembers it: the Boston Massacre.

The name is accurate to the political force of the event and dangerous as a description of what a criminal jury had to decide. A massacre implies one kind of story. A homicide trial requires several smaller ones. Who fired? In what order? What did the crowd do? What could each soldier see? What did each man believe when he pulled a trigger? Which deaths could be attributed to which defendant? Was the shooting murder, manslaughter, self-defense, or something that could not be proven beyond the required standard?

Those questions are less useful to a movement than a martyr is.

Boston had been under strain for years. Parliament's taxes and customs enforcement had intensified conflict between imperial officials and colonists. British troops had been stationed in the town in 1768, ostensibly to maintain order and support enforcement. Their presence made abstract constitutional conflict physical. Soldiers competed with civilians for work. Insults were common. Violence was possible because people who increasingly regarded one another as enemies were sharing streets.

On the evening of March 5, 1770, a confrontation near the Custom House escalated. Snow and ice were reportedly thrown. Clubs and sticks appeared. Townspeople gathered. A lone sentry was reinforced by Captain Thomas Preston and soldiers under his command. In the confusion, shots were fired. Crispus Attucks, Samuel Gray, James Caldwell, Samuel Maverick, and Patrick Carr died from the encounter, some immediately and some after their wounds.

The politics did not wait for the law.

Paul Revere's famous engraving helped turn the shooting into revolutionary propaganda. It presented the scene with the visual clarity politics prefers: a line of British soldiers firing together into helpless colonists under an ominous sky. The image mattered because images survive qualifications. The actual trial record would be full of uncertainty about positioning, provocation, commands, timing, and individual conduct. The engraving had already delivered a verdict.

Then the colonial legal system did something worth lingering over.

It arrested the soldiers and promised them trials.

This sounds ordinary only because the ordinary procedure survived. Boston was angry. The dead were real. The occupying troops were politically hated. The colonial leadership had every incentive to prove that British military power was illegitimate. Yet the legal question was not whether stationing troops in Boston was wise or whether Parliament had abused colonial rights. It was whether named men had committed murder.

Adams agreed to defend them.

There are heroic versions of this choice that turn him into a marble statue immediately. The better version leaves the risk in place. He did not abandon the Patriot cause. He did not become an apologist for imperial policy. He accepted the professional obligation and the political danger of insisting that an unpopular defendant remained a defendant rather than a symbol available for sacrifice.

That distinction is the first reason this is the trial of the 1770s.

The United States did not yet exist. Its revolution had not yet begun in open war. Its Constitution was years away. But one of the political traditions that would later claim national importance was already being tested: could a community demanding liberty for itself extend legal protection to men it regarded as instruments of oppression?

Captain Preston was tried separately first in late October 1770. That separation mattered. The prosecution needed to prove that he had ordered the soldiers to fire. Witnesses gave conflicting accounts. Some believed they heard an order. Others could not say who gave it, or placed Preston in a position that made a command unlikely. The noise and crowd itself became evidence against certainty. Preston was acquitted.

A month later, eight soldiers faced trial.

Adams's task was not to prove that nothing terrible had happened. Five men were dead. Nor did he need to convince jurors to admire soldiers. He had to break a collective political image into individual criminal responsibility.

That is one of the recurring functions of a trial. Public events arrive as wholes. Criminal law disassembles them.

A crowd sees redcoats. An indictment names people.

A movement sees a massacre. A jury must decide whether each charge has been proved.

The distinction becomes especially important in moments of political violence because collective guilt is emotionally efficient. Once a group is treated as the actor, the particular person can disappear inside the uniform. Adams resisted that disappearance. He challenged the reliability of testimony. He argued from the behavior of the crowd. He asked jurors to consider whether the soldiers had reason to fear violence. And he reminded them, in one of the case's enduring principles, that facts do not change simply because people strongly wish them to.

The defense was not a claim that the crowd had no grievance. It was a claim that grievance was not itself evidence of murder.

The jury acquitted six soldiers. Hugh Montgomery and Matthew Kilroy were found guilty of manslaughter rather than murder. Under the law then available to them, they received benefit of clergy, a device with medieval origins that reduced the punishment for certain first offenses. They were branded on the thumb rather than executed.

That outcome was neither total vindication nor the punishment a propagandized public event might have seemed to demand.

It was a differentiated verdict.

That phrase deserves attention because differentiated verdicts are often evidence that a jury has resisted the pressure to decide a case as a single moral block. The jurors did not conclude that the night was harmless. They did not convict every uniform. They distinguished between levels of culpability and between defendants.

A revolution was learning how difficult law becomes when the people in the dock are the people you oppose.

The case also complicates a comforting national myth. Americans often tell the story of the rule of law as though the principle emerged fully formed from the founding generation and then gradually expanded. The Boston trials show something both more admirable and more fragile. Legal restraint was not automatic. It required choices by officials, lawyers, witnesses, judges, and jurors in a city where the political incentives ran the other way.

The choice was also unevenly distributed.

The same society that could give British soldiers counsel and careful trials maintained slavery. Crispus Attucks, remembered as a man of African and Native ancestry and often described as the first person killed in the confrontation, lived in a world in which Black freedom was conditional and contested. Colonial courts could demonstrate procedural sophistication in one case while participating in a social order that denied basic liberty elsewhere.

This is not a reason to discount the trial. It is a reason to understand what trials can and cannot prove about a society.

A fair proceeding for unpopular soldiers does not establish a fair country. It shows that one legal principle was strong enough to survive one kind of pressure.

The next decade will test another.

The Boston Massacre trials mattered immediately because the colonies were in a propaganda contest with Britain. Patriots wanted to show imperial violence. British officials could point to colonial law's capacity to adjudicate rather than lynch. Adams himself would later regard the defense as one of the most honorable services of his life, but the value of the choice is easier to see if we do not borrow his later presidency or the Revolution's outcome to justify it.

In 1770 he could have lost standing. He could have been associated with the hated troops. He could have been blamed if the defendants walked free.

The case therefore asks a question that will recur throughout this book: what is a legal principle worth when applying it helps someone you do not want to help?

Speech rights become expensive when the speaker is despised. Due process becomes expensive when the accused is feared. Equality becomes expensive when it threatens an existing hierarchy. Limits on executive power become expensive during emergency. The presumption of innocence becomes expensive when the public has watched the event and believes it already knows what happened.

Cheap principles fill speeches. Expensive principles fill courtrooms.

The Boston case is particularly useful because political theater existed alongside serious procedure rather than replacing it. The trials did not depoliticize the massacre. They could not. The killings remained a revolutionary grievance. Revere's engraving remained powerful. Orators commemorated the event. The memory of British troops firing on civilians contributed to the story colonists told about occupation and arbitrary power.

Law did something narrower.

It refused to let the political meaning settle the criminal charges by itself.

That narrowness can look unsatisfying. It is also the point.

A courtroom cannot decide whether British imperial policy in North America is legitimate merely because the answer might explain why the crowd assembled. It cannot make the dead less dead. It cannot prevent propagandists from simplifying the outcome. What it can do is require the prosecution to establish the guilt of the human beings it intends to punish.

The distinction between event and defendant becomes harder to maintain as cases grow larger. Later in American history, governments will prosecute people not only for what they personally did but for conspiracies, associations, sedition, and collective violence. Haymarket will test how far guilt can travel through radical speech. The Lincoln assassination commission will test how emergency and conspiracy interact. The Colfax prosecution will ask whether federal law can reach organized racial terror. September 11 will generate a criminal case in which the scale of the event threatens to overwhelm the particular defendant.

Boston is the small early version.

A crowd, a line of armed men, shots, death, politics, and the need to separate all of it into verdicts.

There is another reason the case belongs at the beginning of a 250-year history. The identity of the unpopular defendant changes repeatedly in the chapters ahead.

Here, the defendants are agents of the powerful empire.

That makes due process easy to misread as deference to power. But due process is not supposed to ask whether the accused is structurally weak or strong before it operates. The British soldiers possessed the backing of imperial authority in one sense and faced intense local hostility in another. Their institutional affiliation did not eliminate the need to prove personal guilt.

Later, courts will confront the opposite problem: people with almost no power trying to use law against governments, slaveholders, racial systems, or wealthy institutions. Quock Walker will seek freedom. Dred and Harriet Scott will sue. Homer Plessy will deliberately violate a segregation statute. Clarence Gideon will ask for a lawyer he cannot afford.

The rule of law has to work in both directions.

It must restrain the crowd when the accused is hated.

It must restrain power when the accused or claimant is weak.

American legal history is full of moments when the system managed one and failed at the other.

The Boston trials also warn against treating famous historical imagery as trial evidence. Revere's engraving is a masterpiece of political communication precisely because it removes ambiguity. The soldiers appear organized. The colonists appear passive. The command structure is visually obvious. The street is transformed into a stage on which guilt is already arranged.

Courtrooms are often at their best when they put ambiguity back.

Who stood where?

Which witness could see?

Did someone shout a command?

What did the defendant reasonably perceive?

What can be proved about this shot rather than the event generally?

These questions frustrate people who want moral clarity. Sometimes they should. Technical doubt can be manufactured. Powerful defendants can exploit complexity. Evidence rules can hide truths that everyone outside the courtroom believes they understand. But the alternative—allowing public certainty to substitute for proof—has its own long American record, and it is uglier.

The United States will later produce trials where crowds effectively dictate outcomes, where race determines which witnesses matter, where fear of radicals or foreigners changes standards, and where governments conceal evidence while invoking necessity. The Boston proceedings are important partly because they show that political intensity does not make procedural seriousness impossible.

It has to be chosen.

The revolutionary generation liked to describe liberty in grand terms. The trial of the soldiers forced a practical version of the idea. Liberty included the liberty of the community not to be ruled arbitrarily by troops. It also included the legal protection of the troops when the community accused them of murder.

Those two propositions could coexist.

That coexistence is more sophisticated than propaganda, and more demanding.

John Adams could oppose British policy and defend British soldiers. The jury could recognize that civilians died and still acquit most of the defendants. The event could remain politically incendiary even after the criminal law produced a mixed result.

A mature legal order depends on the capacity to hold several truths without collapsing them into one verdict about everything.

The law is not always mature enough.

Neither is the public.

But the 1770s gave the future country an early example of what the aspiration looked like.

Five men died on King Street. That fact helped move public opinion against British occupation and entered the revolutionary memory. Eight soldiers stood trial. Six were acquitted. Two were convicted of manslaughter. Captain Preston was acquitted separately.

The political story and the legal story did not match perfectly.

They did not have to.

The difference between them is where this book begins.
