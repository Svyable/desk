# Chapter Briefs and Falsification Notes

These are research and argument briefs, not prose outlines. Each chapter should remain a story before it becomes a lesson. “Most important” is the book’s editorial wager, not an assertion that no rival case exists.

## Chapter 1 — 1770s: The Soldiers on King Street

**Principal proceeding:** The 1770 Boston Massacre trials of Captain Thomas Preston and eight British soldiers.

**Job:** Open the book with the counterintuitive proposition that revolutionary liberty was tested by whether hated agents of imperial power received individualized criminal process.

**Evidence to lock:** trial dates; witness conflicts over the order to fire; separate Preston/soldiers proceedings; six soldier acquittals; Montgomery and Kilroy manslaughter verdicts; benefit of clergy; John Adams’s role.

**Counterweight:** Do not turn the defense into proof that colonial Massachusetts was broadly just. Slavery and unequal citizenship coexisted with procedural care for these defendants.

**Close alternatives:** The trials following the Gaspee affair are less useful because they did not produce the same completed courtroom test; the 1778–79 Philadelphia treason prosecutions may better capture wartime loyalty but arrive after the constitutional question this opening needs.

**Falsification note:** Replace or reframe if the chapter implies that Adams alone caused the acquittals or that the trial established a general right to appointed counsel.

## Chapter 2 — 1780s: A Man Named Quock Walker Walks Away

**Principal proceeding:** The linked Quock Walker civil and criminal cases, 1781–1783.

**Job:** Show constitutional language becoming operational when a formerly enslaved man forces “free and equal” into an ordinary assault/property dispute.

**Evidence to lock:** the three proceedings; Walker’s damages verdict; Jennison’s competing property claim; Chief Justice William Cushing’s 1783 instruction; Jennison’s assault conviction; the absence of a single Massachusetts emancipation statute.

**Counterweight:** Historians dispute how much causal weight the cases deserve in slavery’s demise; economic change, Black activism, revolutionary ideology, and other freedom suits also mattered.

**Close alternatives:** Elizabeth Freeman/Brom and Bett v. Ashley, 1781; Trevett v. Weeden, 1786.

**Falsification note:** Do not write that one Quock Walker “Supreme Court decision abolished slavery” as though the history were a single appellate holding.

## Chapter 3 — 1790s: The Congressman in Jail

**Principal proceeding:** United States v. Matthew Lyon, 1798.

**Job:** Test whether a republic that had just constitutionalized speech and press could tolerate organized political opposition during a foreign-policy crisis.

**Evidence to lock:** Sedition Act enactment; indictment and October 9 trial; four-month sentence and $1,000 fine; reelection from jail; 1801 expiration; Jeffersonian pardons.

**Counterweight:** Federalists faced a real Quasi-War and genuinely feared foreign influence. Do not turn every national-security concern into pretext.

**Close alternatives:** Benjamin Franklin Bache prosecution; Thomas Cooper and James Callender Sedition Act trials.

**Falsification note:** The chapter should describe the later constitutional consensus against the Act without claiming the Supreme Court struck it down at the time.

## Chapter 4 — 1800s: The Vice President Accused of Treason

**Principal proceeding:** United States v. Aaron Burr, 1807.

**Job:** Make treason’s intentionally narrow constitutional definition collide with a president determined to prosecute a former vice president he believed dangerous.

**Evidence to lock:** Burr’s western enterprise; Jefferson administration posture; John Marshall’s rulings; two-witness/overt-act requirements; subpoena dispute involving presidential evidence; acquittal.

**Counterweight:** Do not romanticize Burr or imply absence of a treason conviction meant his enterprise was harmless.

**Close alternatives:** The 1800 trial of Thomas Cooper; early prosecutions arising from resistance to federal law.

**Falsification note:** Keep separate what prosecutors suspected from what they could connect to an overt treasonous act under the Constitution.

## Chapter 5 — 1810s: The College That Became a Contract

**Principal proceeding:** Trustees of Dartmouth College v. Woodward, decided 1819 after state litigation.

**Job:** Show the young republic discovering that constitutional limits on states could protect private institutional charters and help create a durable legal environment for corporations and associations.

**Evidence to lock:** New Hampshire’s attempt to alter Dartmouth’s charter; Contract Clause argument; Daniel Webster’s advocacy; Marshall opinion; institutional consequences.

**Counterweight:** Avoid claiming Dartmouth alone “created the corporation” or modern capitalism. State reserved-power doctrines and later constitutional developments qualified its reach.

**Close alternatives:** McCulloch v. Maryland, 1819, is the stronger pure federalism case and must be acknowledged as the decade’s most serious rival.

**Falsification note:** If the manuscript cannot justify choosing a courtroom fight over a college rather than McCulloch’s national-bank conflict, explain the book’s preference for a case with a more intimate institutional plaintiff rather than asserting greater doctrinal importance.

## Chapter 6 — 1820s: The Trial Behind Closed Doors

**Principal proceeding:** The 1822 Charleston proceedings against Denmark Vesey and alleged co-conspirators.

**Job:** Expose what slaveholding power allowed to count as a trial when fear of insurrection made secrecy, coerced or incentivized testimony, limited defense, and mass punishment politically acceptable.

**Evidence to lock:** composition and procedure of the special Court of Magistrates and Freeholders; official report; arrest and execution dates; scale of arrests/executions; restrictions that followed.

**Counterweight:** The evidentiary record is unusually contested. Modern scholarship disputes the official report’s reliability and the scale/coherence of the alleged plot.

**Close alternatives:** State v. Mann, 1829, offers a clearer published legal statement of slaveholder power but less dramatic trial-process exposure.

**Falsification note:** Never narrate the alleged conspiracy’s details as settled fact merely because the 1822 official report states them.

## Chapter 7 — 1830s: Georgia Tries a Missionary

**Principal proceeding:** Georgia’s prosecution of Samuel Worcester, culminating in Worcester v. Georgia, 1832.

**Job:** Separate judicial authority from enforcement power by giving the Cherokee Nation a major legal victory during a political process still capable of removal.

**Evidence to lock:** Georgia statute; Worcester’s conviction; Marshall’s sovereignty analysis; disposition; later political events leading toward removal.

**Counterweight:** Avoid repeating the apocryphal Jackson “now let him enforce it” line as verified quotation. Removal had multiple legal and political stages, and Worcester’s specific release history is more complicated than a simple Supreme Court order ignored on day one.

**Close alternatives:** Cherokee Nation v. Georgia, 1831; Barron v. Baltimore, 1833.

**Falsification note:** Do not claim the Supreme Court ordered federal troops to stop removal or that the Trail of Tears was mechanically caused by non-enforcement of this one judgment.

## Chapter 8 — 1840s: The Cargo That Spoke

**Principal proceeding:** United States v. The Amistad, 1841, after district and circuit litigation.

**Job:** Show how classification determines liberty: were the Africans lawful property under treaty claims or kidnapped people illegally transported into slavery?

**Evidence to lock:** capture and revolt aboard La Amistad; competing Spanish/property/salvage claims; evidence of illegal transatlantic transport; John Quincy Adams’s Supreme Court argument; judgment freeing the Africans.

**Counterweight:** The case did not condemn American slavery generally and occurred within a legal system still protecting slavery elsewhere.

**Close alternatives:** Prigg v. Pennsylvania, 1842, is a powerful rival on fugitive-slave federalism.

**Falsification note:** Do not imply Amistad established a universal constitutional right to freedom for enslaved people in the United States.

## Chapter 9 — 1850s: Dred and Harriet Scott Ask to Be Free

**Principal proceeding:** The Scott freedom litigation culminating in Dred Scott v. Sandford, 1857.

**Job:** Show a Supreme Court attempting to settle the slavery crisis through an expansive judgment that instead deepened the crisis and became a canonical example of judicial overreach and racial exclusion.

**Evidence to lock:** Dred and Harriet Scott’s long litigation; residence in free jurisdictions; citizenship/jurisdiction holding; Missouri Compromise ruling; dissents; later emancipation of the Scotts; constitutional repudiation through Civil War amendments.

**Counterweight:** Keep Harriet Scott visible and distinguish the Missouri trial history from the federal Supreme Court case.

**Close alternatives:** Anthony Burns fugitive-slave proceedings, 1854; Ableman v. Booth, 1859.

**Falsification note:** Avoid a monocausal claim that Dred Scott “caused the Civil War.”

## Chapter 10 — 1860s: The Conspiracy Court

**Principal proceeding:** The 1865 military commission trial of eight alleged Lincoln-assassination conspirators.

**Job:** Ask what due process looks like in the immediate aftermath of presidential assassination and civil war, especially when civilians are tried by military tribunal in Washington.

**Evidence to lock:** Johnson’s decision to use a military commission; eight defendants; evidentiary/procedural rules; convictions and four executions; John Surratt’s later civilian trial and hung jury; Ex parte Milligan as constitutional context rather than direct appeal of the commission.

**Counterweight:** The government faced an actual assassination conspiracy in a nation emerging from rebellion. Do not reduce every military-procedure choice to vengeance.

**Close alternatives:** Ex parte Milligan, 1866; Jefferson Davis treason proceedings.

**Falsification note:** Distinguish Mary Surratt’s contested culpability from the broader procedural criticism; do not declare factual innocence without evidence.

## Chapter 11 — 1870s: Colfax After the Killing

**Principal proceeding:** Federal prosecutions arising from the Colfax massacre, culminating in United States v. Cruikshank, 1876.

**Job:** Show Reconstruction rights failing when federal enforcement doctrine narrows the national government’s ability to punish private racial terror.

**Evidence to lock:** Colfax violence; Enforcement Act indictments; convictions of selected defendants; Supreme Court treatment of pleading/federal power; practical effect on civil-rights enforcement.

**Counterweight:** Cruikshank included technical defects in the indictments and reflected then-contested theories of federalism. Avoid reducing the opinion to a one-line permission slip for violence.

**Close alternatives:** United States v. Reese, 1876; contested election litigation around 1876.

**Falsification note:** Quantitative claims about Colfax deaths vary; use ranges and source them.

## Chapter 12 — 1880s: Haymarket

**Principal proceeding:** The 1886 Chicago murder trial of eight anarchists after the Haymarket bombing.

**Job:** Examine how fear and ideology can convert association, speech, and a broad conspiracy theory into criminal guilt when the bomb thrower’s identity is uncertain.

**Evidence to lock:** May 4 bombing; defendants and charges; trial transcript; prosecution conspiracy theory; verdicts; executions; 1893 Altgeld pardon of surviving defendants.

**Counterweight:** Several defendants advocated revolutionary violence; the chapter should not sanitize their politics to criticize the trial.

**Close alternatives:** The trial of Charles Guiteau, 1881–82, is a strong rival on insanity and presidential assassination.

**Falsification note:** Do not state that every Haymarket defendant was proven to be absent from the meeting or wholly unconnected to violent advocacy; specify what evidence linked each man to the charged murder theory.

## Chapter 13 — 1890s: The Seat Plessy Chose

**Principal proceeding:** Homer Plessy’s deliberate 1892 arrest and the litigation culminating in Plessy v. Ferguson, 1896.

**Job:** Show strategic test-case litigation producing a catastrophic constitutional rule, while recovering the Citizens’ Committee and Plessy’s agency from a story often told only through the Supreme Court opinion.

**Evidence to lock:** Louisiana Separate Car Act; planned test; Plessy’s arrest; state-court path; Supreme Court majority and Harlan dissent; long afterlife; later pardon.

**Counterweight:** “Separate but equal” was not invented from nothing in one opinion; segregation developed through statutes, custom, violence, and earlier doctrine.

**Close alternatives:** Pollock v. Farmers’ Loan & Trust, 1895; trial of Lizzie Borden, 1893, for cultural significance.

**Falsification note:** Do not imply the Citizens’ Committee wanted or anticipated the Supreme Court’s adverse constitutional settlement.

## Chapter 14 — 1900s: The Supreme Court Tries a Sheriff

**Principal proceeding:** United States v. Shipp, 1906–1909, the Supreme Court’s original contempt prosecution after Ed Johnson was lynched in Chattanooga despite a stay.

**Job:** Put enforcement itself on trial by showing the Supreme Court acting as a criminal tribunal against local officials and participants accused of defying its authority.

**Evidence to lock:** Ed Johnson case and stay; lynching; contempt charge; Supreme Court original jurisdiction; 1906 jurisdiction opinion; 1909 guilt findings and sentences.

**Counterweight:** Separate what was proven about Sheriff Shipp and individual defendants from the broader community’s racial terror.

**Close alternatives:** Harry Thaw murder trial, 1907; Standard Oil litigation began in this decade but concluded later.

**Falsification note:** Be precise that the Supreme Court was adjudicating contempt of its authority, not retrying Johnson’s rape conviction or directly prosecuting murder.

## Chapter 15 — 1910s: Leo Frank

**Principal proceeding:** The 1913 Atlanta murder trial of Leo Frank for the killing of Mary Phagan.

**Job:** Explore the point at which a formally completed trial loses legitimacy under mob pressure, antisemitism, sensational press, disputed witness evidence, and the state’s later failure to protect the prisoner after executive commutation.

**Evidence to lock:** Phagan’s murder; Jim Conley’s testimony; trial atmosphere; absence of Frank and defense counsel when verdict read; appeals; Governor Slaton’s 1915 commutation; Frank’s lynching; 1986 posthumous pardon and its stated basis.

**Counterweight:** The 1986 pardon did not declare Frank factually innocent; it focused on Georgia’s failure to protect him and preserve legal process. Scholarship on guilt remains contested.

**Close alternatives:** Triangle Shirtwaist factory manslaughter trial, 1911; IWW/free-speech prosecutions.

**Falsification note:** Never present the pardon as an official exoneration on innocence grounds.

## Chapter 16 — 1920s: The Monkey Trial

**Principal proceeding:** State of Tennessee v. John T. Scopes, 1925.

**Job:** Show a deliberately engineered test case becoming mass media and turning a small statutory prosecution into a national conflict over science, religion, education, modernity, and who controls public-school curriculum.

**Evidence to lock:** Butler Act; Dayton civic strategy and ACLU involvement; Scopes’s agreement to serve as defendant; Darrow/Bryan confrontation; guilty verdict and $100 fine; Tennessee Supreme Court reversal on the fine while upholding the statute’s constitutionality.

**Counterweight:** Do not write the film *Inherit the Wind* back into the historical record. Scopes was not persecuted in the ordinary sense, and the test case was welcomed by local boosters.

**Close alternatives:** Sacco and Vanzetti, whose 1921 trial and 1927 executions may be the decade’s strongest competitor on immigration, radicalism, and procedural legitimacy.

**Falsification note:** The chapter must explicitly defend choosing Scopes over Sacco-Vanzetti rather than pretending there is no contest.

## Chapter 17 — 1930s: Scottsboro

**Principal proceeding:** The Scottsboro prosecutions beginning in 1931, with Powell v. Alabama (1932) and Norris v. Alabama (1935) as constitutional afterlives.

**Job:** Show speed, race, capital punishment, counsel, and jury exclusion combining to make a series of state trials a laboratory for Fourteenth Amendment criminal procedure.

**Evidence to lock:** nine Black teenagers charged with rape; first rapid trials and death sentences; Powell right-to-counsel ruling in capital circumstances; retrials; Norris ruling on systematic exclusion of Black jurors; later outcomes for individual defendants.

**Counterweight:** Keep the cases and defendants distinct; the procedural history is long and not every charge ended identically.

**Close alternatives:** Lindbergh kidnapping trial, 1935; Scottsboro wins on national constitutional consequence.

**Falsification note:** Do not collapse Powell into Gideon; Powell was narrower and tied to the capital-case circumstances before Gideon’s later categorical felony rule.

## Chapter 18 — 1940s: The Citizen Who Refused to Leave

**Principal proceeding:** The criminal prosecution of Fred Korematsu for violating a military exclusion order, culminating in Korematsu v. United States, 1944.

**Job:** Test citizenship under genuine wartime fear and show how judicial deference can convert a group-based security proxy into constitutional permission.

**Evidence to lock:** Executive Order 9066 and military exclusion; Korematsu arrest and conviction; Supreme Court ruling and dissents; suppressed/contrary government intelligence issues; 1983 coram nobis vacatur; Civil Liberties Act of 1988; later Supreme Court repudiation.

**Counterweight:** Do not describe all Japanese American wartime detention through Korematsu’s exclusion-order holding; distinguish exclusion, detention, curfew, and Endo.

**Close alternatives:** Ex parte Endo, 1944; Nuremberg trials are globally larger but not an American domestic trial.

**Falsification note:** State precisely what the 1983 coram nobis court vacated and what it could not erase from Supreme Court precedent.

## Chapter 19 — 1950s: The Trial After Emmett Till

**Principal proceeding:** Mississippi v. J. W. Milam and Roy Bryant, 1955.

**Job:** Show that all the visible forms of a jury trial can exist inside a political order that has preselected whose testimony, life, and citizenship count.

**Evidence to lock:** Till kidnapping and murder; Mose Wright identification; all-white, all-male jury; acquittal; later magazine admission by Bryant and Milam; double-jeopardy finality; later federal investigations and 2021 closure.

**Counterweight:** Later claims about Carolyn Bryant Donham’s recantation are disputed; follow the Justice Department’s closure findings rather than the most dramatic secondary retelling.

**Close alternatives:** Rosenberg espionage trial, 1951, for Cold War significance; Brown was an appellate school case, not a criminal jury trial.

**Falsification note:** Do not attribute a specific jury-room quotation without a reliable source.

## Chapter 20 — 1960s: The Man Who Asked for a Lawyer

**Principal proceeding:** Clarence Gideon’s Florida burglary trial, Supreme Court challenge, and 1963 retrial.

**Job:** Convert a constitutional abstraction into institutional capacity: what does “assistance of counsel” mean when the accused cannot pay?

**Evidence to lock:** initial request and denial; self-representation; conviction and five-year sentence; handwritten petition; Gideon v. Wainwright overruling Betts; W. Fred Turner’s representation at retrial; acquittal.

**Counterweight:** Gideon created a constitutional floor, not a guarantee of adequately funded public defense. Implementation remains uneven.

**Close alternatives:** New York Times v. Sullivan, 1964; Miranda v. Arizona, 1966; Gideon wins the slot because the book can follow one defendant through two trials.

**Falsification note:** Do not imply the acquittal alone proves the first conviction was factually false; it powerfully demonstrates the effect of representation without providing a controlled experiment.

## Chapter 21 — 1970s: The President’s Men

**Principal proceeding:** United States v. Mitchell et al., the 1974–75 Watergate cover-up trial.

**Job:** Show divided institutions allowing criminal process to reach senior officials near presidential power after the president himself escaped prosecution through resignation and pardon.

**Evidence to lock:** cover-up indictment; roles of Mitchell, Haldeman, Ehrlichman, Mardian, Parkinson; tape evidence and United States v. Nixon; verdicts; Parkinson acquittal; later reversal of Mardian conviction; Nixon pardon as separate accountability track.

**Counterweight:** “The system worked” should be treated as a contested conclusion produced by multiple near-failures, not an automatic institutional property.

**Close alternatives:** Patty Hearst trial, 1976; Watergate wins on constitutional structure.

**Falsification note:** Do not state Nixon was criminally tried or convicted in Watergate.

## Chapter 22 — 1980s: The Insanity Verdict

**Principal proceeding:** United States v. John W. Hinckley Jr., 1982.

**Job:** Force a distinction between act, blame, mental disease, dangerousness, and punishment when the physical facts of an attempted presidential assassination are undisputed.

**Evidence to lock:** March 30, 1981 shooting; psychiatric contest; 1982 not-guilty-by-reason-of-insanity verdict on all counts; commitment; public backlash; Insanity Defense Reform Act of 1984; later Brady death and 2015 decision not to prosecute.

**Counterweight:** The verdict did not mean immediate release. Later confinement/release decisions used different legal questions from criminal responsibility at the time of the shooting.

**Close alternatives:** McMartin Preschool prosecution, which began in the 1980s and demonstrates panic and evidentiary failure but concluded in 1990.

**Falsification note:** Tie each claimed statutory change to the 1984 federal act rather than using Hinckley as a catchall explanation for every state insanity reform.

## Chapter 23 — 1990s: The Trial on Television

**Principal proceeding:** People v. O. J. Simpson, 1995.

**Job:** Show mass media, celebrity, race, police legitimacy, defense resources, and rapidly maturing DNA evidence colliding inside a single burden-of-proof question.

**Evidence to lock:** murders of Nicole Brown Simpson and Ronald Goldman; Bronco pursuit; forensic evidence; chain-of-custody attacks; Mark Fuhrman credibility collapse; glove demonstration; acquittal; later civil wrongful-death verdict under lower burden.

**Counterweight:** Avoid treating either the criminal acquittal as a finding of factual innocence or the civil verdict as a retroactive criminal conviction.

**Close alternatives:** Rodney King officers’ trials, 1992–93, may better capture police violence and race but Simpson better captures the decade’s new media-forensics system.

**Falsification note:** Forensic-statistical claims must be sourced to trial evidence or reliable contemporary records, not remembered television shorthand.

## Chapter 24 — 2000s: The Trial 9/11 Did Get

**Principal proceeding:** United States v. Zacarias Moussaoui, guilty plea 2005 and capital sentencing trial 2006.

**Job:** Ask whether ordinary federal criminal process can absorb terrorism without allowing the defendant, the attack, or national fear to dictate a special legal category.

**Evidence to lock:** pre-9/11 detention; six conspiracy charges; classified/detainee evidence litigation; guilty plea; capital eligibility and causation theory; jury’s refusal to impose death; life-without-parole sentence.

**Counterweight:** Moussaoui was not the “20th hijacker” in the simple popular sense established as trial fact; his own self-aggrandizing accounts and other al Qaeda evidence require careful separation.

**Close alternatives:** United States v. Padilla; Guantánamo military commission proceedings. Moussaoui wins because a completed civilian federal case directly tests the normal-court alternative.

**Falsification note:** Distinguish facts admitted in the plea from contested sentencing claims about his precise operational role.

## Chapter 25 — 2010s: The Night in Sanford

**Principal proceeding:** State of Florida v. George Zimmerman, 2013.

**Job:** Put “reasonable fear” on trial and examine how self-defense law, race, firearms, public pressure, and incomplete evidence interact when the surviving shooter supplies the central account of the final seconds.

**Evidence to lock:** February 26, 2012 encounter; delay before charge; second-degree murder indictment/information; self-defense evidence; jury instructions including no-duty-to-retreat language; July 13, 2013 acquittal; 2015 DOJ civil-rights closure.

**Counterweight:** The defense did not end the case through a pretrial stand-your-ground immunity ruling. Do not reduce the entire trial to a single statute or claim the jury made a finding on racial profiling.

**Close alternatives:** Boston Marathon bombing trial, 2015; Zimmerman wins on the decade’s wider debate over reasonable threat perception and citizen use of force.

**Falsification note:** Separate structural racial analysis from the specific intent elements prosecutors would have had to prove in a federal civil-rights case.

## Chapter 26 — 2020s: A Former President in the Defendant’s Chair

**Principal proceeding:** People v. Donald J. Trump, New York criminal trial, 2024, with post-verdict litigation tracked through September 6, 2026.

**Job:** End the 250-year sequence with a threshold event: ordinary state criminal process applied to a former president and active presidential candidate, followed by a guilty verdict, reelection, unconditional discharge, and continuing challenges.

**Evidence to lock:** 34 falsifying-business-record counts; 22 trial witnesses; May 30, 2024 unanimous guilty verdict on all counts; July 2024 presidential-immunity decision as posttrial context; January 10, 2025 unconditional discharge; return to presidency January 20, 2025; August 28, 2026 federal removal ruling; unresolved appellate horizon.

**Counterweight:** The chapter must separate the legal sufficiency of a prosecution from the political argument over prosecutorial discretion. It should not tell the reader what future appellate courts will decide.

**Close alternatives:** Federal January 6 election-obstruction prosecution, Fulton County election case, and January 6 seditious-conspiracy trials. The New York case wins because it is the first completed criminal trial and conviction of a former U.S. president.

**Falsification note:** Refresh immediately before release. If the conviction is vacated, reversed, modified, or finally affirmed, the chapter’s ending and metadata must change rather than treating the September 6, 2026 cutoff as timeless.

## Whole-book falsification note

The sequence succeeds only if the chapters remain different. If every case ends with “America failed, then corrected itself,” the structure has become a machine instead of history. Some trials should show restraint that held. Some should show a correct judgment that power ignored. Some should show a bad judgment entrenched for generations. Some should show public outrage producing reform. Some should show formal legal finality surviving later moral condemnation. The diversity of failure modes is the argument.
