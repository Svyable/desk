# The Myth and the Measurement

A good myth preserves the emotional truth of an observation and loses the units.

The Moon rang. That is the emotional truth. The units are frequency, amplitude, decay time, distance, source energy, scattering strength and quality factor. Remove them and a seismic coda becomes a bell. Keep them and it becomes a material property.

Public scientific language often creates these transformations unintentionally. Researchers need metaphors because most people do not think in waveform envelopes. “Rang like a bell” is excellent communication. The trouble begins when the analogy is mistaken for the measurement.

The same pathway produced “the god particle,” “junk DNA,” “dark matter” as if it were visibly dark stuff, and “quantum teleportation” as if matter vanished from one room and appeared in another. Science cannot eliminate metaphor. It can label the boundary.

Apollo's ringing phrase crossed that boundary because it arrived with several cultural advantages. The Moon was already an object of myth. Apollo itself attracted conspiracy. The idea of an artificial satellite had deep science-fiction roots. The observation came from a dramatic deliberate impact. The phrase was attributed to a respected seismologist reacting in real time. Every ingredient was memorable.

The natural explanation, by contrast, accumulated slowly: dry cold rock, weak attenuation, scattering, megaregolith, source dependence, frequency dependence, numerical modeling. It had no single reveal.

That asymmetry is one reason misinformation can remain sticky even after the underlying science is strong. The first story gets a sentence. The correction gets a seminar.

The answer is not to simplify the correction until it becomes another slogan. “The Moon rang because it is dry” is memorable and incomplete. Better public science can tolerate an extra clause: the lunar crust is extraordinarily fractured and heterogeneous, which scatters seismic energy into long codas, while intrinsic attenuation is comparatively low.

That sentence is less viral. It is also closer to the Moon.

There is a moral risk in anomaly writing: the author can become addicted to keeping the mystery alive. Every solved piece removes narrative voltage. The temptation is to preserve uncertainty after the evidence has moved.

The opposite risk is debunker addiction: the author gets status from closing mysteries and begins treating unresolved questions as embarrassments.

Lunar seismology deserves neither. The hollow planetary shell is not supported. The long codas are physically interesting. The detailed partition between scattering and intrinsic attenuation remains a research topic. The farside network gap is real. The depth distribution and mechanics of moonquake nests remain active questions. Shallow-quake hazard for future infrastructure deserves new data.

A mature account can hold all of these at once because “mystery” is not one bucket.

The Moon is less hollow than the myth and less settled than the correction.

That is usually where reality lives.
