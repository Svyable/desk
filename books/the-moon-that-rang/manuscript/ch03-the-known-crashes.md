# The Known Crashes

A natural earthquake is generous with energy and stingy with metadata.

A spacecraft impact is the reverse.

For lunar seismologists, the spent hardware of Apollo offered something close to a controlled-source experiment. Mission planners knew where a stage was supposed to go, approximately how fast it was moving, approximately how much it weighed, and when to expect impact. Some impacts missed their planned aim points. Some locations were revised later. One Saturn stage crater was not visually identified until Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter imagery decades afterward.

None of that ruined the experiment.

An imperfectly located artificial source was still better constrained than a moonquake that began hundreds of kilometers down.

Apollo 12 supplied the canonical surprise.

After Pete Conrad and Alan Bean returned from the surface, the ascent stage of *Intrepid* was deliberately deorbited. NASA's mission history places the impact roughly forty miles from the Apollo 12 seismic station. The impact energy is usually summarized as roughly **one ton of TNT equivalent**.

The station did not respond like an Earth seismometer beside a quarry blast.

Strong motion continued for more than half an hour. Weaker motion persisted for about an hour.

That sentence became folklore because it is so easy to hear metaphorically.

The Moon rang.

But the experiment did not stop there.

Apollo 13 gave the seismologists a much harder strike and a much better comparison.

The mission report records the S-IVB third stage hitting the Moon on April 14, 1970 at about **5,600 miles per hour**, with an impact mass of about **30,700 pounds**, roughly **74 miles west-northwest of the Apollo 12 experiment station**. The engineering mission report converted the impact to about **7.7 tons of TNT equivalent**.

The station saw the first seismic signal **28.4 seconds after impact**.

The signals continued for **more than four hours**.

Peak intensity did not arrive instantly. It built for roughly ten minutes and reached a level about eight times larger than the peak recorded from the Apollo 12 ascent-stage impact.

Now the phrase *rang like a bell* has a denominator.

One known source, about one ton TNT equivalent, produced roughly an hour of detectable disturbance.

A substantially larger known source, at a different range and geometry, produced hours.

That does not make duration a simple linear function of impact energy. It does something better: it turns the coda into a response function that can be compared across known inputs.

The Apollo 13 report gives another number mythology almost never quotes. The initial clear seismic arrival propagated at about **4.8 kilometers per second**, close to velocities expected from laboratory measurements of Apollo 12 crystalline rocks. Contemporary investigators inferred that the early energy sampled lunar material to depths on the order of **20 to 40 kilometers**.

That is already a geological statement.

It is not a bell analogy.

The same report also says the signal character was not fully understood. The rapid buildup to peak amplitude could not be explained solely by the simple rubble-scattering picture seismologists had entertained after the Apollo 12 ascent-stage impact. They considered additional source mechanisms, including an expanding ejecta or gas cloud coupling energy into the surface.

That uncertainty matters.

It prevents us from rewriting the history into a neat sequence in which Apollo 12 rang strangely and scientists immediately knew the answer was scattering in dry rubble.

They did not.

The source itself was under investigation.

The path was under investigation.

The near-surface medium was under investigation.

The interior was under investigation.

This is exactly what a controlled-source program is for: not to make uncertainty disappear, but to stop every unknown from moving at once.

Even the famous TNT numbers require discipline.

NASA records do not always quote the same value for the same impact. The Apollo 13 mission report gives about **7.7 tons TNT equivalent**, while later NASA historical/popular material has quoted about **11.5 tons**. That discrepancy should not be hidden because the phrase "TNT equivalent" sounds precise.

TNT equivalent is derived from assumptions about mass, velocity and which energy budget is being summarized. The seismometer did not measure "7.7 tons" or "11.5 tons."

It measured ground motion versus time.

For technical comparisons in this book, the contemporaneous mission report is the primary anchor. Later rounded public-history values are useful context, not a reason to pretend the derived number is uniquely fixed.

Beginning with Apollo 13, spent S-IVB stages were deliberately sent into the Moon as seismic sources. Apollo 14, 15, 16 and 17 added more controlled impacts, while several lunar-module ascent stages were also deorbited after crew rendezvous.

Each new source improved the geometry.

Apollo 14's booster created a crater later photographed by Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter. Apollo 15's ascent stage was driven into the Moon after its deorbit burn and recorded by the Apollo 12, 14 and 15 stations. Apollo 17's S-IVB impact was recorded by the Apollo 12, 14, 15 and 16 passive seismic stations. Its ascent stage was then intentionally targeted close enough to the landing region to provide another known source after the crew had departed.

Apollo 16 supplies a useful warning about how "known" a known source can be.

Its S-IVB was deliberately aimed at the Moon, and the impact was recorded seismically, but tracking was lost before impact and the precise crater remained uncertain for decades. In 2015, Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter imagery finally identified the booster crater at a location different from the old estimate.

That is not an embarrassment to controlled-source seismology.

It is a lesson in uncertainty propagation.

If the source position moves, inferred travel paths move with it. A better source location can improve an old seismic inversion without changing one sample of the original waveform.

This is why archival science matters.

The artificial impacts teach a methodological lesson that the mythology usually misses. If the Moon were literally hollow in the ordinary sense, "it rang" would not be enough to establish that. You would ask what quantitative features a hollow-shell model predicts: first-arrival times, dispersion, modal frequencies, spatial variation, attenuation, response across impact energies and locations, reflections from the proposed inner free surface, and how those observables change as source-receiver geometry changes.

Then you would compare those predictions against solid but heterogeneous lunar models.

Apollo's deliberate impacts helped precisely because the source side of the equation was partially known.

A waveform is the product of source, path and instrument.

Apollo 13 shows why every term matters.

If a large fraction of the unusual onset were produced by ejecta coupling, that belongs to the source.

If hours of coda were produced by repeated scattering in an intensely fractured near-surface layer, that belongs to the path.

If frequency response or gain changes affected the recorded trace, that belongs to the instrument.

The sentence "the Moon vibrated for four hours" contains all three until the analysis separates them.

The public folklore froze at the moment of surprise. Science did not.

As the network accumulated events, the long coda could be compared across source types. Meteoroid impacts and shallow quakes could be contrasted with deep moonquakes. Frequency dependence mattered. Distance mattered. Source depth mattered. The scattering hypothesis became testable not because somebody thought of a nicer story but because thousands of records exposed regularities.

The Apollo 13 impact itself was already doing interior science. Contemporary analysis used the relatively clean early arrival to infer crystalline velocities in the upper lunar crust. Investigators hoped that larger, more distant future impacts would sample progressively deeper structure.

This is a completely different use of "ringing" from the hollow-Moon story.

The long coda was not the conclusion.

It was the obstacle wrapped around the signal they wanted.

The controlled impacts also show the danger of treating one evocative waveform as a material diagnosis. Change the source and you change the excitation. Change the path and you change the scattering. Change the instrument and you change what survives into the record. "Long vibration" by itself does not isolate which part of the chain is responsible.

This seems obvious when written down. It is routinely forgotten when an anomaly is visually or rhetorically strong. A radar track becomes an aircraft capability. A biological signal becomes a sensory organ. A spectrum becomes an engineered material. A witness report becomes a craft. In every case, the move from observation to ontology needs intermediate steps.

Apollo offers an unusually clean example because we do not have to deny the sensational observation to reject the sensational ontology.

The Moon really did produce astonishingly long seismic codas.

A simple hollow-shell diagnosis still does not follow.

Artificial impacts also created a historical irony. One of the most persistent conspiratorial claims about Apollo depends on data produced by deliberately smashing Apollo hardware into the Moon. The same missions accused in some subcultures of fabrication created the very "ringing" records later used to argue that NASA had discovered a hollow artificial satellite and concealed it.

That does not mean institutional records should be swallowed whole. Mission histories can simplify technical uncertainty. Public-affairs explanations can harden a provisional model into a sentence. The Apollo 13 TNT-equivalent discrepancy is a small example of why one should trace numbers back toward mission reports.

The correct response to institutional simplification is to move closer to the data and contemporary technical record, not farther away into an unfalsifiable alternative.

A known crash is epistemically valuable because it narrows one part of the inverse problem.

It does not solve the entire Moon.

Apollo's artificial impacts were clustered geographically, limited in energy, observed by a tiny near-side network and interpreted through the instruments of their time. They were calibration points, not CT scans.

Still, calibration points are powerful.

A body that responds unexpectedly to a known strike is telling you something real.

Apollo's triumph was not that it made the Moon behave like Earth.

It made the discrepancy measurable.

The strange part, in retrospect, is not that the Moon rang.

It is that engineers arranged for it to be struck repeatedly until a metaphor could be converted into travel times, amplitudes, spectra, source models and geology.
