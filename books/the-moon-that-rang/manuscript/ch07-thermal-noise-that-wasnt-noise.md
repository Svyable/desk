# Thermal Noise That Wasn't Noise

At sunrise on the Moon, even the rocks complain.

Apollo seismometers detected a class of small events associated with the lunar day-night cycle. The simplest version of the explanation is thermal: surface materials expand as sunlight returns and contract as night takes over. Cracks open or close, grains move, structures settle. What would be background nuisance in another experiment becomes a source class of its own.

Thermal moonquakes are useful because they expose the boundary between instrument and environment. A seismometer sits in a world that swings through enormous temperatures. Its cable, housing, emplacement and surrounding regolith all experience that cycle. Some transient signals come from the ground; some may be coupled through deployed hardware. Separating the Moon from the experiment requires knowing how the experiment physically touches the Moon.

This problem appears everywhere in anomaly science. A detector reports an event. The temptation is to start with distant causes. Good experimentalists start with the mounting bracket.

Apollo had plenty of mounting brackets.

The thermal events are not glamorous, but they may matter more to future surface operations than many deep interior questions. Repeated thermal cycling changes slopes, boulders, regolith contacts and human hardware. A base at the pole experiences a different illumination regime from an equatorial Apollo site. Structures designed to survive decades will create new mechanical paths between temperature and ground motion.

Modern lunar seismology will therefore face a delightful contamination problem. Humans returning to the Moon will generate a dense anthropogenic seismic environment: landers, rovers, drills, excavators, pumps, compressors, footsteps, construction impacts, pressure changes, rocket plumes. The cleanest future station may need to be far away from the settlement whose safety it is meant to protect.

Apollo already demonstrated the diagnostic value of human-generated signals. Astronaut motion and known impacts helped calibrate instruments. The future will produce orders of magnitude more such sources. A working lunar base could become an active seismic experiment if machinery locations and timing are logged precisely. A drill is both an industrial tool and a source wavelet.

That is the productive way to think about “noise.” Noise is not a metaphysical category. It is signal whose origin is irrelevant to the question you are asking. Change the question and the noise can become data.

Thermal cracking also offers a scale warning. A tiny event near a station can produce a visible trace without representing global tectonic activity. Counts cannot be compared across source classes without accounting for detection range. Thousands of small local events do not make the Moon more seismically energetic than a few larger shallow quakes.

This is another place where raw catalog numbers mislead. Apollo's event classes were shaped by detectability. Thermal events close to a station are easier to see than equivalent sources on the far side. The catalog is an observation process, not a census conducted by an omniscient clerk.

The distinction sounds dry until someone uses “Apollo detected thousands of moonquakes” as evidence for a dramatic interior claim. Which moonquakes? At what depth? At what energy? Detected by how many stations? Under which thermal conditions? Classification is not pedantry. It is the difference between geology and numerology.

The Moon's day-night cycle is slow enough that thermal stresses have time to work through the shallow material. Sunrise is not a switch. A thermal front moves. Rocks with different albedo, geometry and contact conditions heat differently. Cracks concentrate stress. Some events recur in similar local-time windows.

These details are reminders that even a body without weather has an environment. Vacuum is not absence. Sunlight is weather enough when there is no atmosphere to buffer it.

Apollo went looking for the interior and discovered that the surface itself had a clock.
