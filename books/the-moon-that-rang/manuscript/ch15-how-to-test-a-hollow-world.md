# How to Test a Hollow World

Suppose we take the hollow-Moon claim seriously for one afternoon.

The first task is to stop saying “hollow.” A cavity occupying one percent of lunar volume is a different object from a shell with an empty central region. An artificial honeycomb is different from a natural low-density mantle. A lava-tube system is different from all of them. Each geometry changes mass distribution, elastic modes, gravity and wave propagation.

Choose the strongest version: a large planetary shell surrounding a substantial empty interior.

Mass is the immediate constraint. The Moon's total mass is measured accurately from orbital dynamics. Its radius is measured. From those numbers comes a mean density of about 3.34 grams per cubic centimeter, lower than Earth's because the Moon has a smaller iron fraction, not because a huge void is required. A shell model can match total mass only by increasing the density of the remaining material or changing shell thickness.

Moment of inertia tightens the problem because it measures how that mass is radially distributed. A thin dense shell and a differentiated rocky body with a small metal core do not rotate identically. Lunar laser ranging and spacecraft gravity data constrain that distribution.

Seismology tightens it again. A shell has boundaries. Seismic energy reflecting from a giant internal free surface should produce characteristic phases and normal modes. Those predictions can be calculated. The Apollo data instead support crust, mantle and a small core, with uncertainties but no need for a planetary-scale cavity.

Gravity mapping adds lateral structure. NASA's GRAIL mission measured the lunar gravity field with extraordinary precision, revealing crustal thickness variations, impact structures and mass concentrations. An artificial shell would need to reproduce not only average gravity but these regional correlations with observed geology.

Then there is formation. Natural Moon models must explain isotopic similarities with Earth, angular momentum, volatile depletion and differentiation. They are imperfect and have evolved, but they are constrained by samples and dynamics. An artificial-shell theory needs a construction history that does not erase these constraints. “Advanced technology” is not a mechanism; it is a permission slip.

None of these tests absolutely proves no intelligence ever modified the Moon. Absolute proof of universal negatives is rarely available. They do something more useful: they make the large-shell hypothesis carry an enormous explanatory burden compared with geological models that already fit independent data.

There are narrower versions worth testing. Large lava tubes are plausible. Buried voids under pits can be sought with radar, gravimetry and active seismology. A network of geophones around a suspected cavity can image it. If an anomalously regular structure were found, drill it.

Notice how quickly the debate improves once scale enters.

This exercise is not unique to the Moon. Extraordinary technological claims often become scientifically tractable when converted into engineering parameters. A craft has acceleration, power, heat and material limits. A retrieved alloy has composition, isotopes, microstructure and chain of custody. A hidden cavity has dimensions and a seismic transfer function.

The phrase “extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence” is memorable but incomplete. What extraordinary claims really require is ordinary measurement done unusually well.

A hollow world should be easier to test than a vague one.
