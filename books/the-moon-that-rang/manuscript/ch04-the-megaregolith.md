# The Megaregolith

The lunar surface is not a surface in the ordinary terrestrial sense. It is the visible top of an impact archive.

For billions of years, asteroids and meteoroids have struck the Moon without oceans, weather or plate tectonics to erase the damage. Large impacts excavated, shattered and redistributed crust. Smaller impacts ground that debris. Still smaller ones churned the upper regolith. Beneath the powder visible in Apollo photographs lies a much thicker damaged zone: fractured rock, breccia, ejecta blocks, voids and interfaces extending downward through what planetary scientists call the megaregolith.

Seismic waves hate simplicity less than interpreters do. Give a wave a comparatively uniform material and its path can remain legible. Give it a planetary layer packed with heterogeneity across many scales and it scatters. Energy leaves the direct path, ricochets through alternatives and leaks back into the seismometer over time. The coda lengthens.

This is now one of the strongest physical explanations for the lunar seismic signature. A 2012 study examined coda from hundreds of higher-quality Apollo seismograms. The authors found that intrinsic attenuation, scattering and near-surface effects all contribute, but the pattern for shallow sources—especially at higher frequencies and longer distances—pointed strongly toward scattering in a global near-surface layer. They proposed the megaregolith.

A decade later, numerical work used Apollo's ultra-long codas to estimate just how heterogeneous that shallow layer may be. The inferred variations in seismic velocity and density were very large—at least tens of percent in the modeling—and larger than earlier pictures had comfortably assumed. The Moon's ringing became a measurement of violence preserved in rock.

This changes the metaphor. A bell is manufactured to be coherent. The megaregolith is manufactured by catastrophe. Its long response emerges from disorder.

There is a useful inversion here. The phenomenon once used to argue for a hidden cavity is evidence for an overabundance of structure. Cracks, blocks, contacts and voids create paths. The wave does not persist because there is nothing inside the Moon. It persists because the upper Moon gives the energy too many ways to travel while dissipating relatively little of it as heat.

The word “dry” often appears in popular explanations, and it belongs in the story with care. The Moon is not literally devoid of water; polar deposits and hydrated materials ended that old simplification. Nor does a small amount of lunar water turn the crust into terrestrial rock. The mechanical and thermal state of the Moon differs profoundly from Earth's. Cold, old, fractured material can have high seismic quality factors—low intrinsic damping—while the megaregolith supplies scattering. Both processes can lengthen the record.

Separating scattering from intrinsic attenuation is itself a difficult inverse problem. A long coda can be produced by waves surviving a long time, by waves taking long complicated paths, or by both. Modern analyses try to partition these contributions using frequency, distance and source class. They do not reduce to “no moisture means ringing.”

This matters because bad debunking is almost as corrosive as bad anomaly promotion. If a skeptic says the Moon rang only because it is dry, as if one variable closes the subject, the skeptic has preserved the same error as the hollow-Moon enthusiast: a complicated waveform becomes a one-cause slogan.

The megaregolith is richer. It connects Apollo seismology to crater chronology, impact mechanics, future construction and resource exploration. A lunar base will not sit on an abstract sphere. It will sit inside this damaged, blocky, thermally extreme geological medium. Seismic scattering that confused Apollo analysts becomes practical information about what lies below landing pads, habitats and drill sites.

It also complicates attempts to use small numbers of stations to infer deep structure. Before a wave can tell you about the mantle or core, it has to survive the crustal filter. Near-surface heterogeneity scrambles the timing and amplitude information seismologists want. Better instruments help, but geometry helps more.

The strange coda is therefore not a dead curiosity from Apollo. It is a reminder that the Moon has an outer layer with no close terrestrial analog at planetary scale. Earth's crust is broken, of course, but rain enters it, heat circulates, faults recycle it, sediments bury it and tectonics rebuild it. The Moon keeps much more of the damage.

Ring a world that remembers nearly every blow and the answer should not sound familiar.
