# The Meteorite Detector

Before a global camera network watched fireballs over Earth, the Moon was already serving as a detector plate.

Meteoroids striking the lunar surface generate seismic waves. Apollo's network recorded them in large numbers. Unlike Earth, the Moon has no atmosphere to burn up the smaller incoming bodies and no ocean to hide most crater-forming impacts. Every hit couples some fraction of its energy into the ground.

This turned lunar seismology into a strange branch of astronomy. A sensor anchored in regolith could infer populations of objects moving through cislunar space.

The inference was not straightforward. Converting seismic amplitude into impactor mass requires assumptions about velocity, coupling efficiency, distance, source physics and lunar attenuation. Early estimates of meteoroid flux from the seismic data did not line up neatly with Earth-based estimates. A 1979 Apollo passive-seismic summary still called the discrepancy a mystery while noting emerging correlations.

That is important because it shows mature science leaving a loose end in the official record. The Apollo literature is not a triumphalist archive in which every anomaly was tidied before publication. Researchers documented mismatches.

Impact seismology has improved since then, and modern lunar missions can pair seismic detections with optical flash observations and high-resolution before-and-after imagery. The three modalities answer different questions. A flash timestamps the impact. A new crater localizes it and constrains energy. A seismogram tells how the ground transmitted the strike. Together they reduce the degeneracies that haunted Apollo.

The lesson is familiar by now: independent modalities matter most when they constrain different parts of the causal chain.

A bright flash without a crater might be an optical artifact or a poorly located event. A seismic transient without imagery might be a moonquake. A crater without timing could be years old. Coincidence across clocks and instruments turns ambiguity into a physical event.

This architecture matters for the future Moon because impact risk is not historical trivia. Human habitats, power systems and surface logistics will live beneath a sky with no atmospheric shield. Micrometeoroids threaten equipment directly; larger but rarer objects create ejecta and shaking. A seismic network can become part of a hazard-monitoring system.

The lunar background produced by continuous tiny impacts also has a deeper use. NASA's planned Farside Seismic Suite is expected to measure a seismic background driven in part by micrometeorite impacts. That background can constrain how often small particles hit the surface. What Apollo treated partly as event catalog becomes ambient vibration.

There is an attractive symmetry here. On Earth, ocean waves create microseisms that seismologists once considered nuisance and now use to study the crust. On the Moon, a rain of small rocks can provide persistent excitation. A world can probe itself with whatever keeps tapping it.

Meteoroid impacts also clarify why a long coda is not evidence for a resonant shell. Natural impacts occur across a range of locations and energies. Their records can be analyzed statistically. If a simple shell mode dominated, we would expect stable modal fingerprints. Instead, much of the observed complexity tracks source depth, frequency, distance and scattering through heterogeneous near-surface material.

The impact archive is a check against the single-event imagination.

It also forces humility about source energy. A spacecraft crash is known better than a random meteoroid but not perfectly. A meteoroid's speed and angle may be uncertain by factors that matter to coupling. The ground motion is not a direct scale measuring impactor mass.

When Apollo inferred the sky from the ground, every estimate passed through the Moon.

That is the point. Sensors never measure the object you care about directly. They measure an interaction. The Moon's seismic response is part of the instrument whether you want it to be or not.
