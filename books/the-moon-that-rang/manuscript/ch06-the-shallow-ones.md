# The Shallow Ones

The deep moonquakes are numerous, weak and rhythmic. The shallow ones are rare enough to count by hand and strong enough to worry about.

Apollo's mature network recorded twenty-eight shallow moonquakes between 1969 and 1977. Their locations were initially uncertain because the network was sparse and their waveforms complicated. Unlike the deep tidal nests, shallow events look in important ways like intraplate earthquakes: brittle failure in cold rock, not gentle recurring tidal slip hundreds of kilometers down.

For decades, the events sat uneasily inside the story of a geologically dead Moon. Then orbital imaging improved.

The Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter Camera revealed thousands of small lobate scarps—cliffs produced by thrust faults—distributed across the Moon. Their crisp morphology and relationships with young surface features implied geologically recent contraction. The Moon has been cooling since formation; a cooling body shrinks. The crust cannot contract everywhere smoothly, so stress is relieved on faults.

In 2019, Thomas Watters and colleagues revisited the Apollo shallow-moonquake locations with a modern algorithm designed for sparse networks. The relocation was still uncertain, but eight of the twenty-eight events fell within thirty kilometers of mapped young faults. The timing of some events also lined up with portions of the lunar tidal cycle when fault stresses would be elevated. The conclusion was careful: some shallow moonquakes may represent ongoing activity on these young thrust faults.

This is not the kind of conclusion conspiracy culture usually notices. It is too conditional and too consequential. The Moon may still be tectonically active in a limited sense, and the evidence emerged by combining forty-year-old seismograms with orbital images acquired by a spacecraft launched in 2009.

The word “active” needs discipline. It does not mean lunar continents drift. It does not mean lava is erupting under Apollo sites. It means some faults may still slip as the Moon cools and contracts. Geological life comes in more than one form.

The shallow events also return us to hazard. Apollo crews stayed for days. Future lunar infrastructure is meant to stay for years. A shallow moonquake of moderate magnitude in a low-attenuation, strongly scattering crust can produce long-duration shaking unlike the same magnitude on Earth. The hazard depends on distance, local geology, structure and frequency content, not just a headline magnitude.

NASA's public summaries note that some shallow moonquakes can persist for many minutes. The number is vivid, but engineering needs the spectrum. A habitat, antenna, cryogenic plant or tall tower cares about frequencies that couple to its own resonances. The same scattered wavefield that made Apollo's records strange becomes a design load.

There is an irony in the way the Moon's ringing story evolved. The early surprise was interpreted by outsiders as evidence that the Moon was less solid than expected. The later evidence says nearly the opposite: parts of the Moon are brittle enough to break today, and the fractured crust holds seismic energy for a long time.

The shallow quakes also sharpen an old limit. Twenty-eight events in eight years is a small catalog. Relocations depend on a network whose stations occupied one portion of the near side. Statistical association with faults is informative but not the same as watching a mapped fault rupture beneath a modern dense network. The difference between suggestive tectonics and operational hazard is exactly where a new seismic era should begin.

A future network could combine seismometers with high-resolution imaging before and after events, laser ranging, gravimetry and local geodesy. If a scarp moves during a detected quake, the causal chain tightens dramatically. If quakes repeatedly occur far from young faults, the contraction story will need revision.

The Moon is offering a good experiment and forcing us to admit that Apollo did not have enough stations to run it.

That is a better reason to return than mystery for mystery's sake.
