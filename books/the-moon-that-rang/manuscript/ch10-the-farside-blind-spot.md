# The Farside Blind Spot

A map of Apollo seismic stations looks less like a planetary network than a neighborhood.

That was unavoidable. Humans landed where mission geometry, lighting, terrain and communications allowed. Science instruments rode with them. The four long-lived passive seismic stations therefore occupied the near side, separated by useful but limited distances. No station listened from the hemisphere that never faces Earth.

This geometry created one of the most interesting unresolved features in the deep-moonquake catalog. Yosio Nakamura's later reanalysis identified roughly thirty nests likely located on the farside. Yet a broad region around the lunar antipode appeared to contain few detected nests. He argued that ordinary distance alone could not explain the deficit.

Two explanations remain conceptually different. The first is seismic geography: perhaps that part of the deep Moon is genuinely less active. The second is propagation: perhaps waves from that region are strongly attenuated, deflected or otherwise prevented from reaching the Apollo network.

One missing station prevents those stories from being separated cleanly.

This is why network design is theory design. Place every detector on one side of a body and some questions become impossible no matter how sensitive the detectors are. Sensitivity cannot substitute for baseline.

The planned Farside Seismic Suite matters for this reason. NASA and JPL describe it as a mission to place sensitive instruments in Schrödinger basin on the far side, with the goal of recording farside moonquakes and impacts and probing the deep interior. Its Very Broadband seismometer descends from technology developed for Mars InSight. For the first time since Apollo, a long-lived lunar seismic experiment can listen from the hemisphere Apollo could not.

A farside station will not instantly solve lunar structure. One new point is still one point. But it changes ray paths through the Moon. Events hidden by the old geometry may become local. The ambiguous antipodal quiet zone can be tested from the other side.

The phrase “far side” itself attracts nonsense because it sounds secret. It is not permanently dark, and spacecraft routinely observe it. Its scientific value comes from separation from Earth-facing infrastructure and from geometry, not occult geography.

Radio astronomers prize the farside because the Moon can shield terrestrial radio interference. Seismologists prize it because the Moon itself lies between the new station and Apollo's old observational biases. Different disciplines want the same hemisphere for different kinds of silence.

There is a general lesson here for disputed phenomena. If a claim survives only in the blind spot of an existing network, the highest-value experiment is often not more argument about existing data. It is a detector placed inside the blind spot.

This sounds trivial and is expensive. A debate can continue for fifty years because moving one seismometer to another world is harder than publishing fifty papers.

Apollo's farside ambiguity is unusually healthy because it is stated in a form that can lose. If the new station finds abundant local deep moonquakes where Apollo saw none, propagation and geometry become central. If it also finds a real quiet region, lunar internal asymmetry becomes harder to dismiss. Either outcome improves the model.

Compare that with a theory that converts every possible result into confirmation. If a farside station detects quakes, the hidden mechanism is active. If it detects none, the hidden mechanism is hiding. That is not frontier science; it is insulation.

The Moon has waited half a century for better geometry. It will not care which hypothesis wins.
