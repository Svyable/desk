# Wagon Wheel Stops Before the Shot

The most important thing about Project Wagon Wheel is that the nuclear explosives stayed hypothetical.

The project was planned south of Pinedale, Wyoming, as another experiment in stimulating low-permeability natural gas. An exploratory hole was drilled. Hydrological wells were installed. Core was studied. Lawrence Radiation Laboratory and El Paso Natural Gas modeled what nuclear explosions would do inside Wagon Wheel sandstone. Technical papers calculated cavity radii, fracture zones, chimney behavior, and the possibility of multiple detonations at different depths.

Then no nuclear shot occurred.

This makes Wagon Wheel unusually valuable because it preserves the boundary between scientific readiness and institutional refusal. A cancelled experiment is often treated as a missing data point. In fact it can be a data point about the system surrounding the science.

The technical work was not imaginary. One surviving report on predicted explosion effects used equation-of-state measurements from core recovered thousands of feet down the Wagon Wheel well and estimated the extent of cavity formation, shear fracturing, and stress. The project had moved far beyond a cocktail-napkin proposal. It was learning the rock.

DOE's historical Plowshare chronology lists Wagon Wheel among proposed projects that were not executed, with activity extending from 1968 into 1974. The purpose was explicit: natural-gas stimulation at various depths. The supporting work left physical traces even without a detonation.

Why stop after investing enough to drill and model?

A clean monocausal answer would be convenient and likely wrong. By the early 1970s Plowshare gas stimulation was accumulating technical, economic, environmental, and political uncertainty at the same time. Gasbuggy and Rulison had proved that nuclear explosions could stimulate gas and that produced gas could remain radiologically troublesome. Rio Blanco would test a more ambitious multi-device geometry. Conventional massive hydraulic fracturing was advancing. Federal environmental review had become more formal. Public opposition to nuclear industrial projects was stronger. The national appetite for routine peaceful nuclear explosions was narrowing.

Wagon Wheel sat inside that convergence.

The project therefore illustrates a form of evidence that engineers sometimes underweight: cancellation after partial preparation. A system that repeatedly produces plans, models, holes, and support infrastructure but does not cross into deployment may be revealing an obstacle that no single technical report contains.

This does not mean cancellation proves the engineering would have failed. On the contrary, the useful epistemic posture is more disciplined. Wagon Wheel cannot tell us what production rate its nuclear stimulation would have achieved because the event never occurred. It cannot tell us what radionuclide concentrations would have appeared in its gas. It cannot be counted as another failed shot.

It can tell us that by the time the program had enough experience to design the experiment, the threshold for justifying another nuclear event had risen.

That is a different kind of failure.

Technological programs often begin with a low evidentiary bar for exploration and acquire a higher bar for continuation. Early uncertainty is a reason to experiment. Later uncertainty becomes less defensible if previous experiments have already identified recurring burdens. The same unanswered question changes meaning over time.

Before Gasbuggy, "Will the product gas contain troublesome radionuclides?" was a research question. After Gasbuggy and Rulison, it was a known class of problem whose magnitude and mitigation varied by design.

Before field testing, "Will nuclear fractures access enough reservoir?" was a question of feasibility. After disappointing fracture reach, another experiment had to justify why new geometry would alter the economics rather than merely the local data.

Wagon Wheel's technical sophistication therefore counts against the idea that Plowshare died because no one knew how to engineer the next test. Engineers knew enough to make detailed predictions. The question was whether the next prediction still deserved a nuclear experiment.

There is a subtle shift here from *Can we learn something?* to *Is the knowledge worth the intervention?*

Every large scientific infrastructure faces this transition. A particle accelerator, fusion experiment, Mars mission, or deep-ocean observatory can almost always answer new questions. The existence of learnable questions is not sufficient to justify any particular experiment. The cost, irreversibility, and opportunity cost of obtaining the answer matter.

For Wagon Wheel, irreversibility was literal. Once detonated, the nuclear cavity and residual radionuclides would remain part of the Wyoming subsurface for generations. A cancelled project left drill holes and records. An executed project would have left a stewardship site.

That difference is visible in DOE's modern legacy portfolio. Some Plowshare sites require ongoing surveillance because nuclear events occurred. Others, where work stopped before detonation, can eventually be reclaimed or reduced to records management. The fork in the project plan becomes a different federal obligation half a century later.

This is a useful way to think about technological externalities: not only what damage occurs, but what monitoring institution has to exist afterward because uncertainty cannot be made to disappear.

Gasbuggy, Rulison, and Rio Blanco created future work for people who had no role in their original optimism. Wagon Wheel created much less of it by stopping.

The decision also matters because the broader tight-gas problem did not disappear. Wyoming and Colorado would later become major unconventional-gas regions. Geologists and petroleum engineers continued studying the same formations. The resources were not written off as inaccessible forever. The technical problem migrated into a different development path.

That is what makes Wagon Wheel a better ending for the nuclear gas sequence than a spectacular accident would have been. There was no final catastrophe to dramatize the lesson. The program simply reached a point where another hole could be studied without placing a nuclear explosive at the bottom.

The alternative technologies had become good enough to make restraint look like engineering rather than surrender.

Plowshare's planners had always understood that nuclear stimulation competed with conventional methods. By the 1970s, high-volume hydraulic fracturing was no longer a minor baseline. It was becoming the rival future.
