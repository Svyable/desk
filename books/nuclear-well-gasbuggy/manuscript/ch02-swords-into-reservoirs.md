# Swords Into Reservoirs

Plowshare's name was chosen before its problems had names.

That is one reason the program is easy to caricature. A biblical phrase, a thermonuclear age, and a federal bureaucracy eager to find civilian work for its most spectacular technology seem almost designed for retrospective irony. Yet the first years of Plowshare are more interesting if the irony is held back. The people involved had just watched nuclear explosives transform military engineering. They had also watched underground testing reveal something genuinely new: an explosion could be contained, measured, and made to do predictable mechanical work inside rock.

The key event was Rainier, fired underground at the Nevada Test Site in September 1957. Rainier was not itself a peaceful nuclear explosion. It was a weapons test, but its underground setting supplied data that mattered to civilian proposals. A nuclear device placed deep enough in competent rock could create an enormous cavity and fracture zone while keeping most radioactive material underground. The resulting enthusiasm was not based only on atomic-age symbolism. It came from a new empirical object: the contained underground nuclear explosion.

The U.S. Atomic Energy Commission formally established a program for nonmilitary applications of nuclear explosives in 1957. The menu grew quickly. Nuclear explosives might excavate harbors or canals, create underground storage cavities, break ore, recover hydrocarbons, extinguish difficult fires, make isotopes, or expose matter to neutron fluxes unavailable by ordinary laboratory means. Some proposals now read like concept art for a civilization that never existed. Others produced field experiments, technical reports, and enough measured success to survive several budget cycles.

The distinction between proposal and program matters. Plowshare was not one idea. It was an institutional container for different physical mechanisms that shared only the explosive. Excavating a canal by throwing radioactive earth into the air is not the same engineering problem as fracturing a deeply buried gas reservoir. Producing transplutonic elements in the neutron environment of a nuclear explosion is not the same problem as making a salt cavity. Treating Plowshare as a single giant failure hides the specific reasons particular branches lived or died.

Excavation encountered its own brutal constraint: the easiest way to use a nuclear explosion as a shovel is to place it shallow enough that much of the displaced earth—and some radioactive material—enters the atmosphere. The physics of making a crater and the politics of atmospheric contamination were not separate. They were the same geometry seen from different institutions.

Gas stimulation looked better because the geometry was inverted. Put the explosive deep underground. Do not excavate. Let the overburden help contain the radioactivity. Use the shock to fracture reservoir rock rather than to throw it away. The desired product, natural gas, was already underground and already expected to travel to the surface through a well. In that sense gas stimulation offered Plowshare a less theatrical and potentially more commercial future.

Project Gnome, detonated in December 1961 in a salt formation near Carlsbad, New Mexico, showed both sides of the program's early confidence. The event was designed for multiple purposes, including studies of heat, neutron effects, and the behavior of a nuclear explosion in salt. Salt's ability to deform and close fractures later made it central to thinking about geologic waste disposal; at the time it was attractive because a large underground cavity might remain stable. Gnome also produced a release of radioactive gases after a leak developed in the stemming system. Plowshare's story would repeatedly return to this gap between containing most radioactivity and creating an industrial process that could tolerate the rest.

The 1963 Limited Test Ban Treaty reshaped the program without killing it. The treaty prohibited nuclear explosions in the atmosphere, underwater, and in outer space and also prohibited underground explosions that caused radioactive debris to cross national borders. Underground tests that remained contained were not banned. This pushed Plowshare's most plausible applications toward the subsurface even as public concern about fallout and environmental contamination made excavation projects harder to defend.

That institutional pressure coincided with another shift: Plowshare needed industrial problems large enough to justify nuclear-scale energy but bounded enough to keep radioactive material underground. Tight natural-gas reservoirs looked like one.

The attraction was arithmetic before it was geology. The western United States contained large gas resources in formations that did not produce at economic rates through ordinary completions. If one underground explosive could connect a well to a vastly larger reservoir volume, the gas recovered over years might dwarf the cost of the device and site. The energy released by the explosive was enormous compared with conventional well stimulation. Nuclear devices could also be emplaced at depths relevant to gas production, where containment was much more plausible than in a cratering application.

The problem was that rock is not a number.

The explosion delivered energy radially into a medium whose useful features were not radial. Gas reservoirs have beds, laminations, faults, stress fields, water, variable permeability, mineral composition, and thickness. The well does not need merely fractured rock; it needs fractures that connect gas-bearing rock to the well while avoiding undesirable fluids and remaining open under pressure. A bigger damage zone is not automatically a better completion. Plowshare's gas program would become an expensive lesson in the difference between volume disturbed and reservoir volume economically connected.

Before Gasbuggy, however, that lesson was mostly theoretical. Engineers had data from underground weapons tests, high-explosive experiments, conventional oilfield stimulation, and models of how explosions couple into rock. They did not have a production history from a nuclear-stimulated gas reservoir. That absence mattered more than the strangeness of the proposal. A technology can remain plausible for a long time when the decisive test is both expensive and unprecedented.

This is where the public narrative of Plowshare tends to reverse cause and effect. We remember the failed projects and imagine that only propaganda could have carried them into the field. Propaganda existed. The phrase "peaceful atom" carried political value. The AEC had incentives to demonstrate that nuclear technology could build rather than only destroy. Livermore scientists had professional and institutional reasons to find applications for devices and calculations developed in the weapons complex. Industry partners had incentives to gain federal support for difficult resource problems. Local officials could see investment and infrastructure.

All of that is true without being sufficient.

The stronger reason Plowshare persisted is that some of its component physics worked. Underground explosions made cavities. They fractured rock. They generated heat. They produced intense neutron fluxes. They moved earth. The program's weakness was not a complete absence of effects. It was a surplus of effects, each arriving with unwanted companions.

This pattern is worth naming because it recurs beyond nuclear engineering. A technology is easiest to reject when its central mechanism fails. It is harder when the mechanism succeeds but the product system does not. The temptation is to treat every surrounding obstacle as secondary: contamination can be cleaned, economics can improve with scale, regulation can adapt, the public can be educated, designs can be refined, and competitors can be outperformed. Each sentence may be individually reasonable. Together they can keep a technology alive long after the number of miracles required for deployment has become the most important fact about it.

Plowshare generated its own language for making the extraordinary administrable. Devices became "nuclear explosives." Events became "experiments." Craters, chimneys, fracture radii, radionuclide inventories, dose pathways, and production forecasts could be assigned values. The vocabulary was not fraudulent. It was necessary to do serious technical work. But technical language can also narrow the field of view. Once a question becomes "What yield maximizes stimulated reservoir volume?" it is possible to spend years improving the answer before returning to the prior question: should a commercial gas field contain repeated nuclear detonations at all?

The nuclear gas program did not avoid that question. It tried to answer it by proving the industrial case.

Gasbuggy would be first. It would use a low-permeability gas formation in the San Juan Basin, combine federal nuclear expertise with a major pipeline company's reservoir knowledge, and measure what came back after the explosion. Rulison would follow in Colorado at higher yield. Rio Blanco would use three devices in a vertical array, attempting to create a taller stimulated region through a thick gas-bearing interval. Wagon Wheel in Wyoming was planned as another ambitious step.

The progression itself is revealing. When an experiment disappoints but does not decisively fail, engineers often respond by making the next experiment better aligned with the model. A single explosive may not stimulate enough vertical interval; use multiple explosives. Fractures may not reach as far as hoped; adjust yield or placement. Flow may decline; increase the volume connected. Radioactivity may contaminate early gas; study cleanup, decay, dilution, and handling. The program learns.

Learning can improve a technology. It can also make the boundary between research and commitment difficult to see.

By the time a field has accumulated specialized reports, devices, models, contractor relationships, environmental surveys, regulatory routines, and future project sites, stopping is no longer the same decision as declining to begin. Knowledge has acquired infrastructure. Careers and expectations have attached to it. A technology that has become an institution can interpret each failed assumption as a research question rather than as a reason to leave.

Plowshare did eventually leave. The reasons were cumulative: environmental concern, political opposition, arms-control complications, economics, technical disappointments, and nonnuclear alternatives. It is tempting to ask which one killed the program. That framing gives too much power to a single cause.

Technologies often die the way reservoirs deplete: not because one molecule stops moving, but because the pressure gradient no longer pays for the effort.

Gasbuggy belongs to the period before that pressure was gone. The language of peaceful nuclear explosives still had institutional force. The federal government still imagined a national resource problem large enough to justify extraordinary engineering. Industry still had a reason to test whether a nuclear pulse could make tight rock behave like better rock.

The biblical image had already done its work. A sword had not literally become a plowshare. A weapons technology had been translated into a reservoir tool.

Now the tool needed a reservoir.
