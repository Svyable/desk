# Four Thousand Feet Down

The engineering romance of Gasbuggy is concentrated in the wrong object.

The nuclear explosive gets the attention because it is nuclear. The more instructive artifact is the hole.

A borehole is an agreement with depth. Everything that follows depends on keeping that agreement intact: drilling straight enough, characterizing the formations crossed, casing where necessary, controlling fluids and pressure, running instruments, lowering equipment whose failure may be unrecoverable, sealing the annulus, and knowing where the bottom really is. Oilfield engineering had spent decades making these operations routine. Gasbuggy asked the routine to accommodate a device whose purpose was to destroy the rock around it.

The contradiction was only apparent. The well had to be exquisitely controlled until the instant control was surrendered to the explosion. Depth, placement, stemming, instrumentation, and geological knowledge were the conditions that allowed a violent event to become an experiment instead of merely an event.

At roughly 4,227 feet beneath the surface, the explosive was deep enough for the overlying rock to perform part of the containment system. The principle of underground containment seems simple from the surface: bury the device deeply enough that the pressure pulse cannot vent. In practice, containment involves geology, explosive yield, emplacement geometry, shafts and boreholes, sealing materials, faults, fractures, water, and the possibility of unexpected pathways. The history of underground testing had already demonstrated both impressive containment and uncomfortable exceptions.

Plowshare's gas application made the problem stranger. The well could not simply be sealed forever after the shot. The project intended to return.

After the explosion and a period for conditions to stabilize, a reentry well would reach the newly created collapse chimney. Gas would be produced through a system connected to the zone the explosion had just transformed. The emplacement was therefore not merely a burial. It was phase one of a production experiment.

The device itself is often described through yield, approximately 29 kilotons. Yield is a useful number because it lets us compare energy releases. It is also an abstraction that can mislead. Twenty-nine kilotons of TNT equivalent does not tell you directly how much gas will be recovered. The conversion chain is long: nuclear energy becomes heat and shock; shock becomes cavity formation, crushing, and fracture; fracture becomes permeability; permeability becomes pressure communication; pressure communication becomes gas flow; gas flow becomes saleable energy only after composition, radioactivity, processing, transport, and cost are accounted for.

Every arrow loses something.

This is where the nuclear proposal differed most from the rhetoric of "using the bomb's tremendous energy." The device had plenty of energy. Energy abundance was not the scarce resource. Useful coupling was.

A hammer can deliver more energy than a lock pick and still be a worse way to open a particular door.

The same principle applies underground. A reservoir engineer does not want the maximum possible disturbance. The engineer wants disturbance in the right places, with the right orientation, conductivity, and persistence. The explosion's indifference to those preferences was hidden by the sheer scale of the fracture zone it could create.

The emplacement operation also reveals another forgotten feature of peaceful nuclear explosives: they inherited an industrial architecture from the weapons complex whether or not the goal was military. Device security, transportation, custody, diagnostic systems, command procedures, exclusion areas, and radiological readiness do not disappear because the project has an energy-company partner. The "tool" cannot be rented from the same yard as a pump truck.

This mattered for economics. A nuclear completion was never only the price of the explosive. It required the institutional capacity to handle a nuclear explosive legally and safely. In early projections, some of those costs could be treated as standardized or perhaps reduced through scale. But scale itself created a political paradox. The more successful the technology became, the more frequently civilian industry would need the state apparatus associated with nuclear detonations.

A few experimental shots could remain exceptional. Hundreds of commercial shots would require exception to become routine.

That is why the 1972 projection of hundreds of nuclear-stimulated wells is so useful. It forces the thought experiment past the dramatic prototype. Imagine not Gasbuggy as an isolated event, but an annual field program. Crews prepare emplacement holes. Devices are transported to gas fields. Nuclear-event schedules become part of resource planning. Seismic networks distinguish commercial PNEs from other underground events. Produced gas is sampled for radioactive constituents. Regulatory agencies track product streams. Communities learn which parts of ordinary oilfield work now fall under nuclear rules.

The scenario is not impossible in a physical sense. That is what makes it revealing. Technologies can be physically possible and institutionally grotesque.

The people running Gasbuggy did not need to solve the complete industrial future before the test. Demonstration projects rarely do. Their job is to reduce a particular uncertainty. Can a nuclear explosive create a stimulated region in a tight gas reservoir that materially increases production? Once that answer exists, other institutions can decide what to do with it.

But the selection of uncertainty is itself a decision. If a demonstration isolates the variable most favorable to the new technology, success can postpone rather than resolve the system question.

Gasbuggy's designers did more than that; they planned radiological studies and production testing precisely because the unwanted effects mattered. Yet the broader pattern remains. Nuclear engineering could measure explosive performance more directly than market acceptance. Reservoir engineering could measure flow more directly than social license. Government could pay experimental costs that a commercial operator would later have to internalize.

The hole down to the device was therefore also a tunnel between institutions. At the bottom sat the AEC's technology. Around it lay El Paso Natural Gas Company's reservoir problem. Above it sat federal land, nearby tribal land, environmental pathways, and a national argument about peaceful uses of atomic energy. The shot would connect all of them whether or not the fracture network did.

On December 10, the emplacement sequence ended.

There is no need to manufacture a cinematic countdown. The technical record is enough. A deeply buried nuclear explosive was fired inside a producing geological province as part of a joint government-industry experiment. Seismic energy traveled outward. Rock nearest the device vaporized and melted. A cavity opened under extreme pressure. Surrounding material was crushed and fractured. As the cavity cooled and pressure dropped, the overlying rock began to collapse into it.

The surface did not become the giant crater that popular imagination expects from a nuclear blast. That absence was part of the engineering success. The device's mechanical work was supposed to remain underground.

What had been a continuous formation now contained a new architecture: fused material, a cavity region, broken rock, a collapse chimney, and fractures extending into the surrounding sandstone. The exact shape could not be inspected like a building. It had to be reconstructed from models, drilling, pressure behavior, seismic data, and later production.

This is a recurring fact of subsurface engineering. The object being engineered is largely inaccessible. We infer it from signals at wells and at the surface. The more complicated the intervention, the more tempting it becomes to mistake the model of the underground object for the object itself.

Gasbuggy now had the largest possible version of that problem. The explosion had created a structure no one could walk through and no camera could simply photograph. Its usefulness depended on dimensions that mattered differently to different disciplines. Nuclear-effects scientists cared about cavity and chimney behavior. Geologists cared about fracture patterns and lithology. Reservoir engineers cared about connected productive volume. Radiochemists cared about where radionuclides partitioned and how they moved. Seismologists cared about the event's signal. Public-health and environmental monitors cared about pathways to people and water.

The same underground geometry was five different objects.

The project now had to recover it from evidence.

For a while, the best evidence was absence. No catastrophic vent to the surface. No visible crater swallowing the site. The containment case looked strong enough to proceed. But containment had a specific meaning at that moment: the explosion and most of its radioactive products had remained underground.

Production would change the meaning.

To test the well, engineers would deliberately establish a path from the altered underground zone to the surface and reduce pressure. Gas would move. Some of the information trapped in the reservoir would move with it.

The reentry operation is one of the most important conceptual moments in the entire Plowshare gas program. The program stopped asking, "Can we contain the explosion?" and began asking, "What happens when we produce from it?"

Those are not opposites. They are sequential requirements. A commercial nuclear-stimulation technology would have to do both reliably: contain what must remain and recover what must leave.

The nuclear event had finished in seconds. The separation problem had just begun.
