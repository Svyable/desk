# Picking the Rock

A nuclear explosion is less flexible than a drilling rig.

A rig can move. A completion can be modified. A well can be sidetracked, recompleted, stimulated again, abandoned, or replaced. Once a nuclear device is lowered into a borehole and the hole is stemmed above it, the geology is no longer one variable among many. It is the experiment.

That made site selection at Gasbuggy a peculiar form of commitment. The program needed a reservoir poor enough that extraordinary stimulation might matter, rich enough that success could be measured, deep enough that the explosion could be contained, thick and mechanically suitable enough to yield a useful fractured region, accessible enough for heavy equipment, and embedded in an existing gas province so that the result could be compared with ordinary wells. It also needed land and institutional arrangements that could carry the legal and operational weight of a nuclear detonation.

The San Juan Basin offered that combination.

Northwestern New Mexico was already a gas landscape. The Pictured Cliffs sandstone, a Late Cretaceous unit extending through the basin, had producing wells and a reputation for low permeability. The formation's name is almost comically gentle beside the event later placed inside it. Yet the geology gave Gasbuggy its rationale. This was not a laboratory block of homogeneous stone. It was a real reservoir with real heterogeneity, a real production history, and therefore a chance to test whether nuclear fracture theory survived contact with the industrial subsurface.

The selected site lay on what is now Carson National Forest land near the eastern boundary adjoining the Jicarilla Apache Nation. That geography matters for reasons the early technical literature could treat as peripheral. Underground engineering projects do not exist only at depth. Their surface institutions sit in jurisdictions, watersheds, grazing areas, tribal geographies, resource leases, road networks, and human histories. A test can be thousands of feet underground and still have neighbors.

The reservoir problem was three-dimensional. The Pictured Cliffs interval was not simply a slab waiting to be shattered. Engineers had to model the depth of burial, overburden, rock strength, porosity, permeability, gas pressure, water content, and the likely response to shock. They had data from conventional wells and from the growing science of underground nuclear effects, but the most consequential quantity—the useful fracture network after a blast in this specific reservoir—could not be measured until the blast happened.

This is the hidden asymmetry in many large experiments. The variables easiest to measure beforehand are not always the variables that determine success afterward.

Yield could be estimated. Device depth could be controlled. Borehole geometry could be engineered. Seismic stations could be placed. Gas samples could be planned. But fracture connectivity at reservoir scale had to be inferred from production, pressure behavior, reentry drilling, and models after the event. The program could know the energy deposited more confidently than it could know the industrial shape that energy would take.

That distinction would haunt the gas-stimulation series.

A nuclear explosion creates different zones in the surrounding rock. Closest to the device, temperatures and pressures are high enough to vaporize and melt material. Outside that cavity, rock is crushed and fractured. As pressure falls, the cavity may remain temporarily open until its roof collapses, producing a vertical chimney of broken rock. Fractures can extend beyond the chimney. In a reservoir, engineers hoped those fractures would act as durable flow paths into gas-bearing strata.

The ideal image is easy to draw: a well intersects a huge connected volume of newly permeable reservoir. The actual subsurface is impolite. Bedding planes redirect stress. Strong and weak layers respond differently. Fractures may be abundant where they contribute little production and sparse where they are needed. Some may close as pressure changes. The chimney itself can offer high permeability without extending useful connectivity far enough into the surrounding formation.

Gasbuggy's design also contained a commercial assumption: the gas-bearing formation would remain a gas reservoir after it became a nuclear environment. This sounds facetious, but it is the central constraint. A civil engineering blast can treat broken rock as waste. A gas-stimulation blast needs the material surrounding the explosive to continue behaving as a resource.

That includes chemistry.

The detonation would produce radionuclides. Some products would be immobilized in fused or melted rock; others could enter gas phases or interact with formation fluids. The very permeability the project wanted to increase could create transport paths. The production system would then deliberately reduce pressure and move gas toward the well. In other words, the project combined two goals that have opposite intuitions: contain nuclear byproducts underground and efficiently transport reservoir gas out of the altered zone.

The solution was measurement rather than denial. Gas would be sampled. Production could be flared rather than sold. Radioactive constituents could be identified and tracked. The program could ask how concentrations changed with time and production. This was serious radiochemical engineering, not a hand wave.

But the need for it exposed the difference between an experiment and a commodity system. An experimental gas stream is allowed to be interesting. A commercial gas stream is supposed to be boring.

Natural gas already has specifications because pipelines and appliances depend on composition and heating value. Add radioactivity and the product acquires an entirely different regulatory and social meaning. Even if concentrations could be reduced to acceptable levels through decay, processing, or dilution, the system would need rules for proving that acceptance continuously. The commodity would carry a story from the wellhead into the market.

Site selection therefore had to do two things at once. It had to maximize the chance of a technically interpretable result and minimize the chance that the result escaped the administrative boundaries built around it. That is a different problem from finding the geologically best reservoir.

This tradeoff appears all through nuclear history. Test sites are chosen partly for physics and partly for remoteness. Waste repositories are judged partly by geology and partly by political jurisdiction. Uranium mines, enrichment plants, reactors, and weapons complexes become landscapes in which engineering and governance cannot be cleanly separated. Gasbuggy looked like an oilfield experiment because much of its surface equipment came from oilfield practice. It remained a nuclear site because the source of stimulation changed the meaning of every failure mode.

The relationship with El Paso Natural Gas sharpened the experimental design. A private gas company knew what a useful well looked like. That gave the project a practical reference point. It also meant that success could not be defined only through cavity dimensions or seismic records. Gas had to flow.

This sounds like a virtue, and it was. It is also where many government-industry demonstration projects become most revealing. The public partner may be satisfied by learning; the commercial partner ultimately needs a product. Research can call an unexpected outcome valuable because it reduces uncertainty. Industry can learn the same thing and decide not to build anything.

Gasbuggy's site was thus both laboratory and prospective well. The experiment would produce at least four distinct outputs: data on underground nuclear effects, data on reservoir stimulation, a stream of natural gas, and a political demonstration of peaceful nuclear technology. Those outputs could disagree.

A technically valuable test could be a commercial failure. A commercially interesting production increase could be politically unacceptable. A well-contained event could still produce an awkward radioactive commodity. A publicity success could outlive the engineering case. There was no single meter labeled SUCCESS.

This matters because histories written after cancellation often assign one. They say the gas was radioactive, therefore the project failed; or that production increased, therefore it worked; or that opposition killed it, therefore politics defeated engineering. Each claim selects one output and promotes it to the whole system.

The site itself prevents that simplification.

The device was emplaced at roughly 4,200 feet below ground. DOE's later stewardship material gives a depth of 4,227 feet; some historical summaries round differently. The estimated yield is generally given at about 29 kilotons. These numbers have the deceptive cleanliness of a physics problem. At detonation, however, they became inputs into a geological body that had never agreed to be clean.

Rock absorbs a shock according to its properties. Fractures go where stress allows. Gas moves according to pressure and permeability. Radioactive species follow chemistry rather than program intent.

The experiment's most important results would therefore emerge slowly. The shot itself lasted an instant. The reservoir answer took years.

This inversion is easy to miss because nuclear history is drawn toward the flash, even when the flash is underground. Gasbuggy's famous moment is December 10, 1967. Its more consequential moments occurred during reentry, testing, flaring, sampling, pressure decline, and interpretation. The bomb created the object of study. Production revealed whether the object was useful.

That means the engineering story does not climax when the device detonates. It begins there.

Before that could happen, though, the project had to transform an ordinary borehole into something the oil and gas industry had never before needed: a well designed to survive being connected to a nuclear explosion.
