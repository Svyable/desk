# Nuclear Well

*Project Gasbuggy and the Bomb Beneath the Gas Field*

Sven Hardy Benson

Gasbuggy survives in photographs as a piece of atomic-age theater: drilling equipment, a remote patch of New Mexico, men standing beside machinery, and the knowledge that a nuclear explosive is somewhere below their boots. The photograph invites us to ask the wrong question first. What kind of civilization puts a bomb at the bottom of a gas well?

A more useful question is why competent people thought it might work.

They had a real reservoir problem. Vast quantities of natural gas sat in formations whose low permeability made them difficult to produce. They had a new body of underground-nuclear-effects knowledge showing that deeply buried explosions could create cavities and extensive fracture zones while remaining largely contained. They had an industrial partner that understood gas fields. And they had a federal program—Plowshare—built around the proposition that the extraordinary energy of nuclear explosives might be used for something other than war.

The experiments that followed are usually remembered through the answer history eventually supplied: no commercial nuclear-fracked gas industry exists. This book begins earlier, while the answer was still being measured.

Its evidence rule is simple. Documented program facts remain facts even when later interpretation changes. Contemporary technical claims are attributed rather than promoted into truth. Testimony and reporting are not proof. Later monitoring cannot retroactively erase earlier uncertainty, and earlier fear cannot substitute for evidence of later contamination. Patents and proposals establish that ideas existed, not that they worked. Where the record is incomplete, the uncertainty stays visible.

That discipline matters because Gasbuggy is most interesting in the middle. The nuclear explosion did fracture rock. The stimulated well did produce more gas than nearby conventional wells. The product gas did contain measurable radioactive constituents. The fracture network did disappoint expectations. Federal planners really did model futures containing hundreds of nuclear-stimulated wells. Those propositions can coexist.

The book follows what happened when they did.

The aim is not to rescue Plowshare from ridicule or to turn it into a morality play about atomic hubris. It is to understand a harder category of technological failure: the technology whose central effect is real enough to keep hope alive while the number of surrounding conditions required for an industry keeps growing.

Gasbuggy is a book about that number.
