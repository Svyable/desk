# Rulison Goes Bigger

Project Rulison was deeper, larger, and harder to dismiss as a curiosity.

On September 10, 1969, a nuclear explosive was detonated more than eight thousand feet below the surface in Garfield County, Colorado. DOE's modern technical work generally describes the yield as 40 kilotons. The target was the low-permeability Williams Fork Formation of the Mesaverde Group, where gas occurred in sandstone lenses interbedded with shale. The project was a joint experiment involving the Atomic Energy Commission, industry, and the Department of the Interior. Its stated purpose was not mysterious: test the economic and technical feasibility of stimulating natural-gas production with an underground nuclear explosion.

If Gasbuggy had been an opening argument, Rulison was the reply to several objections.

The device was placed much deeper. The reservoir was different. The yield was larger. The industrial partner was different. The program now possessed field data from Gasbuggy rather than relying entirely on theory and weapons-test analogies. The question was whether a better-configured experiment could turn proof of effect into proof of method.

The shot was contained. A cavity formed, later collapsed, and left a chimney. Production testing followed. The gas again told the program something it did not want to hear cleanly.

According to DOE's current Rulison fact sheet, production testing continued from 1970 into April 1971. Produced gas was flared. Gas and water samples were analyzed to track radioactivity. Radionuclide concentrations declined during testing but did not disappear sufficiently to make the gas marketable. The reentry well was closed after the final test and eventually plugged and abandoned.

That outcome is important because it removes one of the easiest ways to explain Gasbuggy away. The first experiment's radioactive product was not simply a freak feature of one shallowish reservoir or one device. A second serious field test encountered the same category of commercial difficulty.

The details were not identical. They never are. Rulison's radionuclide inventory and migration pathways depended on its device, geology, depth, and post-shot production. DOE's later groundwater modeling emphasizes that most radionuclides were retained in solidified melt glass, while mobile gas-phase radionuclides had different fates. Krypton-85 and carbon-14 were largely removed during production testing; tritium remained a longer-term migration concern because of its mobility in water and gas-related species.

This is exactly the kind of nuance that disappears when a story becomes "the gas was radioactive." The nuclear event created a complicated source, not a uniform poison cloud. Most radioactivity stayed near the cavity. Some mobile species moved. Production deliberately removed some. Long-term stewardship focuses on the pathways that remain plausible.

For commercialization, however, the distinction offered limited comfort. A pipeline does not sell "mostly uncontaminated according to source partitioning." It sells gas that meets specifications.

Rulison therefore sharpened the product problem.

It also sharpened the land problem because the site sat in a region whose natural-gas value did not end when the nuclear experiment did. Later commercial drilling around Rulison forced the federal government and Colorado regulators to confront a bizarre inheritance: valuable gas remained near a cavity containing residual radioactivity from an abandoned stimulation technology.

Modern stewardship now includes institutional controls restricting subsurface disturbance around the site and review of drilling proposed nearby. DOE monitors gas, groundwater, and surface water for radioactivity. The historical project wanted to increase permeability around a well; the contemporary management problem includes preventing future wells from unintentionally creating a pathway into the contaminated zone.

The reversal could not be cleaner.

Rulison also illustrates how a failed technology can remain physically present inside the success of its competitor. Modern gas development expanded through western Colorado using conventional drilling and hydraulic fracturing. As that happened, operators approached areas near the old nuclear test. The resource Plowshare had tried to unlock with a bomb became commercially interesting through other means, and the bomb became a constraint on accessing it.

There is no better physical demonstration of technological substitution.

The Rulison site did not merely become obsolete. It had to be routed around.

This later history makes the original economic optimism more poignant. The Plowshare program's argument was never that natural gas could not be produced without nuclear stimulation anywhere. It was that low-productivity formations contained resources conventional methods could not efficiently recover. In regions where those resources later became valuable through nonnuclear technology, the nuclear experiment's subsurface remains became a small zone of permanent exceptionalism inside an otherwise ordinary gas province.

The comparison also warns against triumphalist stories about modern hydraulic fracturing. The fact that a nonnuclear technology won does not make its environmental questions trivial. Hydraulic fracturing has its own water, methane, induced-seismicity, surface-footprint, waste, and community impacts. Technology choice is rarely a contest between harm and no harm. Rulison shows something narrower: the competing method did not require an underground nuclear explosion or radioactive product stream, and that difference became overwhelming once nonnuclear productivity improved enough.

Rulison's production tests also weaken another retrospective defense of Plowshare—that the central obstacle was public irrationality about the word *nuclear*. The marketability problem was measured at the well. Concentrations declined but remained a barrier. Public perception may have magnified that barrier, but it did not invent the radionuclides.

At the same time, opponents who treat any residual radioactivity as proof of regional disaster overstate the record. Decades of monitoring and modeling are designed precisely to test whether material is moving from the cavity into surrounding resources. The contemporary program does not describe widespread off-site contamination as established. The site remains under institutional control because uncertainty and residual source material matter, not because every feared pathway has been observed.

This evidence posture is essential for the book. Plowshare does not need catastrophe to fail. The more interesting failure is ordinary enough to be measured in a spreadsheet: product cannot be sold, special controls persist, and a cheaper competitor improves.

After Rulison, however, the program did not stop. The result could still be interpreted as a design problem. One explosion stimulated one vertical region. Western tight-gas formations could extend through thick intervals. Perhaps the way to make nuclear stimulation competitive was to distribute the explosive energy more intelligently through the reservoir.

That thought led toward Rio Blanco and its three-device array.

But between Rulison and Rio Blanco, the political environment was changing as quickly as the engineering.

The National Environmental Policy Act took effect. Environmental review gained a new federal structure. Local opposition to Plowshare projects had precedents, especially Project Chariot in Alaska. Nuclear testing no longer existed in the same public world that had created the program in the 1950s.

A peaceful bomb still needed a place to happen.

And places had people.
