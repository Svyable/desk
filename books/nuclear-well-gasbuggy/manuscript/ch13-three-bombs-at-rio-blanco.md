# Three Bombs at Rio Blanco

Rio Blanco is the point where nuclear gas stimulation stopped pretending that one explosion was naturally shaped like a reservoir.

On May 17, 1973, three nuclear explosives were detonated nearly simultaneously in a single emplacement hole in northwestern Colorado. Each had a reported yield of about 33 kilotons. They sat at different depths—roughly 5,840, 6,230, and 6,690 feet—inside a gas-bearing sandstone sequence. The array was intended to stimulate a larger vertical interval than one source could reach.

It was an elegant answer to the geometry problem.

It was also three bombs.

That tension is Rio Blanco in one line. The program had learned from earlier experiments that the useful fractured region did not automatically fill the reservoir volume planners wanted. The response was to distribute explosive sources through the productive section. In reservoir terms, this was a refinement. In system terms, it multiplied the defining burden of the technology.

A three-source design made physical sense. Tight gas in the Piceance Basin occurred in multiple sandstone lenses and intervals rather than one perfectly uniform bed. If each explosion generated its own cavity, chimney, and fracture zone, the combined system might create overlapping or adjacent regions of enhanced permeability through a taller column. The well could then communicate with more gas-bearing rock.

The idea resembles staged stimulation in the most abstract possible way: attack a long reservoir interval at several points rather than rely on one treatment to reach everything. Modern hydraulic-fracturing operations perform this logic repeatedly along horizontal laterals with many controlled stages. Rio Blanco performed it vertically with nuclear explosives.

The comparison should not be pushed farther than it can carry. Nuclear shock fracturing and hydraulic fracture propagation are physically different. Modern completions use fluid pressure, proppant, perforation clusters, and controlled sequences that can be adjusted across a well. Rio Blanco's devices released energy orders of magnitude more violently and created cavities and nuclear products. What the comparison reveals is not direct lineage. It reveals a shared geometric problem: low-permeability reservoirs demand distributed connectivity.

The Plowshare program had arrived at that insight by escalation.

The event was the final U.S. Plowshare nuclear test. That fact gives Rio Blanco retrospective finality it did not possess in advance. In 1973, the program still had planned projects and commercial ambitions. Wagon Wheel in Wyoming had been studied as another gas-stimulation experiment. Federal energy forecasts still treated nuclear stimulation as potentially significant. Rio Blanco was supposed to provide evidence for what came next.

Instead it became the last shot.

The reasons cannot be read from that date alone. Programs end through decisions spread over budgets, reports, politics, rival technologies, and changing institutional priorities. But Rio Blanco arrived after Gasbuggy and Rulison had already established the pattern: nuclear explosions could create productive fracture systems, and the resulting gas and economics remained problematic.

Rio Blanco's design tried to move one side of that equation.

The array also made radiological accounting more complex. Three devices meant three nuclear source regions. Even if each event remained contained and most radionuclides remained immobilized near the cavities, the production system had to deal with gases and fluids influenced by multiple shot zones. More stimulation meant more nuclear inventory.

This is not a proof that a multi-device system could never have produced marketable gas. It is an engineering trade. The method's performance could be improved by adding the very component that made commercialization difficult.

That is why Rio Blanco deserves more attention than the phrase "another failed test." It shows a technology responding intelligently to its data and becoming less plausible as a system.

The field program around Rio Blanco generated pressure tests, production data, gas samples, geologic studies, and radiological monitoring. Those reports should be read shot by shot and test by test in a publication-ready version of this book. The current public record is sufficient for the larger historical boundary: Rio Blanco did not demonstrate an economically compelling, clean commercial gas-stimulation method that industry then adopted. The U.S. series ended.

Its long-term legacy is physical. DOE now manages the Rio Blanco site through monitoring and land-use controls. The modern oil and gas industry operates in the broader region, which means the old detonation zones must be considered when new drilling is proposed. DOE and Colorado maintain restrictions and review around the site to avoid pathways that could encounter residual radioactive material.

Again the future reversed.

The experiment's purpose was to create extra pathways through tight rock. The stewardship program's purpose includes making sure future pathways do not connect to the wrong rock.

There is something almost architectural about the irony. Rio Blanco built invisible rooms underground and then left future developers instructions not to drill through the walls.

A technology that once promised access became a zone of avoidance.

The three-device array also marks the limit of a particular kind of technological optimism. Early Plowshare rhetoric could imagine the bomb as a universal source of cheap mechanical work. Rio Blanco is what happens when the universal tool meets a specific reservoir. The tool must be placed at three exact depths because geology refuses universality.

By then conventional competitors were learning the same lesson through less absolute means. High-volume hydraulic fracture treatments could be tailored, repeated, and improved without requiring a nuclear event. The advantage of nuclear scale had to exceed not just the performance of old hydraulic fractures but the value of adaptability itself.

Adaptability is difficult to price before a technology learns it needs it.

Rio Blanco also arrived in a policy environment transformed from the one that had launched Plowshare. The environmental movement was institutionally stronger. NEPA had changed federal review. Nuclear power controversies were widening. Arms-control negotiations were moving toward explicit rules for underground tests and peaceful nuclear explosions. A commercial technology based on repeated nuclear events would have to pass through all of those systems.

The three devices went off successfully.

That is the phrase the program could still claim: successfully.

The explosives functioned. The event was conducted. The reservoir was stimulated enough to study. Data came back.

And then the future did not.

No commercial field filled with Rio Blanco arrays. No standardized nuclear gas service emerged. The program's next proposed western gas experiment, Wagon Wheel, would stop before detonation.

Sometimes the most informative experiment is the one a mature program decides not to perform.
