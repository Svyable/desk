# The Geometry That Did Not Cooperate

Engineers are not usually accused of excessive faith in circles, but reservoir diagrams make the temptation understandable.

Draw a well as a vertical line. Put a point source beside it. Draw concentric regions for vaporization, crushing, fracturing, and elastic disturbance. Add a scale bar. The whole intervention can be held in one glance.

The drawing is not wrong. The reservoir is simply less obedient than the drawing.

Gasbuggy's disappointing fracture reach exposed the problem. The detonation created a cavity and chimney and fractured surrounding rock, but official summaries later noted that fractures did not extend through the gas-bearing formation as extensively as expected. The difference between expected and useful extent was not a minor geometric correction. It went directly into economics because drainage volume determines how much additional gas a well can ultimately recover.

The first source of trouble is layering. Sedimentary formations are records of changing environments. Sandstone beds can be interbedded with shales, siltstones, coal, and other units whose mechanical properties differ. Interfaces can arrest or redirect fractures. The Pictured Cliffs is not a homogeneous block cut from a quarry for an experiment. It is geology with memory.

The second source is stress. Rock deep underground is already loaded. Fractures do not propagate into a neutral material; they respond to the existing stress field. Hydraulic-fracturing engineers exploit this by using fluid pressure to open fractures preferentially along predictable orientations. A nuclear explosion overwhelms local strength near the source, but the farther its disturbance travels, the more the regional stress field matters.

The third source is usefulness. A mechanically damaged crack is not necessarily a conductive path after pressures normalize. Fractures can close. Fine material can reduce conductivity. A fracture can extend into a layer with little gas. A region can be intensely broken and still contribute less cumulative production than its apparent size suggests.

The project therefore faced an inverse problem. It knew what had been put in: explosive yield, depth, geology as characterized before the shot. It measured what came out: pressures, production rates, gas composition, seismic observations. From those signals it had to infer the shape of the underground network.

Inverse problems reward humility because multiple hidden structures can generate similar surface behavior. They also reward models because there is no alternative to inference.

This is where Gasbuggy becomes unexpectedly modern. Contemporary unconventional-reservoir engineering still wrestles with the gap between stimulated rock volume and productive rock volume. Microseismic events can map where rock responds during hydraulic fracturing, but not every event marks a propped, conductive fracture. Pressure interference can reveal connectivity that imaging cannot. Production history supplies a long-term verdict that initial diagnostic maps only approximate.

The tools have improved. The conceptual difficulty remains.

Nuclear stimulation tried to solve uncertainty with scale. If the fracture network could be made large enough, perhaps local heterogeneity would matter less. But increasing yield has diminishing usefulness if the added damage extends into nonproductive rock or if fracture conductivity does not scale as hoped. It can also increase containment requirements and radioactive inventory.

The program's next experiments can be read as attempts to make the geometry more aligned with reservoirs. Rulison used a larger explosive in a different tight-gas setting. Rio Blanco went further by placing three explosives vertically, spaced so their effects could stimulate a thicker interval.

This is a crucial design progression. The problem was no longer simply "make a bigger fractured sphere." It was "shape the stimulated region so it overlaps more of the gas-bearing column."

Multiple devices offered a crude form of geometry control. Instead of asking one explosion to reach every productive layer, place sources at multiple depths. The approach resembles the logic of staged stimulation, though the mechanisms and scales are radically different. Create repeated zones of connectivity rather than one center.

The price of that insight was repetition of the nuclear event itself.

The technology was learning its way toward complexity. A single bomb had sounded elegant because nuclear energy density promised to replace many smaller interventions. Field results pushed the design toward multiple charges, more precise placement, more diagnostics, and more site-specific modeling. The system began to lose the simplicity that justified its extremity.

This is another general pattern worth rescuing from the history. Radical technologies are often sold as simplifications: one reactor replaces mountains of fuel; one launch replaces many; one powerful model automates many tasks; one explosive stimulates an enormous reservoir. When reality forces the radical technology to acquire the same incremental controls as its conventional competitors, its exceptional burdens become harder to justify.

Gasbuggy had still taught something valuable. It showed that explosion-generated fractures could improve well productivity and also gave a field calibration for models that had previously leaned on weapons-test data and smaller-scale experiments. Even a commercial failure can be a scientific success if it maps the limits correctly.

The difficulty is that programs rarely know at the time which kind of success they are producing.

A research organization can take a disappointing fracture radius and call it information. A commercial operator must ask whether the information makes the next well more profitable. Plowshare occupied both worlds. The AEC could justify another experiment to refine the physics. El Paso and later industrial partners needed a path toward deployable economics.

The geometry question therefore migrated into forecasts. How many wells would a field need? What spacing? What yield? How much gas per stimulated well over twenty years? How quickly would production decline? At what gas price would nuclear stimulation beat competing completions?

Once those values entered a national energy model, the irregular underground fracture network became a line item in a future economy.

That translation—from uncertain geometry to projected infrastructure—is where chapter ten begins.

One federal scenario eventually contained a number so specific it almost looks like a result rather than an assumption: 676 nuclear-stimulated wells completed by 1985.

The rock had not cooperated with the circle.

The forecast was about to cooperate with multiplication.
