# The Well With a Bomb at the Bottom

There is a concrete marker in the Carson National Forest where the future used to be. The surrounding country does not look like the remains of an atomic experiment. It looks like northwestern New Mexico: ponderosa and piñon, open ground, roads cut for ordinary resource work, the San Juan Basin doing what sedimentary basins do—holding old carbon in layers that industry has spent a century learning how to persuade upward.

More than four thousand feet below that marker is the part of the story that cannot be reclaimed into scenery. On December 10, 1967, the United States Atomic Energy Commission and El Paso Natural Gas Company detonated a nuclear explosive underground in the Pictured Cliffs Formation. The event was called Gasbuggy. Its job was not to test a warhead against a target or to excavate a harbor. Its job was to improve a gas well.

That sentence still has enough absurdity in it to protect us from understanding what happened.

A bomb in a gas reservoir sounds like the punch line. It was actually the engineering proposition. The Pictured Cliffs held natural gas in rock whose permeability was low enough that an ordinary well could intersect a large quantity of gas and still fail to produce it quickly. Permeability is a cruelly practical property. A reservoir may contain hydrocarbons in abundance and remain commercially disappointing because the fluid cannot move through the rock fast enough to reach the wellbore. The problem is less like finding an empty warehouse than like finding a full warehouse whose aisles have been filled with concrete.

The Plowshare idea was to make aisles.

A deeply buried nuclear explosion does not simply leave a spherical hole. In the first instant, rock close to the device is vaporized or melted; farther out, it is crushed and fractured. A cavity forms. The cavity can collapse, creating a rubble-filled chimney. Fractures propagate into the surrounding formation. If the explosive is placed inside a gas-bearing layer, the new fracture network might connect volumes of previously reluctant reservoir to a production well. Instead of pumping fluid at pressure to crack the rock from the well outward, the proposed method put an enormous pulse of energy inside the formation and let the geology absorb it.

The attraction is easier to see if we refuse the hindsight supplied by modern shale development. A reader living after horizontal drilling and massive multistage hydraulic fracturing has to work to imagine a time when low-permeability gas looked like an enormous stranded resource without an obvious industrial completion method. Plowshare planners did not invent the underlying problem. They proposed a spectacular solution to a real one.

Gasbuggy was also not a lone fever dream. It belonged to a federal program created to investigate peaceful applications for nuclear explosives: excavation, mineral recovery, underground engineering, isotope production, and stimulation of oil and gas reservoirs. The name Plowshare came from the biblical image of beating swords into plowshares, an image whose moral neatness the program never managed to inherit. The explosive remained a nuclear explosive. Only the intended use had changed.

This distinction generated a peculiar institutional confidence. If a device could be designed, emplaced, contained, and predicted well enough for military testing, perhaps its energy could be treated as an industrial tool. The bomb becomes, in this vocabulary, a source term. Rock becomes a medium. Fallout becomes containment. The word *peaceful* does a great deal of work, but so does *engineering*. A terrifying machine can become administratively ordinary when every part of it acquires a measurement.

The Department of Energy's current Gasbuggy fact sheet gives the later story with bureaucratic compression. A reentry well was drilled into the collapse chimney. Production tests between July 1968 and October 1969 recovered roughly 213 million cubic feet of natural gas. Production was greater than in nearby conventional wells. The gas nevertheless contained measurable radioactive constituents. Its heating value was significantly lower. Fracturing into the gas-bearing formation above the chimney did not extend as far as expected. These are not the results of an experiment in which nothing happened. They are worse for any simple story than that.

Suppose Gasbuggy had produced no useful fracture network at all. The program could have died as a clean engineering failure. Suppose, at the other extreme, it had produced huge quantities of clean gas at low cost with a stable and predictable fracture volume. The moral questions would remain, but the technical argument would be straightforward.

Instead, Gasbuggy entered the dangerous middle category: a demonstration that made its mechanism more credible while making the technology around that mechanism harder to defend.

This category appears repeatedly in frontier engineering. A prototype proves the centerpiece and exposes the system. Flight is possible; now build airports, maintenance organizations, air-traffic control, weather services, training standards, insurance, noise rules, and a business model. Nuclear fission works; now invent fuel cycles, containment, regulation, waste handling, security, emergency planning, and long-lived institutions. A reservoir can be fractured with a nuclear explosion; now explain what happens when the product itself carries radionuclides, when fracture geometry differs from the model, when the gas has to enter commerce, when every well is also a nuclear event, and when the method is repeated hundreds of times.

Plowshare's most revealing documents are sometimes the optimistic ones because they show how quickly a successful experiment can turn into an industrial assumption. A 1972 federal energy outlook treated technical feasibility for nuclear gas stimulation as established by Gasbuggy and Rulison and modeled scenarios in which hundreds of nuclear-stimulated wells might be completed by 1985 if policy issues were resolved and later projects performed favorably. One case contemplated 676 wells by that date.

Six hundred seventy-six is the number that turns Gasbuggy from a historical curiosity into a systems problem.

One underground nuclear explosion can be described as an experiment. Six hundred seventy-six begin to resemble an industry. An industry has supply chains, trained crews, standard designs, regulatory categories, waste streams, accounting conventions, accident histories, neighbors, lawyers, and the slow habit of making extraordinary things ordinary. The crucial question is not whether the first event can be contained. It is what repeated normal operation looks like after the novelty has been removed.

The number also reveals something about technological forecasting. Planners frequently scale the desired output more cleanly than they scale the exception handling. If one nuclear-stimulated well produces a certain quantity of gas, multiplying the output by hundreds is arithmetically easy. But the institutional burden does not necessarily scale linearly. Each site has geology. Each event has emplacement and containment questions. Each gas stream has radiological accounting. Each community has its own threshold for accepting an atomic industrial process nearby. Each explosion exists simultaneously as an energy project and as a nuclear detonation in an international system trying to distinguish peaceful explosions from weapons tests.

The method's defenders did not ignore all of this. It would be unfair—and historically lazy—to make them into people who could see only the blast. Plowshare generated extraordinary amounts of monitoring, modeling, geological work, radiochemistry, seismic analysis, production testing, and economic calculation. The nuclear bureaucracy understood better than most institutions that a nuclear event leaves evidence. Its habit was to instrument that evidence.

But instrumentation has a seduction of its own. A risk that can be measured can begin to feel like a risk that has been mastered. A contaminant with a concentration becomes a number in a table. A fracture network becomes an inferred radius. A projected decline curve becomes a production forecast. The difficulty is that the commercial and political system does not consume tables. It consumes gas, land, trust, treaties, and money.

There is another hindsight trap here. Modern hydraulic fracturing tempts us to say that Gasbuggy was simply an inferior precursor to fracking. That is too tidy. Hydraulic fracturing was already an established technique before Gasbuggy; the competition between nuclear stimulation and increasingly large nonnuclear fracture treatments was part of the historical decision space. What changed was not the invention of the idea that rock could be fractured from a well. What changed was the scale, precision, repeatability, economics, and industrial learning available to do it without a nuclear explosive.

That difference matters because it keeps the book from becoming a parable about foolish ancestors. The Plowshare engineers identified an important constraint correctly. Low permeability really did stand between enormous gas resources and production. The industry that eventually unlocked those resources attacked the same physical bottleneck through another technological lineage.

The failure, then, was not noticing the wrong problem. It was believing for too long that the largest available pulse of energy was necessarily the best way to solve it.

Gasbuggy's physical remains now sit inside a stewardship system rather than an energy system. The Atomic Energy Commission decommissioned the site; later cleanup removed surface facilities and contaminated material; the Department of Energy's Office of Legacy Management now maintains records and monitoring responsibilities. Surface and groundwater sampling began decades ago. DOE notes that monitoring frequency was reduced after repeated sampling did not identify Gasbuggy-related contamination in the monitored waters. The land is used again for recreation and grazing.

That outcome is often offered as reassurance, and reasonably so. It is also worth noticing what kind of success it represents. The site is no longer asked to make gas. It is asked to remain legible.

A technology that was once advertised through its future production is now managed through its records.

The switch is almost too clean. In 1967, the ground was a reservoir to be opened. Today it is a historical system to be watched. The same subsurface that engineers wanted to make more permeable is surrounded by an institution whose job is to know where contamination is not moving.

This book follows that reversal. It will stay close to the experiments because the details keep correcting the legend. Gasbuggy was not an attempt to make an earthquake. It was not a secret strike against the American West. It was not a hoax. It was a serious, joint government-industry effort to use a nuclear explosive as a reservoir-engineering tool. Its product tests generated real data. Its successors—Rulison and Rio Blanco—made the proposition more ambitious. Wagon Wheel came close enough to show what the program expected next. International arms-control negotiations eventually had to treat peaceful nuclear explosions as a distinct verification problem. Meanwhile, conventional fracturing improved until the nuclear option lost not only its political appeal but much of its technical reason for existing.

The first task is to resist the urge to begin at the end.

On December 10, 1967, the question was still open. A device sat more than four thousand feet underground. Above it were cables, instruments, drilling equipment, engineers, officials, workers, and a landscape whose geology had been converted into a forecast. Below it was a gas reservoir with a permeability problem.

Then the explosive went off.

The rock answered first.
