# The First Gas Back

Production is where theories become fluids.

Before reentry, Gasbuggy could be discussed through seismic records, cavity models, emplacement data, and calculations of fracture radius. Once the well began to produce, the reservoir gained a voice more difficult to translate. Pressure fell. Gas moved. Rates changed. Samples came to the surface. The underground object began reporting itself in the units that industry understood.

DOE's current summary says production testing started in July 1968 and continued through October 1969 in five tests. Roughly 213 million cubic feet of gas were produced during that program. The stimulated well produced more gas than nearby conventional wells.

That is the strongest sentence available to anyone who wants to argue that Gasbuggy worked.

It deserves to stand without immediate qualification.

An underground nuclear explosion had been used to alter a low-permeability natural-gas reservoir, and the altered system produced at higher rates than nearby conventional wells. The project had therefore crossed a threshold no laboratory model could cross. The proposed mechanism had become field performance.

Now the qualifications can return, because they are not an attempt to erase the result. They explain why the result was insufficient.

Production rate is a time series, not a trophy. A well can begin strongly and decline quickly. A completion can create excellent conductivity close to the well while adding less connected reservoir volume than expected. The initial rate may reflect pressure stored in the newly opened region rather than durable access to a large drainage area. Gasbuggy's rates declined toward more ordinary behavior over time, and later federal descriptions emphasize that the fracture system did not extend through the productive formation as extensively as planners had expected.

The first lesson is therefore one the petroleum industry already knew but technological demonstrations repeatedly forget: early performance and ultimate recovery are different quantities.

The second lesson was chemical.

Natural gas is called natural because marketing departments were allowed to name it before chemists could object. A pipeline gas stream is never simply methane. It can include heavier hydrocarbons, carbon dioxide, nitrogen, water vapor, hydrogen sulfide, helium, and other components depending on the field. Processing exists to turn reservoir gas into a commodity with acceptable heating value and composition.

Gasbuggy added a category no ordinary gas plant wanted: radionuclides associated with the nuclear event.

Produced gas was flared during testing rather than sent directly into commerce. Flaring made the experiment possible because engineers could draw down the reservoir, measure rates, and sample composition without pretending the product had already passed the social and regulatory test of becoming pipeline gas. The flame at the surface was therefore both a production tool and an admission. The well could make gas; the project had not yet made a commodity.

This distinction was not permanent in the optimistic program model. Nuclear-stimulation proponents explored whether radioactive constituents would decline with time and production, whether gas could be processed or blended, and whether eventual concentrations could meet standards. The commercial future depended partly on separating temporary post-shot behavior from persistent product quality.

The relevant nuclides had different origins and half-lives. Tritium could appear in water and hydrocarbon species. Carbon-14, krypton-85, and other radionuclides could matter differently depending on chemistry and pathway. The technical literature around the Plowshare gas experiments is full of exactly the kind of work that popular summaries skip: where a nuclide resides, how fast it is produced, how it partitions, how it moves, how much reaches the well, and what dose might follow from a plausible use pathway.

Those questions are essential because "radioactive gas" is too crude for science and potentially too forgiving for commerce.

A gas can be radiologically measurable without being acutely dangerous. It can also be below a health threshold and still be commercially unacceptable. Commodity standards include more than toxicology. Customers care about reliability, regulators care about compliance, utilities care about liability, and the word *nuclear* changes risk perception in ways a concentration number cannot neutralize.

Plowshare's economic models therefore faced a peculiar burden. They had to convert a stigma into a process specification.

If radioactive constituents fell rapidly enough, perhaps the well could be shut in for decay before production. If they could be removed during gas processing, perhaps the treatment cost could be folded into economics. If concentrations were low enough, perhaps gas could be blended with uncontaminated production. Every option moved the technology away from the elegant picture of one explosive creating one superior gas well and toward a specialized system built around the consequences of the explosive.

There was also a straightforward energy penalty. DOE's later Gasbuggy summary states that the produced gas had significantly lower heating value. The blast had not merely changed how easily gas flowed; it had changed what came out with it. Gas quality matters because sales are based on usable energy and because inert or undesirable constituents occupy pipeline capacity without delivering the same heat.

This is another place where "more production" can deceive. A higher volumetric flow of lower-value gas carrying constituents that require special handling is not directly comparable to the same flow of ordinary pipeline-quality gas.

Technological demonstrations love unit mismatches. The prototype is judged in the unit where it looks strongest, while the market pays in another.

Gasbuggy's strongest unit was probably raw flow improvement. Its weaker units included cumulative recovery, thermal value, contamination burden, and cost per saleable unit of energy.

The production tests were also doing something conceptually important to the containment argument. Before reentry, success meant that the nuclear event had remained underground. During production, the project intentionally transported material from the affected zone upward. This did not mean containment had failed. It meant containment was no longer a binary property.

The right question became selective containment: could the system keep the radioactive inventory where it belonged while allowing commercially valuable hydrocarbons to leave?

This is the sort of question nuclear engineering answers with barriers, chemistry, time, and measurement. It is also the sort of question an ordinary gas producer prefers not to have.

The difference between those cultures shaped the program. The AEC was accustomed to complex radionuclide accounting. A gas company was accustomed to complex reservoir and pipeline accounting. Gasbuggy forced both sets of ledgers onto the same product.

The experiment's five production tests therefore matter for more than the final 213 million cubic feet. They are a record of a technology trying to convert an extraordinary post-shot environment into something with a decline curve.

There is a strange dignity in that effort. The project did not stop at the dramatic event. It stayed for the unglamorous work of watching rates fall.

Most technological futures disappear in the unglamorous phase.

The first graph is exciting because it moves sharply. The fifth graph asks whether anyone should build another one.

By the end of Gasbuggy's test period, the answer was not yet simply no. The program could point to increased production. It could argue that a better reservoir, larger yield, improved placement, or multiple explosives might create more useful stimulation. It could treat radioactivity as an engineering and decay problem rather than an absolute prohibition. It could improve its models with field data.

And so Plowshare did what research programs do after a result that is neither triumph nor disaster.

It designed the next experiment.

Before moving to Rulison, however, Gasbuggy's gas deserves one more chapter. The word *radioactive* has been used so often in nuclear history that it can function as either incantation or dismissal. To understand why the product stream mattered, we need to be more exact about what it means for a fuel to contain the signature of the explosive that released it.
