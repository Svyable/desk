# The Nonnuclear Rival

The technology that defeated nuclear gas stimulation did not have to be elegant. It had to be repeatable.

Hydraulic fracturing had been used commercially since the late 1940s. By the time Gasbuggy was conceived, oilfield engineers already understood the basic maneuver: raise fluid pressure in a well until the formation fractures, then use the created pathway to improve flow. The method had limitations. Treatments were smaller than the enormous disturbed volumes nuclear proponents imagined. Fluids and proppants had to be pumped, handled, and designed for the specific formation. Fractures could fail to reach the desired region or close after treatment.

In other words, hydraulic fracturing had many of the same reservoir problems and almost none of the nuclear ones.

That did not make it automatically superior in the 1960s. If a low-permeability reservoir required a much larger stimulated volume than pumps and available completion designs could create economically, the nuclear option retained an argument. One contained explosive could in principle deliver vastly more energy underground than a conventional treatment.

The problem was that the conventional rival was allowed to improve incrementally.

A pump job can be made larger. Fluid chemistry can change. Proppant can improve. Wellbore placement can improve. Diagnostics can improve. A failed treatment can teach the next treatment without leaving a nuclear cavity. Operators can test variations across many wells and build statistical knowledge. Each experiment is comparatively cheap and reversible.

This is an underestimated advantage in technological competition. The winning technology is not always the one with the strongest central mechanism. It may be the one that can afford more learning cycles.

Plowshare's own open-file compilations acknowledged massive hydraulic fracturing as a supplement or alternative to nuclear stimulation. That fact is important because it prevents us from inventing a later rivalry that historical actors could not see. They could see it. By the mid-1970s, the AEC and successor institutions were comparing nuclear stimulation with increasingly ambitious nonnuclear methods.

The comparison became harsher as the nuclear program learned what its own scale cost.

A hydraulic fracture treatment can be stopped, redesigned, or repeated. A nuclear detonation cannot be un-fired. It creates a permanent radiological source region even when fully contained in the environmental sense. It requires device custody and nuclear-specific security. It creates an underground event visible to seismic networks and relevant to arms-control regimes. Its product stream may require radiological management.

The nuclear method had to outperform enough to pay for all of that.

If conventional stimulation achieved only half the production increase at one-tenth the institutional difficulty, the comparison could favor the conventional method even before direct monetary cost was calculated. Adaptability itself has economic value.

The later shale revolution made the contrast overwhelming, but it should not be read backward as inevitable. Horizontal drilling and multistage hydraulic fracturing emerged through decades of separate development, federal and private research, field experimentation, changes in gas markets, tax policy, computing, downhole tools, and service-company capability. There was no single invention waiting in 1967 to make Gasbuggy foolish.

What later development does show is that Plowshare had correctly identified permeability as a bottleneck and incorrectly treated nuclear-scale energy as the decisive scarce input.

The scarce inputs turned out to include control and repetition.

Modern unconventional completions are extraordinarily energetic industrial operations, but their energy is divided into stages. A horizontal well can place fracture treatments across thousands of feet of reservoir. Operators can vary cluster spacing, pump schedules, proppant loading, and fluid volume. They can compare neighboring wells and refine designs. This distributed intervention resembles the geometry problem Rio Blanco tried to solve with three nuclear devices, but it solves it with hundreds of smaller decisions rather than several irreversible ones.

The historical contrast is almost philosophical. Nuclear stimulation concentrated energy and centralized control. Hydraulic fracturing distributed energy and decentralized learning.

That does not imply a moral hierarchy. Hydraulic fracturing has generated serious environmental and social disputes: water use, wastewater disposal, induced seismicity from injection, methane emissions, local air pollution, truck traffic, land fragmentation, and boom-bust economics. A history that turns it into the clean hero would merely substitute one technological myth for another.

The relevant comparison is narrower. The nonnuclear method could improve without requiring society to normalize civilian nuclear detonations.

Once that became true, Plowshare's distinctive advantage—energy scale—was not enough.

This is a recurring pattern in technology. A radical system enters because incumbent performance seems physically bounded. The radical system demonstrates a new regime. Meanwhile the incumbent adopts better materials, controls, software, manufacturing, or operational knowledge and moves the boundary. The radical system is left carrying extraordinary complexity for an advantage that has shrunk.

Supersonic transports compete with improving subsonic aircraft. Exotic energy storage competes with better batteries. centralized mainframes compete with networks of cheaper machines. Giant one-shot interventions compete with modular systems that learn faster.

Nuclear gas stimulation was a giant one-shot intervention.

The comparison also changes how we should interpret Gasbuggy's technical success. The fact that production increased remains true. It may even be impressive. But engineering success is relative to available alternatives and to the cost of learning. A result can stay scientifically valid while becoming technologically irrelevant.

This is one reason old demonstration projects can look simultaneously prescient and absurd. They reveal the problem correctly and the solution incorrectly.

Plowshare saw stranded gas in tight formations decades before unconventional gas transformed U.S. energy markets. It understood that permeability, not merely resource quantity, controlled production. It invested in fracture mechanics, reservoir modeling, radiochemistry, seismic monitoring, and long-term production tests. Those are not the fingerprints of stupidity.

The failure was architectural. The proposed method imported an entire nuclear institution to solve a petroleum problem.

Conventional fracturing kept the problem inside petroleum engineering.

That separation mattered politically, economically, and internationally. The more a gas well could be treated as a gas well rather than as a nuclear event, the easier it was to repeat. Industrial repetition then generated learning, and learning widened the performance gap.

By the time modern tight-gas and shale development matured, the historical nuclear alternative had become almost impossible to imagine commercially. A company proposing to stimulate a reservoir with nuclear explosives today would not be offering a stronger version of fracking. It would be reintroducing an abandoned category of infrastructure with treaty, security, environmental, and public-acceptance implications that ordinary completions do not carry.

The disappearance is so complete that it can hide how seriously the alternative was once considered.

That is why the federal 1972 projections matter. They freeze the moment before the race was over. Hundreds of nuclear-stimulated wells were still imaginable. The conventional rival had not yet rendered the nuclear path absurd in administrative forecasts.

Technological inevitability is usually written after the contest.

The contest itself was messier.

One more institution helped close it: arms control. A nuclear explosion does not stop being a nuclear explosion when the invoice says natural gas.
