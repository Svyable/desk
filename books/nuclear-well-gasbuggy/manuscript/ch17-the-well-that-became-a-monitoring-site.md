# The Well That Became a Monitoring Site

The final product of Project Gasbuggy is a monitoring schedule.

That sounds harsher than it is. DOE's modern stewardship record is, in many ways, evidence of success. Surface facilities were removed. The land returned to recreation and grazing. Decades of sampling at springs, ponds, drainages, ranch wells, livestock wells, and other locations did not identify Gasbuggy-related contamination in the monitored surface and groundwater. Monitoring frequency was reduced from annual sampling to a five-year cycle after the long negative record.

A site created by an atomic experiment did not become a regional radiological disaster.

It also did not become a gas field built around nuclear completions.

Those two facts belong together because they reveal what long-term stewardship is for. Stewardship is not the cleanup department of a story whose only possible ending is catastrophe. It is the institution that remains when a past intervention is irreversible enough that future people must preserve knowledge about it.

Gasbuggy's underground cavity and residual radionuclides cannot be removed in the way a surface tank can be excavated. The management strategy is therefore partly epistemic: know where the event occurred, know what remains, know which pathways matter, keep records, sample plausible environmental media, coordinate with land managers and nearby stakeholders, and prevent future activities from unknowingly turning a contained source into a transport pathway.

The project began by asking how to make gas move.

The stewardship program asks whether radioactivity is moving.

This reversal is even sharper at Rulison and Rio Blanco, where modern natural-gas development has approached the old test areas. DOE and Colorado regulators review proposed drilling near the sites. Institutional controls restrict subsurface disturbance. At Rulison, federal control of subsurface rights around a roughly forty-acre area prevents drilling below specified depths. At Rio Blanco, a larger federal withdrawal and state review zones help keep new wells from intersecting or hydraulically communicating with the detonation zones.

The old technology has become a boundary condition for the new one.

This is not merely poetic. It is a practical cost that standard project economics rarely capture well. In 1969, the future value of Rulison was estimated through gas production. Fifty years later, its residual value includes the cost of maintaining enough institutional memory that a drilling boom does not forget where the nuclear cavity is.

The accounting horizon changed.

Nuclear technologies are especially good at forcing this change because radioactive materials can remain relevant longer than commercial organizations, regulations, and local memories. A company can merge or disappear. A federal agency can be abolished and replaced. A well can be plugged. The physical source remains.

The Atomic Energy Commission became the Energy Research and Development Administration and then part of the Department of Energy. The responsibility migrated through institutions, eventually arriving in the Office of Legacy Management. That continuity is an achievement. It is also evidence that the original intervention created obligations with no natural commercial endpoint.

Ordinary oil and gas wells also create long-lived liabilities. Orphan wells leak methane and fluids. Abandoned infrastructure can require plugging and reclamation decades later. The nuclear sites are not unique in having legacies. They are unique in the type of source and the consequences of losing location and pathway knowledge.

This matters for any fair comparison. The lesson is not "nuclear bad, drilling ordinary." The lesson is that the cleanup tail belongs in the technology's system boundary from the beginning.

Gasbuggy's current site management also provides a useful correction to conspiracy narratives. Long-term federal monitoring can look, from a distance, like evidence that the government is guarding a hidden catastrophe. The actual monitoring record is more prosaic. A known nuclear event created a residual source deep underground. Agencies sample water and maintain records because that is what responsible stewardship requires even when results remain reassuring.

Monitoring is not proof of contamination.

At the same time, repeated negative samples do not make the original intervention reversible. They tell us about sampled pathways under present conditions. Stewardship remains necessary partly because future drilling, erosion, land use, or other changes could alter how the site interacts with its surroundings.

This distinction—between no observed migration and no remaining obligation—is one of the most useful lessons in the entire Plowshare record.

Modern infrastructure debates often demand a binary forecast: safe forever or dangerous. Long-term management is built around a more realistic category. A site can be stable enough for ordinary surface use and still require records and controls. Risk can be low without being forgotten.

Gasbuggy also sits near the Jicarilla Apache Nation, making stewardship a relationship rather than just a sampling protocol. DOE's Legacy Management program now explicitly identifies collaboration with the Jicarilla Apache as part of Gasbuggy management. This contemporary practice should not be projected backward as evidence about the quality of consultation in 1967. It does show that the site's future is shared with communities whose relationship to the land did not begin with Plowshare.

The long tail of a technological experiment therefore includes people who inherit it without inheriting its original enthusiasm.

This is why the word *legacy* can be too gentle. A legacy sounds like something bestowed. The old Plowshare sites are closer to assignments.

Keep these records. Maintain this restriction. Sample this well. Review this drilling permit. Explain this marker. Answer this stakeholder question. Transfer the knowledge when staff retire.

None of those tasks is dramatic. Together they are the price of not forgetting an irreversible intervention.

There is a beautiful contrast between the original and current documentation. Early Plowshare reports look forward: expected production, fracture radius, gas reserves, industrial applications, future well counts. Legacy Management documents look sideways and backward: boundaries, monitoring locations, institutional controls, historical records, stakeholder support.

The future tense changed.

This is not unique to failed technologies. Successful infrastructure also becomes maintenance. Bridges turn into inspection schedules. Dams become sediment and spillway management. Reactors become decommissioning plans. The difference at Gasbuggy is that the maintenance institution outlived the productive technology itself.

The experiment's commercial future vanished. Its responsibility survived.

That survival is one reason the site deserves more than a roadside-oddity treatment. Gasbuggy is not merely a place where America once did something strange. It is a working example of how technological decisions cross generations. The marker on the surface is attached to an administrative chain that has lasted longer than the program's active optimism.

The final chapter returns to the question that made the whole project possible: what counts as working?

Gasbuggy fractured rock. It increased gas flow. Its nuclear products were largely contained underground, and long-term water monitoring has been reassuring. By several definitions, it succeeded.

There is no nuclear-fracked gas industry.

By another definition, it failed completely.

The interesting history lives between those sentences.
