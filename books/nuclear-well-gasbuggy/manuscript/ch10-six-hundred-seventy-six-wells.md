# Six Hundred Seventy-Six Wells

Forecasts are the place where prototypes reproduce without friction.

A field experiment may require years of planning, custom hardware, special permissions, large teams, and a tolerance for surprises. A forecast gives it children by changing a cell.

The 1972 federal energy outlook on oil and gas availability did something that now reads almost like alternate history. It treated technical feasibility of nuclear gas stimulation as having been established by Gasbuggy and Rulison, then explored production scenarios in which the method expanded through the western gas fields. Under one set of assumptions, 676 nuclear-stimulated wells could be completed by 1985. Other cases contemplated 500.

The document did not pretend these outcomes were certain. It explicitly conditioned them on favorable experimental results and resolution of policy issues. That qualification is exactly why the numbers matter. They show what federal planners considered worth modeling before Rio Blanco had even delivered its final lesson.

A future containing hundreds of nuclear gas wells was not science fiction in the administrative sense. It was a scenario.

Scenarios are not promises. They are organized conditional statements. If the technology performs, if costs decline, if regulation allows, if gas demand and prices support deployment, if policy issues are resolved, then this is what production might look like. The danger comes when each *if* is modeled separately and their dependence is ignored.

Gasbuggy suggests that the conditions were coupled.

Improve production by increasing explosive yield and containment requirements may rise. Add devices to cover a thicker interval and cost, radiological inventory, and verification burden rise with them. Reduce commercial risk by delaying production for radioactive decay and revenue moves farther from the expensive event. Standardize operations to reduce cost and communities face more repeated nuclear detonations. Expand to hundreds of wells and what was politically tolerable as an experiment becomes infrastructure.

The forecast's 676 wells therefore represent more than arithmetic scale. They reveal a phase transition.

At one well, extraordinary procedures are manageable because the whole project is extraordinary. At hundreds, the procedures have to become routine. Device manufacture and custody become recurring industrial inputs. Borehole emplacement becomes a production schedule. Radiological sampling becomes operating overhead. Nuclear-explosion notifications and seismic signatures become part of national and international normalcy. Regulators need durable categories. Insurers and courts need precedents. Landowners need contracts that contemplate a bomb beneath the lease.

The phrase *peaceful nuclear explosion* would have needed to survive repetition.

This is one reason commercialization questions cannot be answered by cost-per-well alone. Repetition creates institutional costs that may not exist during demonstration. It can also reduce costs through learning, of course. Standardized designs, experienced crews, predictable permitting, and supply-chain investment are how many technologies become cheap. The Plowshare argument depended partly on this expectation. Nuclear devices and operations might be expensive as experiments and less so as a mature program.

But learning curves work best when repetition is socially allowed.

An aircraft manufacturer can lower cost by building thousands of similar aircraft because society accepts the factory and the product category. A commercial nuclear-explosive industry would need permission to repeat the act that generates its learning. Every event would be both production and a nuclear detonation.

That dual identity also affected international verification. Underground explosions produce seismic signals. A country observing another country's "peaceful" explosions has to distinguish industrial use from weapons development or testing. The technical knowledge, devices, and organizations overlap too deeply for intent alone to provide confidence. This is why peaceful nuclear explosions became an arms-control category rather than merely an energy regulation issue.

The 676-well future would therefore have scaled a verification problem along with a gas technology.

The economic side was equally fragile. Nuclear stimulation made most sense where conventional methods performed poorly. But that meant the technology was aimed at marginal reservoirs, and marginal reservoirs punish extra cost. If massive hydraulic fracturing improved enough to recover the same gas without nuclear-specific burdens, the competitive window could close even if nuclear performance improved.

This is a familiar problem in innovation history. A radical technology can be racing not against a static incumbent but against an incumbent learning under pressure. The more credible the challenger becomes, the more incentive exists to improve alternatives.

Massive hydraulic fracturing did not require Gasbuggy in order to develop, and it should not be narrated as a direct response without evidence. But the comparison matters. Federal open-file compilations from the Plowshare era explicitly listed high-volume hydraulic fracturing research as a supplement or alternative to nuclear stimulation. The program itself knew there was a nonnuclear race.

By the mid-1970s, the radical option was losing the privilege of being the only path to large stimulated volumes.

The production forecasts also carried assumptions about gas markets. A project can be uneconomic at one gas price and attractive at another. Energy scarcity can make extraordinary extraction methods look reasonable. This is why forecasts from periods of perceived shortage often contain futures that disappear when prices, policy, or competing technologies change.

Plowshare's gas vision was partly a scarcity technology. It promised access to resources that ordinary wells left behind. As long as those resources appeared necessary and inaccessible, the case for extraordinary stimulation retained force.

Once conventional methods improved, nuclear stimulation had to justify itself not against geology but against another completion crew.

There was a second arithmetic problem hidden in the hundreds-of-wells scenarios: liability accumulates differently from output. If each event has a very low probability of an unacceptable release, multiplying events increases the chance that the system eventually experiences one. A mature industry therefore needs not only a low event-level risk but confidence in the tails of the distribution. Underground geology is heterogeneous enough that transferring containment confidence from one site to another is itself a technical problem.

This does not prove that hundreds of shots would have caused a disaster. It explains why the regulatory burden of the 676th well cannot simply be assumed equal to the first.

The forecast made another assumption modern readers may miss: a commercial nuclear-explosive service could exist as a stable interface between federal control of devices and private resource development. The state would retain extraordinary authority while industry integrated the result into ordinary energy production. That hybrid model had no easy precedent at the scale envisioned.

Gasbuggy itself demonstrated that the partnership could work for an experiment. The future required it to become a market institution.

There is a temptation to laugh at the 676 number because history chose zero commercial wells instead. The more useful response is to recognize its honesty. The model tells us what commercialization actually meant. The program could not become important by performing three or four shots. It needed repetition on a scale that transformed the politics of the technology.

That is why the next experiments mattered so much. Gasbuggy had produced encouraging flow and discouraging geometry and gas quality. A larger, better-placed shot in another reservoir could move the assumptions.

Project Rulison would test whether the problem had been Gasbuggy rather than nuclear stimulation itself.

The forecast was waiting for better data.
