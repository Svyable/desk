# Radioactive Product

There is a comforting idea hidden inside the phrase *contained underground nuclear explosion*.

The idea is that the underground is a box.

It is not. It is a medium.

Rock contains, transports, adsorbs, fractures, seals, dissolves, exchanges, melts, cools, and moves fluids. A nuclear explosion changes all of those properties locally at once. The achievement of underground containment is therefore not that nothing can ever leave the region. It is that the pathways and inventories can be controlled well enough that unacceptable releases do not occur.

Gasbuggy complicated this by making release part of the mission. The project wanted hydrocarbons to leave.

That is why the radioactive gas question cannot be reduced to either "the bomb was safely contained" or "the gas was contaminated." Both can be true. Most of the radioactive inventory can remain trapped deep underground while some mobile radionuclides appear in produced gas. An environmental pathway can be acceptably bounded while a commodity pathway remains commercially troublesome.

The distinction is uncomfortable because public arguments prefer thresholds. Safe or unsafe. Contained or leaked. Clean or contaminated. An industrial product inhabits more dimensions. What concentration exists at the wellhead? How does it change with time and cumulative production? In what chemical species? What processing removes it? Where does removed material go? What dose results from combustion or use? What standards apply? Who measures compliance? What happens when a customer learns how the gas was produced?

The Gasbuggy tests created data for these questions. They did not create a culture in which the questions became ordinary.

Radioactivity in produced gas was not surprising in principle. Nuclear detonations create fission products, activation products, and tritium through multiple reactions. The exact inventory depends on device design, yield, surrounding materials, and the chemical environment after the shot. Many products are refractory or become incorporated into fused rock. Others are volatile or can enter gas and water phases.

A gas reservoir is especially interested in the mobile fraction because mobility is the point of the well.

The program's hope rested partly on time. Some relevant radionuclides decay quickly. A well could remain shut in while concentrations fell. Production testing itself could remove a contaminated initial inventory. Processing might separate unwanted components. In a sufficiently large commercial system, blending with ordinary gas could reduce concentrations.

Every one of these ideas has a technical logic. Together they create a revealing picture of what commercialization would have meant.

A nuclear-stimulated gas field would not simply have wells. It would have radiological startup periods. It would have sampling protocols. It would have rules governing when product gas could enter pipelines. It might have treatment streams carrying concentrated radioactive material. It would have models connecting wellhead concentrations to consumer dose. It would require confidence that operators repeated the measurements correctly across many sites.

This is the infrastructure hidden behind the word *acceptable*.

There is no contradiction in saying that such a system could be designed and that the need to design it was itself a commercial disadvantage. Nuclear technologies often survive burdens that would kill an ordinary industrial process because their energy density or strategic value is large enough to pay for extraordinary controls. A power reactor can justify containment structures, security, specialized operators, and waste management because its output is enormous and because society has chosen to build institutions around it. A gas well competes against other gas wells.

Gasbuggy's product therefore had to carry nuclear exceptionalism into a commodity business with thin tolerance for exceptionalism.

The heat-value problem made the comparison worse. Gas recovered from the altered zone included components that reduced its usable energy per unit volume. Even if radioactivity could be managed, lower-Btu gas is less valuable unless processing removes the diluents. An explosion intended to improve access to the reservoir had also changed the composition of what the well delivered.

This is another general rule of technological intervention: the more energetic the treatment, the less safe it is to assume that the object afterward is simply more of the object before.

The blast did not only increase permeability. It created new chemistry.

The problem could have been smaller at larger scale if later production drew increasingly from unaffected reservoir rock and diluted the early cavity signature. That was one of the questions long-term production testing could answer. The larger program also had reasons to believe other reservoir designs might partition radionuclides more favorably. Device design itself could be optimized for peaceful applications, including lower fission fractions for some uses.

But the gas-stimulation projects could never eliminate the most awkward source term: a nuclear explosive had happened inside the reservoir.

The program's response was to quantify. This was rational and, in many respects, admirable. Dose calculations and radiochemical measurements are better than fear. Yet quantification can become politically naïve when it assumes that public acceptance is a function of technical dose alone.

Consumers do not buy risk by the millirem. They buy categories.

The natural-gas system is full of invisible hazards—pressure, combustion, carbon monoxide, pipeline rupture—that people accept through familiarity and regulation. Radioactive gas from a nuclear-stimulated reservoir would have entered that system as a new category whose danger was difficult for nonexperts to evaluate independently. Trust in the measurement institution would therefore become part of the product.

That trust was not unlimited in the 1960s and 1970s. Atmospheric weapons testing had made fallout a household concept. The AEC's dual role as promoter and regulator of nuclear technology attracted criticism. Environmental politics was changing rapidly. The National Environmental Policy Act would take effect in 1970, between Gasbuggy and Rio Blanco. Nuclear projects increasingly had to operate in a public world that did not grant technical institutions unilateral authority over acceptable risk.

It is easy to portray this as irrational resistance defeating a clever engineering solution. The record does not support such simplicity. The commercial value proposition itself was uncertain. The contaminated product problem was real. The fracture geometry was disappointing. Costs mattered. Conventional alternatives were improving. Public opposition did not have to defeat a dominant technology. It had only to add friction to one already struggling to prove superiority.

This matters for how we interpret technological backlash. Politics is often blamed for killing systems whose engineering case is described in isolation. But political cost is part of engineering when deployment requires repeated interaction with land, communities, regulators, and markets. A technology that works only if people treat its external institutions as free is not fully engineered.

Gasbuggy's radiological record also requires discipline in the other direction. The presence of radionuclides in produced gas does not establish that regional groundwater was poisoned or that the site caused widespread off-site disease. DOE's long-term monitoring record says Gasbuggy-related contamination has not been detected in the monitored surface and groundwater locations, including locations on nearby lands. Monitoring frequency was eventually reduced after years of negative results. Claims of broad contamination need to be tested against that record, not inferred from the word *nuclear*.

This is one of the book's central evidence rules: local product contamination and regional environmental contamination are different claims.

The first is documented. The second requires its own pathway evidence.

Keeping those claims separate makes the Gasbuggy case stronger, not weaker. The program does not need a hidden catastrophe to be revealing. A technology can fail because its known burdens are too high, even when its worst imagined harms do not materialize.

That is a less dramatic story and a more useful one.

The gas-stimulation program had reached a point where the key questions were not secret. The explosion fractured rock. Production increased. Radioactive constituents appeared in the gas. The heating value was poorer. Fracture reach disappointed. None of these facts alone dictated cancellation.

The next move was to ask whether Gasbuggy had simply been too small, too geologically constrained, or too early.

That is how Rulison enters the story: not as repetition, but as escalation.

Before the program went bigger, however, it had to understand why the first fracture network had not behaved like the clean radius on the planning page.

The geometry was about to become an argument.
