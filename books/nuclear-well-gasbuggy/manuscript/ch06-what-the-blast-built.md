# What the Blast Built

The easiest picture of an underground nuclear explosion is a sphere.

That is why it is dangerous.

A sphere is clean. It invites a radius, a volume, and then a multiplication. Increase yield, increase radius; increase radius, connect more reservoir. A production forecast can grow from a geometry lesson before the rock has had a chance to object.

Gasbuggy did create something roughly centered on the explosive, but the useful object was never the cavity alone. The useful object was the connected network extending from the collapse chimney into gas-bearing rock. That network was irregular, layered, stress-dependent, and mostly invisible.

Immediately after detonation, the environment around the device existed at temperatures and pressures that petroleum engineers do not normally ask a reservoir to survive. Rock near the device was vaporized and melted. The outward shock crushed and fractured material. A cavity formed. As it cooled and lost pressure, the roof failed and overlying rock fell into the void. The collapse propagated upward until a chimney of broken rock occupied the region above the original cavity.

The chimney was, from one point of view, a magnificent permeability enhancement. Rubblized rock contains abundant void space and interconnected pathways compared with intact tight sandstone. But a gas well does not live on chimney volume alone. The economic hope was that fractures outside the chimney had penetrated far enough into the reservoir to drain a much larger volume.

Here the language of nuclear effects and reservoir engineering quietly diverged.

An effects physicist can call rock fractured when the blast has damaged it. A reservoir engineer wants a fracture that contributes flow. The distinction depends on aperture, connectivity, pressure, orientation, whether the fracture intersects gas-bearing rock, and whether it remains transmissive as stresses change. A large mechanically affected radius may contain a much smaller hydraulically useful radius.

The program had indirect ways to estimate the latter. Pressure-transient behavior could be compared with reservoir models. Production rate and decline could be compared with nearby wells. Reentry drilling offered physical evidence of the chimney. Gas composition could reveal contact with altered zones. Seismic and geologic measurements constrained the event. No single measurement delivered a map labeled THIS PART PRODUCES.

That ambiguity matters because it is exactly where technological optimism can hide without lying. A model can be consistent with the available data and still allocate too much value to uncertain geometry. When later production disappoints, the result may be described as a need for improved placement rather than as a failure of the underlying concept.

Gasbuggy's later official summary is unusually blunt on the point. DOE says fracturing into the gas-bearing formation outward from the chimney, particularly above it, did not penetrate as extensively as expected. That is the sentence around which the whole experiment turns.

The explosive did fracture rock. It did not fracture enough of the right rock in the right way.

This is the difference between mechanism and architecture. Mechanism asks whether an explosion produces fractures. Architecture asks whether those fractures arrange themselves into a productive reservoir system. The first can be almost guaranteed by sufficient energy. The second cannot.

A useful comparison is a building demolished by controlled explosives. No one doubts that the explosives can break concrete. The engineering problem is making the structure fail in a desired sequence and direction. In a reservoir, there is no empty lot around the target and no camera view of the collapse. The material remains under confining stress, saturated with fluids, layered by geological history, and expected to function after being damaged.

Gasbuggy was attempting constructive destruction.

The phrase sounds contradictory until one notices how much subsurface engineering depends on it. Hydraulic fracturing creates damage deliberately. Mining breaks rock to gain access to ore. Enhanced geothermal systems seek fractures that allow fluid circulation through hot formations. Underground excavation removes or redistributes stress. The problem is not whether rock can be broken. It is whether breaking it creates the transport network the project wants without creating a more expensive one it does not.

The nuclear method came with a special difficulty: it could not tune the fracture treatment in stages after observing initial response. Modern stimulation often proceeds incrementally along a wellbore. A nuclear event is singular. Its energy arrives nearly instantaneously. The reservoir cannot be asked whether it would prefer another twenty percent.

That singularity gave the method conceptual elegance. One device, one event, one enormous stimulated region. It also reduced adaptability.

The later Rio Blanco experiment would attack this problem by stacking three explosives vertically through a thicker gas interval. The design itself can be read as a response to what Gasbuggy and Rulison had taught: one source has a limited useful geometry. If the reservoir interval is long in the vertical direction, create multiple centers of disturbance.

But every additional explosive changed more than geometry. It multiplied emplacement complexity, radionuclide inventory, device cost, security, verification burden, and the number of opportunities for containment failure. The engineering improvement and the system penalty were tied together.

This pattern is the book's recurring argument in physical form. The central mechanism can often be improved by adding more of the central mechanism. The surrounding system becomes less plausible at exactly the same time.

Gasbuggy also created chemical architecture. The cavity and chimney were not passive voids. Melted material cooled and incorporated radionuclides. Gases formed. Water and hydrocarbons interacted with hot rock and nuclear products. Some radionuclides were effectively trapped in solids; others could enter produced gas. The altered zone had a chemistry that did not exist in an ordinary completion.

This is one reason the word *contamination* needs precision. Not every radioactive atom created by the explosion had the same mobility or relevance. A nuclide locked in fused material deep underground presents a different pathway from a gaseous radionuclide produced through the well. The project measured because pathway matters.

That precision can make the story sound less alarming and more alarming at the same time. Less alarming because "radioactive reservoir" does not mean every part of the reservoir is equally hazardous or that contamination necessarily migrates widely. More alarming because the product stream need only carry a comparatively small fraction of the radioactive inventory to create a commercial problem.

An industrial gas well is a machine for selecting mobile molecules from rock.

Gasbuggy had created a new set of mobile molecules.

The program's next phase would therefore become an inventory of what the blast built that the gas market did not want. This required reentry, controlled production, flaring, sampling, and enough time for short-lived species to decay and reservoir pressures to reveal themselves.

The explosion had made an underground object. The production test would perform an autopsy on it while it was still functioning.

There is something almost admirable in the seriousness of the attempt. Engineers did not stop after seeing a larger flow rate and declare victory. They measured gas composition. They followed production through multiple tests. They watched the decline. They examined heating value. The program generated the evidence that would eventually narrow its own future.

That is an important correction to the idea that failed government megaprojects persist only because institutions conceal bad news. Sometimes institutions persist while producing increasingly good evidence against themselves. The difficulty is deciding when the evidence has become cumulative enough to change the mission.

Gasbuggy's first post-shot question was still open enough to sustain optimism: how much better would the well flow?

The second question was less friendly.

What, exactly, would be flowing?
