## Thesis

Project Gasbuggy and the nuclear gas-stimulation experiments that followed are often filed under a comfortable label: failed Cold War absurdity. The label is not wrong, exactly. It is too efficient. In December 1967 the United States and El Paso Natural Gas Company lowered a nuclear explosive more than four thousand feet beneath northwestern New Mexico and detonated it inside a low-permeability gas formation. The blast created a cavity, a collapse chimney, fractures, and a larger path for gas to reach the well. Production testing later showed increased flow relative to nearby conventional wells. In the narrowest engineering sense, something worked.

That fact is where this book begins rather than where it ends. The gas also carried radioactive constituents. Its heating value was lower than expected. The fracture network did not extend as usefully through the reservoir as planners had hoped. Production declined. Scaling the method from an experiment to an industry implied not one dramatic underground event but repeated nuclear detonations across working energy landscapes, each surrounded by drilling, exclusion zones, monitoring, product-handling rules, treaty questions, liability, and public acceptance. A 1972 federal energy outlook could imagine hundreds of nuclear-stimulated wells by 1985. That future never arrived.

The useful question is therefore not simply why nuclear fracking failed. It is how an effect that was physically real, technically measurable, and institutionally serious failed to cross the much wider distance between demonstration and infrastructure. Gasbuggy offers a clean case because every layer can be inspected: explosive-ground interaction, reservoir geometry, radionuclide production, gas composition, decline curves, economics, regulation, arms control, local legitimacy, industrial alternatives, cleanup, and half a century of monitoring. It is a history of a technology whose problem was not that nothing happened.

## Reader promise

By the end, the reader should be able to distinguish at least five claims that are usually compressed into one sentence about Plowshare. A nuclear explosion can fracture rock. A fractured reservoir can produce more gas through a particular well. More gas does not necessarily mean better saleable gas. A technically stimulated well does not establish that a repeatable field-development method is economic or socially acceptable. And a method that appears competitive against one generation of conventional completion technology can become irrational when a nonnuclear alternative improves.

The reader should also understand why the historical record is unusually valuable now. Modern hydraulic fracturing made low-permeability gas formations economically central without requiring nuclear explosives. That later success does not prove that Plowshare engineers were foolish to focus on permeability. It isolates the variable they were right about while making the cost of their chosen solution easier to see.

## Strongest competing explanation

The simplest account is that nuclear gas stimulation was an obvious dead end: radioactivity makes fuel commercially unacceptable, bombs are expensive and politically toxic, and conventional hydraulic fracturing was bound to win. There is a lot of truth in that explanation. The book does not rescue Plowshare by pretending the answer was closer than it was.

But hindsight can flatten the decision environment. Federal projections in the early 1970s treated tight-gas supply as a serious resource problem and nuclear stimulation as technically feasible enough to model at industrial scale. Gasbuggy, Rulison, and Rio Blanco were built precisely because the relevant questions had moved past chalkboard speculation. The stronger account is that the program failed through interacting constraints whose weights changed over time: fracture geometry, contaminated product streams, energy value, reservoir heterogeneity, cost, well spacing, regulatory burden, international verification, and the rapid improvement of massive hydraulic fracturing. If one of those constraints is treated as the whole explanation, the history becomes easier and less useful.

## Evidence discipline

1. **Verified / documented:** the U.S. Atomic Energy Commission established Plowshare to investigate peaceful uses of nuclear explosives; Gasbuggy was detonated underground in New Mexico in December 1967 as a joint government-industry gas-stimulation experiment; later production testing recovered natural gas containing measurable radioactive constituents; Rulison and Rio Blanco extended the gas-stimulation series; federal documents projected possible industrial deployment if technical and policy questions were resolved; the United States ultimately did not commercialize nuclear gas stimulation; DOE continues long-term stewardship at Gasbuggy, Rulison, and Rio Blanco.
2. **Credible but incomplete / model-dependent:** exact counterfactual production gains relative to the best conventional completions available at each date; the field-scale fracture volume created by each event; how much different explosive placement, yield, spacing, or reservoir selection could have improved economics; precise historical weight of each political, regulatory, commercial, and treaty constraint in cancellation decisions.
3. **Disputed / not established:** that Gasbuggy poisoned regional groundwater; that the tests were covert weapons experiments disguised as energy research; that nuclear stimulation would have become economically dominant absent environmental opposition; that modern hydraulic fracturing is simply a direct technological descendant of Plowshare; or that the absence of detected off-site contamination means the technology posed no material radiological or institutional risk.
4. **Speculative synthesis:** Plowshare gas stimulation as a case of the "effect-to-industry gap"; the idea that technological optimism is most dangerous when a demonstration succeeds narrowly enough to keep scaling assumptions alive; and the proposition that infrastructure should be evaluated by the number of exceptional systems it requires, not merely by the performance of its central mechanism.
