# The National Labs Try Again

The Advanced Photon Source at Argonne was an unfair opponent for a dental x-ray machine, which is exactly why the experiment mattered.

Researchers from Argonne, Lawrence Livermore and Los Alamos used intense synchrotron radiation to search for photon-triggered depletion of hafnium-178m2. The beam intensity exceeded the earlier source by enormous factors. The target was purpose-made. The nuclear spectroscopy was mature. If the previously inferred cross section described a real process in the examined energy ranges, the new setup should have produced an unmistakable response.

It did not.

The collaboration reported no detectable enhancement consistent with the earlier claims and set upper limits many orders of magnitude lower. An Argonne summary later described the discrepancy as roughly five to six orders of magnitude in the relevant cross sections. Lawrence Livermore publicly emphasized that the much more intense experiment had failed to reveal the claimed controlled release.

This is class-one evidence in the book: the experiments were performed and the negative results were published. What it proves is narrower. It strongly contradicts the specific triggering strength claimed for the tested conditions. It does not tell us that every imaginable pathway for controlled isomer release is impossible. Nuclear isomers remain real; other excitation mechanisms remain subjects of research.

That boundary is important because failed extraordinary claims often leave behind a scorched field. Scientists who dislike the hype can begin speaking as if the entire neighboring domain were discredited. Enthusiasts respond by treating any surviving theoretical possibility as rescue of the original claim. Both reactions are sloppy.

The national-lab results did something more useful: they compressed the space of plausible mechanisms. A claim that had implied a large, accessible x-ray-triggered effect now had to explain why substantially more capable beams did not see it.

Replication is not a ceremony. It is an attempt to force rival realities into the same apparatus.

The result also created an institutional puzzle. By the early 2000s, serious counterevidence existed. Yet military interest did not immediately vanish. That is sometimes presented as proof that secret evidence must have existed. A more ordinary explanation is available. Defense research organizations do not optimize for consensus. They sometimes pay to close uncertainty around a high-payoff claim precisely because the public literature is confused.

That choice can be rational. It can also become a machine for keeping weak claims alive. Distinguishing the two requires following the money and the milestones.
