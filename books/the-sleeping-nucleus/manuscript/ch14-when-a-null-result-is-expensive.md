# When a Null Result Is Expensive

Negative experiments are often described as producing nothing. That is an accounting error.

The Argonne-led hafnium measurements consumed scarce beam time, specialized samples and national-laboratory expertise. Their product was an exclusion region: under the tested conditions, the large triggering effect reported earlier was not present at the claimed strength.

This is valuable precisely because an extraordinary application was at stake. A null result prevented downstream programs from treating an uncertain cross section as an engineering constant.

The problem is institutional. Positive results generate devices, patents and new proposals. Strong null results generate narrower possibility. The latter is a public good with weak ownership.

Defense R&D is unusually capable of correcting this incentive because it can assign value to killed pathways. A terminated concept can save more money than a marginally improved prototype. Yet bureaucratic culture rarely celebrates a program manager for proving a glamorous idea unusable.

The hafnium case suggests a different metric for high-risk portfolios: uncertainty retired per dollar.

A program that begins with ten speculative mechanisms and conclusively kills nine may be more successful than one that preserves all ten through ambiguous demonstrations. This is almost the opposite of venture-capital theater, where every surviving option can be narrated as upside.

Null results are most powerful when they are designed in advance. Define the effect size that matters for the application. Build an experiment capable of excluding it. Publish the threshold. Then the result has teeth.

Otherwise “more research is needed” becomes a renewable energy source.
