# Five Orders of Magnitude

Scientists sometimes say two experiments disagree. That phrase is too polite for what happened here.

The hafnium dispute involved differences of orders of magnitude in the inferred triggering cross section. Once a disagreement becomes that large, the task changes. We are no longer fine-tuning a model. We are asking whether two groups are measuring the same physical phenomenon at all.

Orders of magnitude are epistemically clarifying. A factor of two can hide in calibration. A factor of ten can still invite complicated systematics. Five or six orders of magnitude demands a mechanism for the disagreement almost as dramatic as the original effect.

This is why the correct response to a frontier claim is not simply “more data.” It is design that attacks the gap. Run blinded source-on/source-off sequences. Exchange samples. Exchange detectors. Predefine the spectral lines and integration windows. Put rival teams in the same beamline. Ask each side to predict the other's apparatus before the run.

Science usually reaches this adversarial architecture only after years of argument because ordinary publication incentives reward novelty more than negotiated falsification. Defense agencies, oddly, may be better positioned to demand the latter. They can pay for inconvenient replication. They can fund the skeptical team and the claiming team simultaneously. They can require a milestone that terminates the application program if the effect falls below a threshold.

But agencies also have the opposite temptation. A potential capability with enormous military payoff creates bureaucratic asymmetry. Killing it produces no future program. Keeping it alive produces options, budget lines and technological surprise insurance. None of this requires bad faith.

The hafnium case therefore belongs to a broader family of “high-payoff residuals.” The expected value can remain attractive even when probability falls, because the imagined payoff is huge. This is mathematically defensible until uncertainty becomes a rhetorical asset rather than something the program is designed to reduce.

A useful test is simple: does each dollar make the claim easier to kill?

If not, the program is not buying knowledge. It is renting possibility.
