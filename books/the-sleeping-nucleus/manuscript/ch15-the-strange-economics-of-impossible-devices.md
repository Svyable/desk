# The Strange Economics of Impossible Devices

A technology with a one-percent chance of changing warfare can look more attractive on a spreadsheet than a technology with a fifty-percent chance of shaving five percent from fuel consumption.

This asymmetry explains part of the ecology around exotic defense research without requiring gullibility. Expected value can justify investigating low-probability claims. The trouble appears when the payoff estimate is allowed to remain enormous while the probability estimate is protected from updating.

Hafnium is a clean example because the technological story depended heavily on one experimental quantity: the effective triggering strength. Independent upper limits should have driven probability downward. Production difficulties should have reduced payoff. Radiation-handling burdens should have reduced deployability.

A rational portfolio can continue funding after bad news, but each bad result should change something measurable: smaller budget, narrower objective, stronger termination criterion or migration toward adjacent science.

This is how high-risk research differs from belief. Belief can survive by reinterpretation. Research must pay rent in discrimination.

There is also an adversarial problem. Intelligence agencies worry that competitors may tolerate technical risk differently. A U.S. analyst who concludes a weapon is unlikely still has to ask whether Russia, China or another state might pursue it. This can generate mirrored programs whose main evidence is each other's interest.

Cold War parapsychology offers one version of that loop. Nuclear isomers offer a more physical one. State interest is evidence of perceived possibility, not of natural fact.

When institutions study one another, curiosity can propagate without the phenomenon doing anything at all.
