# Peer Review Is Not Replication

The 1999 hafnium paper appeared in one of physics' most prestigious journals. Critics published formal comments. The original authors replied. Later experiments were published too. None of this is embarrassing to science. It is science operating in public, with one caveat: readers often misunderstand what publication certifies.

Peer review is a filter for whether a claim is worth entering the literature under the journal's standards. Reviewers do not normally rebuild the apparatus. They inspect reasoning, methods, plausibility and presentation. A subtle systematic error can survive.

Replication asks a different question: can the effect be made to reappear under conditions that should preserve the claimed mechanism?

The hafnium controversy is therefore a useful case against both scientism and anti-science rhetoric. The existence of a *Physical Review Letters* paper did not make the triggering effect established. The later failure of replication did not mean peer review was fraudulent. The literature contained a dispute, and the dispute became more informative as stronger tests arrived.

There is a practical implication for journalists, intelligence analysts and program managers: prestige should not be used as a binary truth flag for high-consequence anomalies. Read the replication graph. Ask which claims have independent laboratories behind them, which depend on one sample or one apparatus, and which critiques generate quantitative predictions.

In frontier programs, replication should be budgeted as infrastructure rather than punishment.

Scientists understandably resist spending years reproducing someone else's result. Agencies can change that incentive. A replication program can be made prestigious by design: shared samples, interlaboratory challenges, pre-registered analyses, publication guarantees for null results and explicit technical milestones.

The expensive part is not skepticism. The expensive part is years spent arguing around an experiment that could have been designed to end the argument.
