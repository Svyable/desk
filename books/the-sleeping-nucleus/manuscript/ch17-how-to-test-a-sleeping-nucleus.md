# How to Test a Sleeping Nucleus

A modern isomer-triggering program should be built to make arguments difficult to sustain.

Start with sample custody. Split one characterized inventory among several laboratories. Blind some samples with matched non-isomeric material. Independently assay isotope content and geometry. Publish detector response functions and dead-time behavior before irradiation.

Then separate discovery from confirmation. One laboratory may scan broadly across photon energies looking for candidate resonances. Any candidate region is frozen before the confirmatory run. A second facility receives the predicted energy, line ratios and scaling law without receiving the original residual plots.

The confirmatory beam should step through on-resonance and off-resonance energies in randomized order. The analysis should be locked. A real depletion pathway should produce a coherent family of consequences: enhancement of specified cascade lines, flux dependence, energy dependence, time dependence and—if enough inventory is affected—measurable change in isomer population.

The decisive quantity is not statistical significance alone. It is cross section with uncertainty. Before the run, define the minimum cross section relevant to any plausible application. If the upper bound falls below that threshold, the application program stops even if interesting nuclear spectroscopy remains.

A separate team performs energy accounting. Trigger source, beamline losses, sample production and gamma output are measured in distinct ledgers. No “breakeven” claim is allowed without stating the system boundary in the title of the figure.

Finally, publish the null.

This architecture is expensive compared with a provocative single-laboratory result. It is cheap compared with a decade of ambiguity.

The strange thing about extraordinary claims is that we often respond by lowering experimental discipline because the subject feels speculative. We should do the opposite.
