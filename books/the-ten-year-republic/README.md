## Premise

America is usually told as a continuous story: founding, expansion, Civil War, industrialization, world power, civil rights, digital age. *The Ten-Year Republic* breaks that smooth line. It divides the nation's first 250 years into twenty-five ten-year windows beginning in 1776, chooses one event from each window that most altered the country's downstream possibilities, and then tells the human story behind the choice.

The point is not that history can be ranked by formula. It cannot. The choices are arguments. Each chapter asks what changed because of one decision, law, war, collapse, ruling, attack, breakthrough, or crisis—and what plausible rival event might deserve the slot instead. The organizing wager is that the United States was not made steadily. It was repeatedly remade at hinges, after which yesterday's impossible became tomorrow's normal.

The selection deliberately mixes famous events with less obvious institutional turns. The Declaration and Pearl Harbor belong here. So do the Federal Reserve Act, the end of Reconstruction, the Sherman Antitrust Act, and Paul Volcker's October 1979 shift in monetary policy. The book treats freedom and coercion, expansion and dispossession, prosperity and collapse, domestic law and foreign power as parts of the same national record.

## Editorial note

This is a working historical argument, not a claim that importance is objectively measurable. The book's originality is the founding-anniversary structure: twenty-five consecutive ten-year windows beginning in 1776 rather than conventional calendar decades. The manuscript should not claim that no earlier writer has ever used decade-by-decade history; it should claim only the method it actually uses and defend every selection on its merits.

The complete narrative spine is now drafted. The book remains in a length and source-audit pass until every chapter clears the Desk full-book minimum and chapter-level research has been deepened beyond the initial authoritative-source ledger.
