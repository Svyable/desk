# Chapter briefs — The Ten-Year Republic

The chapter choices below are working judgments. Each brief states why the event currently holds the slot, the strongest rival, and what evidence would justify changing the selection.

## 1. 1776–1785 — Declaration of Independence

**Anchor:** Continental Congress adopts the Declaration, July 4, 1776; signing begins August 2.

**Why it holds the slot:** The Declaration did more than announce separation. It supplied a portable language of equality, rights, consent, and legitimate revolution that later Americans would repeatedly turn against exclusions the founding generation left in place. The war created independence militarily; the Declaration named what the new political order claimed to mean.

**Runner-up:** Yorktown, 1781. Yorktown made negotiated independence dramatically more likely and deserves full treatment as the military event without which the political text could have failed.

**Falsification question:** If the chapter cannot show that the Declaration's language had durable causal life beyond announcing a war already underway, Yorktown should take the slot.

## 2. 1786–1795 — Constitution

**Anchor:** Constitutional Convention signs the Constitution, September 17, 1787; ratification follows.

**Why it holds the slot:** The Constitution replaced the Articles of Confederation with a federal system capable of taxing, borrowing, regulating interstate and foreign commerce, executing laws, and acting directly on individuals. Nearly every later chapter occurs inside machinery created or constrained by this document.

**Runner-up:** Bill of Rights, 1791.

**Falsification question:** If the civil-liberties architecture of the first ten amendments proves more consequential than the underlying frame of government in later chapters, narrow the selection or combine ratification and the Bill of Rights as one constitutional settlement.

## 3. 1796–1805 — Louisiana Purchase

**Anchor:** Treaty signed April 30, 1803; Senate ratifies October 20.

**Why it holds the slot:** The purchase doubled the nation's territorial scale, transformed the politics of western settlement, raised constitutional questions, intensified conflicts over Indigenous land, and created the geographic stage on which later arguments over slavery and continental power would unfold.

**Runner-up:** Election of 1800 and peaceful transfer between rival parties.

**Falsification question:** If peaceful partisan succession appears more foundational to the survival of republican government than territorial expansion was to the country's later development, the election should take the slot.

## 4. 1806–1815 — War of 1812

**Anchor:** Congress declares war on Britain June 18, 1812; Washington burns in 1814; Treaty of Ghent ends the war in 1814 with fighting continuing into 1815.

**Why it holds the slot:** The war tested whether the young republic could survive a second conflict with Britain, weakened Indigenous confederacies resisting U.S. expansion, accelerated domestic manufacturing, and fed a stronger national identity despite an inconclusive peace.

**Runner-up:** Battle of New Orleans, January 1815.

**Falsification question:** If the chapter becomes a generic war summary rather than a demonstration of changed national capacity and western power, narrow it to the burning of Washington and the political consequences of surviving it.

## 5. 1816–1825 — Missouri Compromise

**Anchor:** Congress adopts the Missouri settlement in March 1820.

**Why it holds the slot:** The compromise transformed slavery from a sectional condition into an explicit territorial rule of national politics, admitting Missouri as a slave state and Maine as a free state while drawing the 36°30′ line across much of the Louisiana Purchase. It postponed rather than solved the conflict, making the postponement itself part of the future crisis.

**Runner-up:** Monroe Doctrine, 1823.

**Falsification question:** If later U.S. hemispheric power can be traced more directly to the Monroe Doctrine than the Civil War crisis can be traced to the Missouri settlement, reconsider the choice.

## 6. 1826–1835 — Indian Removal Act

**Anchor:** Andrew Jackson signs the Indian Removal Act, May 28, 1830.

**Why it holds the slot:** The statute turned pressure for Native land into federal removal machinery. It helped drive treaty coercion and forced migrations that opened millions of acres to white settlement and, in the South, the expansion of slavery. The chapter must center Native nations as political actors with governments, law, diplomacy, resistance, and survival.

**Runner-up:** Nat Turner's rebellion, 1831, and the tightening slave regime that followed.

**Falsification question:** If removal is treated as a single Cherokee story or as inevitable westward movement rather than a federal policy choice, the chapter has failed regardless of the slot.

## 7. 1836–1845 — Annexation of Texas

**Anchor:** Texas admitted as the twenty-eighth state on December 29, 1845 after congressional annexation.

**Why it holds the slot:** Annexation converted a decade-old independent republic into a U.S. state, intensified the slavery question, ruptured relations with Mexico, and helped set the immediate conditions for war and the largest territorial acquisition since Louisiana.

**Runner-up:** Telegraph demonstration, 1844.

**Falsification question:** If the chapter cannot separate annexation's causal role from the Mexican-American War that follows in the next window, the telegraph's transformation of information speed deserves a stronger hearing.

## 8. 1846–1855 — Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo

**Anchor:** Treaty signed February 2, 1848.

**Why it holds the slot:** The treaty ended the Mexican-American War and transferred an enormous western territory to U.S. control. It immediately reopened the question of whether slavery would expand into the new land and remade the legal circumstances of Mexican residents whose homes changed national jurisdiction.

**Runner-up:** Compromise of 1850.

**Falsification question:** If the political settlement governing slavery in the territories had greater causal reach than the territorial transfer itself, the Compromise of 1850 should take the slot.

## 9. 1856–1865 — Civil War and emancipation

**Anchor:** War begins at Fort Sumter in April 1861; Emancipation Proclamation takes effect January 1, 1863; Confederate military resistance collapses in spring 1865; Thirteenth Amendment ratified December 1865.

**Why it holds the slot:** No narrower event in the window rivals the war's consequences for union, slavery, federal power, citizenship, industrial mobilization, death, and constitutional change. The chapter should use emancipation as the central transformation inside the war rather than treating it as a separate moral appendix.

**Runner-up:** Dred Scott v. Sandford, 1857.

**Falsification question:** The only plausible reason to narrow the selection would be if “Civil War” becomes too broad to function as an event. If so, center January 1, 1863 as the hinge that changed the war's legal and moral character.

## 10. 1866–1875 — Fourteenth Amendment

**Anchor:** Ratified July 9, 1868; declared ratified later that month.

**Why it holds the slot:** Birthright citizenship, due process, and equal protection created constitutional language that would later reach segregation, incorporation of rights, criminal procedure, reproductive rights, marriage, and many other disputes. Its immediate enforcement was weak; its long legal afterlife is the argument.

**Runner-up:** Fifteenth Amendment, 1870.

**Falsification question:** If the chapter reads later doctrine backward into 1868 without acknowledging Reconstruction-era limits and competing interpretations, the causal claim is overstated.

## 11. 1876–1885 — Election settlement of 1877 and end of Reconstruction

**Anchor:** Disputed 1876 election resolved for Rutherford B. Hayes; federal troops withdrawn from remaining Southern statehouses in 1877.

**Why it holds the slot:** The settlement ended the federal military presence sustaining Republican governments in the South and helped clear the way for the consolidation of white supremacist rule, disfranchisement, and Jim Crow. The event demonstrates how constitutional rights can exist on paper while enforcement collapses politically.

**Runner-up:** Chinese Exclusion Act, 1882.

**Falsification question:** If research shows the familiar “Compromise of 1877” story is too neat or rests on disputed claims about a single bargain, write the chapter as the broader election settlement and troop withdrawal rather than repeating a mythic backroom deal.

## 12. 1886–1895 — Sherman Antitrust Act

**Anchor:** Signed July 2, 1890.

**Why it holds the slot:** The first federal law against monopolistic business practices created a legal vocabulary and federal role that outlived its weak early enforcement. It became a recurring instrument for defining the legitimate scale and conduct of private economic power.

**Runner-up:** Haymarket affair, 1886, or Pullman Strike, 1894.

**Falsification question:** If the act's early weakness makes its later importance mostly retrospective, the labor conflicts may better represent the decade's deeper institutional struggle.

## 13. 1896–1905 — Spanish-American War

**Anchor:** War with Spain in 1898; Treaty of Paris ends the conflict.

**Why it holds the slot:** The short war announced the United States as an overseas imperial power, brought Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines under U.S. control, placed Cuba inside a new relationship with Washington, and forced a republic founded in anti-colonial language to confront its own colonial authority.

**Runner-up:** Plessy v. Ferguson, 1896.

**Falsification question:** If the legal entrenchment of segregation proves more consequential to American life than the turn toward overseas empire, Plessy should replace the war.

## 14. 1906–1915 — Federal Reserve Act

**Anchor:** Woodrow Wilson signs the Federal Reserve Act December 23, 1913.

**Why it holds the slot:** After the Panic of 1907 exposed the fragility of relying on private financiers as emergency stabilizers, the act created a central banking system that would become one of the most powerful institutions in American economic life.

**Runner-up:** Triangle Shirtwaist fire, 1911.

**Falsification question:** If the chapter cannot show how the Fed changed later crises rather than merely describing its creation, the selection will feel institutionally important but narratively unearned.

## 15. 1916–1925 — Nineteenth Amendment

**Anchor:** Ratified August 18, 1920; certified August 26.

**Why it holds the slot:** The amendment barred denial of the vote on account of sex, transforming the constitutional electorate after a multigenerational movement. The chapter must also show how racism, citizenship restrictions, state practices, violence, and local administration prevented many women—especially Black, Native, Asian American, and other women—from receiving equal practical access to the ballot.

**Runner-up:** U.S. entry into World War I, 1917.

**Falsification question:** If the global and domestic consequences of World War I outweigh the long democratic transformation of women's suffrage across later chapters, the war should take the slot.

## 16. 1926–1935 — Great Depression / banking collapse of 1933

**Anchor:** Downturn begins in 1929; banking crises culminate in March 1933 national bank holiday and emergency reforms.

**Why it holds the slot:** The Depression changed the expected relationship between citizens, markets, and federal government. Centering the banking collapse rather than “Black Tuesday caused the Depression” avoids a common simplification and gives the chapter a concrete hinge into the New Deal state.

**Runner-up:** Social Security Act, 1935.

**Falsification question:** If the event frame becomes too broad, make March 1933 the chapter's chosen event and treat the Depression as the causal field around it.

## 17. 1936–1945 — Pearl Harbor

**Anchor:** Japanese attack, December 7, 1941; U.S. declares war on Japan December 8.

**Why it holds the slot:** Pearl Harbor ended the political possibility of American non-entry into World War II, triggered full wartime mobilization, and accelerated the transformation of the United States into a military, industrial, and global superpower.

**Runner-up:** Atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, 1945.

**Falsification question:** If the nuclear age proves a more consequential long-run transformation than wartime entry itself, the August 1945 bombings could take the slot. The chapter should explain why entry and mobilization still come first.

## 18. 1946–1955 — Brown v. Board of Education

**Anchor:** Supreme Court decision, May 17, 1954.

**Why it holds the slot:** Brown unanimously rejected state-mandated segregation in public schools and supplied constitutional authority to a broader civil-rights struggle. Its importance lies not in immediate compliance—which was fiercely resisted—but in changing the legal terrain.

**Runner-up:** Korean War, 1950–1953.

**Falsification question:** If Brown is narrated as desegregation accomplished rather than a decision followed by resistance and long enforcement struggles, the chapter will exaggerate the event.

## 19. 1956–1965 — Civil Rights Act of 1964

**Anchor:** Signed July 2, 1964.

**Why it holds the slot:** The act outlawed segregation in public accommodations, strengthened federal power against discrimination, and prohibited employment discrimination on protected grounds. It turned movement pressure, presidential action, and congressional struggle into enforceable national law.

**Runner-up:** Cuban Missile Crisis, 1962.

**Falsification question:** If the criterion becomes existential risk rather than institutional change, the Cuban Missile Crisis would win. The chapter must be clear that it is choosing durable domestic transformation over the most dangerous near-miss.

## 20. 1966–1975 — Watergate and Nixon's resignation

**Anchor:** Break-in 1972; Senate hearings 1973; Supreme Court orders tapes in 1974; Nixon resigns August 9, 1974.

**Why it holds the slot:** Watergate changed public trust, campaign-finance law, congressional oversight, investigative journalism, executive secrecy, and the practical meaning of presidential accountability.

**Runner-up:** Apollo 11 moon landing, 1969; Vietnam's Tet Offensive, 1968, is another major rival.

**Falsification question:** If the post-Watergate institutional changes prove shorter-lived than the technological and geopolitical consequences of Apollo or Vietnam, the choice should be revisited.

## 21. 1976–1985 — Volcker anti-inflation turn

**Anchor:** Unscheduled FOMC meeting and policy announcement, October 6, 1979; high-rate campaign continues into early 1980s.

**Why it holds the slot:** The change broke with prior operating practice, accepted deep recession as part of restoring price stability, and helped reset expectations about inflation and central-bank independence. Its costs fell heavily on borrowers, workers, manufacturing communities, farmers, and developing-country debtors.

**Runner-up:** Ronald Reagan's election, 1980.

**Falsification question:** If Reagan's political coalition and policy agenda better explain the economic and institutional turn of the 1980s than monetary stabilization does, the election should take the slot.

## 22. 1986–1995 — Fall of the Berlin Wall / end of the Cold War

**Anchor:** Berlin border opens November 9, 1989; German reunification follows in 1990; Soviet Union dissolves in 1991.

**Why it holds the slot:** The collapse of the European communist order removed the organizing adversary around which U.S. national security, alliances, military spending, and global strategy had been built for four decades. It created the unipolar moment and a new set of assumptions that shaped interventions and globalization.

**Runner-up:** Public emergence of the World Wide Web, 1991–1993.

**Falsification question:** If the internet's long-run transformation of economic and social life proves broader than the geopolitical effects of Cold War victory, the web may deserve the slot in a future edition.

## 23. 1996–2005 — September 11 attacks

**Anchor:** September 11, 2001.

**Why it holds the slot:** The attacks killed nearly three thousand people and reorganized U.S. security policy, war-making, intelligence, aviation, surveillance, immigration practice, public spending, and foreign relations for years.

**Runner-up:** China joins the World Trade Organization, 2001, with enormous consequences for U.S. trade and manufacturing.

**Falsification question:** If the structural economic consequences of China's WTO entry prove more durable than the security architecture created after 9/11, the runner-up deserves a stronger case.

## 24. 2006–2015 — Financial crisis of 2008

**Anchor:** Housing and credit stress accelerates; Lehman Brothers files for bankruptcy September 15, 2008; emergency rescues and monetary interventions follow.

**Why it holds the slot:** The crisis destroyed wealth and employment, exposed fragility in securitized finance, transformed central banking, produced major financial reforms, influenced political trust and populism, and changed a generation's expectations about housing and economic security.

**Runner-up:** Introduction of the iPhone, 2007.

**Falsification question:** If the smartphone's social, economic, informational, and political consequences prove broader than the crisis's long shadow, the technological event may eventually take the slot.

## 25. 2016–2025 — COVID-19 pandemic

**Anchor:** First U.S. laboratory-confirmed case reported January 2020; national emergency declared March 13; widespread shutdowns begin in March; mass vaccination begins later in 2020.

**Why it holds the slot:** COVID touched mortality, hospitals, schooling, work, fiscal policy, monetary policy, supply chains, science, vaccination, misinformation, federalism, household life, and public trust simultaneously. Its effects persisted after the emergency phase through work patterns, learning loss, debt, health outcomes, and political conflict.

**Runner-up:** January 6, 2021; Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization, 2022; public arrival of generative AI, 2022–2025.

**Falsification question:** This is the least settled choice because the window has only just closed. Future editions should be willing to replace it if later evidence shows another event had greater durable reach.

## Epilogue — 2026

The epilogue does not choose a winner for the next ten-year window. Its job is to show why real hinges are difficult to recognize in the present tense and to identify the recurring signatures visible across the previous twenty-five selections: institutional failure, scale mismatch, legitimacy crisis, sudden changes in information or force, and bargains whose deferred costs appear only later.
