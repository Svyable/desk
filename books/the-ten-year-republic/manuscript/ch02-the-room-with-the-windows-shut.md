# 1786–1795: The Room with the Windows Shut

The men who met in Philadelphia in 1787 were supposed to repair a government. They ended up replacing it.

That fact sounds less radical now than it was. The Constitution has accumulated so much age, ceremony, litigation, reverence, anger, symbolism, and routine that it can be difficult to see it as an insurgent act of institutional design. The document sits behind the United States the way old stone sits behind a city: foundational enough to feel natural.

It was not natural. It was a response to failure.

The Articles of Confederation had carried the states through the end of the Revolution, but the arrangement exposed a problem the Declaration could not solve. Independence had removed the authority of Parliament and Crown. It had not created an effective substitute capable of acting as a national government.

Congress could ask the states for money. It could not reliably compel payment. It could conduct diplomacy while lacking control over the trade policies of the states whose interests it was supposed to represent. It carried debts without an adequate independent revenue stream. Interstate commercial disputes accumulated. Western territories raised questions about security, settlement, Native sovereignty, and foreign influence. The war had produced a union strong enough to resist Britain, yet peace revealed how much of that strength had depended on emergency, military necessity, and the personal authority of leaders who could not make requisitions become cash.

The most important event of 1786–1795 is the creation and ratification of the Constitution because it changed the machine through which almost every later American conflict would run.

The Bill of Rights is the strongest rival. It deserves to be. The first ten amendments became central to American ideas of speech, religion, criminal procedure, arms, federalism, and individual liberty. But rights guarantees require a government to constrain. The Constitution created that government. The amendments modified and limited the frame after the frame had been built.

The first problem confronting the framers was therefore not abstract political philosophy. It was capacity.

The young United States owed money. States had their own debts and fiscal pressures. Britain still occupied frontier posts in the Northwest for years after the peace treaty. Spain controlled the mouth of the Mississippi, a major concern for western settlers. Commercial policies varied by state. Congress could negotiate but lacked the economic instruments of a modern sovereign state. The political order created in revolution had preserved state autonomy so aggressively that collective action became difficult precisely when the new country needed it.

Shays’ Rebellion in Massachusetts in 1786 and 1787 became a symbol of the disorder. The uprising grew from severe economic distress, taxes, debt enforcement, and the grievances of farmers, many of them veterans. The rebellion did not by itself cause the Constitution. The weaknesses of the Confederation were already widely discussed, and efforts at interstate cooperation were underway. But the episode concentrated elite fear that republican government might be unable to maintain order or protect property without either collapsing into faction or reaching for coercion in improvised ways.

Fear is not a disqualification from constitutional thought. It is often one of its ingredients.

The Philadelphia Convention formally opened in May 1787 after enough states had sent delegates. The official purpose was to revise the Articles. Revision quickly became a euphemism. Delegates moved toward an entirely new structure: a government with separate legislative, executive, and judicial branches; a Congress able to tax and regulate interstate and foreign commerce; an executive capable of enforcing federal law; federal courts; a supremacy rule; and a system in which national authority would operate directly on people rather than only through state governments.

The change was enormous.

Under the Articles, the union was in important respects a league of states. Under the Constitution, the people of the United States would be governed simultaneously by state and federal systems, each claiming legitimate authority within its sphere. The exact boundary would be contested from the beginning and has never stopped moving. That contest is not evidence that the Constitution failed to define federalism. It is evidence that the Constitution created a durable arena in which federalism could be fought over.

The Convention was secret. The closed windows have become part of its mythology, but the secrecy served a practical purpose. Delegates wanted room to change positions, float unacceptable proposals, bargain, and reconsider without having every statement immediately turned into public commitment. Modern political culture tends to treat transparency as an uncomplicated virtue. The framers used privacy to make compromise possible.

The compromises were not all admirable.

No account of the Constitution can treat slavery as a side bargain attached to an otherwise clean structure. Slavery shaped representation, taxation, fugitive-person rules, limits on congressional power over the international slave trade for a period, and the political balance of the new government. The three-fifths formula counted enslaved people for representation while denying them political rights, increasing the power of slaveholding states in the House and Electoral College. The document avoided the word “slavery,” but avoidance did not mean neutrality.

The Constitution created a stronger republic while embedding protections for an existing system of human bondage.

This is one of the recurring patterns of American institutional development: capacity and exclusion expanding together.

The new federal government would be more capable of collecting taxes, enforcing law, raising military power, and managing commerce. That increased capacity could be used for purposes later generations might celebrate or condemn. A stronger union would eventually help defeat secession and slavery. A stronger federal state would also make continental expansion more effective, facilitate treaty-making on unequal terms with Native nations, enforce fugitive-slave policy, wage wars, collect tariffs, and exercise coercion on a scale the Confederation government could not.

Institutions do not inherit moral direction from strength alone.

The Convention’s representational bargains also reveal how close the system came to different forms. Large states and small states had conflicting interests. The eventual arrangement gave population-based representation to the House and equal state representation to the Senate. That bargain preserved a role for state equality inside the federal legislature and remains one of the most consequential features of American government. It means citizens in different states do not have equal voting weight in the Senate. In 1787 that was the price of union acceptable to smaller states. In later centuries it would shape judicial confirmations, legislation, impeachment trials, and national policy far beyond what the delegates could foresee.

The presidency was another gamble.

The Revolution had been fought against a monarchy. The framers created a single executive with significant powers. They wanted energy without kingship, independence without unaccountability, and enough duration in office to act without making the president sovereign. The electoral system they designed reflected distrust of direct national democracy, expectations about elite judgment, federalism, and the political realities of the time. Political parties, which the constitutional design did not comfortably anticipate, would quickly change how the system operated.

The Supreme Court and federal judiciary introduced another form of power that would grow through practice. The Constitution established a federal judicial branch but did not spell out every feature of the later system. Judicial review, the power associated with courts refusing to enforce laws they conclude violate the Constitution, would become central through later doctrine and political acceptance. The document created offices whose importance would be discovered by people using them.

That is one reason written constitutions are strange objects. They are both text and software environment. The clauses matter. So do the habits, precedents, parties, institutions, emergencies, and interpretations that accumulate around them.

The delegates could design the architecture. They could not control the applications.

Ratification made the entire project uncertain again. The Convention did not possess unquestioned authority to replace the Articles. The proposed Constitution was sent into a public political fight, and supporters had to persuade states to ratify it under the new rules set out in the document itself. The Federalist and Anti-Federalist debates were not a simple contest between enlightened modernizers and backward opponents. Critics raised serious questions about centralized power, standing armies, distant government, taxation, representation, and the absence of an explicit bill of rights.

Many of their fears became permanent American arguments because they were not irrational.

A government capable of acting effectively is also a government capable of acting oppressively. The question is how to get one capacity without the other. The Constitution answered with divided power, elections, federalism, enumerated authority, structural checks, and later explicit rights. None of those mechanisms works automatically. Each depends on political actors willing to use or respect it.

The Constitution also depended on a peculiar theory of legitimacy. It did not rest on unanimous state sovereignty under the old rules. It appealed to “We the People” and provided for ratification by conventions. The opening phrase condensed a profound move: the union would claim authority not merely because states had formed a compact but because a people had constituted a government.

Which people, again, was unresolved.

Women were generally excluded from formal political participation. Enslaved people had no political consent. Native nations were treated through a shifting combination of diplomacy, war, commerce, and expansionist pressure rather than membership in the new polity. Property requirements and state rules limited voting. “We the People” was aspirational and exclusionary at once.

Like the Declaration, the Constitution created language that would later be used against its own limits.

But the document’s greatest immediate achievement was more prosaic. It gave the federal government money and force.

The power to tax is easy to describe as a technical clause until one remembers what happens to governments that cannot reliably raise revenue. Debts go unpaid. Soldiers go unfunded. Credit weakens. Infrastructure becomes harder to build. Foreign governments learn that agreements may not be enforceable. Internal policy becomes a request system.

The Constitution changed that. Congress could levy taxes, borrow on the credit of the United States, regulate commerce, establish uniform rules in key areas, and create the fiscal foundation of national power. Alexander Hamilton would exploit those capacities aggressively in the first Washington administration, building a financial program around federal debt, assumption of state debts, customs revenue, and a national bank. The constitutional machine made the program possible; political conflict decided how far it would run.

That conflict created the first party system.

George Washington became president in 1789 under a Constitution whose authors had not designed a party democracy. Hamilton and Thomas Jefferson developed opposing visions of federal power, finance, foreign alignment, agrarianism, commerce, and constitutional interpretation. The emergence of parties was treated by many leaders as dangerous faction. It also became the practical mechanism through which voters and politicians organized choices at national scale.

Again, the system adapted by becoming something its designers did not quite intend.

The Whiskey Rebellion in the 1790s offered an early test of the new capacity. Western farmers resisted a federal excise tax on distilled spirits. The Washington administration ultimately mobilized militia forces to enforce federal law. The episode was a sharp contrast with the Confederation era. The new government could impose a tax, face organized resistance, and summon coercive power to demonstrate that federal statutes were not suggestions.

This was precisely the kind of capacity constitutional reformers had wanted.

It was also precisely the kind of capacity Anti-Federalists had reason to fear.

Both truths can coexist.

The strongest case for giving this window to the Bill of Rights begins here. Ratification itself had exposed widespread concern that the new federal government lacked explicit protections for individual liberties. James Madison, initially skeptical that a bill of rights was necessary or sufficient, became central to drafting amendments. Ten were ratified in 1791.

They mattered immediately as political reassurance and eventually as some of the most litigated words in American law. Freedom of speech and religion, protections against unreasonable searches and seizures, criminal-procedure guarantees, the right to bear arms, limits on quartering troops, jury protections, the reservation of undelegated powers—these provisions became central to American legal identity.

For much of early history, however, the Bill of Rights constrained the federal government, not the states. Only later, especially through Fourteenth Amendment doctrine, would many of those protections be applied against state governments. The rights text acquired much of its modern reach through a constitutional amendment ratified after the Civil War.

That layered development is exactly why the Constitution takes precedence in this decade. The frame created the amendment process, the federal courts, the national legislature, the presidency, and the legal order through which later rights would be interpreted and enforced.

The Constitution is not merely another important document. It is the operating system in which later documents gain force.

That does not mean it is timeless or self-executing. The system nearly failed catastrophically in the Civil War. It tolerated slavery until war and amendment ended the institution. It allowed disfranchisement and segregation to coexist with equal-protection language for generations. It has supported both expansion of rights and periods of repression. It has been amended, stretched, narrowed, reinterpreted, and occasionally ignored.

Longevity can be evidence of adaptability. It can also conceal how often people outside formal amendment have changed the system by changing practice.

Political parties are the clearest example. The Constitution never lays out a two-party system, yet parties organize Congress, nominations, presidential elections, committee power, fundraising, and voter identity. The administrative state would later add agencies the framers could not imagine. Modern communications would nationalize politics at speeds impossible in 1787. The United States would become a continental and then global power, giving presidential military and diplomatic authority a scale beyond the original republic.

A short document survived partly because it left room for institutions to grow around it.

That flexibility creates a tension at the center of American constitutional culture. People often speak of the Constitution as fixed authority while fighting constantly over what its broad phrases mean in new circumstances. “Commerce,” “due process,” “equal protection,” “executive power,” “speech,” “arms,” and “cruel and unusual punishments” have never been politically inert terms. Their application depends on history, doctrine, technology, social change, and judicial philosophy.

A constitution that required no interpretation would be either impossibly detailed or politically dead.

The framers’ actual achievement was to create a system capable of generating legitimate decisions despite disagreement.

Not perfect decisions. Not always moral decisions. Legitimate enough decisions that losers usually remained inside the system and tried again.

That “usually” carries the entire burden. In 1861, major sections of the country attempted to leave rather than accept the system’s political outcome. The Civil War would answer by force whether the union was dissolvable at will. Other crises would test elections, presidential power, judicial authority, and federal-state conflict. The Constitution did not prevent those tests. It provided the institutions whose survival was being tested.

The ratification struggle also established a habit that would define American politics: constitutional disagreement as ordinary politics.

Americans argue about tax policy as constitutional principle, guns as constitutional identity, abortion as constitutional doctrine, executive orders as separation of powers, campaign rules as speech, civil-rights enforcement as federalism, and voting rules as both state authority and national right. The Constitution does not sit above politics. It is one of the languages in which politics is conducted.

That is a kind of success the delegates could only partly anticipate.

The Constitution also made the United States more legible to creditors and foreign powers. A government with an independent revenue base, an executive, federal courts, and authority over commerce was a more credible counterpart. Hamilton understood that public credit was not simply accounting. It was geopolitical capacity. A state that could borrow reliably could survive emergencies, finance military power, and bind influential economic interests to the national project.

The financial state and the constitutional state grew together.

So did the problem of scale.

The nation in 1787 was concentrated along the Atlantic seaboard but already looked west. The Northwest Ordinance, territorial governance, Native relations, land claims, and foreign borders made expansion a central political fact. A stronger federal government would be able to organize territories and admit new states. That mechanism would become one of the most consequential features of American growth.

Every new state, however, would alter political balance.

Territorial expansion was therefore never merely geographic. It was constitutional arithmetic. Would new states permit slavery? How would they affect the Senate? Who controlled western land? Which government had authority over settlement and treaties? The Louisiana Purchase in the next window would make those questions enormous by doubling the country’s claimed territory almost overnight.

Jefferson, who had spent the 1790s warning against expansive readings of federal authority, would find himself confronting an opportunity the Constitution did not clearly describe: purchasing an empire-sized territory from France.

That coming contradiction is the best evidence that the Constitution did not settle American government. It created a durable way to have unsettled government.

The men in Philadelphia closed the windows because they needed privacy to redesign the republic. What they built would be opened, fought over, amended, interpreted, and inhabited by generations they could not have imagined.

The Declaration had made the United States a claim.

The Constitution made it a machine.

The next great hinge would test what happened when that machine was offered land on a scale large enough to change the country’s destiny—and when a president famous for limited-government principles decided the opportunity was too large to refuse.
