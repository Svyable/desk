# 1986–1995: The Wall Opened

The Berlin Wall did not fall because a man with a hammer defeated communism.

It fell because a political system had become too brittle to survive its own loosening.

On the evening of November 9, 1989, East German official Günter Schabowski appeared at a press conference and announced new travel rules. Asked when they would take effect, he replied, apparently without full clarity about the intended timing, that they were effective immediately. The statement was broadcast. East Berliners went to border crossings. Guards faced growing crowds without usable instructions.

At Bornholmer Straße, the pressure became impossible to manage.

The gates opened.

People crossed. Families embraced. strangers climbed the wall. West Berliners arrived with beer, cameras, chisels, and disbelief. A structure that had symbolized the division of Europe since 1961 became a public demolition site before governments had decided what the new order would be.

The opening of the Berlin Wall and the end of the Cold War are the most important American event of 1986–1995 because the collapse of the Soviet bloc removed the adversary around which the United States had organized national security, alliances, military spending, nuclear strategy, intelligence, foreign aid, and much of its political identity for more than forty years.

The event happened abroad.

Its consequences reorganized America at home.

The strongest runner-up is the public emergence of the World Wide Web in the early 1990s. The web would eventually transform commerce, communication, media, politics, education, finance, entertainment, social life, and the architecture of information itself. If this book is revised in another twenty-five years, the web may well take the slot.

For the decade as it was lived, however, the end of the Cold War changed the set of strategic possibilities immediately and globally. The United States entered the 1990s without a peer military rival for the first time in generations and began acting as if that condition might be permanent.

That assumption became one of the most important facts of the post-Cold War era.

The Cold War had structured American life so deeply that its disappearance was initially hard to imagine.

After World War II, U.S. foreign policy had been built around containment of Soviet power. NATO tied American security to Western Europe. nuclear deterrence created a permanent condition of possible annihilation. the Korean and Vietnam wars were fought in part through Cold War logic. intelligence agencies grew. defense contractors became central to regional economies. federal science funding produced technologies and institutions. domestic political arguments were routinely filtered through anti-communism.

Even civil rights had a Cold War dimension because segregation weakened American claims to democratic leadership abroad.

An adversary can become infrastructure.

By the 1980s the Soviet Union still possessed enormous military power, but its economy was struggling. Central planning had produced impressive achievements in heavy industry, defense, science, and space while generating chronic shortages, weak consumer sectors, technological gaps, and low productivity in important areas. The war in Afghanistan drained resources and legitimacy. Oil-price changes reduced hard-currency earnings. Eastern European states carried debt and public dissatisfaction.

Then Mikhail Gorbachev became general secretary of the Communist Party in 1985.

His reforms—*perestroika* and *glasnost*—were attempts to strengthen the Soviet system through economic restructuring and greater openness. They had destabilizing effects because openness revealed failures the political order had previously suppressed while partial reform disrupted old controls without quickly creating a successful replacement.

The Soviet system depended on fear, censorship, party authority, economic allocation, and the expectation that resistance could be crushed.

Relax one element and the others become visible.

Gorbachev also changed Soviet foreign policy. He pursued arms-control negotiations with the United States, culminating in the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty signed with Ronald Reagan in 1987. The treaty eliminated an entire class of U.S. and Soviet ground-launched missiles.

The symbolism was large because Reagan had entered office talking about the Soviet Union in intensely moral and confrontational terms.

Yet the late Reagan administration negotiated seriously.

That combination—military pressure and diplomatic engagement—became a central argument in later debates about who “won” the Cold War.

Conservatives credited Reagan's defense buildup, ideological confidence, and refusal to accept permanent Soviet parity. Others emphasized structural Soviet economic weakness, the burden of empire, nationalism within the Soviet republics, European political change, oil markets, Gorbachev's reforms, and the long-term success of containment.

No single American policy caused a multinational political system to collapse.

Great-power endings are almost never that flattering to one leader.

Eastern Europe moved first.

Poland's Solidarity movement, suppressed under martial law earlier in the decade, regained legal status and won overwhelming victories in partially free elections in June 1989. Hungary dismantled border controls with Austria. East Germans began using Hungary and Czechoslovakia as routes west. Demonstrations grew in Leipzig and other East German cities.

The critical change was Soviet restraint.

Earlier Soviet leaders had used military force or the threat of force to preserve communist governments in Eastern Europe. Gorbachev signaled that Moscow would not repeat the invasions of Hungary in 1956 or Czechoslovakia in 1968 simply to keep satellite governments in power.

Once everyone believed the tanks might not come, politics changed at extraordinary speed.

Fear is a system resource.

Withdraw it and apparent stability can become mass exit.

East German leader Erich Honecker resigned in October 1989. The government attempted to manage mounting pressure through travel reforms. Schabowski's confused announcement turned reform into immediate physical movement.

The wall opened because guards at the boundary did not know how far the state was still willing to go.

That uncertainty may have saved lives.

A regime with clear orders to shoot could have produced massacre. A regime in disintegration produced hesitation. The people at the checkpoint understood something before the bureaucracy formally admitted it: the old threat structure no longer held.

The American reaction was unusually restrained.

President George H. W. Bush had succeeded Reagan in January 1989. When the wall opened, Bush resisted the temptation to declare triumph aggressively. He understood that Gorbachev was managing political forces that could still turn violent and that humiliating the Soviet leadership might weaken reformers or provoke reaction.

Restraint is difficult to photograph.

It may have been one of the administration's most important choices.

The next problem was German reunification.

A united Germany had been the central strategic problem of European history twice in the twentieth century. Britain and France had reservations. The Soviet Union had deep historical fear rooted in the catastrophic German invasion of World War II. Poland worried about borders. The United States supported reunification within NATO, but the diplomatic process required careful negotiation among East and West Germany and the four Allied powers that retained rights from the postwar settlement.

Germany reunified on October 3, 1990.

The Cold War order was disappearing through treaties rather than war.

Then the Soviet Union itself began to fragment.

National movements in the Baltic states and other republics pressed for sovereignty. Economic conditions worsened. Political authority weakened. In August 1991 hard-line Soviet officials attempted a coup against Gorbachev. The coup failed after public resistance and the refusal of parts of the security apparatus to impose decisive repression.

Russian leader Boris Yeltsin emerged strengthened.

By the end of 1991, the Soviet Union dissolved into fifteen independent states.

The American strategic environment changed more dramatically than any election could have produced.

For decades, the United States had maintained nuclear forces, alliances, intelligence priorities, military deployments, foreign aid, industrial capacity, and political narratives around a bipolar world. Suddenly the bipolar world was gone.

The Pentagon faced the “peace dividend.”

Defense spending fell as a share of the economy. bases closed through formal realignment processes. defense contractors consolidated. military personnel numbers declined. communities dependent on Cold War installations faced economic transition.

At the same time, the United States retained military power far larger than any other country.

This created the condition later described as the unipolar moment.

The first major test came even before the Soviet Union fully disappeared.

In August 1990 Iraq under Saddam Hussein invaded Kuwait. President Bush assembled a broad international coalition under United Nations authority, deployed hundreds of thousands of American troops to the Persian Gulf, and in January 1991 launched Operation Desert Storm after diplomatic deadlines failed.

The war demonstrated a new style of American military power: precision-guided weapons shown on television, stealth aircraft, satellite communications, overwhelming air superiority, logistics across continents, and a coalition including Arab states and European allies.

The ground campaign expelled Iraqi forces from Kuwait quickly.

Bush stopped short of occupying Baghdad and overthrowing Saddam Hussein, partly because the U.N. mandate and coalition had been organized around liberating Kuwait rather than regime change and because occupying Iraq carried uncertain costs.

That restraint would become a point of comparison after 2003.

The Gulf War appeared to validate a post-Vietnam military doctrine: define a clear objective, mobilize overwhelming force, secure broad support, and leave when the mission is accomplished.

It also fed confidence that American technology and military organization had changed the nature of war.

Confidence can become doctrine long after the circumstances that justified it disappear.

The 1990s then presented the United States with a new question: what is a superpower for when there is no superpower opponent?

Humanitarian crises and regional conflicts provided answers one at a time.

The United States intervened in Somalia, first to support humanitarian relief and later in a mission complicated by state collapse and conflict with local armed factions. The 1993 Battle of Mogadishu, in which eighteen U.S. soldiers were killed and bodies of American personnel were dragged through streets, profoundly affected political willingness to intervene in conflicts without clear strategic stakes.

The administration withdrew forces.

The lesson contributed to caution during the 1994 genocide in Rwanda, where the United States and other outside powers failed to stop mass killing on an enormous scale.

Nonintervention had costs too.

In the Balkans, the breakup of Yugoslavia produced wars, ethnic cleansing, siege, and atrocities. The United States initially hesitated to become deeply involved, then played a central role in NATO military action and diplomacy that helped produce the Dayton Accords ending the Bosnian war in 1995.

The post-Cold War world did not become peaceful.

It became less organized.

That distinction shaped American foreign policy for the next generation.

Without the Soviet Union, local conflicts no longer fit easily into a two-bloc map. The United States had greater freedom to intervene and less clarity about when it should.

The same freedom shaped economic policy.

The end of the Cold War coincided with an accelerating belief in market integration. Former communist economies attempted transitions toward capitalism, often under painful conditions. Trade barriers fell. capital markets globalized. multinational supply chains expanded. U.S. policymakers across parties increasingly treated economic integration as both prosperity strategy and political strategy: countries trading in a common market order might become more stable and more liberal.

The North American Free Trade Agreement entered into force in 1994, integrating the United States, Canada, and Mexico more deeply. The World Trade Organization was created in 1995.

China was not yet a WTO member, but American policy was already moving toward economic engagement.

The post-Cold War assumption was that markets and political liberalization might reinforce each other.

That proposition would later be tested severely.

The strongest rival to the wall is the web because it changed the infrastructure through which all these political and economic processes would operate.

Tim Berners-Lee proposed a system for managing information at CERN in 1989 and developed the foundations of the World Wide Web—HTML, HTTP, URLs, and the first web server and browser. The first website went online in the early 1990s. CERN placed the web software into the public domain in 1993, helping enable open adoption.

The Mosaic browser, developed at the National Center for Supercomputing Applications, made the web far more accessible to ordinary users. Netscape followed. Commercial internet service expanded. By the mid-1990s, “going online” was moving from academic and technical communities into households and offices.

The web was not the internet.

The internet's foundations had been built through decades of research, including ARPANET, packet switching, TCP/IP, university networks, and federal support. The web made networked information easier to publish, link, navigate, and eventually commercialize.

A new interface changed an old infrastructure's social reach.

Its eventual consequences would be staggering.

Retail would move online. classified advertising would collapse. newspapers would lose business models. search engines would become information gatekeepers. social networks would reorganize identity and politics. video distribution would become global. software would be delivered as services. cloud computing would change enterprise architecture. smartphones would turn the network into a constant companion.

By those measures, the web looks larger than the end of the Cold War.

Why does it remain runner-up?

Because in 1995 much of that consequence was still unrealized. The web had opened a path; the Soviet collapse had already changed armies, borders, alliances, budgets, wars, and global institutions.

This book is cautious about awarding current importance based entirely on futures later readers can see clearly.

But the two events interacted.

The internet's public expansion occurred in a period of extraordinary American geopolitical and economic confidence. U.S. technology companies grew inside a global order increasingly open to cross-border capital and information. English-language software standards and American venture capital acquired global reach. The end of bipolar competition helped create a world in which commercial networks could spread with fewer ideological borders.

The “open internet” and the “open world” became associated in American imagination.

Neither would remain as open as optimists expected.

Domestically, the end of the Cold War altered politics by removing one source of bipartisan discipline. Anti-communism had provided common ground across major factions even while they fought over methods. Without the Soviet threat, domestic ideological differences had more room to dominate.

The 1990s saw intense battles over taxes, welfare, crime, culture, guns, abortion, and the role of government. Newt Gingrich's Republicans won control of the House in 1994 for the first time in forty years and pursued the “Contract with America.” Government shutdowns followed confrontations with President Bill Clinton.

Partisan competition became more nationalized and more ideologically sorted.

The disappearance of an external enemy did not create polarization.

It removed one reason to suppress it.

Military institutions also had to redefine themselves. The all-volunteer force had matured after Vietnam. technological investment accelerated. precision weapons, stealth, satellite navigation, and networked command systems became central to the idea that the United States could fight with fewer casualties and greater control.

This confidence would shape the response to September 11.

A country that had watched the Soviet Union disappear without a direct superpower war entered the new century believing it possessed overwhelming military and economic advantages.

The danger was that advantages can be real and still fail to answer the next problem.

Al-Qaeda was not a state. It did not present armored divisions along a European frontier. It used transnational finance, training camps, civil aviation, communications networks, and small groups willing to die.

The post-Cold War state had been optimized to deter and defeat large adversaries.

The next attacker would use nineteen hijackers.

The Berlin Wall had opened a world in which the United States seemed to have no peer.

On September 11, 2001, that world discovered that peerlessness was not the same as safety.
