# 1966–1975: The Tapes

The burglary looked small enough to be ordinary.

In the early hours of June 17, 1972, police arrested five men inside the Democratic National Committee headquarters at the Watergate office complex in Washington. They had cameras, wiretapping equipment, cash, and links that would soon lead investigators toward President Richard Nixon's reelection operation and then toward the White House itself.

At first the event seemed almost absurdly minor beside the country surrounding it.

American forces were still fighting in Vietnam. The antiwar movement had convulsed campuses and cities. Martin Luther King Jr. and Robert F. Kennedy had been assassinated in 1968. Urban uprisings had burned neighborhoods. Apollo 11 had put human beings on the Moon. Inflation was rising. The oil shock was approaching. The Supreme Court had transformed criminal procedure and personal-rights doctrine. A president had gone to China and opened relations with a communist power his party had spent decades attacking.

Then five men were caught in an office.

Watergate is the most important American event of 1966–1975 because the investigation that followed changed the practical limits of presidential power, congressional oversight, campaign finance, executive secrecy, journalism, public trust, and the idea that a president could be compelled by law while still in office.

The runner-up is Apollo 11. The Moon landing in July 1969 was a technological and geopolitical achievement of almost incomparable symbolic power. It accelerated computing, aerospace, materials science, telecommunications, and the institutional relationship among government, universities, and contractors. It demonstrated what a state could accomplish by concentrating money, talent, and prestige behind a measurable goal.

Watergate takes the slot because it changed the machinery by which that state was held accountable.

The burglary was not the beginning of the Nixon administration's secrecy or political intelligence operations.

The White House had already created an internal culture in which political enemies were treated as security threats, leaks were met with covert responses, and executive authority was used to gather information outside ordinary channels. After the publication of the Pentagon Papers in 1971 exposed the government's internal history of decision-making in Vietnam, Nixon aides created a special investigations unit remembered as the “Plumbers.” Members later participated in an illegal break-in at the office of Daniel Ellsberg's psychiatrist.

The same administration that publicly emphasized law and order was privately normalizing unlawful methods in the name of political control and national security.

The Watergate burglars had connections to former intelligence personnel and to the Committee for the Re-Election of the President. The important question soon became not why the break-in occurred but what senior officials did after it failed.

Cover-ups usually create clearer legal problems than the conduct being covered.

White House officials and campaign operatives raised hush money, discussed the use of intelligence agencies to impede the FBI investigation, encouraged false testimony, and attempted to contain the scandal. Nixon won reelection in November 1972 by a landslide while much of this remained outside public understanding.

The election result is important because it punctures the comforting idea that democratic accountability automatically catches misconduct at the ballot box.

Voters can only punish what they can know.

Institutions exist partly because information can be hidden until after an election.

Journalism became one of those institutions in the Watergate story. Reporters Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein at *The Washington Post* pursued connections among the burglars, campaign money, and administration officials. Other news organizations contributed over time. Their reporting became legendary, sometimes so legendary that the press appears to have brought down a president by itself.

It did not.

Journalism created information and pressure. Prosecutors issued subpoenas and brought cases. Judges imposed sentences and questioned defendants. congressional committees held hearings. witnesses testified. the Supreme Court decided a confrontation over presidential evidence. Republican leaders eventually told Nixon that congressional support had collapsed.

Watergate was an ecosystem of accountability.

The Senate created the Select Committee on Presidential Campaign Activities in February 1973. The televised hearings turned institutional inquiry into national theater. Millions of Americans watched senators question former White House officials and campaign operatives.

Then Alexander Butterfield supplied the fact that changed the investigation.

In July 1973, the former White House aide disclosed that Nixon had installed a taping system that automatically recorded conversations in the Oval Office and other locations.

The president had created the archive that would destroy his defense.

Before the tapes, much of the investigation depended on conflicting testimony. Former White House counsel John Dean accused Nixon of participating in the cover-up. The administration attacked Dean's credibility. Recorded conversations could establish what the president had actually said and when he had known particular facts.

Evidence changed the constitutional stakes.

Special prosecutor Archibald Cox subpoenaed tapes and documents. Nixon resisted, asserting executive privilege and proposing alternative arrangements. The conflict escalated into the Saturday Night Massacre of October 20, 1973.

Nixon ordered Attorney General Elliot Richardson to fire Cox. Richardson resigned instead. Deputy Attorney General William Ruckelshaus also refused and was removed or resigned amid the confrontation. Solicitor General Robert Bork, acting as the next available Justice Department official, carried out the dismissal.

The reaction was immediate and severe.

Members of Congress introduced impeachment resolutions. Public criticism intensified. The administration had attempted to remove the prosecutor investigating the president and had done so only after senior Justice Department officials refused the order.

The constitutional system had encountered a question it had never needed to answer so directly: what happens when a president uses lawful appointment and removal authority in a way that appears designed to obstruct investigation of the president himself?

The answer was political before it was legal.

Nixon appointed Leon Jaworski as a new special prosecutor under arrangements intended to preserve meaningful independence. The investigation continued.

Meanwhile another constitutional crisis was unfolding around Vice President Spiro Agnew. In October 1973, Agnew resigned after pleading no contest to a tax-evasion charge arising from a corruption investigation unrelated to Watergate. Under the recently ratified Twenty-Fifth Amendment, Nixon nominated House Minority Leader Gerald Ford to become vice president, and Congress confirmed him.

That amendment had been written largely to clarify presidential succession and disability after the Kennedy assassination.

Within a few years, it supplied the mechanism that ensured continuity when one vice president resigned and then, less than a year later, the president resigned too.

Institutional redundancy looks excessive until the unlikely event arrives.

In 1974, the tapes reached the Supreme Court.

A federal grand jury had named Nixon as an unindicted co-conspirator in the Watergate cover-up. Jaworski sought recordings for the criminal trial of former administration officials. Nixon asserted a broad presidential privilege over confidential communications.

The case became *United States v. Nixon*.

The Supreme Court ruled unanimously in July 1974 that the president had to comply with the subpoena. The Court recognized a legitimate interest in confidential presidential communications but rejected an absolute privilege that could defeat the demonstrated needs of the criminal-justice system.

The practical message was extraordinary.

The president of the United States could be ordered to produce evidence.

The decision did not erase executive privilege. It constitutionalized the concept while limiting it.

That balance is important. Accountability systems often survive by refusing both extremes: the executive is not treated like an ordinary employee with no confidential space, and the executive is not treated like a sovereign whose evidence is beyond judicial reach.

When the tapes were released, one conversation became decisive.

A recording from June 23, 1972, six days after the burglary, showed Nixon discussing a plan in which the CIA would be used to tell the FBI to limit parts of its Watergate investigation on national-security grounds. The recording became known as the “smoking gun” because it demonstrated presidential participation in the cover-up earlier than Nixon had admitted.

The House Judiciary Committee had already approved articles of impeachment alleging obstruction of justice, abuse of power, and contempt of Congress.

Republican support collapsed after the tape.

Senior Republican leaders, including Senator Barry Goldwater, told Nixon that his position in Congress had become untenable. Facing near-certain impeachment in the House and likely conviction in the Senate, Nixon announced his resignation on August 8 and left office the next day.

Gerald Ford became president without having been elected president or vice president nationally.

A constitutional system designed in the eighteenth century had survived a twentieth-century executive crisis through impeachment pressure, judicial process, statutory prosecution, legislative investigation, succession rules, party judgment, and public opinion.

The machinery worked.

That should not be confused with saying the machinery worked quickly or automatically.

The burglary occurred in June 1972. Nixon remained in office for more than two years. He won reelection during the cover-up. investigators faced obstruction. institutions fought over evidence. partisan loyalties slowed political abandonment.

Accountability took time because evidence takes time.

The delay was frustrating. It was also part of what distinguished legal process from a coup or a purge.

Ford's next major decision deepened the argument over accountability.

On September 8, 1974, he granted Nixon a full and unconditional pardon for offenses Nixon had committed or might have committed against the United States while president. Ford argued that the pardon was necessary to end a national ordeal and allow the government to move forward.

Critics saw it as proof that elites protected one another and that Nixon had escaped the ordinary consequences faced by less powerful defendants.

Both interpretations helped shape public trust.

Ford later testified before a House subcommittee about the pardon, an unusual act reflecting the administration's need to persuade Congress and the public that no secret deal had been made.

The pardon may have spared the country a prolonged criminal prosecution of a former president.

It also preserved the unresolved question of whether prosecution itself might have strengthened the principle that presidents are not above the law.

Watergate's most durable effects appeared in reforms.

Congress had already begun changing campaign-finance law after abuses revealed by the 1972 election. Amendments to the Federal Election Campaign Act created contribution limits, disclosure requirements, and the Federal Election Commission, though later court decisions would reshape the system significantly.

The post-Watergate era also produced stronger ethics rules, inspector-general-like oversight mechanisms in parts of government, and the independent-counsel framework of the Ethics in Government Act of 1978, though that statute came just beyond the chapter's formal window.

Congress also reasserted institutional power after years of expansion in the presidency.

The War Powers Resolution of 1973 attempted to constrain presidential military action by requiring consultation and congressional authorization or withdrawal under specified conditions. The Congressional Budget and Impoundment Control Act of 1974 strengthened congressional control over budgeting and created the Congressional Budget Office after Nixon had asserted broad power to refuse to spend appropriated funds.

Watergate did not cause every reform, but the same political reaction powered them.

The “imperial presidency” was being cut back.

That term had gained force because Vietnam and Cold War secrecy had expanded executive action. Presidents possessed classified intelligence, military authorities, surveillance tools, diplomatic channels, and administrative control far beyond what earlier constitutional practice had contemplated.

Watergate taught Congress to view secrecy itself as a political risk.

The intelligence community became a target of investigation as well. The Church Committee, established in 1975, examined abuses by the CIA, FBI, NSA, and IRS, uncovering domestic surveillance, covert-action controversies, and programs that had targeted political activists.

The Watergate era thus broadened from one president's misconduct into a larger reconsideration of secret government.

The outcome was new oversight structures, including permanent intelligence committees and later statutory frameworks for foreign-intelligence surveillance.

Again, scandal created administration.

Public trust did not recover so easily.

Polling across the period showed declining confidence in government associated with Vietnam, Watergate, inflation, social conflict, and revelations of official deception. The Pentagon Papers had already shown that successive administrations had privately held doubts about Vietnam while publicly maintaining confidence. Watergate added deliberate criminal concealment at the center of domestic politics.

The resulting skepticism had two faces.

One was healthy. Reporters became more adversarial. Congress became more assertive. citizens became less deferential to official statements. watchdog organizations flourished.

The other was corrosive. If every statement from government is presumed a lie, collective action becomes harder even when the warning is accurate. Distrust can protect democracy from abuse and make democratic institutions incapable of earning legitimacy.

Watergate did not create modern distrust by itself.

It gave distrust evidence.

The comparison with Apollo 11 clarifies the decade's emotional range.

On July 20, 1969, Neil Armstrong and Edwin “Buzz” Aldrin landed the lunar module *Eagle* on the Moon while Michael Collins orbited above in the command module. The landing fulfilled the goal President Kennedy had set in 1961 of sending a man to the Moon and returning him safely before the decade ended.

The project required astonishing coordination across NASA centers, contractors, universities, suppliers, launch facilities, tracking stations, and hundreds of thousands of workers.

Apollo represented high-trust government at maximum ambition.

The public accepted enormous federal expenditure for a project whose economic return was indirect and whose political purpose was partly symbolic competition with the Soviet Union. Scientists, engineers, military-derived technologies, corporations, and bureaucracy worked together visibly.

Five years later, Americans watched a president resign because the same broad national state had concealed crime and abused authority.

Apollo and Watergate are mirror images of institutional capacity.

One asks what government can accomplish when power is focused and competence aligned with legitimacy.

The other asks what protects the country when concentrated executive power is turned inward toward self-preservation.

The Moon landing changed technology and national imagination. Watergate changed the safeguards.

Vietnam also presses hard as a rival.

The Tet Offensive in 1968 was a military defeat for communist forces in many immediate tactical terms but a political shock in the United States because it contradicted official claims that the war was moving toward success. The war divided the country, contributed to Johnson's decision not to seek reelection, expanded the antiwar movement, and ultimately ended with the fall of Saigon in 1975.

The conflict produced more than 58,000 American military deaths and vastly larger Vietnamese, Cambodian, and Laotian casualties. It contributed to the end of the military draft, changed civil-military relations, and produced the “Vietnam syndrome” that shaped later debates over intervention.

Why not choose Vietnam?

Because its political lessons moved through Watergate's institutional aftermath. Distrust of executive war-making fed the War Powers Resolution. skepticism of secrecy fed intelligence oversight. investigative journalism's stature grew through both Pentagon Papers and Watergate. The presidency's credibility crisis was cumulative.

Watergate gave the system a concrete accountability test and a constitutional resolution.

The scandal also established a naming convention that became almost comic. Future controversies acquired the suffix “-gate,” turning one Washington complex into shorthand for scandal itself.

That linguistic afterlife is trivial compared with institutional consequences but revealing about memory. Americans stopped thinking of Watergate as a place. It became the template for official wrongdoing.

The template included several expectations that now feel obvious only because the scandal established them: presidents can be subpoenaed under some circumstances; congressional investigations can expose executive misconduct; special prosecutors may be needed when the Justice Department has a conflict; tapes and documents can outweigh political denials; impeachment is a practical constitutional tool, not only a theoretical one; and a president may resign when legislative support disappears.

Later presidents would contest each of those expectations in new circumstances.

None would begin from zero.

By 1975, the United States had also entered a difficult economic era. The postwar system of relatively stable growth and manageable inflation was breaking down. The oil embargo of 1973–1974 raised energy prices sharply. Productivity growth slowed. inflation and unemployment rose together in the condition remembered as stagflation.

The economic assumptions that had governed policy after the Depression no longer produced reliable answers.

Politicians could stimulate demand and worsen inflation. They could fight inflation and increase unemployment. Oil prices were partly outside domestic control. The collapse of the Bretton Woods fixed exchange-rate system had changed the monetary environment.

The Federal Reserve faced a credibility problem of its own.

By the end of the 1970s, inflation had become embedded in expectations. Workers demanded higher wages anticipating future price increases. companies raised prices anticipating higher costs. lenders demanded inflation compensation. political pressure discouraged sustained monetary restraint.

Then Paul Volcker became chairman of the Federal Reserve.

On a Saturday night in October 1979, he announced a change in operating strategy that signaled the institution was willing to accept a severe recession to break inflation.

The move would hurt millions of people.

It would also help reset the value of money and the political economy of the next generation.
