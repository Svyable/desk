# 1996–2005: Tuesday Morning

The first airplane hit the North Tower at 8:46 a.m.

For several minutes, the event could still be misunderstood.

A commercial aircraft had struck the World Trade Center on a clear September morning. Television cameras turned toward lower Manhattan. News anchors speculated about pilot error, navigation failure, a terrible accident.

Then United Airlines Flight 175 appeared behind the live shot and hit the South Tower at 9:03.

The category changed in public, in real time.

The September 11 attacks are the most important American event of 1996–2005 because they reorganized national security, foreign policy, intelligence, immigration enforcement, aviation, surveillance, military spending, executive power, and public fear around the possibility that a small transnational network could inflict mass casualties inside the United States.

Nearly three thousand people were killed in New York, at the Pentagon, and aboard the four hijacked aircraft. The towers collapsed. part of the Pentagon burned. passengers and crew on United Flight 93 fought the hijackers, and the plane crashed in Pennsylvania before reaching the attackers' intended target.

The physical destruction occurred in hours.

The institutional response lasted decades.

The strongest runner-up is China's accession to the World Trade Organization in December 2001. That event accelerated trade integration between the world's largest developed economy and a rapidly industrializing giant, contributing to lower consumer prices, enormous supply-chain shifts, manufacturing dislocation in parts of the United States, increased corporate profits, Chinese growth, geopolitical interdependence, and a later backlash against the assumption that economic integration would produce political convergence.

On the longest economic horizon, China's WTO entry may rival or exceed 9/11.

September 11 takes the slot because it changed the state immediately and across more domains at once.

The attacks were the end of a long chain, not the beginning of Islamic terrorism or of al-Qaeda.

Osama bin Laden and the network around him had developed through the conflicts and political currents of the late twentieth century, including the anti-Soviet war in Afghanistan, the presence of U.S. forces in Saudi Arabia after the Gulf War, anger over American policy in the Middle East, and a militant ideology that treated civilians as legitimate targets in a global religious war.

Al-Qaeda had attacked American targets before 2001.

The 1993 bombing of the World Trade Center killed six people and injured more than a thousand. The 1998 bombings of U.S. embassies in Kenya and Tanzania killed more than two hundred people, most of them African civilians. The USS *Cole* was attacked in Yemen in 2000, killing seventeen American sailors.

The threat was visible.

It was not assembled institutionally into a warning strong enough to stop the plot.

That distinction became the central finding of the 9/11 Commission's account: pieces of information existed across agencies, but the system was not designed to integrate them efficiently or imagine the operational form of the attack with sufficient urgency.

Intelligence failure is often described as absence of information.

Sometimes it is absence of synthesis.

The nineteen hijackers entered the United States through legal immigration channels, lived in ordinary communities, used banks and identification systems, took flight training, and purchased airline tickets. Several were known in varying ways to intelligence services. Two had attended an al-Qaeda meeting in Malaysia that U.S. intelligence monitored, yet information about them did not move effectively into domestic watch-list and law-enforcement systems before they entered or traveled within the United States.

The attackers exploited seams.

Foreign intelligence and domestic law enforcement operated under legal, cultural, and organizational separations created partly to protect civil liberties and partly through bureaucratic history. Airlines and airport security were designed around older hijacking models in which passengers might be taken hostage rather than used as guided weapons. Cockpit doors were not hardened. passenger screening focused on keeping particular weapons off aircraft rather than preventing suicide teams from taking control of planes.

The system was optimized for yesterday's threat.

At 8:46, American Airlines Flight 11 hit the North Tower.

At 9:03, United Flight 175 hit the South Tower.

At 9:37, American Airlines Flight 77 struck the Pentagon.

At 9:59, the South Tower collapsed.

At 10:03, Flight 93 crashed near Shanksville, Pennsylvania, after passengers and crew learned through phone calls that other hijacked planes had been used as weapons and attempted to regain control.

At 10:28, the North Tower collapsed.

The compressed chronology is difficult to read because later consequences stretch so far that the morning itself can become prelude.

It was not prelude for the people inside it.

Firefighters climbed toward fires while thousands descended. police officers, emergency medical personnel, building staff, office workers, restaurant workers, visitors, airline employees, military personnel, passengers, and civilians made decisions with fragmentary information. Some people waited because they had been told earlier that buildings were secure. others evacuated immediately. phone calls became final records of ordinary human concern.

History eventually turns individuals into categories of casualty.

The morning was made of names.

President George W. Bush was visiting an elementary school in Florida when the second plane made the attack clear. He was moved through a series of secure locations before returning to Washington that evening. Senior officials operated under uncertainty about whether additional attacks were underway and which institutions might be targeted.

The immediate response included the grounding of civilian aircraft across the United States, an unprecedented move that emptied the national airspace.

For days, the ordinary infrastructure of movement became a security system.

Congress responded with extraordinary unity. On September 14 it passed an Authorization for Use of Military Force empowering the president to use necessary and appropriate force against those responsible for the attacks and those who harbored them.

The authorization was short.

Its afterlife was enormous.

Successive presidents used the 2001 AUMF as legal authority for military operations against al-Qaeda, the Taliban, and associated forces in multiple countries over many years. A congressional vote made under immediate crisis became a durable foundation for a global counterterrorism campaign whose geographic and temporal limits were never fully resolved.

Emergency law tends to last longer than emergency emotion.

The first war was Afghanistan.

The Taliban government had provided sanctuary to al-Qaeda. The Bush administration demanded that it turn over bin Laden and dismantle terrorist infrastructure. The Taliban did not comply on American terms. U.S. and allied forces began military operations in October 2001.

The initial campaign combined air power, special operations forces, intelligence officers, and Afghan anti-Taliban factions. The Taliban government collapsed rapidly.

The rapid victory created a problem familiar from earlier American interventions: defeating a government is not the same as building a stable successor.

Afghanistan became a twenty-year war involving counterterrorism, counterinsurgency, state-building, elections, training of Afghan security forces, corruption, development assistance, regional politics, Pakistan's role, Taliban resurgence, and repeated changes in American strategy.

Bin Laden escaped the initial campaign and was not killed by U.S. forces until 2011 in Pakistan.

The war launched as the direct response to September 11 outlasted the administration that started it by three presidencies.

Iraq was different.

Saddam Hussein's government had no operational responsibility for the September 11 attacks. The Bush administration nonetheless argued that Iraq's alleged weapons of mass destruction, defiance of United Nations requirements, history of aggression, and possible future relationships with terrorists posed an unacceptable post-9/11 threat.

The fear structure created by September 11 changed the standard of proof.

Before the attacks, uncertain intelligence about a hostile state's potential capabilities might support containment. After the attacks, administration officials argued that waiting for certainty could mean discovering danger only after catastrophe.

Preventive logic gained political force.

Congress authorized force against Iraq in 2002. U.S.-led forces invaded in March 2003 and quickly overthrew Saddam Hussein.

The central weapons claims did not hold.

No active stockpile of the kind used to justify the war was found. Intelligence had been wrong, overstated, misinterpreted, or presented with more confidence than the underlying evidence warranted in important respects.

The consequences were vast.

Occupation authorities disbanded the Iraqi army and pursued de-Baathification policies that contributed to institutional collapse and resentment. Insurgency grew. sectarian violence escalated. American forces became involved in a long counterinsurgency. Iraqi civilians suffered enormous casualties and displacement. U.S. military deaths exceeded four thousand. Abu Ghraib prison abuse damaged American legitimacy. The war altered regional power by removing Iran's major enemy and empowering Shiite political forces in Iraq.

The invasion became the most consequential example of how 9/11 changed American risk tolerance.

The fear of another surprise attack made action under uncertainty appear safer than restraint under uncertainty.

Sometimes it was not.

Domestic institutions changed just as dramatically.

Congress passed the USA PATRIOT Act in October 2001, expanding and modifying surveillance, information-sharing, financial, immigration, and law-enforcement authorities. Some provisions addressed genuine barriers that had hindered counterterrorism. Others generated intense civil-liberties debate over the scope of government access to records, surveillance standards, secrecy, and the potential use of national-security powers beyond terrorism.

The Department of Homeland Security was created in 2002 and began operating in 2003, consolidating twenty-two federal entities or parts of entities into a cabinet department responsible for border security, transportation security, emergency preparedness, immigration enforcement functions, cybersecurity, and other missions.

The reorganization was one of the largest federal administrative changes since the creation of the Department of Defense.

Airport security became federalized through the Transportation Security Administration.

The change was visible to nearly every traveler. Cockpit doors were hardened. passenger and baggage screening changed. liquids, shoes, identification, watch lists, and prohibited items became part of a new routine. The physical experience of boarding an airplane became a daily monument to one morning in 2001.

Few historical events leave such durable choreography.

Intelligence organization changed too.

The 9/11 Commission recommended reforms intended to overcome fragmentation. Congress created the position of Director of National Intelligence and the National Counterterrorism Center. Information-sharing systems expanded across agencies.

The phrase “connect the dots” became an institutional command.

Yet connecting dots creates its own risks. Systems designed to share more information can accumulate data about enormous numbers of innocent people. Watch lists can produce false positives. intelligence fusion can blur boundaries between foreign threats and domestic activity.

The same architecture that makes another intelligence failure less likely can make surveillance abuse more likely.

Security and liberty are not opposite ends of one slider, but institutional design often forces tradeoffs between them.

The treatment of detainees became another defining controversy.

The administration classified captured terrorism suspects under legal theories intended to permit detention and interrogation outside ordinary criminal process in significant respects. Guantánamo Bay became the most visible detention site. The CIA operated secret detention facilities and used “enhanced interrogation techniques,” including waterboarding, that critics, courts, international bodies, and later U.S. officials debated and in many contexts condemned as torture.

The administration argued that intelligence from dangerous detainees could prevent attacks.

Opponents argued that coercive interrogation was illegal, unreliable, morally corrosive, and damaging to American alliances and legitimacy.

The Supreme Court eventually heard a series of cases limiting aspects of executive detention power and recognizing legal rights to challenge detention.

Again, the post-9/11 state learned its boundaries through conflict after expanding first.

Muslim, Arab, South Asian, and Sikh Americans experienced the domestic social consequences. Hate crimes increased. People were attacked because assailants perceived them as Muslim or Middle Eastern, including Sikhs whose turbans became targets of ignorance. Immigration enforcement intensified. Communities experienced surveillance and suspicion alongside public declarations that the United States was not at war with Islam.

National emergencies create categories faster than individuals can escape them.

The attacks also changed political language.

“Homeland” entered official institutional vocabulary in a country that had rarely used the word that way. color-coded threat levels appeared. public buildings added barriers. sports events adopted security rituals. schools and workplaces updated emergency plans. government warnings became a background feature of daily life.

Terrorism succeeded in part by making possibility omnipresent.

The direct probability that any one American would experience a terrorist attack remained extremely low. The psychological availability of the threat was enormous because the attacks had been witnessed live and replayed continuously.

Public fear altered policy even where statistical risk did not.

That is one reason September 11 outranks China's WTO entry for this window despite the latter's massive economic consequences.

China joined the WTO on December 11, 2001, after years of negotiations and American political debate over trade, human rights, strategic competition, and the expectation that integration into global markets would create incentives for reform.

The accession accelerated trade flows dramatically.

American companies expanded sourcing and production relationships in China. consumers benefited from cheaper imported goods. manufacturers gained access to inputs and a large market. Chinese exports surged. foreign investment supported an extraordinary industrial expansion that lifted incomes and transformed cities.

The adjustment inside the United States was uneven.

Regions concentrated in import-competing manufacturing experienced job losses, wage pressure, and social disruption. Economists later documented a “China shock” in local labor markets whose effects were larger and more persistent than many earlier trade models had assumed.

Trade creates national gains while localizing losses.

The workers who lose a factory do not consume the abstract average.

The political consequences emerged slowly: hostility toward globalization, skepticism of trade agreements, concern over supply-chain dependence, anger at elite predictions that displaced workers would move easily into new opportunities, and eventual bipartisan competition with China over technology, security, industrial policy, and strategic influence.

The expectation that economic integration would necessarily liberalize Chinese politics also proved too confident.

China became wealthier and more technologically capable while the Communist Party retained and in important ways strengthened political control.

That outcome would reshape twenty-first-century American strategy.

Why, then, does 9/11 still hold the slot?

Because September 11 changed the government's operating structure in days and then shaped two major wars, domestic surveillance, executive power, public spending, immigration policy, aviation, intelligence, and political identity for more than a generation.

China's WTO entry was a structural economic hinge whose full meaning emerged gradually.

September 11 was a hinge Americans felt turning beneath them.

The attack also changed federal spending priorities. Defense and homeland-security expenditures rose. veterans' care needs accumulated. intelligence budgets expanded. military contractors and security industries grew. airport and border technology became large markets.

War financing interacted with tax cuts and later economic policy, contributing to deficits and debt.

The fiscal consequences would continue after combat intensity changed.

The all-volunteer military carried the burden unevenly. A relatively small share of American families experienced repeated deployments while much of the country continued ordinary life without a draft or broad wartime taxation.

This created a peculiar form of prolonged war: enormous strategic commitments without full social mobilization.

The country could fight for years without most citizens being asked to change daily behavior beyond airport procedures and patriotic symbolism.

That distance made war easier to sustain politically and harder to understand socially.

By 2005, the immediate unity after the attacks had faded. Iraq was increasingly unpopular. debates over intelligence, torture, surveillance, and executive power deepened. Hurricane Katrina exposed failures in domestic emergency management that damaged confidence in the federal government.

The country had built an enormous security architecture after one surprise.

It remained vulnerable to different kinds of systemic failure.

The next one would begin not with aircraft but with mortgages.

Banks and investment firms had built a financial system around housing debt, securitization, derivatives, leverage, short-term funding, ratings, and the assumption that nationwide housing prices would not fall sharply together.

When that assumption failed, institutions discovered that risks believed to have been distributed had instead been connected.

September 11 taught the United States that a small network could exploit seams in national security.

The financial crisis of 2008 would teach it that a large network could collapse because there were almost no seams left at all.
