# 1896–1905: Ninety Days to Empire

The United States went to war with Spain in the spring of 1898 and, before the year ended, found itself governing islands thousands of miles from the continent.

That speed is part of the event.

Wars can make transformations look inevitable because the map changes faster than public arguments can catch up. In April, the United States declared war amid outrage over Cuba, the explosion of the USS *Maine*, humanitarian concern, strategic calculation, sensational journalism, and a long Cuban struggle for independence from Spain. By August, major combat had largely ended. By December, the Treaty of Paris had formalized a settlement in which Spain relinquished control of Cuba and ceded Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines to the United States, with the United States paying $20 million for the Philippines.

The Spanish-American War is the most important American event of 1896–1905 because it forced the republic to confront a contradiction that continental expansion had partly concealed. The United States had been founded through rebellion against an empire. Now it possessed an empire of its own.

The war announced American power beyond the continent, altered the country's military and diplomatic position, produced a long colonial relationship with Puerto Rico and Guam, led to a brutal war against Filipino independence, established new authority in Cuba, and raised constitutional questions about whether the full rights of the Constitution followed the flag into U.S.-controlled territories.

The runner-up is *Plessy v. Ferguson*, decided in 1896. The Supreme Court's approval of state-mandated racial segregation under the doctrine later summarized as “separate but equal” gave constitutional shelter to Jim Crow and shaped American racial law for more than half a century. It is an extremely strong rival. But 1898 takes the slot because it changed the country's global position while also exporting American racial, constitutional, and military questions into a new imperial setting.

The war did not begin because one battleship exploded.

Cuba had fought repeated wars against Spanish rule. The Ten Years' War from 1868 to 1878 failed to win independence but left unresolved demands. A new war began in 1895 under a broad Cuban independence movement. Spanish counterinsurgency policies included reconcentration, which forced rural civilians into designated areas in an effort to separate rebels from local support. Conditions became catastrophic for many civilians.

American newspapers reported Spanish brutality intensely, sometimes with sensationalism that later gave the era its label of “yellow journalism.” William Randolph Hearst's *New York Journal* and Joseph Pulitzer's *New York World* competed for readers with dramatic coverage. The press mattered, but the old textbook claim that newspapers simply caused the war grants editors too much control over a conflict already rooted in Cuban revolution, Spanish policy, American economic interests, humanitarian outrage, strategic ambition, and domestic politics.

The United States had watched Cuba for generations.

Its proximity to Florida and the Gulf made the island strategically important. American investors had substantial economic interests in Cuban sugar and other businesses. Southern expansionists before the Civil War had imagined acquiring Cuba as possible slave territory. After slavery ended, strategic and commercial interest remained.

By the 1890s, the United States was also a larger industrial economy with a more confident navy and growing interest in overseas markets and bases.

The frontier that had once organized American expansion was no longer available in the same way. Continental boundaries had largely been fixed. Industrial production was rising. Naval theorists such as Alfred Thayer Mahan argued that great powers needed modern fleets, coaling stations, and strategic bases. Hawai‘i was becoming central to Pacific strategy and would be annexed by the United States in 1898 after the overthrow of the Hawaiian monarchy in 1893 by a group that included American and European residents and business interests.

The country was already looking offshore.

Then the *Maine* exploded in Havana harbor on February 15, 1898.

The U.S. battleship had been sent to Cuba during unrest. The explosion killed more than 260 American sailors. At the time, many Americans believed Spain was responsible. Later investigations produced competing theories, and the precise cause became a subject of historical and technical debate.

What matters politically is that uncertainty did not prevent certainty from spreading.

“Remember the Maine” became a rallying cry. Public pressure for intervention increased. President William McKinley had initially pursued diplomacy and was cautious about war, but events narrowed the available political space. Congress recognized Cuban independence in a resolution that also included the Teller Amendment, disclaiming U.S. intention to annex Cuba and promising to leave control of the island to its people after pacification.

The disclaimer was significant because it framed the war as liberation rather than conquest.

Then the first major American offensive occurred not in Cuba but in the Philippines.

On May 1, Commodore George Dewey's Asiatic Squadron destroyed the Spanish fleet in Manila Bay. The victory demonstrated the reach of the modern U.S. Navy and transformed a Caribbean war into a Pacific imperial decision.

Filipino revolutionaries under Emilio Aguinaldo had been fighting Spanish colonial rule. They expected independence. American forces initially benefited from Filipino military pressure against Spain. But the United States did not intend simply to defeat Spain and leave the archipelago.

When the peace treaty transferred the Philippines to American control, the contradiction became unavoidable.

Cuba was being promised independence, though under conditions that preserved significant U.S. influence. The Philippines was being acquired.

Why one and not the other?

Strategic interest, racial ideology, domestic politics, commercial ambition, military circumstance, and assumptions about self-government all played roles. American imperialists argued that the Philippines could provide strategic and commercial access to Asia and that the United States had responsibilities toward the islands after defeating Spain. McKinley publicly used the language of duty and uplift.

Anti-imperialists heard an older vocabulary underneath.

If government derived its just powers from the consent of the governed, how could the United States legitimately rule people who had not consented to American sovereignty?

The Anti-Imperialist League brought together a politically unusual coalition including figures such as Mark Twain, Andrew Carnegie, former political leaders, reformers, labor activists, and others who opposed annexation for reasons ranging from constitutional principle to racism to fears about militarism and overseas entanglement.

Not every anti-imperialist believed in racial equality. Some opposed annexing the Philippines because they did not want nonwhite populations incorporated into the American polity. Moral opposition to empire and racial exclusion could occupy the same coalition.

Again, motives do not line up cleanly.

The Senate ratified the Treaty of Paris in February 1899 by a narrow margin above the required two-thirds threshold.

Then war broke out between American and Filipino forces.

The Philippine-American War was longer and more brutal than the short war with Spain. Filipino forces first fought conventionally and then increasingly used guerrilla tactics. The United States deployed tens of thousands of troops. Counterinsurgency included reconcentration in some areas, village destruction, harsh interrogation methods, and other abuses. Filipino soldiers and civilians died in large numbers from combat, disease, famine, and the broader disruption of war.

American soldiers also died by the thousands, many from disease.

The war exposed the irony of 1898 with almost unbearable clarity. The United States had condemned Spanish reconcentration in Cuba and then adopted coercive counterinsurgency practices while suppressing Filipino independence.

Empires often inherit the methods they condemn in the empire they replace.

The official American explanation emphasized eventual self-government, education, infrastructure, and political development under U.S. supervision. Those projects were real and became part of the colonial state. So was the prior fact of conquest.

The Philippines would not become independent until 1946.

Puerto Rico followed a different path. The United States established civil government under the Foraker Act of 1900. Puerto Ricans later received U.S. citizenship under the Jones-Shafroth Act of 1917. The island remains a U.S. territory, and its political status—statehood, independence, free association, continuation of territorial commonwealth arrangements—remains contested.

Guam remains a U.S. territory as well.

Cuba became formally independent in 1902, but the United States required acceptance of the Platt Amendment framework, which restricted Cuban sovereignty and authorized American intervention under specified circumstances. The United States also obtained a lease for a naval station at Guantánamo Bay.

Liberation came with conditions.

The war therefore created a hierarchy of American political relationships: state, organized territory, unincorporated territory, protectorate-like influence, military occupation, and colonial administration.

The Constitution had been written for states and a continental republic. Empire forced courts to decide whether all constitutional rights automatically applied in territories the United States controlled but did not necessarily intend to incorporate as states.

The Insular Cases, beginning in 1901, produced doctrines distinguishing “incorporated” from “unincorporated” territories and holding that not every constitutional provision applied in the same way to all territories. The cases were shaped by racial and imperial assumptions of the period and remain controversial in modern territorial law.

The country had discovered a way to possess territory without promising statehood.

That was a major break from the nineteenth-century continental model.

Louisiana, Texas, California, and other continental acquisitions had been understood as territory that could become states. The overseas acquisitions created places under U.S. sovereignty whose residents could remain outside equal representation in Congress and presidential voting for generations.

Empire expanded the map without expanding the Senate.

The military consequences were equally significant.

The war exposed logistical and administrative weaknesses in the Army. Mobilization brought problems in supply, disease control, coordination, and management. Reform followed, contributing to a more professional military establishment in the early twentieth century.

The Navy's prestige soared. Dewey became a national hero. Theodore Roosevelt gained fame from his service with the Rough Riders in Cuba, particularly around the fighting near San Juan Heights. His wartime reputation helped propel him into the New York governorship, the vice presidency, and after McKinley's assassination in 1901, the presidency.

A war of ninety-odd days helped elevate the president who would personify a more assertive American role in the world.

Roosevelt's foreign policy drew on the power 1898 had revealed. The United States intervened repeatedly in the Caribbean and Central America, backed Panama's separation from Colombia while securing rights to build the Panama Canal, and asserted a Roosevelt Corollary to the Monroe Doctrine claiming a broader U.S. role in hemispheric financial and political disputes.

The Monroe Doctrine had begun in 1823 as a warning to European powers from a nation with limited enforcement capacity.

After 1898, the United States increasingly had the military power to act on a much larger interpretation.

The war also altered race and citizenship politics at home.

Black soldiers served in Cuba, including units of the U.S. Army's segregated Black regiments. Some African American leaders initially supported intervention against Spanish oppression. The irony of fighting for Cuban freedom while Jim Crow tightened at home was impossible to avoid. Black newspapers and intellectuals debated imperialism, citizenship, military service, and the contradiction of a segregated republic claiming to liberate others.

White-supremacist violence was rising domestically at the same time.

The 1890s saw lynching at horrific levels. Southern states expanded disfranchisement. *Plessy* gave constitutional shelter to segregation. In 1898—the same year Americans celebrated victory over Spain—white supremacists in Wilmington, North Carolina, overthrew a legitimately elected biracial local government through violence and intimidation, killing Black residents and driving political leaders from the city.

The United States could speak the language of freedom in Cuba while a racial coup occurred in North Carolina.

That is not an incidental juxtaposition. It shows how national ideologies work. A country can define liberty externally against a foreign empire while tolerating coercion internally because the categories of people entitled to full liberty are contested.

*Plessy* is therefore the decade's strongest rival.

The case arose after Homer Plessy deliberately challenged Louisiana's Separate Car Act by sitting in a railcar reserved for white passengers. Plessy was of mixed racial ancestry and legally classified as Black under Louisiana's racial system. The Supreme Court upheld the state's segregation law, reasoning that separate facilities did not necessarily violate the Equal Protection Clause if they were equal.

Justice John Marshall Harlan dissented, arguing that the Constitution is color-blind in the relevant sense and that the law imposed a badge of servitude.

The majority's doctrine gave constitutional legitimacy to segregation for decades.

Why choose 1898 instead?

Because the war changed the country's geographic, military, diplomatic, and constitutional position at once. *Plessy* consolidated a domestic racial order whose political foundations had already been built through Reconstruction's collapse. The Spanish-American War created a new category of American power and carried domestic racial assumptions into overseas governance.

The two events are not unrelated.

Imperial thought in 1898 was saturated with racial hierarchy. Supporters and opponents alike sometimes argued about whether Filipinos, Puerto Ricans, Cubans, and other peoples were capable of self-government using pseudo-scientific and civilizational categories common to the era. The same United States that legally segregated Black citizens was unlikely to approach nonwhite colonial subjects with assumptions of political equality.

Race provided a grammar for empire.

So did economics.

Industrialization had produced firms seeking larger markets and investors seeking opportunities abroad. Historians debate how directly business interests caused expansion; commercial motives were important but cannot explain every decision. Strategic naval concerns, humanitarian outrage, domestic politics, ideology, and contingency all mattered.

The best explanation is not one cause but a convergence that made overseas expansion imaginable and then made war a mechanism for accomplishing it.

The war's brevity also shaped public memory.

Short victorious wars are especially easy to romanticize. They create heroes without long domestic exhaustion. The fighting against Spain ended before the American public had to absorb years of casualties. The much longer Philippine conflict was harder to fit into the liberation narrative and received less durable place in popular memory.

National stories tend to stop when victory photographs become inconvenient.

But the empire did not stop.

By 1905 the United States was negotiating from a new position. Roosevelt helped mediate the end of the Russo-Japanese War, for which he later received the Nobel Peace Prize. The Panama Canal project was underway. American naval power mattered globally. The country could no longer plausibly describe itself as a continental republic insulated from great-power politics.

It had arrived.

Arrival produced new vulnerabilities.

A nation with overseas possessions and growing global trade needed a more sophisticated financial system. A country with industrial corporations operating nationally needed regulatory capacity. A state making global commitments needed reliable credit.

Then, in 1907, a financial panic would expose a remarkable weakness: when the national banking system seized up, the United States still depended heavily on a private financier, J. P. Morgan, to organize rescue.

The country that had become an empire did not yet have a modern central bank.

In 1913, it built one.
