# 1976–1985: Saturday Night at the Fed

Paul Volcker announced the most important economic decision of the decade on a Saturday evening.

On October 6, 1979, after an unusual meeting of the Federal Open Market Committee, the Federal Reserve said it would change the way it conducted monetary policy. Instead of trying to hold a short-term interest rate near a particular level, the Fed would place greater emphasis on controlling the growth of bank reserves and the money supply. The technical language concealed the political meaning.

Interest rates would be allowed to move much more violently.

The Federal Reserve was signaling that the United States would no longer treat persistent inflation as a condition to be managed gently around the edges. It would make inflation the problem to defeat, even if the cure caused recession, unemployment, bankruptcies, farm distress, construction collapse, and political fury.

The Volcker anti-inflation turn is the most important American event of 1976–1985 because it changed the credibility of money, the practical independence of the central bank, the economic environment in which the Reagan era unfolded, and the expectations of households, businesses, workers, investors, and politicians for decades afterward.

The runner-up is Ronald Reagan's election in 1980. Reagan changed tax policy, regulation, labor politics, defense spending, judicial appointments, rhetoric about government, and the conservative coalition. His presidency became the emblem of a political turn whose influence lasted far beyond two terms.

But Reagan inherited an economy whose most urgent problem was inflation, and the recession that initially defined his presidency was created in large part by monetary tightening already underway. The economic stability associated with the later 1980s depended heavily on a central bank willing to inflict pain no elected politician would have chosen as an opening platform.

Volcker takes the slot because he changed the constraint under which politics operated.

Inflation had been building for years.

The United States emerged from World War II with a strong industrial economy and, after postwar adjustments, enjoyed decades of growth. By the late 1960s, however, fiscal pressure from the Vietnam War and Great Society programs, monetary accommodation, wage and price dynamics, productivity changes, and global monetary tensions contributed to rising inflation.

The Bretton Woods system added strain. Under the postwar arrangement, the dollar was convertible into gold for foreign official holders at a fixed price while other currencies maintained pegged exchange rates. As dollars accumulated abroad and confidence in convertibility weakened, the system became difficult to sustain.

In August 1971, President Richard Nixon suspended the dollar's convertibility into gold and imposed wage and price controls among other measures.

The decision ended the old monetary anchor without immediately producing a stable replacement.

The oil shocks made the problem worse.

After the 1973 Arab-Israeli war, Arab members of OPEC imposed an oil embargo against countries including the United States and reduced production. Oil prices rose sharply. Gasoline lines became a visible symbol of vulnerability. Energy costs spread through transportation, manufacturing, heating, agriculture, and household budgets.

A second oil shock followed the Iranian Revolution in 1979.

Supply shocks alone do not explain the full inflation of the 1970s, but they made a difficult monetary problem harder because raising interest rates cannot produce oil. A central bank fighting inflation caused by energy scarcity risks suppressing the rest of the economy without fixing the original shortage.

Yet inflation had also become embedded in expectations.

This is the key to the Volcker story.

If workers expect prices to rise ten percent, they negotiate wages accordingly. If firms expect labor, materials, and borrowing costs to rise, they increase prices preemptively. If lenders expect dollars repaid in the future to be worth less, they demand higher nominal interest rates. If consumers expect goods to cost more next month, buying today can become rational.

Expectation becomes behavior.

Behavior sustains expectation.

Inflation can therefore persist after the first shock fades because everyone reorganizes around the belief that inflation is permanent.

The Federal Reserve had tightened policy at various times before Volcker, but political and institutional pressures repeatedly encouraged retreat when unemployment rose. The central bank's credibility suffered because economic actors learned to expect eventual accommodation.

A threat is weak if everyone believes you will abandon it when the cost becomes visible.

Arthur Burns, Fed chairman from 1970 to 1978, later became associated with the institution's failure to contain inflation, though the period's causes were broader than one chairman. G. William Miller served briefly before President Jimmy Carter appointed Volcker in August 1979.

Volcker inherited inflation already near politically intolerable levels.

He also inherited a Federal Reserve that had to convince the public not merely that it wanted lower inflation but that it was willing to accept the unemployment required to restore credibility.

That is a brutal form of persuasion.

The October 6 meeting became the institutional declaration.

The Fed announced new operating procedures and higher reserve requirements in some areas. The shift allowed the federal funds rate and other market interest rates to fluctuate more widely as the central bank targeted monetary aggregates more directly.

The federal funds rate subsequently moved into extraordinary territory, reaching around twenty percent at points in the early 1980s.

Mortgage rates climbed into the high teens. Auto loans became expensive. builders stopped projects. home sales fell. manufacturers dependent on credit cut production. small businesses failed. farmers carrying variable-rate debt faced crushing payments.

The policy was not pain in the abstract.

It arrived as a letter from a bank.

A family who had saved for a house could discover that the monthly payment on the same price home had become unaffordable. A factory with orders could still reduce employment because financing inventory and equipment cost too much. A farmer who had expanded during the 1970s land boom could watch commodity prices weaken while debt service soared.

The anti-inflation program redistributed economic pain toward borrowers and interest-sensitive industries.

That distribution produced political resistance.

Homebuilders mailed pieces of lumber to the Federal Reserve in protest. farmers drove tractors into Washington. members of Congress attacked the Fed. Business owners complained that unelected officials were destroying otherwise viable firms.

The criticism was not irrational.

The policy deliberately reduced demand.

Inflation is often described as if it can be removed surgically. In practice, one way a central bank reduces persistent inflation is by making credit expensive enough to slow spending, investment, hiring, and wage growth. Slack increases. workers lose bargaining power. firms become less able to raise prices. expectations change because people begin to believe the central bank will not rescue every downturn with immediate easing.

The economy is taught a lesson through recession.

The first tightening contributed to a short recession in 1980. The Fed briefly eased amid credit controls and economic weakness, and inflation remained high. Volcker then tightened again.

The recession beginning in 1981 became severe.

Unemployment rose above ten percent nationally. Manufacturing regions suffered deeply. The industrial Midwest experienced plant closures and long-term job loss. Construction collapsed. The farm economy entered a debt crisis. Developing countries that had borrowed heavily in dollars faced soaring debt-service costs, contributing to the Latin American debt crisis after Mexico announced in 1982 that it could no longer meet obligations on existing terms.

A policy made in Washington traveled through the dollar across the world.

That global reach was part of the Federal Reserve's accumulated power since 1913.

The United States issues the world's dominant reserve currency. Dollar interest rates influence international borrowing, capital flows, exchange rates, commodity prices, and debt sustainability. A domestic inflation fight can become an external financial shock for countries whose liabilities are denominated in dollars.

The Volcker shock was national policy with international casualties.

By 1982, inflation had fallen substantially from its peak and continued declining. The Fed eased as recession deepened and price pressures weakened. Economic growth resumed strongly in 1983.

The political economy after the recession was different.

Businesses and workers increasingly believed that the Federal Reserve would not tolerate a return to sustained double-digit inflation. Long-term inflation expectations fell. Bond markets eventually priced a more stable monetary environment. The central bank's credibility increased precisely because it had demonstrated willingness to withstand political pressure.

Credibility had been purchased with unemployment.

This is the moral difficulty of the chapter.

Historical accounts can make Volcker sound heroic because later generations enjoyed lower and more stable inflation. That view is incomplete if it treats the recession as a regrettable statistic rather than a social event.

People lost jobs, homes, farms, businesses, and years of income. Some communities never fully recovered from the industrial restructuring accelerated in the period. Lower inflation created broad future benefits, but the transition burden was not shared evenly.

Macroeconomic stabilization is distributional politics with equations around it.

Reagan entered the White House in January 1981 as this process intensified.

His election deserves the runner-up slot because the political turn was enormous. Reagan campaigned against high taxes, inflation, perceived government overreach, weakness abroad, and the malaise associated with the late Carter years. He presented government not as the default solution but often as the problem.

The rhetoric reversed much of the New Deal and Great Society political vocabulary.

Congress passed the Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981, sharply reducing individual income-tax rates over time and accelerating business tax benefits. The administration pursued deregulation, though important deregulatory movements had begun under Carter. It weakened federal labor power in a symbolic confrontation when Reagan fired more than eleven thousand striking air-traffic controllers after the Professional Air Traffic Controllers Organization defied an order to return to work.

The PATCO episode sent a signal far beyond federal employment.

Employers became more willing to resist unions and use replacement workers. Organized labor's bargaining position weakened as deindustrialization, regional shifts, global competition, automation, and legal strategy were already eroding union density.

The Reagan era did not cause all those forces.

It changed which side of the bargaining table felt history behind it.

Defense spending rose sharply. The administration took a harder line toward the Soviet Union, expanded support for anti-communist forces abroad, and accelerated a military buildup that supporters later credited with helping pressure the Soviet system and critics viewed as dangerous escalation.

Reagan also appointed judges whose influence would extend long after his presidency, including Sandra Day O'Connor, the first woman on the Supreme Court, and Antonin Scalia, who became one of the most consequential conservative jurists of the late twentieth century.

The political turn was real.

Why does monetary policy outrank it?

Because Reagan's political program could succeed only inside the economic environment produced by falling inflation.

High inflation destroys governing agendas. It consumes public attention, erodes real wages, creates unpredictable business conditions, and makes every fiscal choice look suspect. A president presiding over persistent double-digit inflation has little room to redefine politics around taxes, regulation, defense, and culture.

The Volcker Fed removed inflation from the center of American political life.

That allowed other arguments to become dominant.

The interaction between fiscal and monetary policy was complex. Reagan's tax cuts and defense buildup increased federal deficits, especially alongside recession. Traditional conservative concern about balanced budgets collided with supply-side arguments that lower marginal tax rates would improve incentives and growth. The Federal Reserve, meanwhile, maintained enough restraint to prevent fiscal expansion from recreating the previous inflation regime.

The policy mix produced high real interest rates and a strong dollar.

The strong dollar made imports cheaper for American consumers and American exports more expensive abroad. Manufacturing firms faced additional competitive pressure. Trade deficits increased. Financial capital flowed toward dollar assets.

The United States was beginning a period in which finance, services, global supply chains, and consumption would play larger roles relative to older manufacturing centers.

Volcker did not cause globalization.

The anti-inflation regime altered its terms.

The decade also saw the early acceleration of financial deregulation and innovation. Congress passed the Depository Institutions Deregulation and Monetary Control Act of 1980, beginning the phaseout of interest-rate ceilings and changing reserve requirements. The Garn-St Germain Act of 1982 further altered rules for savings and loan institutions.

These reforms responded partly to high inflation, which had made old interest-rate restrictions unsustainable. Depositors moved money away from regulated savings accounts toward market instruments paying higher returns. Financial institutions needed flexibility to compete.

Inflation broke old rules even before Volcker broke inflation.

The resulting deregulation contributed to new risks. The savings-and-loan crisis later in the 1980s and early 1990s would require a costly federal cleanup after many institutions took greater risks under weak supervision and distorted incentives.

Stability in one domain can produce risk-taking in another.

The decade's social consequences were equally complicated.

The recession hit Black unemployment especially hard because Black workers were disproportionately represented in vulnerable sectors and often entered recessions with less wealth and less employment security. Industrial job losses damaged cities already struggling with segregation, fiscal pressure, and population shifts.

At the same time, lower inflation eventually helped households whose wages and savings had been eroded by rising prices. Retirees and fixed-income households benefited from restored price stability even as borrowers suffered from high rates.

There was no single public interest visible at every point in the transition.

That is why central-bank independence is politically difficult.

The Fed's institutional argument is that elected officials may have incentives to favor short-term expansion while underweighting future inflation. An independent central bank can make unpopular decisions with a longer horizon.

But independence does not mean neutrality.

Choosing when to tighten, how far to tighten, what level of unemployment is acceptable, and how quickly to react to inflation are decisions with enormous consequences for workers, borrowers, asset owners, banks, and governments.

The Fed needs distance from daily politics precisely because its politics are so consequential.

Volcker's success therefore created a precedent as important as lower inflation.

Later Federal Reserve chairs inherited the expectation that price stability was a central institutional responsibility and that credibility depended on willingness to act before inflation became entrenched.

Alan Greenspan, Ben Bernanke, Janet Yellen, Jerome Powell, and their colleagues would operate under different conditions, doctrines, and mandates, but the memory of the 1970s and Volcker disinflation remained part of the institution's internal history.

When inflation surged again after the COVID-19 pandemic, policymakers and critics repeatedly invoked Volcker.

The precedent had become a threat from the past: fail to control inflation early and a later central bank may have to impose much more pain.

The comparison with Reagan also reveals something about how Americans remember economic eras.

Presidents receive credit and blame for conditions created partly by institutions they do not control. Reagan received political benefit from the recovery that began after 1982. Carter absorbed blame for inflation whose causes predated his administration and for the recession produced partly by the Fed chairman he appointed.

The presidency is the face of the economy even when the central bank moves the interest rate.

That mismatch creates political incentives for presidents to pressure the Fed and for the Fed to defend independence.

Volcker's appointment by Carter complicates the partisan story. The Democratic president chose the central banker who implemented a policy associated with the painful transition into the Reagan era. Reagan later reappointed Volcker in 1983, accepting an institution whose authority helped stabilize the environment for his own agenda.

Important systems often cross political ownership.

By 1985, inflation had been pushed from the center of national crisis. The economy was growing. the dollar was strong enough that the United States and several major economies negotiated the Plaza Accord to encourage depreciation. Financial markets were increasingly global. Computers were entering offices and homes. Personal computing, telecommunications, and software were beginning to change productivity and information.

The Cold War still organized international strategy.

Few Americans knew it was nearing its end.

The Soviet Union remained a nuclear superpower. Reagan described it in stark ideological terms. defense budgets remained large. Europe was divided by the Iron Curtain.

Then a reform-minded Soviet leader named Mikhail Gorbachev came to power in 1985.

Within four years, the political order of Eastern Europe would begin collapsing faster than diplomats could redesign it.

On November 9, 1989, East German officials mishandled an announcement about travel rules.

Crowds went to the checkpoints.

The guards opened the gates.

The wall that had defined the Cold War became a place people climbed with hammers.
