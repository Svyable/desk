# 1946–1955: Unanimous

The Supreme Court did not integrate a school on May 17, 1954.

It changed which side of the Constitution segregation had to stand on.

That distinction is the reason *Brown v. Board of Education* is the most important American event of 1946–1955. The Court's unanimous decision rejected state-mandated racial segregation in public schools as incompatible with the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. It overturned the constitutional logic that had allowed “separate but equal” to shield segregation for decades and gave the civil-rights movement a new legal position from which to challenge the racial order.

But children did not walk out of the Supreme Court into integrated classrooms.

School districts resisted. politicians organized opposition. southern states adopted evasive plans. white citizens formed councils. some communities closed public schools rather than integrate them. federal judges spent years issuing orders. presidents faced decisions about whether to enforce them. Black families who tried to exercise the right announced in *Brown* faced intimidation, job loss, harassment, and violence.

The decision mattered because law moved before society did.

The runner-up is the Korean War, fought from 1950 to 1953. That war militarized the early Cold War, entrenched a large peacetime defense establishment, accelerated U.S. commitments in Asia, and left the Korean Peninsula divided under an armistice that still structures international security. It is a major event. *Brown* takes the slot because it changed the constitutional legitimacy of America's domestic racial order and created a judicial lever that would be pulled repeatedly in the decade that followed.

The case was a delayed consequence of Reconstruction.

The Fourteenth Amendment had been ratified in 1868. Its Equal Protection Clause prohibited states from denying any person equal protection of the laws. Yet in *Plessy v. Ferguson* in 1896, the Supreme Court upheld Louisiana's segregated railway law and accepted the proposition that legally mandated separation did not necessarily imply unconstitutional inequality.

“Separate but equal” became the doctrine remembered from the case.

In practice, separation was rarely equal and was never neutral.

Segregation organized public resources by race under a white political system. Black schools were routinely underfunded. teachers were paid less. buildings, textbooks, transportation, and facilities reflected unequal investment. But the deepest injury was not a budget line. State law assigned children to schools because of race and made racial hierarchy part of ordinary public administration.

A child learned the racial order while learning arithmetic.

The legal attack on that order was deliberate and long-term.

The National Association for the Advancement of Colored People and its Legal Defense Fund built a litigation strategy over years. Lawyers including Charles Hamilton Houston, Thurgood Marshall, Robert L. Carter, Spottswood Robinson, Oliver Hill, and others challenged inequalities in graduate and professional education before attacking segregation in elementary and secondary schools directly.

The strategy understood precedent as terrain.

If courts could be persuaded that separate institutions were not genuinely equal in higher education, the constitutional credibility of segregation would weaken. Cases involving law schools and graduate programs produced victories. In *Sweatt v. Painter* in 1950, for example, the Supreme Court recognized that intangible factors such as reputation, alumni networks, faculty standing, and professional relationships made a hastily created separate law school for Black students unequal to the University of Texas Law School.

Equality could not be measured only in square footage.

The school cases that became *Brown* arose from several communities, not one heroic family.

In Topeka, Kansas, Oliver Brown was among Black parents whose children were assigned to segregated elementary schools. In Clarendon County, South Carolina, Black families challenged gross inequalities and then segregation itself. Cases from Virginia, Delaware, and the District of Columbia joined the broader litigation, though the D.C. case involved the Fifth Amendment because the Fourteenth Amendment applies directly to states.

The named case can obscure the coalition.

Behind every caption were parents willing to risk local retaliation, children made into constitutional test cases, teachers, ministers, organizers, lawyers, donors, and communities carrying consequences that appellate judges would not share.

The attack on school segregation also had important predecessors outside the exact Black-white framework that *Brown* would decide.

In California, Mexican American families challenged school segregation in *Mendez v. Westminster* during the 1940s. The federal litigation did not produce the same nationwide constitutional holding *Brown* later would, and the legal categories differed. But the case showed that families in the postwar West were already attacking the idea that public authorities could sort children by ancestry and then defend the separation as ordinary administration.

Housing law was moving too.

In *Shelley v. Kraemer* in 1948, the Supreme Court held that courts could not constitutionally enforce racially restrictive private covenants. The covenants themselves had been written by private property owners, but judicial enforcement was state action. The decision did not end residential segregation. Real-estate practices, lending discrimination, neighborhood violence, zoning, federal mortgage policy, local custom, and household wealth continued to produce deeply segregated metropolitan areas.

That distinction became crucial for schools.

A school system can stop assigning children explicitly by race and still serve neighborhoods created through decades of racial exclusion.

The postwar United States was building suburbs at enormous speed. Federal mortgage insurance and veterans' benefits helped millions of families buy homes, while Black families often faced discriminatory underwriting, exclusion from new subdivisions, unequal access to credit, and hostility when they tried to enter white neighborhoods. The GI Bill was federal law, but many of its practical benefits moved through local colleges, banks, real-estate markets, and state institutions that could discriminate.

The result was a widening gap between formal national citizenship and the geography in which citizenship was lived.

White families who obtained subsidized mortgages in appreciating suburbs could accumulate home equity and pass wealth forward. Black families excluded from the same markets lost not only a house in one year but the compound effect of decades of asset appreciation, school access, neighborhood services, and inheritance.

Schools sat inside that geography.

That is why *Brown* could be both revolutionary and incomplete from the day it was announced.

The Court could prohibit a state from ordering Black children to one school and white children to another because of race. It could not, in one opinion, unwind the housing market, municipal boundaries, tax bases, lending system, or wealth distribution that made school populations unequal even after explicit racial assignment disappeared.

The distinction between **de jure** segregation created by law and segregation produced through a mixture of public and private decisions would become one of the central remedial arguments of the next generation.

The Supreme Court first heard arguments in *Brown* in 1952.

The justices were divided and asked for reargument on historical questions concerning the Fourteenth Amendment and public education. Then Chief Justice Fred Vinson died in 1953. President Dwight Eisenhower appointed California governor Earl Warren as chief justice.

Warren became central to producing unanimity.

Unanimity mattered because a 5–4 decision striking at the social and political order of much of the South would have invited opponents to portray the ruling as fragile judicial preference. A unanimous Court could not eliminate resistance, but it could deny resistance the argument that nearly half the justices agreed segregation was constitutional.

On May 17, 1954, Warren read the Court's opinion.

The decision concluded that separate educational facilities are inherently unequal and that segregated public schooling deprived Black children of equal protection.

The opinion is notable for what it did not attempt. It did not write an enormous historical treatise proving exactly what every ratifier of the Fourteenth Amendment expected about public schools. The historical evidence was inconclusive for the Court's purpose in part because public education in 1868 was not organized nationally as it was by the mid-twentieth century.

The Court looked instead at the importance of education in modern life and the consequences of state-imposed separation.

That approach became one reason *Brown* remained both canonical and contested in constitutional theory.

Critics of judicial power could ask whether the Court had properly derived the rule from constitutional text and history. Defenders could answer that equal protection cannot permit a state to assign citizens to separate institutions by race in a system built on hierarchy.

The legal debate mattered.

So did the practical reality that the political system had failed for decades to dismantle segregation.

Black voters in much of the South remained disfranchised. Southern senators and representatives held powerful congressional positions through seniority. National parties depended on southern electoral coalitions. Congress had repeatedly failed to pass federal anti-lynching legislation despite decades of racial terror.

The courts became important partly because other democratic institutions were not democratically open to the people seeking change.

This is a recurring paradox in constitutional government. Judicial intervention can be criticized as countermajoritarian. But if the majority itself is partly constructed by excluding citizens from voting, leaving rights entirely to ordinary politics can preserve the exclusion that prevents political correction.

*Brown* sat inside that paradox.

The decision also depended on the Cold War setting in ways that should not be exaggerated but cannot be ignored.

The United States was competing with the Soviet Union for influence across newly independent and decolonizing countries. American officials understood that legal segregation and racial violence damaged claims that the United States represented freedom and democracy. Soviet propaganda used American racism relentlessly.

Foreign policy did not create Black Americans' demand for equality.

It raised the international cost of ignoring it.

The Truman administration had already taken steps. In 1948 President Harry Truman ordered desegregation of the armed forces through Executive Order 9981. The process unfolded over years and accelerated during the Korean War as manpower needs and military policy changed unit assignments.

The armed forces became one of the first major national institutions to undergo formal racial integration in the postwar era.

That experience undermined claims that integration was administratively impossible.

The Korean War itself deserves more attention than its status as runner-up usually receives.

North Korea invaded South Korea in June 1950. The United States entered under a United Nations command after the Soviet representative was absent from the Security Council vote. American and South Korean forces were pushed into a perimeter around Pusan before General Douglas MacArthur's amphibious landing at Inchon reversed the battlefield.

UN forces advanced north toward the Chinese border. China entered the war in massive force. American and allied troops retreated. The front eventually stabilized near the original dividing line.

MacArthur publicly challenged administration policy and advocated widening the war. Truman fired him in April 1951.

The dismissal was a constitutional event inside a military one.

A popular general wanted a more aggressive strategy against China. The president insisted on civilian control and a limited war designed to avoid wider conflict with China and potentially the Soviet Union. Truman's approval ratings suffered. But the principle that generals execute national policy rather than make it remained intact.

The war continued until an armistice in July 1953.

There was no peace treaty.

The divided peninsula became a permanent Cold War front. The United States maintained troops in South Korea. Defense spending remained far above prewar levels. NATO commitments deepened. The National Security Council's NSC-68 vision of major military buildup became more politically achievable after the invasion.

The Cold War state became durable.

Why does *Brown* still win?

Because the constitutional delegitimization of segregation created a mechanism of domestic change that touched schools first and the wider civil-rights movement immediately after.

The year following *Brown* brought the Montgomery bus boycott after Rosa Parks was arrested for refusing to surrender her seat under segregated bus rules. The boycott elevated Martin Luther King Jr. and demonstrated the power of sustained mass action. Court litigation eventually struck down bus segregation.

In 1957, nine Black students attempted to enter Central High School in Little Rock, Arkansas, under federal court orders. Governor Orval Faubus used the Arkansas National Guard to block entry. Mobs threatened the students. Eisenhower federalized the Guard and sent soldiers from the 101st Airborne Division to enforce integration.

The scene made *Brown*'s meaning concrete.

The Supreme Court had declared a right. A governor resisted. A president used federal military power to enforce judicial authority against a state.

Reconstruction had returned in institutional form.

Not because federal troops permanently occupied the South, but because the constitutional question was the same: will the national government protect federal rights when a state refuses?

The answer at Little Rock was yes.

Elsewhere the answer was slower.

The Supreme Court's follow-up decision in *Brown II* in 1955 instructed that desegregation proceed with “all deliberate speed,” language that allowed federal judges to manage local implementation but also gave resistant districts room to delay. Southern politicians organized “Massive Resistance.” Virginia adopted policies designed to obstruct integration. Prince Edward County later closed its public schools for years rather than operate an integrated system, leaving Black children without public education while private academies served many white students.

A constitutional command can be clear while remedy remains contested.

That lesson would shape later disputes over school busing, district boundaries, housing segregation, affirmative action, and the distinction between de jure segregation required by law and de facto segregation produced by housing patterns, local policy, wealth, and private action.

*Brown* won the principle faster than the country solved the structure.

The case also changed the civil-rights movement's sense of possibility.

Litigation alone was not enough. Years of court victories had shown that even favorable rulings required organization and enforcement. But *Brown* demonstrated that one branch of the federal government could be made to reject segregation categorically.

That mattered to activists deciding where to invest effort.

Local direct-action campaigns, voter registration drives, boycotts, sit-ins, Freedom Rides, marches, and national lobbying would pressure the executive branch and Congress in ways litigation could not.

The legal and mass movements reinforced each other.

Television and national news helped turn local violence into national political events. White attacks on peaceful demonstrators could be seen outside the South. The federal government faced growing pressure not merely to announce rights but to protect them.

The path from *Brown* to the Civil Rights Act of 1964 was not automatic.

It was built by people using the decision as one lever among many.

The case's constitutional afterlife also expanded beyond race.

*Brown* became a moral and legal model for arguments that state classifications marking groups as inferior could violate equal protection. Later movements for women's rights, disability rights, gay rights, and other forms of equality did not simply copy the case, and their doctrinal paths differed. But the idea that formal law could encode social hierarchy and that courts could demand justification became central to modern constitutional politics.

The decision also changed what segregation defenders had to say.

Before *Brown*, officials could argue segregation was constitutionally legitimate under *Plessy*. After *Brown*, they had to resist implementation, attack judicial authority, invoke states' rights, redesign policies, or shift to ostensibly race-neutral mechanisms.

Law changed the vocabulary of resistance.

That is a form of power even before compliance.

The case's unanimity mattered here. Warren worked to avoid separate concurrences that might expose divergent rationales. A single opinion gave the country a clear constitutional statement.

Clarity did not eliminate debate over why segregation was unconstitutional, how quickly it must end, or what remedies were permissible.

It made the bottom line harder to fracture.

There is a danger in making the Supreme Court the hero of this chapter.

Courts did not discover segregation was wrong before Black Americans did. The plaintiffs and lawyers brought the cases. Black communities financed and sustained the organizations. activists endured reprisals. Social science evidence, legal argument, historical research, and political organizing supplied the Court with a record and a context.

The justices changed law because people had spent decades making the old law untenable.

That is how institutional change usually happens. The formal actor receives the headline. The pressure accumulates elsewhere.

*Brown* also reminds us that landmark decisions can be less powerful than they appear if the surrounding political coalition is weak.

The ruling required Congress eventually to create stronger enforcement mechanisms. The Civil Rights Act of 1964 would authorize federal action against segregation and discrimination in ways judicial decrees alone could not. The Voting Rights Act of 1965 would attack voting barriers that the Fifteenth Amendment had prohibited in principle nearly a century earlier.

Rights needed administration.

That need will define the next chapter.

The most important event of 1956–1965 will not be the most dangerous moment of the Cold War, though the Cuban Missile Crisis brought the world close to nuclear catastrophe. It will not be the March on Washington, though the movement demonstrated extraordinary national power. It will not be the assassination of John F. Kennedy, though that changed political possibilities.

It will be a statute signed on July 2, 1964.

The law would do something *Brown* could not do by itself: give the federal government broad tools to make civil rights an operating rule across public accommodations, employment, education, and federally supported programs.

*Brown* changed the constitutional side.

The Civil Rights Act would change the enforcement side.
