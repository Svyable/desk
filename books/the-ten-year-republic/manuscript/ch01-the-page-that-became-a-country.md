# 1776–1785: The Page That Became a Country

The most important sentence written in Philadelphia in the summer of 1776 was not the first one Thomas Jefferson drafted, and it was not the one Americans now recite most easily. It was the sentence Congress had already spent weeks avoiding.

Richard Henry Lee of Virginia put it plainly on June 7: the colonies were, and ought to be, free and independent states. That was the decision. The Declaration of Independence was the explanation.

The distinction matters because national memory has compressed the summer into a single parchment under glass. The order was messier. Congress appointed a committee to draft a statement before it had actually agreed to independence. Delegates argued while armies moved, ports were threatened, New York waited, and the British Empire prepared to crush a rebellion whose political endpoint had not yet been formally declared. The famous document began as something closer to contingency planning. If the colonies were going to cross the line, they would need to tell foreign governments, undecided colonists, future allies, and themselves why.

A country would eventually grow around the explanation.

The choice for the first ten-year window of this book is therefore the Declaration rather than the military victory at Yorktown. That is not because paper defeated Britain. It did not. Independence required soldiers, taxes, state governments, loans, foreign alliance, luck, endurance, French military power, British political calculation, and staggering sacrifice. If George Washington’s army had been destroyed, or if France had stayed out, Jefferson’s prose could have become a magnificent statement attached to a failed revolt.

Yorktown is the strongest rival for this chapter precisely because it reminds us not to confuse a nation’s story about itself with the material conditions that allowed the story to survive.

But Yorktown ended a campaign. The Declaration kept beginning new ones.

The remarkable thing about the document is not that its authors fulfilled its principles in 1776. They did not. It is that they wrote principles expansive enough to become evidence against the political order they were creating. The language of equality and unalienable rights would be taken up by people the founding generation excluded, constrained, enslaved, displaced, or ignored. The Declaration became a promise that could be quoted by people whose lives exposed the gap between American ideals and American practice.

That afterlife is why it takes the slot.

To understand the risk of July 1776, it helps to forget the ending. The colonies had been fighting Britain since the spring of 1775, but fighting and independence were not synonyms. Armed resistance could still be imagined as pressure for reconciliation. Many colonists had British identities, British legal traditions, British commercial ties, and no experience of a continental republic. The political problem was not simply whether Britain had governed badly. It was whether thirteen separate colonies could become something else together.

Even the word “independence” hid several problems. Independent from whom? United under what? Governed by which institutions? With what claim to western land? Protected by whose navy? Financed how? The Declaration answered almost none of those operational questions. It did something more basic. It supplied legitimacy for the rupture.

The Continental Congress had to act in a world where European monarchies were the normal units of power. A rebellion that wanted recognition and aid needed to become a recognizable political entity. Lee’s resolution did that in the language of sovereignty. Jefferson’s draft and Congress’s revisions did it in the language of political theory and indictment.

The document begins with separation because separation is the immediate act. It then rises into a set of claims about human equality, rights, government by consent, and the right to alter or abolish a government destructive of those ends. After that comes the long accusation against George III. The structure is almost legal: principle, standard, evidence, remedy.

The most famous lines are universal. The political body adopting them was not.

That contradiction was present before the ink dried. Slavery existed throughout the colonies, deeply embedded in the economy and law of several of them and present in the lives of many political leaders. Jefferson himself enslaved human beings. Congress removed a passage from his draft condemning the British king over the slave trade, a passage whose own moral logic was compromised by the fact that colonial demand and colonial law had sustained slavery. The final Declaration did not abolish slavery or extend political equality to women, Native nations, free Black people, people without property, or many others whose rights would later be contested.

The words “all men are created equal” therefore did not describe the United States at its birth. They created a standard the United States could fail.

That turned out to be historically explosive.

A narrower declaration might have worked perfectly well as diplomacy. Congress could have said that Britain had violated colonial charters, taxed without legitimate representation, deployed troops improperly, and therefore forfeited authority. Those claims were already powerful within the British constitutional tradition. The Declaration did more. It described rights as prior to government and legitimate government as dependent on consent.

Once written that way, the argument could travel.

It traveled into antislavery politics. It traveled into women’s rights arguments. It traveled into abolitionist speeches, Black political organizing, labor claims, civil-rights movements, and later demands that American institutions live by principles older than any particular statute. The document did not automatically produce those movements. People did. But it gave them language with unusual leverage because the language sat near the foundation of national legitimacy.

Frederick Douglass’s famous Fourth of July address in 1852 would derive much of its force from the gap between the nation’s celebration of founding principles and the continued existence of slavery. Abraham Lincoln would treat the Declaration’s equality principle as a central statement of the nation’s purpose, even while constitutional law and political reality lagged behind it. Martin Luther King Jr. would frame civil rights as a demand that the republic honor a promissory inheritance. Again and again, the founding document became useful to people arguing that the founding settlement was unfinished.

That is a strange kind of political technology. A text written by a limited political class becomes more powerful because its language exceeds the class that wrote it.

Congress did not set out to manufacture two and a half centuries of moral litigation against the United States. It needed to justify independence. The durable effect came from the scale of the justification.

There is another reason the Declaration matters more than a simple announcement of sovereignty. It helped turn thirteen colonial disputes into one public cause. Before independence, the colonies had different economies, religions, political cultures, and relationships with Britain. Their cooperation had been growing through imperial crisis and war, but unity remained fragile. A common statement did not erase those differences. It gave them a shared object.

The “United States of America” existed in language before it became a durable federal state.

That linguistic unity should not be romanticized. The states guarded their sovereignty. The Articles of Confederation, drafted during the Revolution and ratified later, created a deliberately weak central structure. The next chapter exists because independence did not solve the problem of union. But the Declaration made it harder to return to the older political identity. Once representatives publicly pledged themselves to independence, compromise with Britain became not merely a policy reversal but a repudiation of a new political claim.

The war sharpened that commitment. Independence required institutions before the United States had settled what the United States was. States wrote constitutions. Congress raised armies, borrowed money, sought foreign recognition, and attempted to coordinate a conflict across enormous distances with unreliable communications. Washington’s army became both a military force and a symbol of continental authority that remained politically fragile.

The British state understood the stakes. The summer of 1776 brought a massive military campaign centered on New York. The Declaration was adopted in Philadelphia while one of the most powerful empires in the world assembled the means to make the announcement irrelevant.

This is where hindsight usually cheats.

We know the parchment survived. The delegates did not.

They could not know that France would formally ally with the United States in 1778. They could not know that British strategy would become stretched across theaters, that the war would grind on for years, or that Cornwallis would surrender at Yorktown in 1781. They could not know that Britain would eventually recognize American independence in the Treaty of Paris in 1783. They were making an irreversible declaration before the outcome that made it look sensible.

A successful revolution is later narrated as the birth of a nation. A failed one is often remembered as rebellion.

The Declaration was written in that unstable interval.

Its authors also could not know how quickly independence would create new contradictions. If legitimate government rested on consent, what exactly counted as consent in states where voting was restricted? If rights were unalienable, what was slavery? If people could dissolve political bonds when government became destructive, what prevented later regions from making the same claim against the United States? If the states were independent, how much authority could any continental government exercise over them?

The document opened questions it did not answer.

The next 250 years can be read as arguments over who is allowed to use its premises.

Native nations posed one of the most immediate challenges to the new republic’s language of sovereignty. The Declaration’s indictment of George III included a notoriously hostile reference to Indigenous peoples on the frontier. The revolution was not merely a contest between colonists and Britain; it was also fought across lands where Native nations made strategic decisions about alliances, survival, trade, territory, and the relative danger posed by imperial and colonial expansion. American independence did not create an empty continent west of the Appalachians. It created a new state with strong incentives to claim land occupied and governed by others.

The later chapters on the Louisiana Purchase, Indian Removal, Texas, and the Mexican Cession all descend from this unresolved geography.

So does slavery. The Revolution destabilized slavery in some places and intensified arguments against it. Northern states began paths toward abolition. Enslaved people pursued freedom through military service, escape, petition, litigation, and the chaos of war. Britain offered freedom in certain contexts to enslaved people who left rebel masters; the revolutionary conflict disrupted old arrangements in ways no legislature fully controlled. Yet slavery survived independence and became more deeply tied to the expanding cotton economy in the decades that followed.

The nation could declare universal rights and build material prosperity on human bondage at the same time.

That was not an incidental hypocrisy waiting for later generations to notice. It was a structural contradiction that shaped congressional representation, territorial expansion, party systems, constitutional interpretation, and eventually civil war.

The Declaration’s power rests partly in that contradiction because later reformers did not have to invent an external moral standard. They could accuse the republic using the republic’s own founding vocabulary.

This produces an uncomfortable question: would the United States have been better served by a more honest declaration, one that stated plainly that the political community envisioned in 1776 was limited?

Perhaps it would have been more accurate. It would also have been less useful to later generations.

Universal language can be cynical when uttered by people unwilling to live under it. It can also escape the control of its authors. Once a political order says that rights belong to persons rather than being gifts from rulers, every exclusion demands an explanation. The explanation may survive for generations. It may be backed by law and violence. But the burden of justification has shifted.

That shift does not liberate anyone by itself. It gives political struggle a different terrain.

Yorktown, the runner-up, deserves its case. On October 19, 1781, after the combined pressure of American and French forces and crucial French naval action, Cornwallis’s army surrendered. The victory shattered Britain’s practical prospect of restoring control through the same war. It altered politics in London and set the stage for peace negotiations. Without it, American independence might have remained aspirational.

Why not call Yorktown the most important event of 1776–1785?

Because military victory secured the existence of the new state, while the Declaration supplied a recurring argument about what that state was for.

The distinction becomes clearer if we imagine two counterfactuals. In one, the colonies win independence through war but issue a narrow statement of sovereignty with no memorable theory of equality or rights. The United States still exists. Its later reform movements lose one of their most potent founding texts, though they might find other language. In the other, the Declaration exists but the war is lost. The text survives as the charter of a failed revolution and perhaps influences later movements, but there is no United States as we know it.

Yorktown is indispensable to existence. The Declaration is indispensable to identity.

The choice of the Declaration therefore depends on what this book means by “made America.” It does not mean only what kept the state alive. It means what created the recurring institutions, claims, contradictions, and possibilities through which later Americans would fight over the state.

The Declaration did that with unusual reach.

It also created a national habit that will recur throughout these chapters: transforming a crisis into a general principle.

The Constitution would transform weakness under the Articles into a new theory of federal power. The Fourteenth Amendment would transform the wreckage of slavery and civil war into national guarantees of citizenship, due process, and equal protection. The Federal Reserve Act would transform financial panics into an argument for a central banking institution. The Civil Rights Act would transform movement pressure and resistance into national rules. The crisis of 2008 would transform financial collapse into a new regulatory and monetary architecture.

Americans repeatedly solve immediate problems by building machinery that later generations inherit.

Sometimes the machinery works. Sometimes it produces new problems. Sometimes the principle is broader than the settlement. Sometimes the settlement betrays the principle almost immediately.

July 1776 established the pattern.

By 1783 Britain had recognized the independence of the United States. The military problem that had dominated the revolutionary years was receding. A different weakness became visible. The states had won freedom from imperial authority but had not created a national government strong enough to manage debt, commerce, taxation, security, and conflict among themselves with confidence.

Independence had answered the question “Who rules us?”

It had not answered “How will we rule ourselves?”

That is why the most important event of the next ten-year window would take place not on a battlefield and not before a cheering crowd, but inside a room in Philadelphia where the windows were kept shut and the delegates decided that the system created during the Revolution was not enough.
