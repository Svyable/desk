# 1906–1915: The Night America Built a Central Bank

In October 1907, the United States discovered that one of the most important public institutions in the country was a private man with a library.

J. Pierpont Morgan did not hold public office. He controlled capital, relationships, information, and enough authority over other bankers to organize collective action when panic spread through New York's financial system. As trust companies faced runs and credit markets froze, financiers gathered under his influence. Morgan helped determine which institutions would be supported, how funds would be assembled, and where private rescue might prevent a wider collapse.

The episode became one of the most persuasive arguments for a central bank.

The Federal Reserve Act of 1913 is the most important American event of 1906–1915 because it changed who would stand behind the financial system when private markets failed to coordinate themselves. The law created a network of regional Federal Reserve Banks overseen by a federal board and established an elastic currency and lender-of-last-resort architecture that would evolve into the modern Federal Reserve System.

The act did not create the modern Fed in one night. Its powers, operating procedures, independence, and intellectual framework changed over decades. The institution made grave mistakes during the Great Depression, adapted during wartime, gained new responsibilities, and eventually became one of the most powerful economic actors in the world.

That long afterlife is why 1913 takes the slot.

The runner-up is the Triangle Shirtwaist factory fire of 1911. The deaths of 146 workers, most of them young immigrant women, transformed labor and workplace-safety politics in New York and became a national symbol of the human cost of unregulated industrial production. Triangle helped build modern social regulation. The Federal Reserve Act, however, created national financial machinery whose decisions would later reach interest rates, employment, inflation, banking, housing, recessions, wars, and global markets.

The central-bank story begins with a historical irony.

The United States had possessed central-bank-like institutions before. Alexander Hamilton had championed the First Bank of the United States, chartered in 1791. Its charter expired in 1811 amid political opposition. Wartime financial difficulties helped produce the Second Bank of the United States in 1816. Andrew Jackson then made destroying that institution a central political project, vetoing recharter and removing federal deposits.

Americans had argued about central banking almost from the beginning of the republic.

The arguments mixed constitutional theory, regional interests, agrarian suspicion, democratic hostility to concentrated finance, and practical concern about monetary stability. Critics feared a privileged financial institution tied to wealthy eastern interests. Supporters feared a fragmented banking system unable to supply a stable national currency or manage crises.

Jackson won the political fight.

The United States then spent much of the nineteenth century without a central bank.

That did not mean it lacked banks or national financial rules. The Civil War produced a national banking system and federally regulated banknotes backed by government bonds. The Treasury performed some stabilizing functions. New York banks developed clearinghouse arrangements through which institutions could settle accounts and cooperate during stress.

But the system remained unusually decentralized and prone to seasonal and cyclical pressures.

Banks held reserves against deposits. Agricultural cycles generated predictable demands for currency and credit, especially during planting and harvest seasons. When demand for cash surged, interest rates could spike. The supply of currency did not expand smoothly with economic need. Reserves were concentrated in correspondent relationships that could transmit distress. Trust companies grew outside parts of the traditional national-bank framework and operated with different reserve structures.

The country had national markets and nineteenth-century crisis machinery.

Financial panics repeatedly revealed the mismatch.

The Panic of 1873 helped trigger a long depression. The Panic of 1893 produced severe bank and business failures. By 1907, the United States had become the world's largest industrial economy by important measures, yet a confidence shock could still trigger cascading withdrawals because no public institution stood ready to supply liquidity broadly and credibly.

The 1907 panic began amid failed speculation and distrust involving financial institutions in New York. Runs spread to trust companies. The Knickerbocker Trust Company suspended operations after depositors demanded cash. Fear moved through the system.

A bank run is a peculiar social technology.

A sound institution can become unsound if enough depositors demand money at once. Banks transform short-term deposits into longer-term loans and investments; they cannot convert every asset into cash immediately without losses. If each depositor believes everyone else will withdraw, withdrawing becomes individually rational and collectively destructive.

Confidence is therefore not decorative in banking.

It is balance-sheet infrastructure.

In a modern system, a credible central bank can interrupt a panic by lending against good collateral, expanding reserves, and convincing institutions and depositors that cash will be available. In 1907, that role had to be improvised.

Morgan and other bankers organized rescues, pooled resources, examined institutions, and decided which firms might be saved. Treasury Secretary George Cortelyou deposited government funds into national banks to support liquidity. John D. Rockefeller publicly expressed confidence and provided money. New York's clearinghouse system issued loan certificates that helped banks conserve cash.

The response worked well enough to stop complete collapse.

Its success made the weakness more obvious.

A modern industrial republic should not need to hope that an aging private financier is available, trusted, informed, and willing to lock bankers in a library until they agree to a rescue.

Morgan's intervention also raised a democratic problem. If financial stabilization is a public necessity, why should the power to decide which institutions survive belong to an unelected private network operating through personal authority?

The same concentration of finance that worried Progressive reformers was being asked to save the system from itself.

Crisis made central banking politically possible in a country with a long anti-central-bank tradition.

Congress passed the Aldrich-Vreeland Act in 1908, creating temporary mechanisms for emergency currency and establishing the National Monetary Commission to study reform. Senator Nelson Aldrich, an influential Republican, led the commission. American policymakers studied European central banks and financial systems.

The reform process then produced one of the most famous secret meetings in financial history.

In November 1910, a small group of bankers and policy experts traveled discreetly to Jekyll Island, Georgia, where they worked on a plan for a reserve system. Participants included Aldrich and representatives connected to major financial institutions. Secrecy was politically necessary because any plan openly described as designed by Wall Street bankers could be dead on arrival in the Progressive Era.

The meeting later became fertile ground for conspiracy theories.

The documented story is already interesting enough. Financial elites helped design a system intended to solve a genuine public problem. That fact does not prove hidden control of later policy; it demonstrates the difficulty of building financial institutions without relying on people who understand finance and who also possess interests in the system being regulated.

Expertise and capture often sit uncomfortably close.

The Aldrich plan envisioned a National Reserve Association with substantial banker influence. It failed politically after Democrats gained power and Progressive suspicion of Wall Street remained strong.

Woodrow Wilson's election in 1912 changed the reform coalition.

Democrats wanted a system less visibly controlled by private bankers. Representative Carter Glass of Virginia and Senator Robert Owen of Oklahoma became central congressional architects. William Jennings Bryan, secretary of state and longtime critic of financial concentration, pushed for stronger public authority. Bankers fought aspects of the proposal. Progressives disagreed about how centralized the system should be.

The eventual Federal Reserve Act was a political compromise embodied as organizational design.

It created regional Federal Reserve Banks owned in a special statutory sense by member commercial banks but operating under federal law and public purposes, alongside a Federal Reserve Board in Washington appointed through the political system. The structure reflected distrust of both Wall Street concentration and centralized government.

Twelve regional banks spread authority geographically.

The design was not accidental federalism. It was a map of political suspicion.

New York finance was too powerful to ignore and too unpopular to dominate openly. Agricultural and commercial regions wanted representation. Banks wanted influence over a system that would hold their reserves and support liquidity. Progressives wanted public oversight.

The result was neither a single European-style central bank nor a loose private association.

It was an American hybrid.

Wilson signed the Federal Reserve Act on December 23, 1913.

The date sits awkwardly near Christmas, which contributes to myths that Congress secretly passed the law when nobody was watching. In reality, central-bank reform had been debated publicly for years. The legislative process was contentious and well known.

The act's importance lay in several functions.

It created a mechanism through which banks could rediscount eligible commercial paper, allowing them to convert certain loans into reserve funds. It provided for a more elastic currency through Federal Reserve notes. It centralized and reorganized reserve management. It gave the system tools to respond to seasonal demands and financial stress.

Those functions sound technical because finance becomes visible mainly when it fails.

Consider what a bank panic does outside Wall Street. A solvent business cannot renew a short-term loan and misses payroll. A farmer cannot finance inventory. A merchant cannot obtain credit for seasonal goods. A household loses savings in a bank failure. Construction stops. employers fire workers. prices of assets collapse as everyone tries to raise cash.

Liquidity is the plumbing beneath ordinary life.

The Federal Reserve was built because plumbing had become a national concern.

Yet creating the institution did not create competent policy automatically.

The Fed's early years were shaped by World War I. The system helped support wartime finance and adapted to enormous gold flows and government borrowing. After the war, it confronted inflation and recession. Its structure generated tensions between regional Reserve Banks and the Washington board.

The Great Depression would expose profound failures.

Between 1929 and 1933, thousands of banks failed. The money supply contracted sharply. The Federal Reserve did not provide the broad, aggressive monetary support later central-bank doctrine would consider necessary. Historians and economists have debated the precise mechanisms and responsibility, but the institution created to reduce banking instability did not prevent the worst financial collapse in American history.

Institutions learn painfully.

The Depression led to major reforms: deposit insurance through the FDIC, changes in Fed structure and power, separation of some banking activities under Glass-Steagall, securities regulation, and greater federal involvement in finance.

The Fed survived its failure and became stronger.

That pattern is common in American government. Agencies rarely emerge complete. Crisis reveals weaknesses, Congress reallocates authority, doctrine changes, and the institution becomes something its founders would only partly recognize.

The Fed's later power over interest rates makes 1913 loom larger in retrospect.

Monetary policy influences the price and availability of credit across the economy. Mortgage rates affect housing. corporate borrowing affects investment. bank funding affects lending. interest-rate differences move international capital and currency values. unemployment and inflation respond, imperfectly and with delays, to shifts in financial conditions.

A committee meeting in Washington can therefore alter the monthly payment on a house in Ohio, the exchange rate facing an exporter in Texas, the financing plan of a software company in California, and the debt burden of a developing country borrowing in dollars.

That reach was not built overnight in 1913.

The legal institution made it possible.

The Triangle Shirtwaist fire, the runner-up, demonstrates the parallel expansion of government into social regulation.

On March 25, 1911, fire broke out at the Triangle Waist Company factory in New York City's Greenwich Village. The workforce was composed largely of young immigrant women, many Jewish and Italian. Inadequate exits, locked or obstructed doors, insufficient fire-safety systems, and the building's conditions contributed to catastrophe. Workers jumped from upper floors to escape flames. One hundred forty-six people died.

The fire shocked the city because industrial risk became visible in bodies on a sidewalk.

Reformers, labor organizers, survivors, families, and investigators pushed for stronger workplace and building regulations. New York enacted numerous safety laws. Frances Perkins, who witnessed the aftermath and later became Franklin Roosevelt's labor secretary, would connect the tragedy to her reform career.

Triangle helped establish the principle that workplace safety is not merely a private contract between employer and employee.

Government has a role because workers may lack bargaining power, information, or practical freedom to refuse dangerous conditions.

That principle belongs beside antitrust in the development of the regulatory state.

Why does the Fed still take the slot?

Because central banking became the institution through which the national government could influence the price of money itself. Safety rules regulate particular conditions. Monetary policy alters the financial environment in which almost every economic decision occurs.

The comparison is not moral. The 146 deaths at Triangle are concrete in a way reserve ratios are not. Importance is not a contest in suffering.

It is a question of downstream structure.

The Federal Reserve Act also illustrates a recurring theme of these twenty-five windows: America builds public machinery after private coordination fails at sufficient scale.

The Articles failed to coordinate states; the Constitution strengthened the federal government. State law struggled with national trusts; Congress passed antitrust law. Private financiers could rescue one panic but were an inadequate permanent lender of last resort; Congress created a reserve system.

Scale is one of the hidden authors of government.

As markets become national, regulation becomes national. As capital moves faster, stabilizing institutions need larger reach. As companies and banks become interconnected, individual failure can become systemic.

The Fed was an answer to interconnectedness.

Its creation also sharpened a democratic tension that never disappeared: how independent should experts be from elected politics when their decisions can create enormous winners and losers?

Central banks need credibility. If politicians can force easy money before every election, inflationary pressures may follow. But insulation creates its own legitimacy problem. Interest-rate increases can produce unemployment. lender-of-last-resort choices can save some institutions and not others. Asset purchases can raise prices of securities and homes. Financial rules distribute risk.

Technical language does not make those choices apolitical.

It makes their politics harder to see.

The Federal Reserve would spend the next century negotiating that boundary between democratic accountability and expert discretion.

One of its most consequential leaders, Paul Volcker, would appear in a later chapter. In October 1979, faced with entrenched inflation, the Fed would change its operating approach and accept severe economic pain in pursuit of price stability.

That decision would only be possible because the institution built in 1913 had accumulated the authority to make it.

The road from J. P. Morgan's library to Volcker's Saturday-night press conference runs through the Federal Reserve Act.

But before the country reached that monetary crisis, it faced a democratic transformation of another kind.

For generations, women had organized, petitioned, marched, lobbied, published, resisted arrest, divided over strategy, and demanded political citizenship.

In 1920, the Constitution would declare that the right to vote could not be denied on account of sex.

The electorate would never be the same size again.
