# Back Matter

## A note on choosing one event

This book uses a deliberately unfair form.

Each ten-year window beginning with 1776 gets one event. History does not.

A decade can contain a war, an amendment, a financial crisis, a technological breakthrough, a movement, a court decision, and a demographic transformation whose consequences unfold on different time scales. Any list pretending those events can be placed on one objective numerical scale would be false precision.

The selections are arguments based on four recurring questions: institutional reach, breadth, duration, and path dependence. Did the event change the rules, powers, borders, or machinery under which later decisions occurred? How many domains of American life did it reach? Did its consequences persist? Did it make later outcomes materially easier, harder, or newly possible?

Those questions do not produce a score.

They force judgment.

Every chapter therefore names at least one serious runner-up and states the reason it loses. The rival event is not included as a gesture toward balance. It is a falsification device. If later research shows that the runner-up had greater causal reach than the selected event, a future edition should change the selection.

The book is meant to be revised by history.

The final window, 2016–2025, is especially provisional. COVID-19 currently holds the slot because of its reach across mortality, work, schools, fiscal and monetary policy, science, supply chains, housing, elections, and public trust. January 6, *Dobbs*, and generative artificial intelligence remain plausible alternatives whose long-run consequences are not yet fully visible.

## Why the windows begin in 1776

Most decade histories use calendar decades: the 1790s, 1800s, 1810s, and so on.

This book does not.

The first window is 1776–1785 and each subsequent window advances ten years. The final complete window is therefore 2016–2025, with 2026 treated separately in the epilogue.

The structure makes the semiquincentennial set the clock. The United States did not begin on January 1 of a year ending in zero, and its 250th anniversary should not need to be forced into a calendar convention invented later.

The result is occasionally awkward. Major events Americans associate with a named decade can fall into a neighboring chapter. That awkwardness is useful because it breaks some inherited categories and forces direct comparison among events that ordinary decade labels keep apart.

## On causation

The chapters use causal language carefully.

An event can be necessary without being sufficient. It can accelerate a process it did not create. It can create institutional capacity later actors use for purposes the original participants never intended. It can matter because of the response it provokes rather than because of its immediate effect.

The Declaration did not cause abolition. It supplied language later abolitionists and civil-rights leaders could use against American exclusion. The Louisiana Purchase did not cause the Civil War. It greatly enlarged the territorial arena in which slavery's expansion became a recurring political crisis. The Spanish-American War did not create every feature of American global power. It made overseas possession and sustained imperial administration an American governmental reality. The Federal Reserve Act did not create modern monetary policy in 1913. It created the institution that later acquired those tools and expectations.

Where a chapter describes a chain of consequences, the intended claim is therefore usually one of changed possibility rather than single-cause determinism.

History has too many actors for monocausal confidence.

## On moral accounting

National histories often hide distribution inside verbs.

Territory “expanded.” finance “stabilized.” production “mobilized.” schools “integrated.” markets “recovered.”

Those verbs can be accurate and incomplete.

Expansion displaced Native nations and expanded slavery. stabilization through the Volcker disinflation imposed severe unemployment and debt distress. World War II mobilization created unprecedented national capacity while Japanese Americans were incarcerated. financial rescue in 2008 prevented deeper collapse while households experienced foreclosures and long recovery. pandemic response protected many lives and incomes while school closures and service-sector shutdowns imposed costs unevenly.

The book therefore asks who gained authority, who lost protection, who paid first, and who benefited later.

That does not require every chapter to end in moral equivalence. Some actions were plainly more just than others. Abolishing slavery and enforcing civil rights were morally different from removal and racial exclusion. The point is not to flatten judgment.

It is to keep consequence attached to people.

## On quotations and scenes

The manuscript avoids invented dialogue, private thoughts, sensory description, and reconstructed quotations unsupported by the record.

Historical narrative becomes more vivid when a writer pretends to know what a room smelled like or what a person privately felt before a documented decision. That vividness is not worth falsifying the scene.

Where exact wording appears in the manuscript, publication editing should verify it directly against a primary source or reliable transcript. Famous quotations with uncertain provenance should be omitted or described as disputed rather than repeated because they are familiar.

The commonly repeated line attributed to Andrew Jackson after *Worcester v. Georgia*, for example, is not treated as a verified quotation in the removal chapter. The documented institutional fact—that the federal government did not stop the larger removal project—is strong enough without a theatrical sentence.

## Primary institutional sources

The working research trail privileges primary documents and institutional archives for dates, statutory language, official actions, court holdings, and government records. The chapter-level source ledger in `research/source-ledger.csv` is the machine-readable working list.

Major collections used in the first draft include:

- National Archives founding documents and milestone documents for the Declaration of Independence, Constitution, Louisiana Purchase Treaty, Indian Removal materials, Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, Emancipation Proclamation, Reconstruction amendments, Sherman Antitrust Act, Nineteenth Amendment, *Brown v. Board of Education*, and Civil Rights Act.
- U.S. Senate and House historical offices for congressional crises and institutional history including the Missouri Compromise, the 1876 election dispute, and Watergate.
- Library of Congress research guides and historical collections for the War of 1812, Reconstruction, Spanish-American War, Pearl Harbor, and other events with substantial primary-source holdings.
- U.S. Department of State Office of the Historian for Texas annexation, the Mexican-American War, the end of communist rule in Eastern Europe, German reunification, and the dissolution of the Soviet system.
- Federal Reserve History and Federal Reserve Board materials for the Federal Reserve Act, Great Depression, Volcker anti-inflation turn, financial crisis, and pandemic monetary response.
- National Commission on Terrorist Attacks Upon the United States for the September 11 attacks, intelligence failures, and institutional recommendations.
- Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission for the 2008 crisis and competing interpretations of its causes.
- Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and Bureau of Labor Statistics for the COVID-19 timeline and workplace changes during the pandemic.

## Secondary research still required before release

This manuscript is a full narrative draft, not a finished scholarly apparatus.

Before release, every chapter should receive a chapter-specific source audit built around serious secondary histories representing more than one interpretation where the causal argument is contested. The audit should be particularly deep for chapters where the event itself is a historiographical argument rather than a simple factual milestone.

Priority areas include:

- the relative causal weight of the Declaration versus Yorktown;
- the role of slavery, federalism, and party power in the Missouri crisis;
- Native political strategy and nation-specific experiences under removal;
- slavery's role in Texas independence and annexation;
- the political and military causation linking Texas annexation to war with Mexico;
- the relationship between the Civil War, emancipation, Black self-liberation, and constitutional abolition;
- the disputed simplicity of the “Compromise of 1877” narrative;
- early antitrust enforcement and the relationship between competition policy and labor;
- motives for American imperial expansion in 1898 and the Philippine-American War;
- the legislative and institutional history of the Federal Reserve;
- racial and organizational diversity inside the suffrage movement;
- competing monetary explanations of the Great Depression;
- the decision-making context around atomic use in 1945;
- the legal strategy leading to *Brown* and the political coalition behind the Civil Rights Act;
- Vietnam, Watergate, and the wider crisis of executive legitimacy;
- the distributional effects and international consequences of the Volcker disinflation;
- causal explanations for the Soviet collapse and the limits of claims that any one American president “won” the Cold War;
- the legal and intelligence history of post-9/11 counterterrorism;
- mortgage securitization, regulatory failure, and alternative explanations of the 2008 crisis;
- pandemic mortality, school effects, inflation, fiscal response, vaccination, and Long COVID using final or revised datasets rather than figures available during the emergency.

The purpose of that second pass is not to bury the narrative under citations. It is to make sure the narrative has survived contact with the strongest competing history.

## A note on the runners-up

Several chapter choices were close enough that readers may reasonably disagree.

**1776–1785:** Yorktown could replace the Declaration if the criterion is existence of the state rather than the durable political language of the state.

**1796–1805:** the election of 1800 could replace the Louisiana Purchase if peaceful partisan transfer is weighted more heavily than continental expansion.

**1816–1825:** the Monroe Doctrine could replace the Missouri Compromise if hemispheric strategy is treated as more consequential than the political geography of slavery.

**1896–1905:** *Plessy* could replace the Spanish-American War if domestic racial law is weighted more heavily than overseas empire.

**1916–1925:** U.S. entry into World War I could replace the Nineteenth Amendment if wartime state-building and global power are weighted more heavily than permanent expansion of the electorate.

**1936–1945:** the atomic bombings could replace Pearl Harbor if the nuclear age is treated as the dominant long-term transformation.

**1956–1965:** the Cuban Missile Crisis could replace the Civil Rights Act if existential risk is treated as more important than durable institutional change.

**1966–1975:** Apollo 11 or Vietnam could replace Watergate under technological or geopolitical criteria.

**1976–1985:** Reagan's election could replace Volcker under an electoral-realignment model of history.

**1986–1995:** the World Wide Web could replace the fall of the Berlin Wall under a long-run technological model.

**1996–2005:** China's WTO accession could replace September 11 under a global-economic model.

**2006–2015:** the iPhone could replace the financial crisis under a technology-and-behavior model.

**2016–2025:** this selection should be treated as explicitly provisional.

A different list is not necessarily a refutation of the book.

It may reveal a different theory of what makes history matter.

## Acknowledgment of incompleteness

Two hundred fifty years cannot be reduced to twenty-five chapters without loss.

This book omits events that deserve books of their own: the Haitian Revolution's effect on American expansion; the development of the cotton gin and domestic slave trade; the Erie Canal and railroad network; abolitionism; Seneca Falls; California gold; Homestead and transcontinental railroads; the reservation system; mass immigration; labor wars; electricity; public health; lynching and anti-lynching movements; World War I; the influenza pandemic of 1918; immigration restriction; the Harlem Renaissance; the Dust Bowl; Social Security; the Holocaust; the atomic bomb; suburbanization; the interstate highway system; television; the space program; immigration reform in 1965; Vietnam; Stonewall and the gay-rights movement; environmental law; Roe; deregulation; personal computing; the web; NAFTA; mass incarceration; China's rise; the smartphone; same-sex marriage; climate policy; January 6; *Dobbs*; and generative AI.

Exclusion from the twenty-five is not a claim of unimportance.

It is the cost of the form.

The hope is that the constraint creates something a comprehensive chronology does not: a visible chain of hinges showing how one institutional settlement becomes the starting condition for the next.

## Closing note

The United States has never had one permanent answer to what the federal government is for, who belongs to the political community, how markets should be governed, what rights states must respect, how much emergency power executives should possess, or how the country should use its strength abroad.

It has had 250 years of revisions.

That is the story this selection is trying to keep in view.
