# The Ten-Year Republic

*One Defining Event from Every Ten Years of America, 1776–2026*

Sven Hardy Benson

This is a work of narrative nonfiction. The events, dates, institutions, laws, court decisions, and public actions described here are historical. Scenes are built only from documented circumstances. Where the record does not support private thoughts, invented dialogue, or sensory detail, the manuscript does not supply them.

## Author’s note

A country does not turn 250 all at once.

It arrives there by surviving ten years, then another ten, then another. Most of those years are full of events that once seemed urgent and later became footnotes. A few become hinges. After them, the country is still recognizable, but it is no longer operating inside the same range of possibilities.

The Declaration of Independence is an obvious hinge. The Constitution is another. Pearl Harbor and September 11 belong to the category of days people recognized as history while they were still living through them. Other turning points are quieter. A line across a map in the Missouri Compromise. A law written against trusts. A banking system redesigned after a private financier had repeatedly been asked to rescue the public one. A Saturday night change in Federal Reserve operating procedure that sounds bloodless until you follow it into mortgage payments, factory towns, elections, and the end of an inflationary era.

This book is an argument about those hinges.

It divides the United States’ first 250 years into twenty-five consecutive ten-year windows beginning with 1776–1785 and ending with 2016–2025. That means these are not the calendar decades we normally use as mental filing cabinets. The 1790s are split. So are the 1960s. The structure is intentionally awkward in that way. The republic did not begin on January 1 of a year ending in zero, and its quarter-millennium does not arrive at the end of a tidy decade. Starting with 1776 forces the anniversary itself to set the clock.

For each window I choose one event that, in my judgment, changed the downstream possibilities of American life more than any rival event in that same span. “Most important” is not a scientific measurement. No instrument can compare the weight of a constitutional amendment with the weight of a war, a financial panic, an act of dispossession, a court ruling, a technological system, or a terrorist attack. There is no common unit.

So the standard here is causal reach.

Did the event change who could belong to the political community? Did it alter the powers of the federal government or the states? Did it move borders, reorder markets, change the country’s position in the world, or create institutions that later generations would take for granted? Did it close paths that had once been open? Did it open paths that had once been closed? Did later events become more likely because this one happened?

That last question matters because history is full of famous events that are consequences rather than causes. A dramatic day may be memorable without being structurally decisive. A statute may look dull on the page and still reorganize a century.

The choices also carry a second obligation: the book has to remember the people for whom a national “turning point” was a catastrophe. Expansion has victims. State capacity can protect and coerce. Financial stabilization can preserve an economy while distributing pain brutally. War can create political unity at a price counted in bodies. Civil-rights victories can be both transformative and incomplete. The United States has repeatedly widened the language of liberty while narrowing its application to particular people, then fought over the contradiction.

That is not a flaw in the story. It is much of the story.

The first chapter begins with a document that announced universal principles while leaving slavery intact and Indigenous sovereignty outside the new nation’s promised political equality. Later chapters return to those exclusions because they were not side issues. The Missouri Compromise, Indian Removal, the Mexican Cession, the Civil War, Reconstruction, the end of Reconstruction, Brown, and the Civil Rights Act are not separate morality plays. They are successive attempts to answer a question the founding did not settle: who is the republic for, and what may the republic do in order to become what it says it is?

The book is also deliberately suspicious of hindsight. The Constitution feels inevitable after more than two centuries under it. It was not. The Louisiana Purchase looks like a masterstroke when viewed on a modern map. Thomas Jefferson worried about its constitutional basis. The Union victory in the Civil War can look foreordained when summarized by industrial capacity; the people living through 1862 did not have the Appomattox ending available to them. Brown now sits in the canon of landmark decisions, but its enforcement was resisted for years. The fall of the Berlin Wall is remembered as the opening scene of a post-Cold War world whose later shape nobody in the crowd could yet know.

History becomes dishonest when uncertainty is removed from people who had to act without knowing what came next.

Every chapter therefore begins as close as possible to the hinge itself. Then it widens. What pressure built before the event? Which people and institutions made it possible? Who resisted it? What did contemporaries misunderstand? What changed immediately, and what only became visible later? What rival event from the same ten-year window could reasonably have been chosen instead?

The rival matters. If a selection cannot survive comparison, it does not deserve the slot.

In 1966–1975, for example, the moon landing is an obvious candidate. So are the Tet Offensive and the end of the Vietnam War. I choose Watergate because the tapes, the congressional investigation, the Supreme Court confrontation, and Richard Nixon’s resignation changed the operating assumptions of American political power and public trust for decades. Reasonable readers can disagree. The disagreement is part of the book’s purpose. A list that cannot be argued with is either trivial or pretending to an authority it does not possess.

In 1976–1985 I choose Paul Volcker’s anti-inflation turn rather than Ronald Reagan’s election. That is a less famous selection, and intentionally so. Reagan’s presidency mattered enormously. But the brutal interest-rate medicine that broke the inflationary psychology of the 1970s also helped create the economic landscape in which a new political order could consolidate. Monetary policy sounds technical until it decides whether a family can finance a house, whether a factory can carry inventory, whether a farmer can refinance land, and whether unemployment becomes the price of restoring confidence in money.

In 2016–2025 I choose COVID-19 rather than the 2016 election, January 6, the Dobbs decision, or the rapid public arrival of generative artificial intelligence. The pandemic altered mortality, schooling, work, fiscal policy, supply chains, public health, trust, urban life, and the relationship between household and workplace at the same time. Its consequences were distributed so broadly that even Americans who never became seriously ill lived inside institutions changed by it.

The epilogue stops in 2026 for a reason. This book is being written during the semiquincentennial itself. The temptation to identify the next defining event is irresistible and mostly foolish. Importance is easiest to exaggerate in the present tense. The honest anniversary question is not which headline will someday own the 2026–2035 chapter. It is what the previous twenty-five windows teach us about recognizing a hinge before its consequences have finished unfolding.

One pattern appears again and again: the most durable events do not merely change policy. They change the machinery that produces later policy.

The Constitution created a governing system. The Louisiana Purchase changed the scale on which that system would operate. The Fourteenth Amendment altered the relationship between citizenship and state power. The Federal Reserve Act created a new monetary institution. Brown changed constitutional doctrine. The Civil Rights Act created enforcement powers and national rules for public accommodations and employment. Watergate produced reforms and a new suspicion of executive secrecy. The financial crisis brought a fresh architecture of regulation and central-bank intervention. COVID normalized forms of emergency governance, remote work, rapid vaccine development, and fiscal response whose boundaries are still being negotiated.

Another pattern is darker. American capacity often expands through crisis before its moral boundaries are settled. The same government that can mobilize enormous resources can also remove Native peoples, intern citizens and residents, enforce racial hierarchy, wage controversial wars, or build surveillance systems. The question is never only whether the state is strong. It is strong for what, accountable to whom, and reversible by what mechanism?

A third pattern concerns scale. The country repeatedly becomes too large, too interconnected, or too complex for an older arrangement to keep working. The Articles of Confederation cannot manage the postwar union. Private banking rescues cannot substitute indefinitely for a central bank. Local and state civil-rights regimes cannot resolve nationally entrenched segregation. A financial system built around securitized global credit cannot be stabilized with the tools of an earlier banking age. A pathogen crosses state lines faster than fifty separate public-health systems can improvise common answers.

The republic keeps discovering that yesterday’s solution was built for yesterday’s scale.

And still, the country is not simply a machine upgrading itself. Every institutional solution rearranges power. Someone gains authority. Someone loses discretion. Someone pays first. Someone benefits later. Sometimes the people who bear the cost never receive the eventual benefit. National histories often hide that asymmetry inside verbs such as “expanded,” “modernized,” “stabilized,” and “reformed.” This book tries not to.

There is one final reason for choosing a single event from each window. Constraint forces judgment.

A comprehensive history can include everything and still avoid saying what mattered most. A ranked list can be glib. The form here sits between them. Twenty-five selections are enough to cover the full span and few enough that each exclusion hurts. That pain is useful. It reveals assumptions.

Why the Missouri Compromise rather than the Monroe Doctrine? Why the Sherman Antitrust Act rather than the closing of the frontier? Why the Federal Reserve Act rather than the Triangle Shirtwaist fire? Why Brown rather than the Korean War? Why Watergate rather than Apollo 11? Why the financial crisis rather than the iPhone?

The answers are the book.

At 250, the United States has accumulated too much history for a single clean story. It has also accumulated enough history to see recurring forms beneath the differences: bargains that postpone conflicts, institutions created after failure, rights promised before they are enforced, wars that redraw the meaning of citizenship, technologies that change power before law catches up, crises that reveal which parts of the system were load-bearing all along.

The anniversary invites celebration. It also invites accounting.

This book is an attempt at the second in a form compact enough to keep the whole republic in view: twenty-five windows, twenty-five hinges, and the stories of the people who stood near each door before anyone knew which way it would swing.
