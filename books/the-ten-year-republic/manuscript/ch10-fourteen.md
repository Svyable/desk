# 1866–1875: Fourteen

The Fourteenth Amendment begins by telling the country who belongs to it.

“All persons born or naturalized in the United States,” its first section declares, are citizens of the United States and of the state in which they reside. The sentence looks straightforward after more than a century and a half of repetition. In 1868 it was an answer to a constitutional catastrophe.

The original Constitution had never supplied a complete national definition of citizenship. Slavery had made the omission useful. Dred Scott had exploited it brutally, declaring that Black people descended from enslaved Africans were excluded from the political community contemplated by the Constitution in the manner necessary for federal citizenship. The Civil War destroyed slavery, but abolition by itself did not establish what freedom required states to recognize.

The Fourteenth Amendment is the most important American event of 1866–1875 because it rewrote the relationship among person, state, and nation. It constitutionalized birthright citizenship. It prohibited states from depriving any person of life, liberty, or property without due process of law. It prohibited states from denying any person equal protection of the laws. Those clauses became legal instruments far beyond the immediate Reconstruction crisis, reaching segregation, criminal procedure, voting disputes, incorporation of much of the Bill of Rights against the states, marriage, reproductive liberty, education, immigration, and countless other conflicts.

The Fifteenth Amendment is the strongest runner-up. Ratified in 1870, it prohibited federal and state governments from denying the right to vote on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude. That guarantee was fundamental and repeatedly violated. But the Fourteenth supplied the broader constitutional architecture through which national citizenship and state obligations would be litigated for generations.

The amendment emerged from a simple problem with no simple answer.

The Confederacy had lost. Its states had to return to ordinary participation in the Union. Millions of formerly enslaved people were free. Former Confederate leaders wanted political restoration. President Andrew Johnson, who assumed office after Lincoln's assassination, favored rapid readmission with limited federal protection for freedpeople. Southern state governments enacted Black Codes restricting the rights, movement, labor, and economic opportunities of Black people.

A military victory had ended slavery without ending the social order that had depended on it.

Congress confronted a choice. If former Confederate states regained representation quickly under political systems dominated by white elites while Black citizens lacked meaningful rights, the South could recover substantial power in the federal government. Abolition created an additional representational irony: under slavery, the three-fifths clause had partially counted enslaved people for House representation. Once slavery ended, Black southerners would count fully toward representation even if states prevented them from voting.

White southern political power could increase because enslaved people had been freed.

Reconstruction was therefore about democratic principle and partisan power at the same time.

Those motives are often separated because moral histories prefer pure actors. The Republicans who drove congressional Reconstruction included genuine advocates of Black civil and political rights, politicians worried about former Confederates returning to national power, and people whose positions mixed principle, strategy, racial prejudice, constitutional theory, and electoral calculation.

Politics rarely offers one motive per person.

The Civil Rights Act of 1866 was an early congressional answer. It declared people born in the United States and not subject to a foreign power, excluding certain categories under the language of the time, to be citizens and protected specified civil rights. Johnson vetoed it. Congress overrode him.

The confrontation raised a deeper question: could an ordinary statute protecting citizenship survive future political change or hostile courts?

A constitutional amendment could offer firmer ground.

Congress proposed the Fourteenth Amendment in June 1866. Ratification became a condition tied to the Reconstruction process for former Confederate states. The amendment was declared ratified in 1868.

Its first section contained several legal revolutions in compact form.

The Citizenship Clause repudiated the core racial citizenship holding of Dred Scott. Birth in the United States, subject to the jurisdictional qualification, became the constitutional baseline. Citizenship no longer depended on a state's willingness to recognize a person or on racial descent in the way the antebellum order had allowed.

The Privileges or Immunities Clause prohibited states from abridging the privileges or immunities of U.S. citizens. Its potential breadth was soon narrowed by the Supreme Court in the Slaughter-House Cases of 1873, which distinguished privileges of national citizenship from the broader range of civil rights critics hoped the clause might protect.

That judicial narrowing had enormous consequences.

The Due Process and Equal Protection Clauses would eventually carry much of the work later lawyers might have expected the Privileges or Immunities Clause to perform.

The amendment's remaining sections addressed representation, disqualification from office for certain people who had sworn oaths and then engaged in insurrection or rebellion, public debt, and congressional enforcement power. These provisions reflected a Reconstruction world in which the country was actively deciding how former rebels could return to power and which obligations of the defeated Confederacy the United States would refuse to recognize.

The amendment was not written as timeless abstraction.

It was a settlement after civil war.

That origin matters when modern arguments extract a clause from the Reconstruction circumstances that produced it. The framers of the amendment were dealing with Black Codes, racial violence, state resistance, citizenship, rebel political power, and the practical problem of whether freedom could exist if states retained broad authority to define the rights of Black residents.

The answer was to give national constitutional law a stronger claim over states.

The Civil War had already expanded federal power through military necessity. The Fourteenth Amendment made part of that national role constitutional in peacetime.

Before the war, much of the Bill of Rights was understood primarily as a restriction on the federal government. State governments could regulate speech, criminal procedure, religion, and other matters subject to their own constitutions in ways that would later raise federal constitutional questions. Over the twentieth century, the Supreme Court used the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause to apply, or “incorporate,” most protections in the Bill of Rights against the states.

That later doctrinal development made the amendment a bridge between two constitutional eras.

The First Amendment's speech protections became enforceable against states through the Fourteenth. So did protections involving searches, counsel, self-incrimination, criminal procedure, and other rights. The modern American expectation that constitutional rights constrain local police, state prosecutors, school boards, and state legislatures owes much of its legal structure to the amendment ratified during Reconstruction.

The authors of 1868 did not prewrite every twentieth-century case.

They changed the constitutional relationship that made those cases possible.

Equal protection would have an equally long afterlife.

The clause was born in a struggle over race and the treatment of freedpeople. Yet its language says “any person,” not only citizens and not only Black Americans. Courts later applied equal-protection principles across classifications involving race, sex, legitimacy, marriage, and other legal distinctions. Some applications fit comfortably inside broad principles of equality. Others remain contested because the historical understanding of 1868 does not map neatly onto modern disputes.

A constitutional clause can be both historically situated and linguistically larger than its first application.

The amendment's durability comes partly from that tension.

At the time, however, constitutional text was only one part of Reconstruction.

Congress passed Reconstruction Acts placing former Confederate states under military administration and requiring new constitutional arrangements. Black men voted in large numbers and held public office at local, state, and federal levels. State governments created public-school systems, expanded social services, revised constitutions, and attempted to build biracial political coalitions.

This period was later subjected to generations of racist mythology depicting Reconstruction governments as uniquely corrupt and Black political participation as inherently incompetent. Corruption existed, as it did throughout nineteenth-century American politics, but the older narrative served a political purpose: it justified the restoration of white supremacy by treating democratic participation itself as a failure.

Reconstruction was more radical and more fragile than that story allows.

Freedpeople organized politically with extraordinary speed. Churches, mutual aid societies, conventions, Union League organizations, schools, newspapers, and family networks became political infrastructure. Black voters did not wait passively for rights to be delivered by Congress. They demanded land, education, safety, wages, legal recognition of families separated by slavery, access to courts, and political representation.

Freedom was being defined from below while the Constitution was being amended from above.

The conflict over land revealed the limits of the transformation.

Many freedpeople understood that meaningful independence required access to land. Wartime experiments in redistributing abandoned or confiscated property created expectations that were later reversed. Johnson restored substantial land to pardoned former Confederates. Sharecropping and other labor arrangements emerged in a countryside where freed families sought autonomy but often lacked capital and land.

Legal freedom did not erase economic dependency.

The Fourteenth Amendment prohibited certain state abuses. It did not redistribute wealth.

That boundary would become characteristic of American civil-rights law: the Constitution could forbid discrimination or state deprivation while leaving enormous inequalities in property and economic power intact.

White resistance was violent.

The Ku Klux Klan and other organizations attacked Black voters, officeholders, teachers, ministers, Republican organizers, and white allies. Violence was not random criminality occurring alongside politics. It was politics by other means, designed to change who could vote, assemble, work, own property, testify, teach, or hold office.

Congress responded with Enforcement Acts in the early 1870s, including legislation empowering federal action against conspiracies that interfered with constitutional rights. President Ulysses S. Grant's administration used federal prosecutions and military power against the Klan in some areas with meaningful short-term success.

This is important because Reconstruction did not fail simply because federal enforcement was impossible.

At moments, enforcement worked.

The later collapse was political.

That distinction matters for understanding rights. A constitutional guarantee can be real while protection remains contingent on institutions willing to enforce it. The amendment did not disappear when federal commitment receded. Its practical reach did.

The Supreme Court also narrowed Reconstruction protections in a series of decisions. The Slaughter-House Cases limited the Privileges or Immunities Clause. United States v. Cruikshank in 1876, just beyond this chapter's window, constrained federal prosecution after the Colfax Massacre and treated important rights as not directly created by the federal Constitution in ways that weakened national protection against private racial violence.

The Court was not merely interpreting text in a vacuum. It was participating in a broader retreat from Reconstruction.

The Fifteenth Amendment deserves its runner-up status because voting was the mechanism that could make other rights durable.

Ratified in 1870, it prohibited denial or abridgment of voting rights on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude. Black male political participation expanded dramatically during Reconstruction. The amendment created a national constitutional barrier to explicitly racial voting exclusions.

White southern governments later learned to evade it.

Poll taxes, literacy tests, understanding clauses, complex registration rules, white primaries, fraud, economic retaliation, and violence could suppress Black voting without writing “Black citizens may not vote” into law. Grandfather clauses sometimes protected white voters from restrictions while excluding Black voters whose grandfathers had been enslaved and legally disenfranchised.

The history demonstrates a basic problem of law: rules aimed at stated motives can be defeated by proxies.

The Fifteenth Amendment remained available for later litigation and federal enforcement, but for much of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, Black southern political participation was crushed despite its text.

Why, then, call the Fourteenth Amendment the defining event if its promises were so routinely violated?

Because historical importance is not the same as immediate success.

A law can fail in enforcement and still change the terrain on which later fights occur. The Fourteenth Amendment placed citizenship, due process, and equal protection into the Constitution. Opponents could evade, narrow, or ignore those commitments. They could not erase them without another constitutional amendment.

That permanence gave later movements something Reconstruction itself could not guarantee: a legal claim waiting for political conditions to make it usable again.

Brown v. Board of Education in 1954 would rely on the Fourteenth Amendment to reject state-mandated school segregation. Civil-rights lawyers spent years building doctrine under its Equal Protection Clause. The incorporation of the Bill of Rights would transform criminal procedure and civil liberties. Loving v. Virginia would use equal protection and due process to strike down bans on interracial marriage. Later cases would extend Fourteenth Amendment doctrines into disputes that its ratifiers could not have anticipated in detail.

The amendment became constitutional capital accumulated by one generation and spent by others.

That does not mean every later interpretation was inevitable or uncontested. Legal scholars and judges disagree deeply over original meaning, judicial methodology, substantive due process, the proper scope of equal protection, and the amendment's enforcement power. The text created a field of argument, not a single automatic answer.

But a field of argument can be a major institutional achievement.

The amendment also changed the meaning of federalism.

Before the Civil War, states were often invoked as shields against federal power. After Reconstruction, federalism acquired a second moral dimension: state autonomy could itself become a threat to individual rights. The national government might be required not merely to refrain from oppression but to protect citizens against oppressive state law.

American politics has never settled the tension.

Civil-rights enforcement, voting rules, education, policing, abortion, marriage, firearms, immigration, and election administration repeatedly return to the question of how much autonomy states possess when individual constitutional rights are claimed against them.

The Fourteenth Amendment sits inside nearly all of those fights.

It also sits inside the problem of insurrection.

Section Three disqualified certain people who had taken an oath to support the Constitution and then engaged in insurrection or rebellion from holding specified offices unless Congress removed the disability. Written for former Confederates, the provision returned to public attention more than 150 years later in litigation after the January 6, 2021 attack on the U.S. Capitol.

A clause designed for the aftermath of the Civil War became relevant in a radically different political crisis because constitutional text can outlive the circumstances that give birth to it.

That afterlife is almost impossible for authors to predict.

The most important features of a constitutional amendment may be the uses its ratifiers did not need yet.

By 1875, Reconstruction still possessed formal federal support, but the political coalition behind it was weakening. The Panic of 1873 caused a severe economic depression that shifted attention and damaged Republicans. Northern voters grew weary of southern intervention. White Democratic “Redeemer” governments regained control across much of the South through elections, intimidation, violence, and coalition changes.

The constitutional revolution remained on paper while its enforcement environment deteriorated.

That divergence sets up the next chapter.

The disputed presidential election of 1876 would produce an extraordinary national crisis. Electoral votes from several states were contested. Congress created a commission. Republican Rutherford B. Hayes emerged with the presidency. In the political settlement surrounding the transition, federal troops were withdrawn from the remaining southern statehouses in 1877.

Reconstruction did not end in one clean bargain exactly as later textbook shorthand often suggests. The retreat was already underway, and historians dispute the simplicity of the so-called Compromise of 1877.

But the result is unmistakable.

Federal commitment to sustaining biracial Republican governments in the South collapsed. White supremacist rule consolidated. Black voting and civil rights were attacked for generations.

The Fourteenth Amendment survived the retreat.

That was its peculiar power and its tragedy.

The country could abandon enforcement without deleting the promise.

The promise would wait.
