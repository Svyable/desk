# 2006–2015: The Week Credit Stopped

The most frightening thing about Lehman Brothers was not that one investment bank failed.

It was that nobody knew which balance sheet would fail next.

On September 15, 2008, Lehman filed for bankruptcy after the federal government declined to provide the kind of support that had enabled an earlier rescue of Bear Stearns. Lehman's collapse did not cause the entire financial crisis, and the crisis did not begin that morning. Housing prices had already fallen. mortgage delinquencies were rising. structured securities had lost value. several institutions had failed or been rescued. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac had been placed into government conservatorship days earlier.

But Lehman changed the market's belief about the rules.

If a major investment bank could fail without an arranged rescue, every creditor had to ask which other institution might be allowed to fail. If no one knew, lending to nearly everyone became more dangerous.

Trust disappeared faster than assets could be valued.

The financial crisis of 2008 is the most important American event of 2006–2015 because it changed central banking, financial regulation, public debt, housing, household wealth, politics, inequality, and an entire generation's understanding of economic security. It transformed the Federal Reserve from a central bank operating mainly through interest rates and traditional lending into an institution willing to create extraordinary facilities, buy large quantities of securities, and act as the ultimate liquidity provider to markets far beyond conventional banks.

The runner-up is the introduction of the iPhone in 2007.

That device and the smartphone ecosystem it helped create reorganized communication, media, commerce, navigation, photography, work, dating, entertainment, politics, advertising, software distribution, and attention. The smartphone may prove the more pervasive social technology over a century.

The financial crisis takes the slot because it altered the economic and political environment in which the smartphone revolution unfolded and because its consequences included institutional changes designed specifically to prevent another depression.

The crisis began in a housing boom that appeared safer than earlier speculative episodes because risk had been engineered into sophisticated forms.

For years, American home prices rose. low interest rates, abundant global capital, financial innovation, relaxed underwriting, securitization, and widespread belief in housing as a stable appreciating asset encouraged more borrowing and more lending.

Mortgage credit expanded to borrowers who previously might not have qualified under traditional standards. Some loans featured low introductory rates that later reset higher. others required little documentation of income or assets. brokers and lenders could earn fees by originating loans and then selling them rather than holding the credit risk to maturity.

The old relationship between lender and borrower changed.

A local bank that keeps a thirty-year mortgage has a strong reason to care whether the borrower can repay in year seven. An originator that sells the loan quickly may care more about whether the loan meets the requirements of the next buyer.

Securitization was supposed to distribute risk.

Mortgages were pooled into mortgage-backed securities. Cash flows were divided into tranches with different priorities. More complex instruments repackaged exposures. Credit-rating agencies assigned high ratings to senior pieces based on models and assumptions about default correlation and housing prices.

Investors around the world bought American housing risk without needing to know individual borrowers.

Distance made the market larger.

It also made responsibility thinner.

Mortgage brokers could blame lenders. lenders could blame investors. investors could blame rating agencies. rating agencies could point to models and supplied data. banks could point to hedges. regulators could point to jurisdictional boundaries.

The system contained many sophisticated participants and no participant with a complete view of the system.

Leverage amplified the danger.

Investment banks financed large portfolios with substantial borrowed money, often relying on short-term funding markets such as repurchase agreements. If asset values fell modestly, equity could be wiped out quickly. If short-term lenders refused to roll over funding, an institution could fail even if some assets might eventually recover value.

Liquidity risk and solvency risk began feeding each other.

The shadow banking system—institutions and markets performing bank-like credit intermediation without the same regulatory structure or deposit insurance as traditional banks—had grown enormously.

The system was designed to function in normal markets.

Crisis is when normal markets stop being a useful assumption.

Housing prices peaked in many areas around 2006 and began to decline. Borrowers with weak equity or resetting payments defaulted. Securities backed by mortgages became harder to value. If investors did not know the quality of the underlying loans, they could not easily distinguish a temporarily mispriced asset from a permanently impaired one.

Uncertainty widened bid-ask spreads and reduced trading.

The first major failures appeared in 2007.

Subprime mortgage lenders collapsed. hedge funds exposed to mortgage securities failed. interbank funding stress increased. The Federal Reserve cut interest rates and created new lending facilities.

In March 2008, Bear Stearns faced a run by short-term creditors. The Fed supported a transaction in which JPMorgan Chase acquired the firm, with the central bank facilitating financing against a portfolio of Bear assets.

The rescue created expectations.

Market participants could reasonably infer that another systemically important investment bank might also receive support.

That inference shaped Lehman.

During the summer, the government confronted growing stress at Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, the government-sponsored enterprises central to U.S. mortgage finance. In September, regulators placed both into conservatorship.

Lehman was next.

Federal Reserve and Treasury officials encouraged major financial institutions to find a private solution. Potential buyers faced concerns about Lehman's losses and the availability of government backing. No workable rescue emerged.

Lehman filed for bankruptcy.

The failure immediately disrupted markets because Lehman was not an isolated business. It was a counterparty in derivatives, a borrower and lender in short-term funding, a broker for hedge funds, a participant in securities markets, and an employer and creditor connected to thousands of transactions.

Its bankruptcy forced institutions to ask what claims were worth and when they would be paid.

The accounting uncertainty became economic paralysis.

Then the Reserve Primary Fund, a money-market mutual fund, “broke the buck” after losses on Lehman debt caused its net asset value to fall below one dollar per share.

Money-market funds had been treated by many investors as cash-like instruments. The break triggered withdrawals across the sector.

A run had jumped from investment banks into a core cash-management system used by corporations and households.

The government responded with emergency guarantees.

American International Group, AIG, faced its own collapse because of enormous exposures including credit-default swaps written on mortgage-related securities. The company was not a bank, but its counterparties were woven through the global financial system.

The Federal Reserve provided extraordinary support in exchange for substantial government control and collateral arrangements.

The contrast with Lehman was politically explosive.

Why save AIG after letting Lehman fail?

The answer was systemic risk, but systemic risk is easier to define after a system begins collapsing than before. Officials feared AIG's failure would force immediate losses on counterparties and produce cascading instability.

The rescue also meant that public money and central-bank credit indirectly protected institutions that had traded with AIG.

The public saw executives and financiers rescued while households were losing jobs and homes.

That perception would define the politics of the crisis.

Credit markets froze in ways ordinary people could not see directly.

Commercial paper—short-term debt companies use to finance payroll, inventory, and working capital—became harder to issue. Banks hoarded liquidity. interbank lending rates reflected fear. securitization markets shut. companies drew down credit lines because they worried future funding might disappear.

A financial crisis becomes an economic crisis when firms unable to finance ordinary operations cut spending and employment.

The week credit stopped became months of layoffs.

Treasury Secretary Henry Paulson and Federal Reserve Chairman Ben Bernanke went to Congress seeking authority for a massive rescue program. The initial proposal focused on purchasing troubled assets from financial institutions.

Congress rejected the first version on September 29, 2008.

The stock market fell sharply.

Political resistance reflected anger at the idea of bailing out Wall Street and distrust of granting broad discretion to the executive branch. Members faced constituents who wanted failing banks punished, not rescued.

The problem was that punishment could destroy the credit system those constituents depended on.

Congress passed a revised package days later, creating the Troubled Asset Relief Program under the Emergency Economic Stabilization Act.

The program ultimately used much of its authority to inject capital directly into banks rather than simply purchase toxic mortgage assets.

That shift illustrated crisis governance in real time: lawmakers authorize one concept, administrators discover another tool is more effective, and policy evolves before public understanding catches up.

The Federal Reserve expanded even more aggressively.

It cut its policy interest rate to near zero. It created or enlarged facilities supporting commercial paper, money-market funds, primary dealers, asset-backed securities, and other markets. It established currency swap lines with foreign central banks to provide dollar liquidity internationally.

The Fed had become the central bank not only of American banks but, in crisis, of the dollar-based global financial system.

This was the institutional descendant of 1913 at a scale the creators of the Federal Reserve could never have imagined.

Once short-term rates reached near zero, the Fed began purchasing large quantities of longer-term securities, including agency mortgage-backed securities and Treasury securities, through policies known as quantitative easing.

The mechanism was intended to lower longer-term interest rates, support asset prices, improve market functioning, and stimulate borrowing and investment.

The policy also fueled political arguments over inequality.

Asset purchases tend to support financial asset prices. households owning stocks and homes can benefit. households without assets may receive benefits indirectly through stronger employment and lower borrowing costs, but the distribution is uneven and difficult to measure politically.

A central bank acting to prevent depression can look like a machine for making asset owners whole.

Both perceptions can contain truth.

The real economy contracted sharply.

Employers eliminated millions of jobs. unemployment eventually reached ten percent nationally. construction collapsed. auto sales fell. state and local governments lost tax revenue. graduates entered a weak labor market. retirement accounts shrank.

The housing crisis was especially destructive because homes were the primary source of wealth for many middle-class and working-class households.

Foreclosures surged.

Neighborhoods with concentrated defaults suffered vacant properties, declining tax bases, crime risks, and falling nearby values. Black and Latino households were hit particularly hard, in part because many had entered the housing boom with lower inherited wealth and were disproportionately targeted for higher-cost mortgage products even at similar income levels in some markets.

The crisis widened racial wealth gaps.

Housing had long been one of America's main wealth-building systems.

That system had not been equally accessible. New Deal mortgage institutions and postwar suburban development had disproportionately benefited white households through discriminatory underwriting, restrictive covenants, local zoning, and segregation. By 2008, Black and Latino families often held a larger share of wealth in housing and had less diversified financial assets.

When housing collapsed, historical inequality amplified the loss.

The government responded with mortgage-modification programs and housing support, but relief was widely criticized as too slow, too complex, or too protective of financial institutions relative to borrowers.

A bank can be recapitalized with one transaction.

Millions of mortgages require millions of individual decisions.

Administrative scale shaped fairness.

The Obama administration entered office in January 2009 amid rapid economic contraction. Congress passed the American Recovery and Reinvestment Act, combining tax cuts, aid to state and local governments, infrastructure spending, unemployment support, energy investment, and other measures.

Supporters argued that fiscal stimulus was necessary because private demand had collapsed and monetary policy was constrained by near-zero rates. Critics argued the package was wasteful, too large, too small in the wrong places, or a dangerous expansion of government depending on political perspective.

The economy returned to growth in 2009, but the recovery in employment and household balance sheets was slow.

That slowness had political consequences.

The public watched financial firms return to profitability and bonuses while unemployment remained high. The crisis appeared to confirm that sophisticated elites had privatized gains during the boom and socialized losses during the bust.

The Tea Party movement rose partly in reaction to bailouts, stimulus, deficits, and the Obama administration's broader agenda. Occupy Wall Street emerged in 2011 around inequality, finance, debt, and the concentration of wealth.

The same crisis fueled populism on both right and left.

Distrust of institutions deepened.

Congress responded with the Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010. The law created a broad new regulatory framework, including the Financial Stability Oversight Council, enhanced supervision of systemically important firms, derivatives reforms, living-will requirements, resolution mechanisms, and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau.

The aim was not to prevent every bank failure.

It was to prevent the failure of a large institution from forcing the government to choose between uncontrolled collapse and improvised rescue.

“Too big to fail” had become a design problem.

Banks were required to hold more capital and liquidity under domestic and international reforms. stress tests became a regular supervisory tool. derivatives moved toward greater clearing and reporting. regulators gained new information about systemic exposures.

The system became more resilient in important respects.

It also became more complex.

Regulation often does that. Rules responding to complex finance become complex themselves, increasing compliance costs and creating opportunities for firms with large legal and technical staffs to navigate the system more effectively than smaller competitors.

Safety and concentration can reinforce each other.

The crisis also changed the intellectual climate.

Before 2008, many policymakers and economists placed considerable confidence in market discipline, financial innovation, inflation-targeting monetary policy, and the ability of sophisticated firms to manage their own risk. The crisis did not erase those ideas, but it weakened the assumption that individually rational institutions would collectively produce a stable system.

Systemic risk became a mainstream concept.

The system could be fragile even if each participant believed it had hedged.

The iPhone, the runner-up, was released in June 2007, almost at the top of the housing boom.

Apple combined a touchscreen interface, mobile internet access, phone, camera, music, and computing platform in a device whose later App Store ecosystem allowed third-party developers to turn the smartphone into thousands of specialized tools.

The smartphone changed how people interacted with the financial crisis itself.

News traveled through mobile devices. workers searched for jobs online. social media organized political movements. banking moved onto phones. real-estate listings became portable. gig-work platforms later turned location-aware smartphones into labor-market infrastructure.

The device became the remote control for modern life.

Why doesn't it take the decade?

Because the financial crisis changed the price of risk and the authority of government at the same time. It produced laws, agencies, central-bank tools, political coalitions, debt trajectories, housing losses, and a generation marked by weak early-career earnings.

The smartphone changed behavior profoundly.

The crisis changed constraints.

The two transformations eventually merged. Technology firms grew during the low-interest-rate environment that followed the crisis. Venture capital and public markets rewarded long-duration growth. cheap capital supported expansion in software, cloud computing, social media, ride-hailing, e-commerce, and other sectors.

Central-bank policy helped create the financial climate of the platform economy even though it was not designed for that purpose.

This is how one decade's crisis becomes another decade's business model.

By 2015, the economy had largely recovered in aggregate measures. unemployment had fallen substantially. banks were better capitalized. housing prices had recovered in many areas. the Fed was preparing to move interest rates gradually away from the emergency floor.

Yet the political scars remained.

Many households had never regained lost wealth. younger adults delayed homeownership and family formation. regions damaged by foreclosure and unemployment carried distrust forward. populist anger toward Wall Street and Washington did not disappear when GDP recovered.

Economic statistics can recover before legitimacy does.

The crisis had also taught policymakers a powerful lesson: in a systemic emergency, act quickly and at enormous scale.

That lesson would be remembered in 2020.

When a novel coronavirus began spreading through the United States, Congress, the Federal Reserve, Treasury, state governments, public-health agencies, hospitals, schools, and businesses faced a crisis unlike 2008 in origin but similar in one respect: waiting for certainty could allow cascading failure.

This time policymakers moved faster.

The economy was deliberately shut down in large parts to slow disease transmission. Congress authorized trillions of dollars in emergency support. The Fed reopened and expanded crisis facilities almost immediately.

The lesson of Lehman was clear.

Do not let liquidity failure turn the first shock into a second disaster.

The problem was that money could not make a virus stop moving.
