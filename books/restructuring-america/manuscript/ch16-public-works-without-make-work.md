# Chapter 16: Public Works Without Make-Work

The most dangerous argument for public works in the age of AI is also the most intuitive.

If machines take jobs, government should create jobs.

The sentence has historical weight. During the Great Depression, mass unemployment was itself an emergency. Public employment restored income, dignity, and demand while leaving behind useful assets. The Works Progress Administration was not an abstract thought experiment about post-work society. It was a response to millions of people who needed work in a collapsed economy.

The AI transition may create moments when direct public employment is useful again.

But making employment the permanent purpose of infrastructure would invert the relationship between technology and prosperity.

A society should not preserve expensive methods merely because they consume labor.

If one crew can safely replace twice as much water pipe using better equipment, that is progress. If automated surveying reduces a week of field work to a day, that is progress. If AI eliminates months of clerical effort in project development, that is progress.

The saved labor is not a problem to be recreated.

The political problem is who receives the value of the saving.

## The hole in the ground test

Economists sometimes use absurd examples to clarify the difference between activity and value.

Hire people to dig holes and fill them back in. Employment rises. Output statistics may even record some spending. Nothing useful remains.

Real make-work is rarely that obvious.

It appears as a process that survives because eliminating it would threaten a budget. A form no one reads. A staffing rule disconnected from workload. A project selected because money has to be spent before a deadline. A construction requirement preserved after technology changes its purpose.

Public works are especially vulnerable because the spending is large and the assets are politically visible.

An AI-era infrastructure program needs a simple discipline: every project should be defensible even if no jobs were created.

Would society still want the bridge if robots built most of it?

Would it still want the water main if installation required half the crew?

Would it still want the park if maintenance became highly automated?

If the answer is yes, the project has a public purpose independent of employment.

Then labor policy can address how the work is organized and how transition costs are shared.

This separation protects both sides. Infrastructure does not become a warehouse for obsolete tasks. Workers do not have to depend on waste for economic security.

## A task is not a worker

Automation debates become confused when a task, a job, and a person are treated as the same thing.

A job is a bundle of tasks held together by an organization. Inspect. Drive. Diagnose. Document. Schedule. Call. Approve. Repair. Explain. Lift. Decide.

Technology can remove one part of that bundle while making another part more valuable.

An inspector may spend less time writing routine reports and more time examining ambiguous conditions. A project manager may spend less time reconciling documents and more time negotiating scope. A crew may use automated equipment for repetitive or dangerous work while retaining judgment about site conditions and safety.

Sometimes the bundle shrinks enough that fewer workers are required.

That possibility should not be hidden behind comforting language about augmentation.

But policy should still distinguish preserving a person from preserving every task that person once performed.

The person has claims on a decent transition because livelihoods, skills, families, and communities do not adjust at machine speed.

The obsolete task does not have a moral claim to survive.

This distinction creates more room for humane productivity policy. Redeployment, training, retirement, attrition, shorter hours, or a different job can absorb some change before displacement where circumstances allow. Where they do not, income support and transition institutions should carry the burden openly.

Economic security should attach to people.

Inefficiency should not have to impersonate social insurance.

## The utilization trap

Public organizations can mistake keeping people busy for producing value.

A crew at 100 percent utilization may sound efficient.

It can also mean there is no capacity for emergencies, training, preventive work, or variability. A permit reviewer with a full queue every hour has no room for the unusual case. A maintenance department scheduled down to the minute becomes brittle when equipment fails unexpectedly.

AI systems often optimize utilization because idle time is easy to see.

Slack is harder to value.

Useful slack is not make-work.

A spare crew that can respond to a failure, an apprenticeship hour that builds future skill, or a maintenance window reserved for prevention may look idle under the wrong metric while increasing system reliability.

Public works should measure throughput, reliability, quality, and backlog instead.

A crew that finishes more useful work with 85 percent planned utilization may outperform a crew that appears fully occupied while constantly rescheduling.

The objective should not be to maximize paid activity.

It should be to maximize useful service while preserving enough capacity for the system to remain reliable.

## Measure service, not spending

Public programs often treat expenditure as success because expenditure is easy to count.

Money obligated. Grants awarded. Contracts signed. Jobs announced.

These metrics reveal activity, not outcome.

A serious public-works program should measure service.

Miles of water main replaced before failure. Megawatts of reliable grid capacity added. Housing units permitted and completed. Travel time reduced. Schools brought within temperature and air-quality standards. Lead service lines removed. Acres of high-risk vegetation treated. Transit reliability improved. Bridge restrictions eliminated. Construction time shortened.

The metrics will differ by asset, and they can be gamed if chosen poorly.

That is still better than counting dollars as though inflation were achievement.

AI makes outcome measurement more practical because agencies can integrate operational data with capital programs. A road project can be evaluated against actual safety and travel outcomes after opening. A building retrofit can be compared with measured energy performance. A maintenance program can track failure rates.

This creates feedback.

Projects that work can become templates. Projects that underperform should teach the next generation of procurement.

Public works become a learning portfolio rather than a list of announcements.

The difficulty is political. A project may be beloved locally even if a formal metric is weak. Some benefits are hard to quantify: beauty, civic pride, access, neighborhood cohesion. A narrow benefit-cost model can miss these.

Measurement should discipline judgment, not replace it.

The standard is not "everything must have one score."

It is "money should buy an outcome we can name."

## Output floors are better than input promises

A program can protect itself from make-work by defining minimum service outcomes before funding begins.

A housing program might require completed units within a cost range adjusted for local conditions. A broadband program might require actual service quality. A grid program might require usable capacity, not only construction spending. A maintenance program might require backlog reduction.

The exact thresholds will be imperfect.

The discipline matters.

Input promises are easy to satisfy by spending more. Output floors force agencies to ask whether the production system is improving.

AI can help compare projects and detect when costs drift without corresponding service gains.

The floor should not become a reason to abandon difficult communities where unit costs are naturally higher. Remote water systems, disaster-prone regions, and legacy urban infrastructure can be expensive for reasons unrelated to incompetence.

Context belongs in the benchmark.

But a program should know when one project costs twice as much as comparable work and ask why.

## The productivity bargain

Imagine a decade in which AI and automation raise construction productivity significantly.

A fixed infrastructure budget can then buy more.

There are several places the gain can land.

Taxpayers can receive some through lower fiscal cost. Infrastructure users can receive some through more service or lower charges. Workers can receive some through safer work, higher compensation, shorter hours, or supported transitions. Contractors and suppliers need enough return to justify real investment in productivity. Backlogs can absorb capacity that used to be consumed by routine administration or slower production.

The important thing is to make the division visible.

Productivity politics becomes poisonous when workers see automation as a one-way transfer: technology improves, owners save money, employment becomes less secure, and the public sees no obvious improvement in daily life.

A public-works boom can create a different bargain.

If an automated system allows a road program to resurface more lane-miles with the same budget, citizens can see the gain. If workers receive safer equipment and training to operate it, they can see the gain. If contractors earn returns for investing in productivity, they can see the gain.

This is politically healthier than making productivity synonymous with layoffs.

Public procurement can support the bargain by rewarding verified performance improvements rather than prescribing labor inputs. Labor agreements can support training and transition. Capital plans can absorb saved capacity into backlogs.

The result is not zero conflict.

Distribution is conflict.

The goal is to make the productivity dividend large enough and legible enough that multiple parties have a reason to support it.

## Give the savings somewhere to land

Public budgets often contain an anti-productivity incentive.

A department finds a way to deliver a service more cheaply. The next budget reduces its appropriation by the amount saved.

Managers learn quickly.

Efficiency becomes self-punishment.

The opposite incentive can be bad too. If an agency keeps every dollar it saves forever, "savings" can become a justification for organizational growth detached from service.

A credible productivity system therefore needs separate ledgers.

First, did unit cost or processing time actually improve without degrading quality, safety, or access?

Second, what part of the gain should become additional service or backlog reduction?

Third, what transition costs were imposed on workers and contractors that need to adapt?

Fourth, what portion can return to the general budget or ratepayer?

The answer will differ by system and over time. The discipline is to prevent one automatic rule from swallowing every gain.

AI adoption makes the issue immediate.

If a permitting office automates clerical work and cuts review time, the entire gain should not necessarily be removed before the backlog is cleared. If a maintenance shop reduces diagnostic time, some capacity can move toward preventive work. Once service is healthy, later savings may reasonably reduce cost.

A productivity bargain needs an institutional place for the dividend to land.

Otherwise employees rationally fear every improvement will be used only to shrink them, while taxpayers rationally fear every promised efficiency will become a permanently larger budget.

Both fears can be true under bad rules.

Good rules make the trade explicit.

## Do not subsidize yesterday's method

Public policy often freezes technology through eligibility rules.

A grant program defines reimbursable costs according to old project categories. A labor rule assumes one staffing pattern. A specification requires a familiar material even when a better alternative exists. A procurement process penalizes contractors who propose a different sequence.

Some rigidity is necessary. Taxpayers should know what they are buying. Safety standards matter. Proven methods deserve trust.

But the AI age will punish programs that subsidize inputs rather than outcomes.

Suppose robotic inspection can reduce the need for dangerous manual access on a bridge. A funding program should not require the old inspection method to preserve reimbursable labor hours. Suppose prefabrication shortens a school project. Prevailing-wage protections can apply without demanding that the work occur onsite if the policy is written thoughtfully.

The objective is good jobs, not obsolete choreography.

This distinction will be politically difficult because methods become attached to constituencies.

Every mature technology ecosystem develops organizations that benefit from current rules. Reform can sound like an attack on workers when it is actually a change in production method.

The answer is transition support, not technological veto.

Workers affected by a method change should have training pathways and income protection. Contractors should have time to adapt equipment. Standards should change based on evidence rather than fashion.

A productive public sector must be allowed to learn.

## Automation incentives should reward actual adoption risk

New technology often has a first-mover problem.

A contractor bears the cost of buying equipment, training workers, integrating software, and learning a method. If the public owner captures all savings immediately through lower bids, the firm may have little reason to make the investment.

Procurement can respond without guaranteeing monopoly returns.

Pilot allowances. Shared savings. Performance bonuses. Multiyear contracts. Equipment grants tied to open competition. Public test facilities. Standard specifications that recognize validated methods.

The principle is to let innovators capture enough of the productivity gain to justify taking the risk.

Then competition should spread the benefit over time.

AI can help measure whether the claimed gain is real.

A contractor should not receive a productivity bonus because it bought a robot for a press release. The project should show better output, safety, quality, or cost.

Innovation policy needs a meter.

## Countercyclical capacity without permanent inefficiency

There is still a strong macroeconomic case for public works during downturns.

Construction is cyclical. Private development can collapse during recessions, releasing workers and equipment just as public borrowing costs may fall. Governments can accelerate already-planned projects to support demand.

The phrase *already planned* matters.

The great weakness of "shovel-ready" stimulus is that truly valuable infrastructure often takes years to prepare. By the time design and review are complete, the recession may be over.

A better system maintains a shelf of advanced projects in normal times.

Design work proceeds. Environmental questions are resolved. Right-of-way needs are understood. Procurement packages are prepared. If the economy weakens, funding can accelerate construction without inventing projects in a panic.

AI can reduce the cost of maintaining this pipeline by updating estimates, designs, and regulatory requirements as conditions change.

This is countercyclical state capacity rather than make-work.

The projects are worth building anyway. Their timing becomes a macroeconomic tool.

That is a more disciplined heir to the New Deal employment logic.

## A jobs guarantee is a different instrument

Some proposals go further than countercyclical public works and argue for a standing public job available to anyone who wants one.

That idea should be evaluated on its own terms.

A jobs guarantee could provide income, bargaining power, community service, and a floor under employment. It also raises hard questions about management, useful project pipelines, wage setting, geography, disability, transition into private work, and the administrative capacity required to supervise large numbers of workers.

Public infrastructure should not be forced to settle the argument.

If society wants an employment guarantee, design one explicitly.

Do not disguise it as a requirement that every bridge consume a certain amount of labor.

The bridge should be built by the best safe method that satisfies public standards.

Employment policy should serve workers directly.

Separating the instruments allows both to be judged honestly.

## The maintenance backlog is a shock absorber

Maintenance is particularly suited to flexible public investment.

Every infrastructure system has a backlog of useful work that can be accelerated when labor and contractor capacity become available. Pavement rehabilitation, building repairs, vegetation management, pipe replacement, energy retrofits, and accessibility upgrades often have shorter planning horizons than megaprojects.

A country with good asset data can rank these projects continuously.

If a downturn hits one region, public agencies can move forward on the next tranche of high-value maintenance rather than designing emergency employment programs from zero.

AI helps because prioritization can update with condition, costs, and risk.

The work remains real.

This creates a stabilizer inside the capital system.

During booms, when private construction bids up labor and materials, agencies can avoid overpaying for discretionary work. During downturns, they can accelerate.

Public capital budgets rarely operate this smoothly because appropriations, grants, and political commitments lock projects to calendars. A more adaptive system would preserve a portfolio of projects at different readiness stages.

The macroeconomic gain comes from timing, not from choosing useless projects.

## Work sharing can preserve skill through a downturn

A construction recession can destroy productive capacity that is expensive to rebuild.

Skilled workers leave the industry. Small contractors sell equipment. Apprenticeship programs shrink. Suppliers exit local markets.

When demand returns, costs rise because the ecosystem has to be recreated.

Public work can smooth the cycle.

So can work-sharing arrangements that reduce hours across a workforce rather than concentrating the shock in layoffs, where law and operations allow.

AI-driven productivity may make this option more relevant.

If a crew can produce the required output in fewer hours during a soft period, workers can retain attachment to the employer while infrastructure agencies maintain capacity for the next surge.

The policy is not free. Someone absorbs the income difference.

But preserving skilled networks has economic value beyond the immediate project.

The construction industry is a form of capital embodied partly in people and firms.

## Jobs counts can lie

Economic-development announcements love job numbers.

Ten thousand jobs. Fifty thousand jobs. Hundreds of thousands of jobs supported.

The categories are often slippery.

Construction jobs may be temporary person-years rather than permanent positions. Indirect jobs depend on modeling assumptions. A project can "support" jobs that already exist. Automated facilities can require enormous capital and relatively few onsite employees.

Public works should use labor numbers carefully.

Temporary construction employment is valuable. It should be reported as such. Permanent operations employment is different. Induced regional effects are different again.

A data center and a manufacturing plant of similar capital cost may have very different employment profiles. That does not make the data center worthless. It means tax incentives should not be justified using a job-creation story that belongs to another type of facility.

The AI era will contain more capital-intensive, low-employment projects.

Public policy needs a vocabulary of value beyond jobs.

Tax base. Grid investment. Research spillovers. Service capacity. Resilience. Emissions. Land use. Local supplier development. Housing demand. Water use.

A project can be good with few jobs if it produces enough other value.

A project can be bad with many jobs if the work exists only because the method is wasteful.

## Local benefits should survive the construction trailer

Communities hosting major projects reasonably ask what remains after construction crews leave.

A project may create temporary employment while imposing long-term land, traffic, power, water, noise, or environmental burdens.

The public bargain should focus on durable capacity.

Grid upgrades that strengthen regional service. Roads that remain useful. Training that produces portable credentials. Tax revenue not erased by incentives. Housing infrastructure. Parks. Broadband. Water capacity. Supplier networks capable of winning future work.

This is better than maximizing the number of short-term jobs counted at groundbreaking.

AI can help track commitments over time.

Community-benefit promises often disappear into separate agreements and annual reports. A public dashboard can show whether the durable obligations were actually met.

The visible artifact should be the benefit, not the promise.

## Work requirements versus outcome requirements

Public investment often comes with requirements designed to spread benefits.

Local hiring. Apprenticeship ratios. Domestic content. Small-business participation. Wage standards.

These goals can be legitimate and important.

They can also conflict with productivity if written as fixed production recipes.

The better approach is outcome-oriented where possible.

Require apprenticeship opportunities across a project portfolio rather than a rigid ratio on a task where automation has reduced headcount. Require wage standards without dictating unnecessary labor hours. Support domestic manufacturing by creating predictable demand and performance standards, not by insulating poor suppliers indefinitely.

The public sector should be a demanding customer and a fair employer by proxy.

It should not become a museum of production methods.

AI will force this issue because task composition will change faster than statutes typically do.

Rules should be reviewed periodically against actual outcomes.

Did the apprenticeship requirement produce journey-level workers who stayed in the industry? Did the local-hiring rule improve incomes or merely change paperwork? Did the domestic-content provision create new capacity or raise costs without supply response?

Evidence should be allowed to change the program.

## Anti-boondoggle institutions matter more when money moves fast

Large public-investment waves attract good projects and bad projects.

Political enthusiasm can weaken scrutiny because everyone wants to move quickly.

The answer is not to make every project impossible.

It is to build rejection into the system.

Independent cost estimates. Published alternatives. Clear scope baselines. Staged funding. Post-project evaluation. Audits that compare promised and delivered benefits. Cancellation points before sunk costs become political arguments for continuing.

AI can make project screening faster and surface warning patterns from prior failures.

It can also make bad projects look more sophisticated by generating polished analysis on demand.

The antidote is adversarial review.

Every major proposal should have somebody tasked with asking what would make the case fail.

Public works need institutional skepticism so public ambition remains credible.

## The right to useful transition

Rejecting make-work does not mean telling displaced workers to accept market outcomes with a smile.

Technological transition creates concentrated losses.

A worker can spend twenty years building expertise in a task that loses value quickly. The economy may benefit in aggregate while that person's household absorbs the shock. Saying "productivity is good" does not pay the mortgage.

A serious AI social contract needs transition institutions.

Unemployment insurance that works. Wage insurance experiments. Training tied to actual hiring. Portable benefits. Relocation assistance where desired. Stronger career services. Apprenticeships accessible to adults. Community-college programs that can change faster than four-year curricula.

Public works can provide demand for some of these transitions, but they should not be forced to absorb everyone.

The economy is larger than infrastructure.

The principle is that people should not bear the full social cost of a technological change they did not control while the gains accrue broadly elsewhere.

That is a distribution problem, not an infrastructure design problem.

Keeping the categories separate produces better policy.

## Public employment still has a place

Rejecting permanent make-work should not produce an ideological ban on direct public employment.

Some work is naturally public.

Parks crews. Conservation work. Emergency response. Public maintenance. Inspection. Community health. Disaster cleanup. Records modernization. Care and service roles in public institutions.

During severe labor-market disruption, governments may reasonably expand such employment faster than contractors can absorb workers.

The discipline is the same as for contracted public works.

The work should produce a service worth having.

Programs need competent supervision, real equipment, training, safety, and paths onward. A worker should not be parked in a symbolic role because the political system wanted to count a placement.

Useful public employment is public capacity.

Make-work is activity without a reason.

The difference deserves protection.

## The abundance test

The deepest test for public works in the AI era is whether technological abundance becomes physical abundance.

If design gets cheaper, do we get more homes?

If project administration gets faster, do we get more repaired schools?

If construction robotics improves, do we get more infrastructure per dollar?

If maintenance prediction improves, do failures decline?

If machine intelligence spreads expert knowledge, do small communities execute better projects?

If the answer is no, then productivity is being absorbed somewhere in the system.

Maybe land scarcity captures it. Maybe permitting does. Maybe monopoly pricing does. Maybe budgets shrink as soon as costs fall. Maybe public agencies add new requirements until the old expense returns in another form.

This is why AI policy cannot stop at adoption.

The question is what the adoption changes in the world.

A public agency boasting that 80 percent of employees use AI is telling us almost nothing. Did roads get safer? Did the queue get shorter? Did costs fall? Did workers spend more time on judgment and less on paperwork? Did citizens receive better service?

Measure the outcome.

## Build what is worth building

The phrase *jobs program* can be politically useful because jobs are tangible and morally resonant.

The more durable case for public works is stronger.

Build because the bridge is needed.

Build because the homes are missing.

Build because clean water should not depend on a pipe that is already failing.

Build because electricity is becoming a binding constraint on economic growth.

Build because a school should be safe in the climate its students actually inhabit.

Build because resilience is cheaper before the disaster.

Build because public space matters.

Then build as efficiently as competence allows.

If machines reduce the labor required, use the saved capacity to solve the next real problem.

America does not face a shortage of useful things to do.

It faces a shortage of institutions capable of turning abundance into completion.

The answer to automation is not to make work.

It is to make abundance visible.
