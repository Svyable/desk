# Chapter 1: The Country Under the Software

The first thing an artificial-intelligence boom asks for is not intelligence.

It asks for electricity.

Then land. Fiber. Transformers. Cooling. Roads wide enough for heavy equipment. Water in some designs and dry air in others. Engineers who can sign drawings. Electricians who can terminate cable correctly. Utility crews who can energize a substation. Local officials who can decide whether a site may be built. A transmission system that can deliver power after the servers arrive.

The software may be weightless to the person using it.

The system underneath it is not.

That is the starting point of this book.

For much of the last generation, Americans learned to think of the most dynamic parts of the economy as increasingly detached from the physical country. A software company could reach hundreds of millions of people without constructing a store in every town. A financial service could move onto a phone. A meeting could become a video call. A newspaper could become a feed. The economic frontier appeared to be moving from atoms toward bits.

Artificial intelligence seems, at first glance, like the culmination of that movement. It turns language, images, code, analysis, and prediction into software services. It makes some kinds of cognitive work reproducible at machine speed. It can feel like a technology that finally escapes the old constraints of geography.

Then somebody tries to build the data center.

The country under the software comes back into view.

This is not primarily a book about predicting exactly how intelligent AI becomes or when a particular benchmark is crossed. Forecasts matter, but the stronger question is more durable.

What does a country need if useful cognition becomes materially cheaper?

The answer is complements.

Energy. Housing. Water. Transport. Construction. Maintenance. Skilled field work. Permitting. Procurement. Institutions capable of deciding, coordinating, and remembering.

If AI progress is slower than its enthusiasts expect, these things remain useful.

If AI progress is faster, they become harder to ignore.

## The bottleneck moves

Every productive system has a limiting factor somewhere.

When one input becomes cheaper, another often matters more.

If calculation is expensive, firms organize around scarce calculation. If communication is expensive, they organize around distance. If skilled drafting takes months, a project may be limited by design capacity. If AI makes portions of drafting, analysis, scheduling, translation, coding, and administrative work faster, the project does not become unconstrained.

It reaches the next constraint sooner.

Sometimes the next constraint is a person with legal authority.

Sometimes it is a transformer with a long manufacturing lead time.

Sometimes it is the fact that a city has not permitted enough housing for the workers a growing industry needs.

This is not a prediction that software becomes unimportant. It is the opposite. Powerful software can make its complements more valuable. A better routing system makes a truck fleet more productive, which can increase the value of warehouse access and road capacity. Better drug discovery can increase the value of laboratories, clinical trials, manufacturing, and regulatory competence. Better engineering software can produce more designs, which raises the importance of construction capacity and permitting.

The economic question is therefore not merely what AI can do.

It is what becomes scarce after AI does it.

That is where public works enter the story.

Governments control or influence many of the complements whose supply responds slowly: transportation rights-of-way, land-use rules, transmission approvals, utility systems, streets, water, public buildings, environmental review, procurement, education, and the maintenance of existing assets. Private firms build much of the physical economy, but they build inside these public systems.

A country can have extraordinary models and ordinary bottlenecks at the same time.

## A rich country can be capacity-poor

Money and capacity are related.

They are not identical.

A legislature can authorize billions of dollars faster than a region can produce electricians. A utility can approve a capital plan before a transformer manufacturer has a production slot. A housing subsidy can increase purchasing power faster than a city permits homes. A grant can fund a water project before the town has enough engineering staff to scope and procure it well.

This distinction matters because wealthy societies can mistake financial ability for productive ability.

The money exists.

The thing still does not happen.

Sometimes the reason is bad policy. Sometimes it is genuine physical scarcity. Sometimes an institution has accumulated rules without accumulating throughput. Sometimes a market is too uncertain for suppliers to invest in capacity. Sometimes every individual decision makes sense locally and the system still performs badly in aggregate.

The useful question is not whether America can afford to build.

It is whether America can reliably convert money, knowledge, authority, materials, and labor into completed useful capacity at reasonable cost.

That is a different kind of wealth.

A country can be financially rich and operationally poor.

Public works are one place where the difference becomes impossible to hide.

## The return of load growth

Electricity makes the argument concrete.

For years, many parts of the United States planned around relatively modest growth in electricity demand. Efficiency improved. Heavy industry had changed. Utilities could often treat large new loads as exceptional cases.

That assumption is becoming less comfortable.

Data centers are not the only reason. Manufacturing, electrification, population shifts, and other industrial loads matter too. But AI has made the physical scale of computing harder to ignore. A large computing campus can require power comparable to a substantial industrial facility, and clusters of campuses can force utilities to reconsider generation, transmission, substations, and local distribution at once.

The interesting fact is not any single forecast.

Forecasts move quickly, especially when technology is changing.

The interesting fact is that utilities have to make decisions before uncertainty disappears.

A transmission line can take years to plan, permit, finance, and construct. Large transformers are not downloaded. Generation projects require equipment and interconnection. If planners wait until demand is certain, the physical response may arrive late. If they build for every optimistic projection, customers may be left paying for unused capacity.

This is the kind of problem mature infrastructure institutions are supposed to handle.

They have to make reversible bets where possible, preserve options, stage investments, assign risk, and build the capacity that remains useful across several futures.

AI does not remove the planning problem.

It makes good planning more valuable.

## The industrial clock and the software clock

Software companies are used to iteration measured in days and weeks.

Physical systems keep different time.

A model can be improved after users encounter a problem. A substation foundation cannot be moved cheaply after equipment arrives. A zoning decision can take months before excavation begins. A transmission corridor may still be serving customers when today's AI architecture is a historical curiosity.

This mismatch creates pressure on both sides.

Technology firms can treat utility or permitting timelines as evidence that public institutions are hopelessly slow. Public agencies can treat a company's changing plans as evidence that the demand is speculative and should not shape long-lived investment.

Both may be seeing the same uncertainty from different clocks.

The answer is not to make every public decision at software speed.

It is to design physical commitments in stages where possible. Reserve land before building every component. Upgrade a substation in modules. Preserve a corridor whose full buildout can wait. Require large customers to make financial commitments before utilities socialize dedicated costs. Design permits around clear milestones so a changing project does not restart from the beginning unnecessarily.

A building state in the AI era has to learn the difference between irreversible concrete and reversible options.

The physical system will always be slower than code.

It can become less brittle about uncertainty.

## A data center is a local event

National discussions about AI often describe investment in totals.

Capital expenditure. Chip shipments. Model-training runs. Productivity. Market value.

A community experiences the same boom differently.

It sees a parcel rezoned. It sees grading equipment. It sees a utility application for a new substation. It sees tax negotiations, road traffic, construction crews, water questions, noise complaints, and a building that may employ far fewer permanent workers than its construction cost suggests.

The local bargain can be good.

A major facility can expand the tax base, fund infrastructure, strengthen the grid if upgrades are designed well, create construction demand, and attract complementary investment. It can also impose costs that are easy to socialize: a road expansion paid broadly, a constrained water system, a transmission project whose benefit allocation becomes contentious, or a tax abatement that lasts longer than the political excitement surrounding the announcement.

The point is not that data centers are uniquely burdensome. Many industrial facilities create similar questions.

The point is that the AI economy has addresses.

Those addresses sit in towns, counties, utility territories, watersheds, labor markets, and states. The most advanced digital industry in the world still arrives through institutions that were often built for a slower era.

A public-works strategy begins by treating those institutions as part of the technology stack.

## The project behind the project

A large private investment often depends on public work that never appears in the announcement.

A road intersection is rebuilt. A water line is extended. A fire department acquires equipment appropriate to a new industrial hazard. A substation expands. A county hires additional inspectors. A transit or shuttle route changes. A state improves a highway interchange. A utility reserves generation or transmission capacity.

The private project has a public shadow.

That shadow should be measured before incentives are negotiated rather than discovered afterward.

Economic-development agreements tend to make the firm's commitment explicit: investment, schedule, employment, sometimes wages. The public commitment can be dispersed across agencies and therefore less legible.

AI can help assemble the ledger. Capital plans, utility studies, road projects, tax abatements, service demands, and grant applications can be associated with the facility so officials see the full bargain.

The analysis will still contain uncertainty. A road may serve many future users. A substation may create regional value beyond one customer. A fire-station upgrade may have benefits that outlast the plant.

The point is not to invoice one company for every public improvement nearby.

It is to stop pretending the infrastructure arrives from nowhere.

## The house near the boom

Land becomes another constraint when economic opportunity concentrates.

A growing company can increase output without adding proportionate office space if AI raises worker productivity. Its employees still need somewhere to live.

If new jobs and investment arrive in a region that builds little housing, a portion of the productivity gain can be converted into higher land values and rents. The economy becomes more productive while the cost of access to the productive place rises.

That is not merely a housing-policy problem sitting beside the AI story.

It is part of the distribution of the AI dividend.

A worker who receives a raise and then loses most of it to rent has experienced productivity differently from an owner of scarce land. A new graduate may be unable to move to the city where her skills are most valuable. A hospital, school, utility, or contractor may struggle to recruit because the people needed to operate the growing place cannot afford the growing place.

Housing is therefore infrastructure in a broad but useful sense. It is capacity that allows people to participate in a labor market.

The pipes, roads, schools, and power systems around housing are infrastructure in the conventional sense too. Building more homes is not just a matter of changing a zoning map. High-growth regions need the physical systems that make additional residents possible.

The same complementarity appears again.

Software can make a firm scale faster than a city can add capacity.

## The inherited country

Most of the infrastructure Americans will use during the AI transition already exists.

That is easy to forget in a culture drawn to new projects.

The water main under the street may be older than the software industry. The bridge carrying a new fleet of delivery vehicles may have been designed before anyone imagined e-commerce. A transmission corridor laid out for one generation of power plants may become essential to a different generation. Public buildings built around paper records are being retrofitted for digital systems. Storm drains designed around historical rainfall face new conditions.

This inherited country is an asset and a liability.

It is an asset because replacement cost would be staggering. A functioning water network, street grid, port, rail corridor, or power system embodies decades of capital and institutional knowledge.

It is a liability when governments do not know what they own, how old it is, what condition it is in, or when it will fail.

The AI era creates an opportunity to improve that knowledge.

Records scattered across paper plans, spreadsheets, maintenance systems, inspection reports, and the memories of experienced employees can become easier to search and reconcile. Computer vision can help review imagery. Predictive systems can help prioritize inspection. Language models can make technical histories easier to retrieve.

None of this repairs a pipe.

It can help a utility decide which pipe to inspect before it breaks.

That distinction—between intelligence and execution—runs through the whole book.

## Brownfield intelligence

The inherited country also contains land and structures left by earlier economic eras.

Closed factories. Rail spurs. Warehouses. Industrial parcels. Contaminated sites. Obsolete power infrastructure. Empty commercial buildings. Public agencies often know these places imperfectly because ownership, environmental history, utility capacity, and building condition live in separate records.

The AI-era building boom creates pressure to use land quickly.

That makes old industrial geography valuable again.

A brownfield near transmission, water, freight, and an existing workforce may be more economically useful than a pristine greenfield whose infrastructure must be built from scratch. Reuse can also avoid some sprawl and bring tax value back to places that lost earlier industries.

The obstacles are real. Contamination can be expensive. Buildings may be unusable. Historic utility maps may be wrong. Cleanup liability can frighten buyers.

AI can help assemble the fragmented evidence and compare sites, but remediation remains physical, legal, and financial work.

The broader lesson is that the country already contains enormous stored capital in locations shaped by earlier technology.

Restructuring America does not mean starting over.

It means learning which parts of the inherited geography can carry the next economy.

## The temptation of the smart layer

When a physical system performs poorly, institutions often reach first for a new information layer.

A dashboard. An app. A sensor network. A digital twin. A prediction model.

Sometimes that is exactly what is needed. Better information can reveal leakage, congestion, equipment failure, schedule conflict, or demand patterns that were previously invisible.

Sometimes the smart layer is a way of avoiding the expensive layer.

A city can measure bus delays without giving buses enough priority to run reliably. A water utility can install sensors while postponing replacement of failing mains. A permitting office can build a new portal while preserving a contradictory rulebook. A transportation agency can collect exquisite pavement-condition data while lacking money or contracting capacity to resurface the roads.

AI intensifies this temptation because the software is impressive and comparatively fast to procure.

The discipline should be to ask what physical or institutional decision the intelligence changes.

If the answer is none, the system may be informational decoration.

A country does not become modern because its backlog has an API.

## Sensors create obligations too

Information infrastructure has maintenance costs of its own.

A sensor needs power, communications, calibration, replacement, cybersecurity, data storage, and somebody who knows what its readings mean. A camera installed to inspect a site creates retention and privacy questions. A building-management system can become unsupported before the building itself needs major renovation.

This matters because public agencies can accumulate digital debris just as easily as physical debris.

A grant pays for an impressive pilot. Sensors are installed. The vendor contract expires. No operating budget exists. Five years later the city owns a network of dead devices bolted to poles.

Smart infrastructure should therefore pass the same lifecycle test as any other asset.

Who maintains it? Which decision depends on it? What happens when it fails? Can the data be exported? Is a cheaper periodic inspection better than permanent sensing?

AI can make sensing more useful.

It cannot make sensing free.

The digital layer becomes part of the inherited country too.

## Public works are not only concrete

The phrase *public works* evokes heavy construction.

Dams. Bridges. Roads. Tunnels. Treatment plants.

Those remain central, but the useful definition is wider: durable shared capacity that private actors rely on and that markets alone often underprovide, fragment, or ration poorly.

That includes the administrative machinery that allows physical investment to happen.

A permitting office with clear rules is capacity. A procurement system that attracts capable bidders is capacity. A parcel database that tells engineers where public assets actually are is capacity. A regional planning institution that can make a decision across municipal boundaries is capacity.

Institutional capacity is not a substitute for physical capacity.

It is one of the inputs required to create it.

This matters because the United States can spend more money without building proportionately more useful things. Appropriations are necessary. They are not sufficient. An agency that cannot define scope, manage risk, coordinate utilities, run procurement, and preserve institutional memory can turn additional funding into a larger queue.

The public-works challenge is therefore partly organizational.

The country needs institutions capable of converting money and intelligence into completed assets.

## Throughput is the test

Institutions tend to measure what is easy to count.

Applications received. Dollars awarded. Meetings held. Studies completed. Grants announced. Pages reviewed. Contracts signed.

These measures describe activity.

They do not always describe capacity.

A permitting system should ultimately be judged by whether it produces timely, lawful, defensible decisions. A procurement system should be judged by whether it attracts competition and produces useful performance at reasonable lifecycle cost. A maintenance program should be judged by service reliability and asset condition. A workforce program should be judged partly by whether workers acquire skills that real projects can use.

The same distinction applies to AI.

A public agency can deploy assistants, models, dashboards, and pilots while its throughput barely changes.

The point is not adoption.

The point is whether the handoff gets better.

Does a project move from application to decision with fewer avoidable waits? Does an inspector reach more high-risk assets? Does a project manager discover a dependency before it becomes a delay? Does a town with a small staff gain access to engineering capacity it could not previously afford?

This book will repeatedly return to that test.

Not: did we add intelligence?

Did useful capacity increase?

## The public and private line

A public-works renaissance does not require government to build everything itself.

American infrastructure has always mixed public and private roles. Investor-owned utilities, municipal utilities, federal power agencies, private railroads, public roads, regulated pipelines, private contractors, public authorities, developers, and cooperatives all occupy different parts of the physical system.

The ownership question should follow the economics of the asset and the public obligations attached to it.

Some systems are natural monopolies. Some require public rights-of-way. Some produce large spillovers. Some can support vigorous competition. Some are best financed privately but regulated publicly. Others make sense as public assets operated through contracts.

The mistake is to turn every infrastructure question into a referendum on government versus markets.

The useful question is narrower: what arrangement produces enough capacity, disciplines cost, preserves access, assigns risk coherently, and leaves someone accountable for performance?

AI does not answer that question for us.

It may make the answer easier to measure.

## Public capacity can crowd private capacity in

Government investment is often described as crowding private investment out.

That can happen when public borrowing competes for scarce resources or a subsidized public provider displaces a viable private one.

Infrastructure can produce the opposite effect.

A transmission upgrade makes a factory feasible. A road opens access to industrial land. A water extension allows housing. A public research institution creates knowledge firms can commercialize. A reliable permitting process reduces the cost of investing in a region.

The public asset expands the set of private projects that can happen.

This is the core economic case for many public works.

The difficult question is additionality. Would the private investment have happened anyway? Is the infrastructure general enough to serve multiple users, or has the public effectively built a dedicated asset for one company? Does the project create future options or merely subsidize a transaction already likely?

AI can improve scenario analysis and identify comparable cases.

The counterfactual remains uncertain.

Good public works should lean toward shared capacity whose value survives the fate of any one firm.

## What scarcity feels like

Infrastructure scarcity rarely announces itself with that name.

It appears as a wait.

A generator waits in an interconnection queue. A housing project waits for approval. A contractor waits for a utility relocation. A family waits for a train. A factory waits for service. A resident waits for a permit. A city waits for a replacement component after equipment fails.

The waiting has economic effects even when it never appears as a line item called scarcity.

Capital sits idle. Workers lose time. Projects are redesigned. Financing costs grow. Competitors give up. Households pay higher prices because supply cannot respond.

A great deal of public capacity can be understood as the ability to shorten the right waits without eliminating the review that protects legitimate interests.

This is where AI may be genuinely useful in government.

It can identify missing documents before an application enters a queue. It can compare a proposed design against standard requirements. It can trace dependencies across project schedules. It can summarize prior decisions. It can surface conflicts between agencies earlier.

But speed without authority is only faster confusion.

Someone still has to decide.

## The politics of visible capacity

There is another reason to care about physical investment during a digital boom.

Infrastructure makes economic capacity visible.

A productivity increase inside a firm may be real and socially valuable, but people outside the firm can have difficulty seeing it. A model improves. A workflow shrinks. Profit rises. A service gets slightly cheaper. The aggregate economy records the result later.

A repaired park, a reliable train, abundant housing, a stronger grid, a safer intersection, or clean water has a different political character.

It becomes part of ordinary life.

This does not mean public works should be used as theater. A monument that photographs well and operates poorly destroys trust faster than no monument at all.

The deeper point is that common assets can convert some portion of abstract growth into shared experience.

A richer country should eventually look and feel richer where people live.

If AI creates large gains while public systems visibly deteriorate, the political story will write itself: wealth increased somewhere else.

Public works are one way to prevent that story from becoming true.

## A strategy that survives being wrong about AI

The strongest reason for this agenda is that it does not require certainty about artificial intelligence.

Predictions about AI are unusually fragile. Capabilities improve unevenly. Costs change. Firms experiment. Regulation shifts. Some demonstrations scale; others do not. Labor markets adapt in ways forecasts miss.

A policy strategy that only works if a particular model capability arrives in a particular year is a brittle strategy.

Infrastructure investment can be made more robust.

A stronger transmission system is useful if AI demand explodes and useful if manufacturing or electrification grows instead. More housing in productive regions is useful if AI creates jobs there and useful if ordinary population growth continues. Better water systems, maintenance, transportation, procurement, and permitting remain valuable under almost any plausible technological future.

The AI thesis changes the urgency and the expected return, not the basic usefulness.

This is important because governments cannot build well while chasing every forecast.

They need investments with option value.

## The country beneath the forecast

Much of the debate about artificial intelligence asks what the technology will become.

This book asks a different question.

What country will it encounter?

Will new industrial loads find a grid capable of connecting them without making reliability a zero-sum fight? Will workers be able to live near expanding opportunity? Will public agencies know the condition of the assets they already own? Will project approvals protect real public interests without turning uncertainty into indefinite delay? Will procurement reward better methods? Will construction productivity be allowed to rise? Will communities hosting new infrastructure receive durable benefits?

These questions are less glamorous than capability benchmarks.

They are also closer to the ground.

Artificial intelligence may become the most powerful general-purpose technology of the century. It may advance more gradually. Either way, the United States will still be a physical place with pipes, wires, roads, buildings, rivers, neighborhoods, property lines, budgets, courts, workers, and institutions.

Software does not replace that country.

It reveals how much depends on it.

The task ahead is not to choose between the digital future and the physical past.

It is to build a physical country capable of carrying a digital future.
