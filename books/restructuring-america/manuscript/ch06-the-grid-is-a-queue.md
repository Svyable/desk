# Chapter 6: The Grid Is a Queue

A queue is a place where a system admits that demand has outrun its method of deciding.

The electric grid has several of them.

Generators wait to interconnect. Large loads wait for service studies. Transmission projects wait for siting decisions, cost allocation, permits, equipment, and construction. Developers submit applications early because they fear being late, which makes the queue larger and the signal inside it noisier.

A queue is not proof that every project waiting deserves to be built.

It is proof that order matters.

Artificial intelligence makes this problem more urgent because the grid is being asked to connect new supply and new demand at the same time. Renewable projects, storage, industrial facilities, data centers, manufacturing, electrification, and ordinary growth can all arrive through processes designed around a slower and more predictable system.

The question is not how to make the queue disappear.

It is how to make the queue tell the truth.

## An application is not a power plant

Interconnection statistics are easy to misuse.

A large volume of proposed generation in a queue does not mean that volume will be built. Developers may submit multiple projects, change technology, lose site control, fail to secure financing, or withdraw when network-upgrade costs become clear.

The queue measures interest and process load more directly than future capacity.

That distinction matters because a system designed to study every speculative request in full detail can be overwhelmed by its own openness.

The first reform is therefore screening.

Projects should demonstrate increasing levels of readiness as they move through increasingly expensive studies. Deposits, site control, commercial milestones, and withdrawal penalties can discourage applications whose purpose is merely to reserve a place.

At the same time, readiness rules should not become a moat around incumbents.

A small developer may have less balance-sheet capacity than a large utility affiliate even when its project is serious. Requirements should test commitment to the project, not simply financial size.

The queue needs friction.

It needs the right friction.

## First come is not always first served

A simple queue rewards filing early.

That sounds fair until one project depends on upgrades that also benefit several later projects. Studying each request sequentially can produce repeated engineering work and volatile cost assignments. A developer may withdraw after learning its share of an upgrade, forcing the next project to be restudied.

Cluster studies are one response.

Projects entering within a defined period can be analyzed together. Network upgrades can be planned across the group. Cost allocation can reflect shared effects more coherently.

The improvement is not automatic.

A cluster can become a larger waiting room if the study process remains understaffed or if one weak project destabilizes the cost shares of others.

The deeper lesson is that the grid should plan for plausible portfolios rather than pretending each interconnection is an isolated surprise.

If a region knows that large amounts of generation are likely to develop in an area with excellent resources, waiting for individual projects to trigger every transmission upgrade can be more expensive than planning the corridor in advance.

This is the difference between interconnection and infrastructure planning.

## Withdrawals are not private events

A developer can abandon a project.

The queue cannot always abandon the consequences.

Suppose several projects are studied together and assigned shares of a network upgrade. One leaves. The remaining projects may inherit higher costs. Another then becomes uneconomic and withdraws. Studies have to be rerun because the electrical configuration changed. A third project's schedule moves because the prior study is no longer valid.

One private decision can create a public cascade of engineering work.

That is why queue design has to price withdrawal as an externality without making experimentation impossible.

Early-stage withdrawal should be relatively easy. The system benefits when weak concepts leave before expensive engineering begins. Later withdrawal, after other projects and utilities have relied on a participant's commitment, should carry more consequence.

Financial deposits are one tool. Milestone requirements are another. Study rules can define when a departing project remains responsible for costs it caused. Shared upgrades can be separated from dedicated facilities so the departure of one applicant does not erase infrastructure with broader value.

The larger principle is familiar from construction.

Commitment should rise as irreversibility rises.

A queue works badly when everyone is encouraged to claim optionality while somebody else bears the cost of the option.

## The study is itself a production system

Interconnection reform is often discussed as rulemaking.

It is also operations management.

A study enters a work queue. Data are collected. Models are prepared. Engineers run cases. Results are reviewed. Questions return to the applicant. Assumptions change. Reports are revised. Agreements are drafted.

Each step has capacity.

If a regional operator has enough sophisticated simulation software but not enough engineers to validate inputs, validation becomes the bottleneck. If engineering is fast but every final study waits for a legal review, the queue has merely moved. If applicants submit inconsistent models, scarce specialists spend their time cleaning files rather than studying the network.

This is why published timelines alone do not create speed.

The institution has to measure cycle time by stage.

How long does a request wait before assignment? How often is it returned for incomplete data? Which studies require restudy? Which upgrade assumptions generate repeated disputes? How much time is active technical work and how much is administrative waiting?

The answers can support staffing and redesign.

AI is especially useful at the seams: checking incoming data against required formats, comparing model versions, tracking changed assumptions, generating standard cases, assembling draft reports from verified calculations, and identifying when a new request resembles an earlier study.

The goal is not to replace power engineers.

It is to stop using power engineers as document routers.

## The grid was built around known centers

Electric networks reflect history.

Power plants were built in particular places. Cities grew in particular places. Transmission connected them according to older patterns of generation and demand.

New technologies rearrange the map.

Wind resources may be strongest far from major cities. Solar development may concentrate where land and irradiance align. Data centers may seek locations with inexpensive power and fiber rather than traditional industrial geography. Manufacturing projects can arrive in regions whose distribution systems were not designed for a sudden load of that scale.

The network cannot instantly reorganize around the new map.

That creates locational value.

A project on the right side of a constraint can connect cheaply. Another a few miles away may trigger expensive upgrades. A closed power-plant site may be attractive because it already has transmission infrastructure. A brownfield can carry more electrical value than an empty greenfield parcel.

Public planning should make this information visible enough that developers respond before filing costly applications.

Where can load be served? Where is generation deliverable? Which substations are constrained? Which corridors have planned upgrades?

Better maps do not substitute for studies.

They can keep obviously bad fits out of the queue.

## The reuse value of old electrical sites

A retired generating station is not merely an empty industrial parcel.

It may contain one of the scarcest assets in the new power economy: a connection to the grid.

The old plant may have transmission lines, substation equipment, access roads, water infrastructure, trained workers nearby, and a community accustomed to an industrial tax base. Some of those assets can be reused. Others cannot. Environmental cleanup or equipment age can erase part of the advantage.

But the interconnection position itself has value.

This can make retired fossil or nuclear sites candidates for new generation, storage, synchronous support equipment, industrial load, or combinations of uses that would face much longer timelines on a greenfield site.

Public planning should identify these reuse opportunities before sites fragment or infrastructure is removed without considering future system value.

The principle reaches beyond power plants.

An industrial site with heavy electrical service can be more valuable for new manufacturing than a larger parcel without it. A utility corridor that looks obsolete on a land-use map may be an option on future capacity.

The AI economy will reward places that remember what their old infrastructure can still do.

## Transmission is the long answer

Queue reform can improve process.

It cannot create network capacity that does not exist.

If many viable projects require the same transmission expansion, the lasting solution is to build transmission.

This seems obvious until cost allocation begins.

A transmission line can benefit generators, consumers, reliability, future projects, and regions that do not yet know they will use it. Benefits vary across scenarios. One state may host the line while another receives lower power prices. A utility may resist paying for a project outside its service territory even when the wider network gains resilience.

The accounting problem becomes a political problem.

No formula can identify every future beneficiary with certainty.

That does not justify assigning the whole cost to the first project unlucky enough to reveal the need.

Long-lived shared infrastructure should be financed according to a reasonable portfolio of expected benefits, with regulators explicit about the assumptions.

The alternative is not neutrality.

It is a hidden policy of underbuilding.

## The corridor is more valuable than the line

Transmission planning often begins with a specific project.

A durable system also thinks about corridors.

Rights-of-way become harder to assemble as land develops. Communities become accustomed to landscapes without infrastructure. Every new crossing creates legal and environmental work.

A corridor that can accommodate future upgrades has option value.

This does not mean acquiring unlimited land for speculative lines.

It means recognizing that route preservation can be cheaper than recreating a route after growth makes it impossible.

Existing transportation corridors, rail rights-of-way, highways, and other linear infrastructure sometimes offer opportunities for co-location, though engineering, safety, land rights, and community impacts differ by case.

Advanced planning can also reduce repeated disturbance. If a corridor is likely to need additional capacity, tower design, substation footprints, and environmental review can account for plausible expansion where doing so is economical.

The future grid needs places to go.

Land-use planning should not discover that fact after every path has been built over.

## Rebuild the wire before rebuilding the route

New transmission does not always require a new corridor.

Existing rights-of-way can sometimes carry more power through reconductoring, higher-capacity conductors, equipment upgrades, voltage changes, power-flow controls, or other grid-enhancing technologies. The engineering possibilities depend on towers, clearances, substations, protection systems, stability limits, and the surrounding network.

This is attractive because the hardest part of a transmission project can be securing the route.

If an existing corridor can carry substantially more useful capacity with less land disturbance and shorter construction, that option deserves serious analysis before a new line is proposed elsewhere.

But the same warning applies as with dynamic ratings.

Optimization can become a politics of postponement.

A corridor upgrade may buy several years. It may also reveal that demand continues to grow and a new route is still necessary. Agencies should treat grid-enhancing technologies as part of an expansion ladder: use the cheapest safe capacity first, then build the next layer before the temporary relief becomes another emergency.

The objective is not maximum utilization of every old asset.

It is maximum system value over time.

## The queue on the demand side

Interconnection debates historically focused on generators.

Large loads can create their own queue.

A data center, factory, or industrial campus may request hundreds of megawatts in a location where the local system cannot serve that demand immediately. The utility studies upgrades. The customer waits. Other proposed loads arrive and compete for the same future capacity.

The same problem appears: applications can become speculative.

A developer may reserve capacity before its financing, tenant, or construction schedule is firm. Utilities can be left planning around projects that disappear.

Demand-side readiness rules should therefore resemble generation-side discipline.

Show site control. Make credible financial commitments. Pay appropriately for dedicated studies. Accept consequences for abandoning infrastructure built specifically for the project.

At the same time, utilities should not use uncertainty as an excuse to provide no information until late in the process.

Customers need realistic service windows and cost ranges early enough to choose different sites.

The queue works better when both sides have skin in the forecast.

## Supply and demand queues are becoming one problem

The traditional grid planning story separated generation from load.

A utility forecast demand, procured or planned supply, and studied generators seeking connection. Large industrial customers existed, but they were comparatively familiar objects inside the planning process.

The AI buildout can blur those categories.

A large computing campus may arrive with proposals for dedicated generation, batteries, flexible demand, backup systems, or co-located resources. A manufacturer may want new electrical service at the same time a region is trying to interconnect new generation nearby. A retiring plant can remove supply from a node that immediately becomes attractive for new load because the transmission connection remains valuable.

Studying these decisions in separate institutional lanes can produce absurd results.

The generation queue says a project needs an upgrade. The load study says a new customer needs another upgrade. The transmission plan assumes a third future. Each analysis may be technically sound inside its boundary while the combined plan is unnecessarily expensive.

The more mature approach is co-planning.

Where credible large load is emerging, planners should test supply, demand, storage, transmission, and flexibility together. The customer should not receive a private grid plan. The system should recognize that new demand changes which public investments have value.

AI can help explore the larger scenario space.

Institutional rules have to allow the scenarios to meet.

## A queue is a market signal

Waiting time has economic meaning.

A two-year delay for electrical service can change where a factory locates. A long interconnection process can kill a generation project even if its technology is otherwise competitive. Financing costs continue while the project waits. Equipment orders slip. Tax incentives expire. Land options become expensive.

The delay therefore acts like a price.

Unlike a transparent market price, it is often difficult to compare and hedge.

This creates an advantage for firms with patient capital, regulatory expertise, and large project portfolios. They can absorb failed applications and long carrying periods more easily than smaller entrants.

Queue reform is thus partly competition policy.

A clearer, faster process lowers the fixed cost of attempting to build.

That does not mean every entrant should receive cheap access to infrastructure others paid for. It means the rules of access should be understandable before the entrant spends years discovering them.

Predictability can matter almost as much as speed.

## Study automation should change the workflow

Grid studies involve large amounts of structured technical work.

Engineers model power flows, contingencies, thermal limits, voltage, protection, and other system conditions. The work is safety-critical and cannot be delegated casually to a language model.

It is also full of opportunities for automation.

Data can be validated automatically. Standard cases can be generated. Model changes can be compared. Draft study reports can be assembled from verified outputs. Repetitive checks can be performed consistently. Prior studies can be searched for similar upgrades.

The important distinction is between calculation and judgment.

Power-system software has automated calculations for decades. AI can improve the interfaces around those tools and help engineers navigate the growing volume of scenarios.

But an automated study that produces a result nobody can explain creates a new bottleneck at approval.

Utilities and regional operators need methods that are fast enough to scale and transparent enough to trust.

The objective is not an AI engineer signing interconnection agreements.

It is human engineers spending less time on mechanical assembly and more on the unusual conditions that deserve judgment.

## Data quality is network capacity

A grid model is only as useful as its representation of the actual grid.

That sounds trivial until equipment records, ratings, topology, protection settings, and planned upgrades live across multiple systems.

Errors create conservative assumptions or late surprises.

A line may have a limiting component nobody represented correctly. A substation configuration may differ from the official drawing after years of field modifications. Planned projects can appear in one model and not another.

Improving the network model can unlock capacity without building a new line if operators discover that equipment can safely carry more under certain conditions.

Conversely, better data can reveal that apparent capacity was never real.

This is a useful place for AI because reconciliation is tedious.

Models can compare records, drawings, inspection reports, and work orders to flag inconsistencies for human review. Computer vision can extract equipment information from images. Language tools can search old project files for modifications.

The software does not create electrons.

It can make the existing system more legible.

## Dynamic ratings and other forms of truth

Transmission capacity is often represented by conservative static limits.

Actual conditions vary with weather and equipment state. In some situations, technologies such as dynamic line ratings can estimate how much current a line can safely carry under current conditions rather than using one fixed assumption.

Other grid-enhancing technologies can improve power flow or squeeze more useful service from existing corridors.

These tools are attractive because they can sometimes be deployed faster than new transmission.

They are not substitutes for building the network where fundamental capacity is missing.

They are ways to use existing assets more intelligently.

That distinction matters politically. A cheap technology that delays a much larger construction project can be valuable. A cheap technology used as an excuse never to build the needed project can leave the system perpetually constrained.

The right question is lifecycle value under realistic scenarios.

Optimization and expansion belong in the same plan.

## Curtailment can be a product, not a failure

Grid planning is often organized around firm service because customers need to know what they can rely on.

Not every megawatt has identical value every hour.

Some generators can accept occasional curtailment if that allows them to connect sooner or more cheaply. Some large loads can reduce consumption under defined emergency or congestion conditions. Storage can absorb or release energy depending on the local constraint.

This creates room for differentiated service.

A project might connect under a contract that permits measurable curtailment until a planned network upgrade is complete. A large load might accept an interruptible tier in exchange for faster service or lower infrastructure cost. The terms have to be technically credible and commercially explicit.

This should not become a way to disguise inadequate infrastructure.

A supposedly temporary curtailment regime can become permanent if nobody is responsible for the upgrade. A generator that is curtailed so often its economics collapse was not meaningfully connected. A data center that promises flexibility but refuses to reduce load during actual stress did not provide a grid service.

Performance data matter.

Flexible connection is useful when it converts real operational optionality into real capacity.

The queue becomes smarter when every applicant does not have to buy the same product.

## Interregional lines are a federalism problem

Electricity does not respect political boundaries, but many decisions about transmission do.

A line may cross states with different regulators, sit inside several utility territories, and serve a regional market governed by another institution. Federal agencies may have roles in particular permits or lands. Local governments may control parts of siting.

The more geographically valuable the project, the more institutions it can touch.

This is why interregional transmission is a test of American federalism.

The system needs enough local participation to identify real impacts and enough larger-scale authority to prevent every jurisdiction from treating outside benefits as somebody else's problem.

Compensation can help. Community-benefit agreements can help. Better route design can help. Early engagement can help.

None replaces a decision rule.

At some point, a project either receives permission to cross the map or it does not.

A federation capable of building needs institutions that can reach that point without pretending consensus is the only legitimate outcome.

## Communities host the network

A transmission line is a national or regional abstraction until it crosses someone's land.

The host community sees towers, vegetation management, access roads, construction traffic, property impacts, and landscape change.

It is reasonable for those costs to matter.

The old habit of treating rural or low-income communities as empty corridors creates predictable resistance and can produce unjust outcomes.

A building agenda should make hosting a negotiated public role rather than a sentence imposed after route selection.

That can include direct compensation, local tax revenue, infrastructure improvements, route changes, undergrounding in limited high-impact areas where justified, or other benefits tied to the actual burden.

The goal is not to purchase silence.

It is to recognize that broadly shared infrastructure can impose concentrated local cost.

Distribution matters at the level of the tower footprint as well as the electric bill.

## The equipment queue behind the study queue

A project can complete its interconnection study and still wait for hardware.

Transformers, breakers, switchgear, and specialized components have manufacturing lead times. A surge in projects can move the bottleneck from engineering to procurement.

This is another reason process reform alone cannot solve the grid.

A faster approval system can increase pressure on the supply chain unless manufacturing capacity grows with it.

Predictable procurement helps suppliers invest.

Utilities can coordinate specifications where practical, pool purchasing for common components, and maintain strategic spares for equipment whose failure would otherwise produce long outages. Governments can examine whether domestic production of particularly critical components has strategic value without assuming every item needs a protected domestic industry.

The physical queue sits behind the administrative queue.

Both need attention.

## Construction capacity is part of interconnection capacity

An approved upgrade still has to be built.

Transmission contractors, substation crews, protection engineers, lineworkers, civil contractors, commissioning specialists, and utility inspectors are not infinitely elastic. When generation, load, transmission, and ordinary maintenance all accelerate at once, the same experienced people can appear on several critical paths.

This creates another hidden queue.

A project can have its permits, equipment, and financing and still wait for the crew capable of performing the outage or energization safely.

Utilities should therefore connect workforce planning to queue forecasts.

If the pipeline implies a sustained rise in substation construction, training and contracting capacity should expand before every project reaches the field simultaneously. Standard designs can reduce engineering and training burden. Long-term construction frameworks can give contractors enough certainty to invest in equipment and apprentices.

The grid cannot be expanded by a spreadsheet full of approved projects.

Its human production system has to scale too.

## Connection reform should not socialize bad bets

The political pressure to speed connections can create a new risk: overbuilding dedicated infrastructure for projects that never become productive.

A large customer requesting service can cause a utility to begin work before the customer's final demand is certain. If the project cancels, existing customers can be left with the bill unless contracts assign the risk.

The same applies to generation upgrades that become unnecessary after withdrawals.

A healthy queue requires commitment to increase with cost.

Early information can be cheap. Detailed engineering should require stronger financial stakes. Construction triggered by one participant should include protections against abandonment.

Shared upgrades deserve broader financing when they are genuinely shared.

The principle is not punitive.

It is to keep speculative applications from converting private uncertainty into public liability.

## Markets need available connections

Competition depends on access to networks.

A firm with a preexisting grid connection owns something increasingly valuable in a constrained system. That can be efficient compensation for past investment.

It can also create barriers to entry when no new connections are available.

Building network capacity is therefore one way to keep energy markets contestable.

The same is true for load. If only a few incumbent industrial sites can obtain large reliable service, new manufacturers face a hurdle unrelated to their own productivity.

Infrastructure abundance changes who can compete.

This is why queue reform should not be treated as a narrow technical matter for power developers.

It shapes the industrial structure built on top of the grid.

## The grid should publish a better invitation

A functioning market gives participants useful information before they commit heavily.

Electric infrastructure often does not.

Developers can spend substantial money acquiring sites and designing projects before learning the full cost or timing of connection.

Some uncertainty is unavoidable because network effects are complex.

More can be published than many systems historically offered.

Indicative capacity maps. Planned upgrades. Typical study timelines. Standard technical requirements. Queue status. Historical withdrawal rates. Equipment constraints. Available service options.

The information should come with warnings about uncertainty rather than false precision.

A rough honest map is better than a precise-looking map that users mistake for a commitment.

AI can improve these public interfaces by translating technical capacity data into questions a developer can actually use.

The highest-value interaction may be one that tells a project not to apply at the wrong location.

## The queue should expose its own performance

Applicants are required to reveal more about their readiness as they advance.

The institutions running the queue should face a parallel obligation.

Publish median and distributional study times, not only targets. Show how many applications were returned as incomplete. Show how many projects withdrew after receiving upgrade costs. Show the frequency of restudies. Report which phases are consuming the most elapsed time.

The purpose is not to shame engineers for difficult projects.

It is to distinguish technical difficulty from institutional waiting.

A queue with a transparent performance record creates pressure for the right kind of reform. If incomplete applications dominate, improve intake. If one study phase dominates, add capacity or redesign it. If restudies explode after withdrawals, change milestone and cluster rules. If hardware delivery dominates, the problem is no longer primarily regulatory.

AI makes this operational telemetry cheap to assemble from the workflow itself.

A modern grid queue should be able to explain why it is a queue.

## Planning should absorb the queue

The most mature grid would learn from what is waiting.

A queue is a dataset about where markets want to build, though a noisy one.

If applications repeatedly cluster around the same constrained corridor, planners should ask whether the network should be expanded in anticipation rather than studied one project at a time forever.

If loads repeatedly seek a region, utilities should ask whether distribution and transmission planning still reflects the region's economy.

This closes the loop between interconnection and planning.

The queue should inform the plan. The plan should reduce the queue.

Without that loop, reform becomes perpetual process optimization around a structurally undersized network.

## The queue is a choice

Some waiting is unavoidable.

Engineering takes time. Public review takes time. Equipment takes time. Projects fail for good reasons.

Chronic opaque waiting is not a law of nature.

It is the product of institutions, incentives, data, workforce, and the amount of infrastructure society has chosen to build in advance.

An AI-era grid needs more than faster paperwork.

It needs credible applications, portfolio planning, transmission expansion, better use of existing lines, differentiated connection products, stronger equipment and construction supply, transparent cost allocation, host-community bargaining, and institutions capable of deciding across boundaries.

If those pieces improve, the queue becomes shorter because the system becomes larger and smarter.

If they do not, artificial intelligence will discover an old truth.

The future can arrive early and still have to wait for a connection.
