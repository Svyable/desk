# Chapter 3: When Intelligence Gets Cheap

For most of economic history, competent thought has been attached to a person with a limited day.

A lawyer could read only so many cases. An engineer could check only so many drawings. A programmer could write and debug only so much code. A manager could absorb only so many reports. A translator, analyst, accountant, designer, planner, scheduler, estimator, researcher, or teacher carried expertise inside a body that slept.

Organizations evolved around that scarcity.

They created departments, queues, forms, junior roles, review hierarchies, meetings, and calendars partly because skilled attention was expensive and finite. Work waited for the person who knew how to do it.

Artificial intelligence changes that constraint before it changes anything else.

It makes some kinds of cognitive effort cheaper to reproduce.

Not free. Not perfectly reliable. Not equivalent to a person. Cheaper.

That modest phrasing is enough to produce large consequences.

## A task is not a job

The easiest mistake in automation debates is to confuse tasks with occupations.

A job is a bundle.

A civil engineer may calculate, inspect, negotiate, sign, explain, travel, coordinate, judge risk, supervise people, review contracts, and take professional responsibility. A permitting employee may read plans, answer applicants, interpret rules, verify documents, handle unusual cases, and absorb anger from people who dislike the result.

AI can become very good at one slice while remaining poor or legally irrelevant at another.

That means the first effects often appear as reorganization rather than replacement.

A worker delegates a draft. The worker reviews it. A team handles more cases with the same head count. A junior role changes because the routine tasks that once trained beginners are automated. A manager's span of control grows because reporting and coordination become easier.

Field experiments with generative AI already suggest this uneven pattern. Some workers complete selected tasks faster. Some quality gaps narrow. Some benefits are larger for people with less prior expertise. In other settings, experienced workers use the tool differently or gain more because they know what to ask and what to reject.

The honest conclusion is not a single productivity number.

It is heterogeneity.

AI changes the price of tasks, and organizations decide what that means for jobs.

## Inference becomes an operating input

Organizations have begun to treat machine intelligence less like a piece of software purchased once and more like a variable input consumed through use.

A model call has a cost. Larger context can cost more. More attempts cost more. Verification costs time. A task that once involved one employee may now involve an employee plus several machine passes, retrieval, code execution, or other tools.

The cost can still be far below the human alternative.

The important shift is conceptual.

Intelligence becomes something an organization can budget at the margin.

How much reasoning is this decision worth? Should the system run one analysis or twenty competing ones? Should a routine case receive a cheap model and an unusual case receive a stronger one? Is the value of another simulation larger than its compute cost and the review time it creates?

Those questions sound unfamiliar only because human cognition was rarely priced that way inside ordinary workflows. Salaries came in monthly blocks. Attention was allocated through management.

AI makes the marginal cost of another pass more explicit.

Public institutions will need similar discipline. A city should not run expensive models indiscriminately merely because the invoice is hidden inside a technology contract. It should spend machine effort where more analysis can improve a decision and use simpler automation where the task is mechanical.

Cheap intelligence still has an opportunity cost.

The point is that the cost may become low enough to ask questions institutions previously could not afford to ask.

## Cheap first drafts change institutions

Many institutions are built around the cost of producing a competent first pass.

A planning memo takes time. A contract has to be assembled. A set of comments must be reconciled. A report has to be summarized for a decision-maker. A permit application needs to be checked for completeness. An engineering team searches old projects for comparable conditions.

When the first pass becomes cheap, the queue can shrink.

It can also grow.

People submit more proposals because proposals are easier to produce. Managers ask for more scenarios because scenarios are cheaper. Agencies request more documentation because documentation can be generated. Consultants produce more alternatives because alternatives no longer consume the same labor.

This is a recurring pattern in technology.

Lower cost can reduce burden per unit while increasing the number of units.

The photocopier did not eliminate paper. Email did not eliminate correspondence. Cheap photography did not make people take fewer pictures.

AI may do the same to analysis.

A government that does not redesign its decision process can drown in inexpensive intelligence.

The scarce resource becomes the authority to stop analyzing and decide.

## The proposal explosion

Cheap generation does not only change work inside organizations.

It changes what arrives from outside.

A procurement office may receive more polished bids. A planning department may receive more professionally drafted objections and applications. A grant program can receive more submissions because applicants no longer need the same writing capacity. Legislators can be flooded with individually phrased correspondence generated from common campaigns.

The old signal in polished writing weakens.

A beautiful proposal once implied a certain amount of effort. It never guaranteed quality, but the effort acted as a filter. When the cost of presentation collapses, institutions need stronger ways to evaluate substance.

This favors structured evidence.

Show the site control. Show the engineering assumptions. Show the financing. Show the source for the legal claim. Demonstrate the prototype. Provide references that can be checked. Separate the applicant's assertion from verified data.

AI can help reviewers process the larger volume.

It can also create the larger volume.

The equilibrium will depend on whether institutions redesign gates around information quality rather than document production.

A world of cheap proposals needs more expensive proof.

## The verification tax

Cheap generation creates a new cost: checking.

If an expert writes a technical memo from scratch, the writing process itself forces some confrontation with the evidence. If a model produces a polished memo in seconds, the surface quality can exceed the reliability of the underlying reasoning.

The reviewer therefore inherits a verification task.

This matters especially in public works because the consequences leave the screen.

A fabricated legal citation can delay a procurement. A mistaken quantity can affect a bid. A bad geotechnical assumption can become a field problem. A permit condition applied to the wrong parcel can impose real cost. A maintenance recommendation based on incomplete records can redirect scarce crews away from a more serious risk.

The productivity gain from AI should therefore be measured net of verification.

For low-stakes work, the check may be light. For high-stakes engineering, legal, financial, or safety decisions, it may remain substantial.

The key is to redesign the workflow so machines handle the parts they are good at while responsibility remains legible.

A model can compare two specifications line by line. A licensed engineer should still own the design decision.

A model can summarize public comments. An agency should still know which comments affected the decision and why.

Cheap intelligence is most useful when the checking architecture is deliberate.

## Verification can be cheaper than generation only if sources survive

A generated claim is easier to verify when the evidence travels with it.

This sounds obvious and is frequently violated.

An assistant produces an answer. The user receives polished prose. The path from source to statement disappears. The human then has to recreate the research from scratch.

That turns cheap generation into expensive checking.

Public-sector systems should be designed in the opposite direction.

Retrieve the governing code provision. Link the inspection photograph. show the historical work order. Preserve the calculation. Record which version of a specification was compared. Distinguish model inference from source fact.

The goal is not citation theater.

It is reducing the distance between a claim and the evidence required to accept or reject it.

This matters enormously for institutional memory. If the AI-produced summary becomes the only retained record, future employees inherit an interpretation without the underlying file. If sources remain attached, future systems can reinterpret the evidence as models improve.

Provenance is productivity infrastructure.

It lowers the long-run verification tax.

## Expertise changes shape

When information becomes easier to retrieve, expertise does not disappear.

It moves.

Knowing facts from memory may become less valuable in some settings. Knowing which facts matter can become more valuable. Producing a standard draft may become less scarce. Recognizing when the standard case does not apply can become more scarce.

This favors forms of expertise grounded in judgment and contact with reality.

An experienced water operator may know that a sensor reading is technically possible but inconsistent with what the plant usually does before a pump fails. A project manager may recognize that a contractor's schedule is formally complete but impossible because the same crane is assigned to two places. An inspector may see a crack pattern that does not match the most common category suggested by software.

AI can give these people better recall and broader access to technical material.

It can also tempt organizations to believe that retrieved information substitutes for accumulated judgment.

The distinction is similar to the difference between a map and wayfinding.

A detailed map is enormously useful. It does not mean the traveler has encountered the washed-out road.

The value of expertise may increasingly lie in knowing when the map is wrong.

## Tacit knowledge becomes more valuable when explicit knowledge is cheap

Some knowledge can be written down cleanly.

Codes. formulas. manuals. prior cases. equipment specifications. standard procedures.

Other knowledge is difficult to express until a situation demands it.

The sound a pump makes. The tone of a negotiation. The sequence that actually works in a crowded mechanical room. The neighborhood intersection that floods even though the official map says it should not. The vendor whose schedule looks plausible until one particular submittal appears.

AI makes explicit knowledge easier to retrieve and recombine.

That can increase the value of tacit knowledge because it becomes a larger share of what remains scarce.

Organizations should respond by finding ways to capture enough context for tacit knowledge to travel without pretending it can be fully reduced to text.

Field photographs. narrated repair notes. post-project reviews. mentoring. simulations. video. direct participation by operators in design.

The machine can help organize these traces.

It cannot turn every experienced judgment into a rule.

A productive institution knows which knowledge it can encode and which knowledge it needs to keep embodied in people.

## The apprenticeship problem

Automation creates a specific institutional danger when it removes the tasks through which novices learned.

Junior lawyers once reviewed documents partly because someone had to do it. Young engineers performed calculations that software now handles. Analysts built spreadsheets whose formulas taught them how a business worked. Entry-level programmers fixed small bugs and learned a codebase.

If AI performs these tasks, organizations gain productivity immediately.

They may lose training capacity later.

A senior expert does not appear fully formed. The difficult judgment praised in automation debates usually rests on years of exposure to routine cases.

The solution cannot be to preserve pointless manual work forever.

It is to design apprenticeship explicitly.

Trainees can review AI output against known answers. Simulations can expose them to rare cases. Supervisors can require explanation rather than mere acceptance. Rotations can move people into field settings where the physical system resists abstraction. Credentials can test judgment under conditions where the machine is intentionally withheld or where its answer must be challenged.

AI can become a tutor as well as an automator.

But somebody has to decide that producing future experts is an organizational objective.

Markets under short-term pressure may not do that automatically.

## The organization is the unit of productivity

A worker can save an hour without an organization saving an hour.

This sounds paradoxical until you watch coordination.

Suppose an engineer drafts a report twice as fast. If the report still waits six days for review, the project schedule barely changes. Suppose a procurement analyst summarizes bids instantly. If the award must still pass through five sequential approvals, cycle time remains dominated by the handoffs.

AI productivity therefore depends on process redesign.

The relevant unit is often the organization, not the individual user.

This helps explain why spectacular demonstrations can coexist with modest measured effects. A tool may be genuinely powerful inside a workflow whose other constraints remain untouched.

Public agencies should be especially careful here because they face legal and procedural dependencies that firms can sometimes change more easily.

Buying licenses for every employee is not transformation.

A better question is: which queue governs the outcome?

If permit review is limited by incomplete applications, use AI to catch omissions before submission. If it is limited by a legal requirement for a hearing, faster document drafting will not change the date. If procurement is limited by unclear scope, an automated evaluation system may simply score confusion more quickly.

The bottleneck moves inside organizations too.

## The productivity paradox can be a redesign lag

General-purpose technologies often arrive before organizations know how to use them well.

Early adoption can add cost before it removes cost.

Employees keep the old process and add the new tool. Managers require human review of every automated output without removing earlier approvals. Data have to be cleaned. Systems need integration. Security teams add controls. Workers learn new interfaces while still meeting normal deadlines.

The first year can look less productive.

This does not prove the technology is useless.

It may mean the complementary organizational change has not happened.

The reverse danger is believing every disappointing result is merely a temporary transition. Some tools genuinely fail to create enough value.

Public agencies need staged evaluation.

Measure the baseline. Identify the task. Track error, cycle time, staff burden, and service outcomes. Redesign the process where the tool proves reliable. Stop where it does not.

The adoption curve should contain exits.

A technology capable of improving everything can become an excuse to evaluate nothing.

## Demand may rise when intelligence gets cheaper

Automation is often pictured as a fixed amount of work being divided between humans and machines.

That is only one possibility.

When a service becomes cheaper, people may consume more of it.

If basic legal analysis costs less, more small businesses may seek it. If design iterations become cheaper, more projects may explore alternatives. If translation becomes near-instant, organizations may communicate across more languages. If tutoring becomes inexpensive, students may use more hours of individualized instruction than schools could previously provide.

This can preserve or even increase human demand in complementary roles.

More designs can mean more need for people who approve, construct, sell, or operate what was designed. More software can mean more integration, security, and maintenance. More analysis can mean more need for decision-makers capable of choosing among options.

The direction is not guaranteed.

In some markets, the same output really will require fewer workers. Firms will reduce head count. Occupations will shrink.

The point is that task automation does not mechanically map to employment loss one for one.

Price, demand, organizational redesign, and new complementary work determine the result.

## Why physical work looks different

AI's early strengths are concentrated in environments already represented digitally.

Text. Images. Code. Documents. Databases. Communications.

Physical work has another translation layer.

A machine must perceive the environment, move through it, apply force, remain safe around people, tolerate weather and variation, and recover when the world differs from the model.

This does not make physical work immune to automation.

Industrial robots have transformed factories for decades. Construction equipment contains increasing automation. Warehouses use sophisticated machines. Autonomous systems continue to improve.

But the economics are different from deploying software.

A new model can be copied cheaply to many users. A robot needs hardware, maintenance, power, space, integration, and often site-specific adaptation.

That difference can raise the relative value of physical execution during a period of rapid cognitive automation.

An AI system may produce more engineering designs. Someone still has to build them.

A model may identify failing infrastructure. Someone still has to excavate the street, replace the component, test it, and restore service.

This is why the physical economy returns as a central constraint rather than a nostalgic sector.

## Robotics inherits infrastructure dependence

The line between cheap cognition and expensive action will not stay fixed.

Robots will improve.

When they do, they will bring their own complement requirements.

Charging. maintenance. spare parts. communications. safe operating zones. machine-readable plans. standardized components. technicians. insurance. rules governing operation around people.

A construction robot that works brilliantly on one controlled site may be uneconomic where every project uses different dimensions and data formats. An autonomous vehicle benefits from roads whose markings and geometry are legible. Warehouse robots thrive in buildings designed around their movement.

Physical automation can therefore increase the return to standardization.

It can also reward rebuilding environments around machines in ways that make them worse for people if public goals are weak.

The question is not whether infrastructure should become robot-friendly.

It is whether machine compatibility can be added while preserving safety, accessibility, resilience, and human usability.

The built environment will increasingly serve both human and machine agents.

That is a design problem, not merely a robotics problem.

## Management becomes more powerful and more dangerous

Cheap intelligence can expand what one manager can see.

A model can summarize hundreds of project updates, identify schedule conflicts, draft follow-up questions, compare budgets, and flag anomalies across a portfolio.

This can reduce the information loss that grows with organizational scale.

It can also create an illusion of control.

A dashboard assembled from weak data can look authoritative. A summary can flatten disagreement. A risk score can encourage executives to believe uncertainty has been measured when it has only been categorized.

The manager who gains a larger field of view needs stronger habits of verification, not weaker ones.

This is particularly important in government, where local operators often hold knowledge that centralized systems cannot easily encode.

An AI system may tell a state agency that a project is ninety percent complete because submitted milestones say so. A field engineer may know that the remaining ten percent contains the difficult utility relocation that controls the opening date.

Management intelligence is useful when it creates better questions for the people closest to the work.

It is dangerous when it becomes a reason not to listen to them.

## Cheap persuasion is not cheap legitimacy

Language models can draft explanations, responses, notices, and public-facing material quickly.

Government communication may improve as a result.

Forms can be translated. Technical rules can be explained in ordinary language. Residents can ask questions without knowing which department owns the answer. Public comments can be categorized and routed more efficiently.

These are valuable uses.

They should not be confused with legitimacy.

A beautifully written explanation of an arbitrary decision remains an arbitrary decision. A chatbot that sounds empathetic does not create an appeal right. A synthetic summary of community feedback does not replace the obligation to show what the agency heard and how it responded.

Cheap language can make institutions more accessible.

It can also make bad institutions sound better than they are.

The safeguard is procedural truth: clear rules, traceable decisions, human responsibility, and recourse.

The more fluent the interface becomes, the more important it is to know what lies behind it.

## Labor income and the transition

If AI becomes a strong substitute for some knowledge work, the effects will not be evenly distributed.

A worker whose tasks are complemented may become more productive and more valuable. Another may face reduced demand. A third may find that the occupation survives but entry-level positions disappear. Geographic effects can differ as firms concentrate or disperse.

Aggregate productivity statistics will not resolve these experiences.

A country can become richer while particular people become poorer.

That creates a policy obligation distinct from infrastructure.

Training can help when new demand is real. Wage insurance can soften a move into lower-paid work. Mobility assistance can help people reach opportunity. Income support can prevent a technology transition from becoming an immediate household crisis. Labor standards and bargaining institutions influence how gains are shared.

The right mix is contestable.

What should not be contestable is the need to address worker transition directly rather than forcing every productive system to preserve old task counts.

A public-works program can create demand for valuable physical skills.

It should not become a warehouse for labor society is afraid to help in clearer ways.

## The public-sector opportunity

Government itself contains enormous volumes of cognitive administration.

Applications. Inspections. Grants. contracts. correspondence. eligibility rules. legal research. meeting records. maintenance histories. procurement files. regulatory comments. case management.

Some of this work is necessary because public decisions require records and due process.

Some is friction produced by fragmented systems.

AI creates an opportunity to distinguish the two.

A grant officer should not spend hours manually copying fields between systems if software can do it reliably. An inspector should not search twenty folders for the most recent drawing. A resident should not need to know the internal organization chart to report a broken hydrant.

The gain from automation should appear as shorter queues, more accurate service, better oversight, and more staff time for difficult cases.

If it appears only as more internal documentation, the institution has converted productivity into bureaucracy.

Public-sector AI should therefore be evaluated through service outcomes.

Did the permit cycle get shorter without more errors? Did invoices get paid sooner? Did maintenance crews arrive before failures? Did fewer residents need to call twice?

The point is not adoption.

The point is capacity.

## Cheap expertise can travel farther

Professional expertise is distributed unevenly across geography and organizational size.

A large city can employ specialist engineers, lawyers, planners, procurement staff, and data teams. A small town may need the same categories occasionally and be unable to keep them all on payroll.

AI changes the economics of intermittent expertise.

A source-grounded assistant can help a small utility understand a rule before calling a consultant. A county engineer can search comparable designs from across a state. A local procurement officer can receive help interpreting standard clauses. A rural clinic can reach decision support that previously lived in a distant institution.

The system should not pretend to replace the specialist when the stakes require one.

It can improve the question that reaches the specialist and reduce the time spent on routine retrieval.

This is one of the most promising distributional effects of cheap intelligence.

It can make institutional size less determinative of access to basic cognitive capacity.

Public systems should exploit that possibility deliberately rather than assuming consumer AI will solve it automatically.

The best shared assistants will be grounded in authoritative material, tailored to jurisdiction, and connected to human escalation.

Intelligence becomes infrastructure when access to useful expertise stops depending entirely on whether a town can afford another full-time desk.

## What gets cheaper next

The most consequential effects of AI may come from combinations that are still difficult to observe.

Cheaper design plus automated manufacturing. Better forecasting plus flexible electricity demand. Faster scientific search plus laboratory robotics. Language models plus field-service diagnostics. Computer vision plus maintenance systems. Generative engineering plus modular construction.

These combinations connect cognition to the physical world.

They can increase the return to infrastructure because more possible projects become economically feasible.

They can also expose infrastructure scarcity faster.

If a small team can design and operate a new industrial process with much less administrative overhead, the limiting factor may become the site and its utilities. If software reduces the cost of starting firms, access to power, housing, logistics, and permits can matter more to competition.

This is the larger shift.

Cheap intelligence does not make everything cheap.

It rearranges the price system around what remains hard.

## The discipline of uncertainty

No one knows the final shape of the labor market under advanced AI.

That sentence should survive every revision of this book.

The technology changes too quickly, adoption is uneven, and institutions respond. Forecasts can be useful for scenarios but dangerous as foundations for irreversible policy.

This is why infrastructure is such an attractive complement strategy.

The country does not need to know exactly which occupation will shrink in 2032 to know that reliable electricity is useful. It does not need a precise model of white-collar displacement to know that affordable housing improves worker mobility. It does not need certainty about robotics to improve procurement and maintenance.

Good policy can be robust to technological uncertainty.

Build systems that increase the range of futures the country can handle.

## Intelligence is not completion

A model can propose a hundred ways to solve a problem.

Someone must still choose one.

A model can draft the plan.

Someone must fund it, permit it, sign it, build it, inspect it, operate it, and maintain it.

That chain is where cheap intelligence meets expensive reality.

The danger of the AI era is not only mass unemployment or runaway machines or any of the other futures that dominate public imagination.

There is a quieter danger.

We may become extraordinarily good at describing what should be done while remaining institutionally bad at doing it.

The supply of plans could become infinite.

Completion will still be scarce.

That is why the rest of this book moves from the model to the country it has to work through.
