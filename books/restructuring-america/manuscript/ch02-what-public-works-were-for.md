# Chapter 2: What Public Works Were For

A public work begins as an argument about time.

Someone pays now for something that is supposed to remain useful later.

That sounds simple until the object becomes expensive enough to outlast the people who approved it. A bridge may serve voters who were not born when the bond was issued. A water plant may still be operating after every elected official who cut the ribbon has left office. A park planted as saplings can become most valuable when the people who chose the trees are gone.

This is one reason infrastructure has always carried more political meaning than its engineering drawings suggest.

It asks a community to act as though the future has standing.

In the United States, the strongest memory of public works is often the New Deal: roads, schools, dams, parks, post offices, courthouses, trails, water systems, public art, and an alphabet of agencies created during economic emergency. The memory is useful because it shows what large public programs can leave behind.

It is also dangerous if it becomes nostalgia.

The institutions of the 1930s were built for a different economy, a different legal environment, a different labor market, and a political order that excluded many Americans from equal participation. The lesson is not to recreate the Works Progress Administration with laptops.

The lesson is to remember why societies build together in the first place.

## Turning a flow into a stock

A public budget is a flow.

Money is collected, borrowed, appropriated, and spent over time. A recovery program is temporary. A tax year ends. A technology boom eventually changes form.

A useful public work turns some of that flow into a stock.

A bridge remains after the appropriation is exhausted. A water system keeps delivering service after the construction payroll ends. A transmission corridor preserves options for later generators and loads. A park becomes more useful as its trees mature. A well-designed permitting institution can keep lowering the cost of decisions after the reform team has left.

This conversion is the durable economic logic of public works.

The asset does not have to be government-owned to matter. It does not have to be made of concrete. It does have to leave behind capacity that survives the transaction that created it.

That definition creates a demanding test.

Did the spending leave something useful after the money moved? Does the capacity widen what households, firms, or communities can do? Can the asset or institution be maintained? Does its value survive the fate of the one contractor, company, administration, or forecast that justified it?

A temporary boom can produce temporary consumption.

It can also produce a stronger balance sheet.

The argument of this book is that an AI productivity boom should do some of the second.

## Relief was one purpose

The Great Depression made employment an immediate public problem.

When private demand collapsed, millions of people who were ready to work could not find useful paid work. Public employment programs addressed that emergency directly. They put people on payrolls while constructing assets communities needed.

That combination has shaped the American imagination ever since.

Public works became associated with jobs.

The association is understandable. Construction is visible labor. Crews, equipment, scaffolding, and earthmoving make employment easy to count. Political leaders can point to a project and say both that something was built and that people were paid to build it.

But jobs were never the only purpose of the asset.

If employment alone were the goal, the durability of the bridge would not matter. The road would not need to go anywhere useful. The school could be placed where no children lived.

The object mattered because it was supposed to produce value after the relief program ended.

This distinction becomes critical in the AI era.

If automation can perform part of construction more cheaply or safely, preserving inefficient methods merely to maximize labor hours can undermine the public purpose. Worker security matters. Employment during downturns matters. Transition policy matters.

The infrastructure itself still exists to be useful.

That is the line this book will keep drawing.

## The federal dollar met the local project

The New Deal image can make public works look more centralized than they were.

Washington could raise money and create programs at a scale local governments could not match during the Depression. The actual projects still had addresses.

Local officials proposed sites. States administered programs. Engineers adapted plans to terrain. Communities supplied rights-of-way or operating commitments. Federal agencies brought money, standards, employment, and technical capacity into relationships with governments that already owned streets, schools, parks, water systems, and public land.

That intergovernmental structure mattered.

A national government can finance broad recovery. It cannot know every drainage problem in a town. A local government can know the drainage problem and still have no way to fund the culvert during a fiscal collapse.

Public works worked best when each level supplied something the other lacked.

The same problem returns in a richer country.

A small water utility may know exactly which plant needs replacement and lack bond capacity or specialist staff. A federal agency can offer financing and technical standards but may know little about local operations. A state can pool engineering or procurement expertise across communities.

The AI era does not erase federalism.

It can lower the cost of connecting the levels.

Shared data, source-grounded technical assistance, common application formats, and reusable project templates can make national capacity easier to use without turning local projects into federal ones.

This is a more durable lesson than any specific 1930s agency name.

## Capacity was the durable purpose

Public works expand what a place can do.

A road allows trips that were previously too costly. A sewer permits density that septic systems cannot support. A power line makes industrial equipment usable. A reservoir changes the reliability of water supply. A port expands the feasible scale of trade. A school creates human capacity that does not look like infrastructure on a map but changes the productive possibilities of a community.

Economists describe some of these effects as externalities or public goods. Engineers describe capacity. Households experience them as ordinary life.

The important feature is that the asset changes the opportunity set for other actors.

A privately owned factory can benefit from a publicly maintained road without owning the road. A new apartment building can connect to water and sewer systems built decades earlier. A small business can reach customers over communications infrastructure it did not finance alone.

This is why infrastructure is often complementary to private investment rather than a substitute for it.

The public builds or coordinates a platform. Private actors make thousands of decisions on top of it.

That logic is more relevant, not less, when AI becomes widely available.

If advanced software is accessible to many firms, advantage can move toward the complementary systems that let them act on what the software enables. A company can generate an excellent factory design and still need a site, power, freight access, workers, permits, suppliers, and financing.

Shared capacity determines how many firms can convert intelligence into production.

## Rural electrification was a network problem

Electricity illustrates a different public-works purpose: extending a network where private economics left gaps.

Dense cities and profitable industrial loads were attractive customers early. Sparse rural service was harder. Long lines had to reach fewer users, and the initial capital could be difficult to justify under ordinary private investment criteria.

The policy response combined public finance, institutions, cooperatives, and technical standards to extend service.

The result was not merely more wires.

Electrification changed what farms, homes, workshops, schools, and small businesses could do. It enabled appliances, pumps, refrigeration, machinery, communications, and new forms of production. The infrastructure did not specify the uses. It widened the feasible set.

This is the public-platform logic in unusually clear form.

The AI analogy should be handled carefully. Intelligence services do not require a government wire to every home in the same way electricity did. Private firms may provide AI cheaply to anyone with connectivity.

The relevant public complement lies elsewhere.

Can a rural hospital use the tool because it has broadband, staff, and reliable power? Can a small town use machine assistance because its records are digitized and its rules are accessible? Can a local manufacturer act on better design because freight, electricity, and skilled technicians exist?

A network becomes transformative when the edge can use what the network delivers.

Public works are often about strengthening that edge.

## The old programs were not magic

It is easy to photograph surviving New Deal structures and forget everything that went wrong around them.

Public works have displaced communities. Highway construction divided neighborhoods and helped destroy urban districts, often with racially unequal effects. Water projects reshaped rivers and ecosystems. Large dams produced electricity and irrigation while imposing irreversible environmental and cultural costs. Programs operated inside segregated labor markets and discriminatory institutions. Local political machines influenced where money went.

A serious building agenda has to carry this history with it.

The conclusion should not be that building is inherently oppressive or that delay is inherently protective.

The conclusion is that physical decisions distribute benefits and harms through geography.

A road has a route. A reservoir has an inundation area. A transmission line has a corridor. A housing policy changes land values. A flood project can protect one place while transferring water elsewhere.

The more powerful the public works program, the more seriously those distributions must be governed.

That means environmental review with an actual purpose. It means compensation when property is taken. It means examining displacement before it becomes a fait accompli. It means giving communities information early enough to change design rather than inviting them to comment on a nearly finished plan.

It also means making decisions.

Past harm is not repaired by building nothing. A society that cannot add housing, transmission, clean-energy infrastructure, transit, water capacity, or resilience projects can create different forms of harm through scarcity, high prices, pollution, and vulnerability.

The question is how to build with more knowledge than earlier generations had.

## Standardization made scale possible

Large building programs depend on repetition.

Not identical buildings everywhere. Repeated interfaces.

Materials have grades. Drawings use conventions. Roads use standard signs and markings. Electrical systems use compatible voltages and equipment. Procurement relies on standard clauses. Engineers can move between projects because technical language does not restart at every county line.

This shared vocabulary is a form of public infrastructure.

It makes scale cheaper because every project does not have to negotiate every interface from first principles.

The danger is that standards can freeze mistakes.

A bad detail repeated nationally becomes a national problem. A design assumption suited to one climate may be copied into another. A specification can preserve an obsolete vendor or material long after better options exist.

The right lesson is not “standardize everything.”

It is “standardize the seams, then learn.”

AI increases the value of this discipline because machines are exceptionally good at working across structured, consistent information. A national bridge-inspection dataset becomes more useful when condition terms mean roughly the same thing. A procurement assistant becomes more reliable when standard clauses have stable identifiers.

Public works scale through shared language before they scale through machines.

## Infrastructure made markets larger

One of the quiet effects of public works is that they expand the geographic range of competition.

A better road connects a town to more suppliers. A reliable port lets manufacturers reach distant customers. Standardized infrastructure lowers the cost of entering a market. Communications systems allow services to be delivered across distance.

The relationship between infrastructure and competition is often missed because policy categories separate them.

Antitrust is discussed in one room. Transportation in another. Housing in another. Energy in another.

Yet a bottleneck can create market power even when no firm deliberately monopolizes anything.

If only one site has a feasible grid connection, the owner of that site has leverage. If a city permits very little housing near its job centers, existing landowners benefit from restricted access. If freight must move through one congested corridor, delay becomes a tax on every competitor using it.

Public works can reduce these scarcity rents by increasing shared capacity.

This is one reason abundance matters beyond consumer convenience.

It changes bargaining power.

The public role is not to guarantee every firm equal success. It is to keep common bottlenecks from becoming unnecessary private tollbooths.

## The postwar lesson was scale

After the New Deal came a different era of American building.

The postwar decades produced highways, suburban water systems, schools, airports, power plants, transmission, housing, research facilities, and vast private construction organized around public rules and finance.

The achievements were enormous.

So were the costs.

Highway systems connected regions and expanded freight mobility while demolishing neighborhoods and reinforcing automobile dependence. Suburban infrastructure helped millions of households obtain homes while exclusionary land-use systems kept many others out. Cheap energy supported industrial growth while pollution costs were often ignored.

The useful lesson is that infrastructure shapes development patterns for generations.

Capacity does not merely accommodate growth. It directs it.

A new interchange changes land value. A sewer extension changes which parcels can urbanize. A rail station changes the geography of access. A transmission line changes which generation resources can reach load.

That means an AI-era public works strategy cannot be a list of projects selected independently.

The pieces create a spatial pattern.

Where power becomes available will influence where data centers and factories locate. Where housing can be built will influence which labor markets can grow. Where transit and roads provide access will influence who can reach new opportunity.

The map is part of the policy.

## Network completion is different from network expansion

Early infrastructure eras often asked whether a place would be connected at all.

A road where no road existed. Electric service where none reached. A sewer in a growing district. A telephone line to a remote community.

Mature networks pose a different problem.

The connection may exist but perform poorly.

A neighborhood has electricity, but the feeder cannot support new electrification. A town has broadband, but one middle-mile route creates fragility. A city has transit, but frequencies make transfers impractical. A region has housing, but not enough near jobs. A utility has water mains, but replacement has fallen decades behind.

The public works of a mature society are often about quality, redundancy, capacity, and renewal rather than first connection.

This can make the politics harder because the benefit is incremental rather than binary.

The household already has water. Why pay more to replace the pipe before it fails?

The region already has transmission. Why build another corridor before the queue becomes impossible?

The answer is that networks can become bottlenecks long before they disappear.

AI-era public works will often be the less dramatic work of completing, strengthening, and renewing networks people already take for granted.

## What changed after the building era

By the late twentieth century, the United States was a richer and more built-out country.

That changed the political economy of infrastructure.

Many projects were no longer extending basic networks into places with none. They were modifying systems inside developed communities. Rights-of-way were occupied. Environmental consequences were better understood. Property owners had more legal and political tools to resist disruption. Public participation grew. Major projects became more complex and expensive.

These changes were not simply signs of decline.

Many corrected real abuses.

Environmental law forced governments and firms to account for harms that earlier builders externalized. Civil-rights law challenged discriminatory programs. Safety standards improved. Disability rights changed the design of public space. Public procurement rules responded to corruption and favoritism.

The problem is accumulation without redesign.

A rule written to solve one failure remains. Another is added for the next failure. Agencies build procedures around the rules. Courts interpret them. Consultants emerge to navigate them. Over decades, the process can become difficult to understand even when every individual requirement has a defensible origin.

Mature institutions need maintenance too.

That includes removing procedural layers that no longer improve outcomes.

## Build, operate, maintain

The heroic image of public works centers on construction.

The durable value often comes from operation.

A treatment plant is not useful because it was completed. It is useful because operators show up for the next forty years, chemicals arrive, pumps are serviced, controls are calibrated, records are kept, and the utility collects enough revenue to maintain the system.

The same is true of transit, roads, parks, schools, and power systems.

Public investment therefore creates future obligations.

Every new asset adds something to a maintenance inventory. A city that accepts federal money for a new facility also accepts roofs, elevators, pavement, wiring, software, landscaping, security, insurance, and replacement schedules.

Past building booms sometimes hid these lifecycle costs because the initial construction was the visible political achievement.

An AI-era building program should make them explicit.

Digital asset records can help. Sensors can help. Better forecasting can help. None of those matter if the operating budget remains structurally inadequate.

The best project can be the one a government is capable of caring for.

## A public asset can fail fiscally before it fails physically

Infrastructure condition is not only an engineering matter.

A system can remain physically functional while its financial model deteriorates.

A shrinking water utility loses customers while fixed costs remain. A transit system loses fare revenue while vehicles and track still require maintenance. A toll road underperforms its forecast. A school district inherits more buildings than enrollment justifies. A public facility receives a grant for construction but no durable operating revenue.

The asset still stands.

The institution carrying it weakens.

This is why lifecycle finance belongs in design from the beginning. Who pays for operation? How sensitive is the system to population or demand changes? Which costs are fixed? What happens if a subsidy ends? Can rates rise without becoming unaffordable?

AI can model scenarios quickly, but a model cannot rescue a project whose operating bargain was never plausible.

Public works are promises of service, not merely promises of construction.

## Public works and dignity

Infrastructure has an economic function and a civic one.

A public building communicates how the state expects people to encounter it. A park says something about who is entitled to beauty and leisure. A station tells riders whether their time and bodies are respected. A school building influences how students experience the importance of the institution inside it.

This does not justify decorative excess while basic services fail.

It does mean that public works should not be evaluated only as pipes carrying quantified throughput.

A shaded sidewalk can make an elderly person able to walk outside in summer. A clean restroom can determine whether a family stays in a park. An elevator determines whether a station exists at all for someone using a wheelchair.

These are capacity questions with human form.

The physical public realm is one of the few places where citizens who did not choose one another still share an environment.

That becomes more significant as digital life becomes individualized.

AI can personalize media, shopping, education, entertainment, and work. Physical commons push in the other direction. They place different people in the same system and require rules of mutual accommodation.

Public works are therefore partly about making citizenship tangible.

## The emergency temptation

Large building programs often arrive after crisis.

Depression. War. Disaster. Infrastructure failure. Energy shock.

Emergency creates political permission to act quickly.

It also creates bad incentives.

Governments under pressure can skip careful design, overpay, accept weak controls, or build capacity that makes sense only under temporary conditions. Private firms can exploit urgency. Communities can be told that consultation must wait because the situation is exceptional.

A serious state should not need catastrophe to maintain itself.

One ambition of the maintenance state described later in this book is to move public works from episodic rescue toward routine stewardship.

The same applies to AI.

If policymakers wait until data-center demand overwhelms a region, housing prices spike, or a labor transition becomes politically explosive, emergency measures will look attractive. Better to build capacity while there is still room to choose.

This is the opposite of technological panic.

It is preparation for several plausible futures.

## The financing question

Long-lived assets create an intuitive case for long-lived finance.

Borrowing allows the people who benefit from an asset over time to share its cost over time. Federal grants can move resources toward jurisdictions that cannot raise enough capital alone. User fees can align payment with service. Property taxes can capture some of the location value created by public investment. Public-private structures can place some risks with firms equipped to manage them.

None of these instruments is automatically virtuous.

Debt can finance bad projects. User fees can burden low-income households. Grants can reward political skill rather than project quality. Public-private partnerships can hide expensive guarantees. Value capture can depend on optimistic development assumptions.

Financing should follow a clear account of who benefits, who can pay, who bears risk, and who controls the asset.

AI can improve scenario analysis and contract review. It cannot make a weak bargain strong.

The most important financing discipline is to avoid confusing available money with valuable investment.

A program becomes dangerous when the question shifts from *What should we build?* to *How do we spend the allocation?*

The second question creates projects to serve the funding stream.

The first asks the funding stream to serve the project.

## Employment is a timing tool, not the asset's purpose

AI brings the jobs argument back with unusual force.

If software can perform tasks once associated with secure white-collar work, governments will face pressure to create employment through public investment. There is nothing inherently wrong with that response during a downturn. A society can reasonably accelerate useful construction when private demand is weak.

The distinction is between changing the timing of useful work and changing the purpose of the work.

If a recession releases construction labor and equipment, moving a worthy maintenance program forward can support employment without changing what the program is for. If automated equipment later allows a road crew to complete the same work safely with fewer hours, preserving the old labor input merely to keep the head count high makes the road more expensive for no public gain.

Accept the productivity.

Address the transition directly.

Chapter 16 returns to this problem in detail. The historical point here is simpler: relief can justify *when* useful public works are accelerated without becoming the permanent reason those assets exist.

## What the old builders would recognize

The technology would astonish them.

The institutional problems would not.

Projects still fail because scope is unclear, money arrives at the wrong time, agencies disagree, contractors discover conditions nobody documented, politicians promise schedules the physical work cannot meet, and maintenance is postponed because failure has not happened yet.

Public works have always been a coordination problem as much as an engineering problem.

That is why AI belongs here.

A technology good at searching records, comparing documents, forecasting schedules, translating technical material, and assisting design can improve the coordination layer. It can help institutions remember. It can give a project manager a wider field of view.

But it cannot substitute for legitimacy, authority, craft, or the willingness to make a decision that creates winners and losers.

The old lesson survives the new tool.

Public works are where collective intentions become physical obligations.

## What they are for now

An AI-era public works program should have a clearer purpose than “build more.”

It should convert some of a potentially temporary productivity flow into durable shared capacity.

More reliable and flexible energy. More transmission. More housing in places people want and need to live. Water systems that can survive both age and changing climate. Transportation that reflects actual travel patterns. Maintenance systems capable of preserving valuable assets. Permitting that resolves real questions faster. Procurement that creates competition and learning. Construction markets allowed to automate and standardize where doing so improves value.

It should also build civic capacity where markets have little reason to do so alone: parks, libraries, public spaces, resilience, and institutions that make ordinary life less privately expensive.

Some of this resembles earlier public works.

Some of it is administrative and digital.

The common thread is not the material or the ownership form.

It is durable shared capacity: something useful remains after the appropriation, the construction trailer, the political slogan, and perhaps even the technology cycle that justified the urgency.

The New Deal is useful not because it offers a template but because it reminds Americans that economic transitions can leave physical institutions behind.

The question for this transition is what we want to leave.

Not a museum of human labor preserved against machines.

A stronger public balance sheet.

A country made more capable by them.
