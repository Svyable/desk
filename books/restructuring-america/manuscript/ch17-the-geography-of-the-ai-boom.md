# Chapter 17: The Geography of the AI Boom

Technology likes to describe itself as placeless.

The internet is global. Software scales everywhere. A model can be accessed from a farm, a subway car, a corporate tower, or a school library. Digital products cross borders without trucks.

The production of that placeless experience is intensely geographic.

Data centers concentrate where power, fiber, land, cooling, finance, tax policy, and permitting align. Semiconductor plants concentrate where water, electricity, technical labor, suppliers, and public incentives support them. Research firms cluster around universities, capital, talent, and existing networks. Advanced manufacturing follows its own maps of logistics, workforce, and industrial land.

AI will not spread economic activity evenly across the United States.

It will create new clusters, strengthen some old ones, and bypass other places entirely.

This is not a defect unique to AI. Economic activity has always clustered. Ports create cities. Coal creates mining regions. Automobile production creates supplier belts. Finance creates districts. Universities create research ecosystems.

The public-works question is what happens when the new map meets the old country.

## A data center is a local event

Nationally, a data center is computing capacity.

Locally, it is a land-use decision.

The facility may require hundreds of acres, substantial electric infrastructure, water depending on cooling design, backup generation, road access, and construction labor. It may provide significant property-tax revenue. It may create fewer permanent jobs than residents associate with a facility of similar physical scale.

This creates unusual economic-development politics.

Local officials are accustomed to competing for employers by counting jobs. A data center can justify itself through taxable capital, infrastructure investment, and strategic significance even when the permanent headcount is modest.

The community should evaluate the project on those terms rather than pretending it is a factory from 1975.

What power upgrades are required? Who pays? What water is consumed? How much revenue remains after tax abatements? What public services will the facility require? Does the project occupy scarce industrial land better suited to an employer with more jobs? Does its substation create capacity useful to nearby development? What happens if the operator closes or the equipment cycle changes?

These questions should be part of a standard large-load public bargain.

The goal is not to make approval impossible.

It is to stop negotiating from slogans.

AI infrastructure can be economically valuable with relatively few onsite jobs. A mature community can say yes for the right reasons.

## The substation is part of the development deal

Large-load projects often arrive in economic-development conversations before the utility system is ready to serve them.

That creates a hidden negotiation.

A new substation, feeder, transmission upgrade, generation resource, or other grid work may be required. The cost can be assigned to the customer, spread across utility ratepayers, shared under tariff rules, or recovered through a negotiated arrangement depending on jurisdiction.

Those choices affect who captures the project benefit.

If ordinary ratepayers finance infrastructure dedicated mainly to one facility, the subsidy may be larger than the tax abatement that receives public attention. If the customer pays for an upgrade that later serves the wider region, the bargain may be more favorable than the headline suggests.

Communities need a full infrastructure ledger.

AI can help model load growth and compare cost-allocation scenarios. Utility commissions still have to decide what is fair.

The economic-development agreement and the utility agreement should not live in separate political universes.

Power is part of the project whether or not city hall owns the wires.

## Power-rich places become strategic

For much of the digital era, electricity was a background operating cost for technology companies.

As AI computation becomes more power intensive, regions with abundant, reliable electricity gain strategic value.

This can reshape economic geography.

Places near hydroelectric resources, nuclear plants, gas systems, renewable generation, or strong transmission may attract new investment. Regions capable of permitting and building additional generation quickly can gain advantage. Areas with constrained grids may lose projects even if they possess skilled labor and land.

Power becomes industrial policy.

This creates an opportunity for regions left behind by earlier waves of growth.

A smaller metropolitan area with industrial land, a strong utility, community colleges, and existing transmission may be able to attract data centers, component manufacturing, or AI-enabled industry without recreating a coastal technology hub.

But power alone does not produce a healthy cluster.

If communities offer huge tax incentives for facilities that consume scarce grid capacity without creating complementary businesses, the result can be extraction rather than development. If housing supply cannot expand, new investment raises local costs. If contractors are imported for the entire buildout, little skill remains afterward.

Economic development should therefore ask about ecosystems, not projects.

Can local colleges train electricians and technicians needed by the facility and by the broader region? Can suppliers locate nearby? Does the grid expansion enable additional industry? Can the tax base support schools, parks, and infrastructure after incentives expire?

The best project changes what the region can do next.

## Fiber follows a different map

Power receives most of the attention because it is becoming a visible constraint.

AI infrastructure also needs communications.

Data centers depend on high-capacity fiber routes, redundancy, low latency for some workloads, and reliable connections to other facilities and users. A site with cheap land and abundant electricity can still be poorly positioned if its network connectivity is weak or dependent on one fragile route.

Fiber geography has its own path dependence.

Long-haul corridors, railroad rights-of-way, carrier hotels, metropolitan exchange points, undersea cable landings, and earlier waves of telecommunications investment shape where capacity is easy to extend.

Public policy can influence this map by coordinating conduit with road projects, preserving rights-of-way, supporting middle-mile infrastructure, and making pole and permitting processes predictable.

The goal should not be to build bespoke fiber for every AI facility.

It is to create shared communications capacity that makes more locations economically usable.

A region with both power and fiber has a different option set from one with only one of them.

## The second-city opportunity

Remote work briefly inspired a fantasy that high-value employment would disperse everywhere.

Some did disperse. Many firms and workers retained strong clustering preferences. People value deep labor markets, universities, airports, cultural institutions, professional networks, and the ability to change employers without moving.

AI could push in both directions.

If machine assistance reduces the value of being physically close to certain expertise, smaller cities may compete better. A regional engineering firm can access capabilities once concentrated in a large metropolitan office. A hospital in a smaller city can use AI-supported diagnostics and administration. Entrepreneurs can build with smaller teams.

At the same time, frontier research and capital may concentrate more tightly around elite institutions and companies.

The result could be a hierarchy with more capable second-tier cities but even more concentrated ownership at the top.

Public works can influence which way the balance moves.

A smaller city with affordable housing, reliable power, broadband, a good airport or rail connection, high-quality public space, and functioning schools can become much more attractive when access to cognitive tools is less geographically constrained.

This is a different regional-development strategy from chasing headquarters with subsidies.

Build a place where capable people can operate.

The AI tool comes from the cloud. The reasons to stay are local.

## Universities are regional infrastructure

Research universities are not only education providers.

They are anchors in regional innovation systems.

They train workers, operate laboratories, attract federal research money, create firms, convene networks, and provide technical expertise to public agencies and industry. Smaller colleges and community colleges play different but equally important roles in workforce pipelines.

AI may increase the value of these institutions even as it changes teaching.

A university with compute access, engineering expertise, energy research, public-policy capacity, and strong industry relationships can help a region understand and shape the technology rather than merely host it.

Public works can strengthen the connection.

Shared research facilities. reliable power. broadband. transit. housing for students and researchers. laboratory infrastructure. extension programs that bring expertise to firms and local governments.

A semiconductor fab or data center does not automatically create an innovation cluster.

Institutions that circulate knowledge do.

The public investment strategy should therefore notice the less visible infrastructure around the marquee facility.

## Rural infrastructure after intelligence scarcity

Rural communities have long faced a professional-capacity problem.

A county may need engineering, accounting, legal, medical, planning, and grant expertise without enough demand to support full-time specialists in every category. Distance raises the cost of access.

Broadband reduced some of that disadvantage. AI could reduce it further.

A small manufacturer can receive technical assistance. A farmer can query equipment manuals and agronomic material. A rural hospital can use decision support. A town can navigate infrastructure funding with a machine assistant grounded in current rules.

Again, this is not equivalent to having an experienced expert onsite.

It changes the threshold at which expert help becomes reachable.

Public policy should treat that capability as a complement to broadband. Connectivity without usable services is an empty pipe.

States can build shared technical-assistance platforms for rural governments, cooperatives, small businesses, and utilities. Universities can extend expertise through AI interfaces while maintaining human escalation for difficult cases.

This is a modern version of cooperative extension.

The institutional design matters because generic consumer AI may not have current state rules, local data, or accountability. Public systems can curate the knowledge base and make the source visible.

Rural development in the AI age may depend less on attracting one giant employer and more on raising the capability of many existing institutions.

## Cheap intelligence can make small firms less small

A rural manufacturer or regional contractor faces fixed administrative costs that a national firm spreads across much more revenue.

Estimating. marketing. compliance. document management. accounting. technical research. customer support. procurement navigation.

AI can lower some of those fixed costs.

That does not erase scale advantages in capital, purchasing, logistics, or distribution.

It can let a smaller firm operate with capabilities that previously required a larger office staff.

This matters geographically because small and medium-sized firms are often rooted in places where major corporations are not headquartered.

Public procurement can amplify the effect by making solicitations easier to navigate and paying faster. Technical colleges can help firms adopt digital tools. Regional manufacturing extension programs can connect AI with process improvement rather than selling software as a standalone product.

The development strategy is capability diffusion.

A region becomes stronger when many firms become a little more productive, not only when one giant project arrives.

## The company town returns as a server town

Rapid industrial development can overwhelm local governance.

A small jurisdiction approves a massive facility. Assessed value changes. Road use changes. Utility demand changes. Construction brings temporary population. Housing tightens. The company may become one of the largest taxpayers and political actors in the area.

This resembles an old American pattern: the company town, updated for capital-intensive infrastructure.

The company does not have to own the houses to shape the town.

Fiscal dependence can influence regulation. Local officials may lack the technical staff to negotiate with a corporation whose engineering and legal teams are larger than city hall. Confidentiality claims can limit public understanding of power and water agreements.

This asymmetry argues for regional and state technical support.

A town negotiating a 500-megawatt load should not have to invent contract expertise from scratch. State utility commissions, energy offices, public-interest technical teams, and standardized agreements can help local governments understand long-term risks.

AI can assist by comparing proposed contracts with precedent, flagging unusual clauses, and modeling fiscal outcomes under different scenarios.

But the machine should strengthen local bargaining capacity, not become a channel for corporate complexity.

Public works should reduce asymmetry.

## The construction boom arrives before the permanent economy

Large facilities create a workforce wave.

Thousands of workers can arrive for site preparation, electrical work, concrete, steel, mechanical systems, commissioning, and related construction before permanent staffing settles at a much lower level.

That temporary wave changes local systems.

Hotels fill. rents rise. traffic changes. schools may see temporary enrollment changes. emergency services respond to a larger daytime population. Roads experience heavy loads. Smaller local projects compete for the same trades.

Economic-development agreements should model the construction phase separately from the operating phase.

AI can help forecast labor categories and timing from project schedules.

Local governments can use those forecasts to plan traffic, temporary housing, inspections, public safety, and training.

A boomtown that plans only for the finished facility will experience the buildout as an emergency.

Construction is part of the geography of the project, not a prelude occurring offstage.

## The tax-base illusion

Large capital projects can create impressive assessed values.

Economic-development deals often reduce those taxes through abatements, payments in lieu of taxes, or special agreements intended to win competition with other jurisdictions.

The logic can be rational. A facility that would not locate in the region without an incentive may produce revenue even after the discount.

The problem is the counterfactual.

Would the project truly have gone elsewhere? How much subsidy was necessary? Does the facility create costs that offset the revenue? Are incentives tied to milestones or granted up front? What happens if the project is sold?

AI can make tax-incentive analysis more sophisticated, but it cannot observe the alternative universe in which the deal was different.

Governments should therefore be conservative about precision.

Publish the assumptions. Use ranges. Require clawbacks when commitments are not met. Separate construction jobs from permanent jobs. Include infrastructure costs and foregone revenue.

Most important, do not justify a low-employment project with inflated employment multipliers.

If the strategic case is power infrastructure, say power infrastructure. If the benefit is tax base, show the tax base. If the project anchors a research cluster, explain the mechanism.

Public trust improves when the argument matches the project.

## State competition can destroy bargaining power

Economic-development competition often pits jurisdictions against one another.

A company can credibly say another state is offering more. Officials fear losing a project and bid up incentives.

This can transfer value from taxpayers to mobile capital without changing the national location of investment very much.

The problem is difficult because individual states cannot easily coordinate when the prize is politically salient.

Regional standards, disclosure requirements, common cost accounting, and limits on certain incentive forms can reduce the race without eliminating legitimate competition on infrastructure, workforce, and quality of place.

AI can make incentive packages easier to compare across jurisdictions.

That transparency may strengthen public bargaining.

A state should know not only what another state offered but what similar projects actually delivered five years later.

Competition is healthier when places compete on durable capability rather than secret subsidies.

## Regional winners need regional housing

Economic booms punish housing systems that cannot respond.

A town of fifty thousand can absorb a few hundred new workers gradually. A sudden industrial project employing thousands during construction can overwhelm hotels, rentals, roads, and schools. A cluster of AI-related facilities can turn a previously affordable region into a constrained market quickly.

The housing chapter of economic development should therefore be written before the press release.

Temporary worker housing, infrastructure extensions, zoning capacity, and permanent housing production need coordinated plans. Nearby jurisdictions should share forecasts because workers will live across municipal boundaries.

AI can help estimate workforce timing and housing demand under multiple project scenarios.

The political decision is whether communities allow the units.

A region can receive billions in subsidized industrial investment and then refuse to permit the housing needed by the workers. The result is a public transfer to existing property owners through scarcity.

That is not a side effect.

It is a distributional choice.

## Water-rich and water-constrained are not permanent categories

Water questions around AI facilities are sometimes reduced to a simple map of wet and dry states.

The reality is more local.

A water-rich region can have a constrained municipal system, a vulnerable aquifer, or treatment capacity already committed to growth. A dry region can support certain facilities through reclaimed water, different cooling systems, or carefully planned supplies.

The relevant geography is watershed, utility, and technology together.

Communities should evaluate consumptive use, source reliability, drought conditions, treatment capacity, and the alternatives available to the facility.

AI can help model scenarios and compare cooling strategies.

The final resource bargain should be public enough to understand.

Water scarcity changes over time, and a forty-year facility should not be approved using one normal-year snapshot.

The map of AI infrastructure is partly a map of which regions can make long-term resource commitments credibly.

## Supply chains make clusters sticky

A major industrial plant becomes more valuable to a region when suppliers, service firms, and specialized labor accumulate around it.

This is how clusters become durable.

A semiconductor facility needs gases, chemicals, equipment service, precision components, logistics, construction expertise, and technical labor. A data-center region can support electrical contractors, cooling specialists, equipment maintenance, network firms, and component distribution.

Public policy often celebrates the anchor and underinvests in the supplier ecosystem.

Small suppliers need industrial space, financing, workforce, utility connections, and access to procurement opportunities.

AI can help anchor firms identify capable local suppliers and help smaller firms meet documentation and quality requirements.

The development metric should include how much capability remains transferable to other customers.

A supplier ecosystem tied completely to one company is fragile.

A cluster becomes regional wealth when its firms can sell beyond the anchor.

## Declining places still need maintenance

A national growth strategy naturally focuses on booming regions.

Public works cannot abandon places where population is flat or declining.

Infrastructure networks have high fixed costs. A water system built for fifty thousand people can become financially difficult when only thirty thousand remain. Roads, schools, fire stations, and public buildings do not shrink automatically with the tax base.

AI can help right-size services by identifying underused assets, consolidating facilities, optimizing routes, and forecasting maintenance needs.

But decline requires political decisions that growth avoids.

Which school closes? Which water lines are abandoned? Which roads receive lower service levels? Can neighborhoods be consolidated without destroying community?

These questions are painful because public works embody promises. Closing infrastructure can feel like the government admitting that a place has no future.

The humane approach is not to maintain every asset forever regardless of use.

It is to preserve dignity and essential service while reducing unsustainable obligations deliberately.

A shrinking town may need fewer buildings but better ones. It may convert vacant land into stormwater, habitat, agriculture, or parks. It may focus investment on a compact core where services can remain reliable.

AI can make options visible. The community decides what it wants to remain.

## Place-based policy needs a theory of place

Washington periodically rediscovers place-based policy.

Tax incentives target distressed zones. Grants prioritize disadvantaged communities. Agencies seek geographic equity. Industrial policy directs investment toward particular regions.

The programs often lack a clear theory of how a place becomes self-sustaining.

A factory subsidy is not a regional strategy. A broadband grant is not a regional strategy. A training center is not a regional strategy.

A place becomes economically capable when multiple systems reinforce one another: housing, education, infrastructure, firms, finance, institutions, amenities, and networks.

AI can help weaker regions access expertise and markets, but it cannot compensate for a city where power is unreliable, housing is deteriorating, or basic governance is broken.

Public works are the platform on which place-based policy has to stand.

This suggests a sequencing principle.

Repair the enabling systems before chasing the prestige project.

A reliable water system is economic development. A faster permitting office is economic development. A functioning downtown street is economic development. A community college tied to actual employer demand is economic development.

The project that makes the newspaper may arrive later because the place became capable of receiving it.

## Regional compacts can match regional systems

Labor markets, watersheds, transmission networks, housing markets, and commuting patterns cross municipal boundaries.

Economic-development incentives often do not.

A suburb can offer a facility incentives while neighboring jurisdictions house workers, carry traffic, or supply public services. One county can block housing while benefiting from a regional labor market created by another.

Regional compacts can align some of these incentives.

Revenue sharing. joint infrastructure districts. shared planning. coordinated housing targets. common industrial-site inventories. regional training programs.

Not every region needs another layer of government.

The principle is to match the institution to the system when spillovers are too large for purely local bargaining.

AI can make shared data easier. It cannot create trust among jurisdictions that see one another only as competitors.

The physical economy is regional whether politics wants it to be or not.

## The map is political

Every infrastructure map is an argument about who matters.

Where broadband is funded. Where transmission is built. Which neighborhoods receive flood protection. Which towns get interchanges. Which stations receive frequent service. Which industrial parks receive utility upgrades.

AI can make maps more detailed and persuasive.

That power deserves caution.

A model can optimize investment toward places with the highest economic return and systematically bypass low-income or rural communities where measured demand is smaller. Another can optimize equity using crude demographic categories and miss local priorities.

The objective function becomes geography.

Public agencies should therefore publish not only maps but criteria. Why did this corridor rank first? What changes if weights shift? Which communities lose under the selected objective?

Scenario maps can improve democratic debate because they reveal tradeoffs instead of presenting one technically blessed answer.

The map should be a question, not a verdict.

## A more distributed prosperity

The AI boom will not flatten America.

Some regions will become richer. Some will host energy-intensive infrastructure. Some will produce chips. Some will concentrate research. Some will provide land, power, and industrial capacity. Others may lose routine office work without gaining new physical investment.

A national public-works strategy cannot guarantee equal growth.

It can widen the number of places capable of participating.

Build transmission so power-rich regions can export and industry can locate where land is available. Build housing so successful cities do not turn growth into exclusion. Expand technical assistance so small governments can execute. Modernize water, roads, and public buildings in regions trying to attract investment. Use AI to distribute expertise rather than only centralize corporate control.

Most of all, respect geography.

The country is not one market operating in abstract space. It is thousands of places with different assets, histories, hazards, and capacities.

The AI economy will run through them.

Its physical footprint will be local even when its products are global.

The political stability of the boom may depend on whether communities can look at what arrived and say: this made the place more capable than it was before.
