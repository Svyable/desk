# Chapter 19: Federalism That Can Build

A transmission line does not care where one jurisdiction ends.

A watershed does not stop at a county boundary. A commuter may live in one municipality, work in another, cross infrastructure owned by a third, and depend on electricity generated hundreds of miles away. A freight train can pass through a dozen states while serving a port governed by a local authority and moving goods regulated by federal law. The physical country is braided together in ways its public institutions are not.

That mismatch is not an argument against federalism. It is the reason federalism has to work.

American government was never designed as a single machine. Powers overlap. Money comes from one level, permission from another, operation from a third. A project can be nationally important and locally disruptive at the same time. A city can know its streets better than Washington and still lack the fiscal capacity to rebuild them. A state can control transportation policy while a metropolitan economy ignores the state line running through it.

The question is not whether authority should be centralized or decentralized in the abstract. The question is which decisions belong at which scale, and how the levels fit together when the answer is more than one.

AI raises the cost of getting this wrong. Software, models, chips, data centers, robotics, and automated industry can move faster than the institutions that supply power, land, water, roads, training, and public permission. If every physical expansion becomes a negotiation among governments that cannot see the same map, private capital will route around the slowest places. The result will not be a neutral market outcome. It will be a geography shaped by administrative friction.

A country that wants the AI boom to become a broad building boom needs a federalism of completion.

## The wrong argument

Infrastructure debates often collapse into a familiar quarrel.

One side says Washington should take charge because local governments are too fragmented, too vulnerable to vetoes, or too small to finance major systems. The other says decisions should stay close to the people because federal programs are remote, standardized, and slow.

Both descriptions can be true.

A national government can see continental networks and still misunderstand a neighborhood. A local government can understand a neighborhood and still impose costs on everyone outside it. State agencies can coordinate at useful scale and also protect obsolete procedures. Regional bodies can match real economic geography and suffer from weak democratic accountability.

There is no institutional level that is naturally wise.

Scale should follow the problem.

Transmission planning requires a larger geography than a city council. The design of a neighborhood intersection usually does not. Flood control may require watershed coordination while the placement of a rain garden belongs on a particular block. Housing supply is shaped by local land rules but affects regional labor markets and national economic mobility. Ports are local places embedded in global logistics.

The practical task is to separate decisions that require uniformity from decisions that require local knowledge.

That sounds obvious. American programs routinely do the opposite.

They centralize details and decentralize outcomes.

A federal grant may prescribe process in painstaking detail while leaving dozens of jurisdictions to solve the shared system problem separately. A state may issue a thick design manual while allowing municipalities to block the housing needed around a state-funded station. A regional plan may describe a network elegantly but lack power to compel any member to build its segment.

The result is responsibility without authority and authority without responsibility.

## Assign the decision rights before the money

A project with five participating governments does not necessarily have five owners.

It may have none.

One agency finances. Another permits. A utility controls a connection. A municipality controls land use. A regional body studies the spillovers. Nobody has authority to resolve the conflict among them, yet every participant can plausibly explain why the delay belongs to somebody else.

This is the federalism failure to look for first.

Before designing the grant or the dashboard, name the decision rights.

Who finances the shared benefit?

Who authorizes the local impact?

Who delivers the project?

Who operates and maintains the asset after construction?

Who resolves a conflict when two legitimate authorities disagree?

The answers can belong to different institutions. They usually should.

The discipline is to make the split explicit.

Four tests help assign the scale.

**First: where do the spillovers go?** If the benefits or costs cross a boundary substantially, the decision needs representation at least as large as the spillover. A transmission corridor cannot be governed as though only the host parcel matters. A neighborhood curb cut usually can.

**Second: where does the useful knowledge live?** Drainage patterns, operating constraints, property conditions, construction access, and neighborhood use often sit close to the work. Higher-level authority should not erase information merely because it can overrule the institution that holds it.

**Third: who can carry the fiscal risk?** Some assets are too large, too long-lived, or too unevenly distributed for the smallest beneficiary to finance prudently. National or state finance can be appropriate even when design and operation remain local.

**Fourth: which seams become cheaper when they are uniform?** Safety floors, data schemas, professional credentials, technical interfaces, and recurring contract terms can create value through interoperability even when the substantive decision remains local.

These tests do not produce one ideological answer.

They produce an architecture.

Finance high when the risk pool or national benefit is large. Decide close where local knowledge is decisive. Coordinate at the scale of the spillover. Standardize the seam when incompatibility itself is the cost.

Then name the person or body responsible for completion.

Federalism becomes much easier to defend when citizens can tell who had the power to act.

## Money is not capacity

The federal government is unusually good at raising money.

States and cities face tighter fiscal constraints. Balanced-budget rules, smaller tax bases, borrowing limits, credit concerns, and political competition make it difficult for them to finance large projects through economic cycles. National borrowing can spread the cost of long-lived assets across time and across a broad tax base.

That is a strong case for federal participation in infrastructure finance.

It is not the same as a case for federal micromanagement.

A grant does not pour concrete. It does not relocate a utility, negotiate an easement, inspect a weld, answer a resident's complaint, or decide whether a lane can close Tuesday night. Money becomes infrastructure only after passing through organizations with staff, authority, contractors, information, and operating discipline.

This is why funding surges sometimes expose weakness rather than curing it.

An agency with five stalled projects does not necessarily become capable of delivering ten when its appropriation doubles. It may instead bid against itself for the same engineering firms, project managers, inspectors, and specialized equipment. If every grant carries a different reporting system and deadline, administrative work can expand faster than physical work.

The capacity problem has a recursive quality: governments need capacity to use capacity-building money well.

A federalism that can build therefore treats administrative capability as infrastructure.

Federal funds can support permanent state and local delivery teams, not only project reimbursement. Standard contract language can be reusable. Common data formats can allow a utility, a city, and a state transportation department to work from the same project model. Training can be shared across small jurisdictions that could never maintain specialized expertise alone.

The aim is not a national bureaucracy that executes every curb cut.

It is a national platform that makes competent local execution easier.

## Competitive grants can reward grant-writing

Competitive grants feel meritocratic.

Agencies announce criteria. Applicants propose projects. Reviewers select the strongest cases.

The model can also favor jurisdictions already rich in administrative capacity.

A large city can employ grant writers, consultants, modelers, and lawyers. A small town with the same physical need may have one public-works director trying to repair a pump while assembling the application at night.

The selection process can then confuse application quality with project value.

Formula funding solves a different problem by distributing money according to known rules without requiring every recipient to win a contest. Formula programs can be blunt and may send money where immediate need is lower. Competitive programs can target priorities but impose high transaction costs.

A building federalism needs both.

Use formulas for recurring, broad obligations such as maintenance and baseline capital needs. Use competition where experimentation, exceptional scale, or scarce national resources justify comparison.

Source-grounded application tools can reduce the grant-writing disadvantage for small governments.

The better reform is to avoid making every ordinary public need audition for federal attention.

## Matching funds reveal unequal fiscal capacity

Many grants require recipients to contribute part of project cost.

The rationale is sound.

Local participation tests commitment and prevents every project from being treated as free money.

The same percentage does not mean the same burden everywhere.

A wealthy jurisdiction can finance a 20 percent match easily. A poor rural utility or fiscally distressed city may have a more urgent project and no practical way to raise its share. The matching rule can send resources toward places best able to pay rather than places with the highest social return.

Programs can respond with sliding matches, credit support, low-cost loans, regional pooling, or waivers tied to fiscal capacity.

The design should avoid creating an incentive to appear poor or to shift normal obligations upward.

Comparative fiscal analysis can make debt burden, rate affordability, and project need more visible across applicants.

The political judgment remains one of federalism's oldest questions: how much inequality among places should national finance equalize?

Infrastructure makes the question concrete because pipes and bridges do not care whether the local tax base is unlucky.

## The national floor

Some standards should be national precisely because repeated negotiation adds no useful local knowledge.

Interoperability is the clearest case.

Rail gauges, electrical standards, communications protocols, safety rules, accessibility requirements, procurement identifiers, mapping conventions, and data schemas become more valuable when participants do not have to rediscover them in every jurisdiction.

The same logic can apply to parts of public administration.

A contractor should not need a different basic identity system to bid in every state. A project schedule should be exportable between agencies without losing its meaning. Permitting data should use common fields where the underlying questions are common. Asset condition should be recorded in ways that let a state compare similar structures without translating twenty incompatible spreadsheets.

National standards can reduce transaction costs while leaving substantive choices local.

This is an important distinction because standardization is often mistaken for centralization.

A common electrical outlet does not tell you what lamp to plug into it. A common data schema does not dictate which bridge to repair. Standard contract clauses do not require every project to have the same design.

Good standards create a floor beneath variation.

Bad standards turn every variation into an exception request.

Machine systems make this distinction more valuable because records become easier to compare, retrieve, and audit when the underlying nouns mean the same thing. If thousands of public agencies encode the same kinds of assets, permits, invoices, inspections, and contracts in incompatible forms, computational capacity is wasted translating institutional dialects.

The first public AI project in many agencies should therefore be less glamorous than a chatbot.

It should be cleaning the nouns.

What is an asset? What is a project? What is a change order? What is a permit condition? Which identifier follows an item from plan to purchase to installation to maintenance?

A federal standard can help answer those questions once where uniformity is useful, while local agencies keep authority over the decisions the data describes.

## Preemption should be narrow enough to explain

Sometimes one level of government blocks a policy another level considers essential.

States can preempt local zoning. The federal government can preempt some state rules. Local governments can restrict projects with regional or national consequences.

Preemption is a blunt instrument.

It can break a genuine veto problem. It can also erase useful local adaptation and inflame political resistance far beyond the original issue.

A building federalism should prefer narrow preemption tied to a clear externality.

If a state funds high-capacity transit, it can reasonably require minimum housing permission near stations. If a transmission corridor serves interstate reliability, a single locality should not possess an unlimited ability to block the entire network. If a local rule merely varies the color of a sign without affecting outsiders, national intervention is harder to justify.

The government overriding another government should be able to explain the spillover it is correcting.

Models can help estimate those spillovers.

They cannot make the constitutional or political choice disappear.

## The regional layer

Many American problems are metropolitan or regional but the strongest governments are municipal, state, and federal.

This is a structural gap.

Labor markets cross city lines. Housing shortages in one suburb raise costs elsewhere. Transit networks fail when routes stop at jurisdiction boundaries. Watersheds cross counties. Electricity markets and transmission systems span states. Ports depend on inland rail and road networks far beyond their host city.

Regional institutions exist, but their power varies widely. Some control money. Some plan without implementing. Some coordinate transportation but not land use. Others are special-purpose authorities with narrow mandates and limited democratic visibility.

There is no single model to copy everywhere.

The principle is that problems with regional spillovers need institutions that can represent the spillover.

Consider housing around transit.

A city may pay for a rail line or receive state and federal support for one. A suburban municipality along the route can then restrict new housing near its station. Locally, the restriction may satisfy incumbent voters. Regionally, it wastes expensive transportation capacity and pushes housing demand elsewhere.

A system that funds the rail line at one scale while deciding whether people may live near it at another has created an institutional contradiction.

The answer does not have to be federal zoning.

It can be conditional funding, state housing targets, regional planning requirements, infrastructure pricing, or local rules that automatically allow more homes near high-capacity investments. The instrument matters less than the alignment.

Public money and public permission should recognize the same system.

## Regional bodies need democratic legibility

Regional coordination solves one problem and can create another.

The authority may govern a system people depend on while few residents know who sits on the board, how members are selected, or which official can be held responsible for a bad outcome.

Technical coordination without democratic legibility becomes fragile.

Regional institutions should therefore publish decisions, financing, performance, and representation clearly. Major choices need public processes proportionate to their consequence. Member governments should not be allowed to celebrate regional benefits while denying responsibility for regional costs.

The record should make it possible to trace how costs, benefits, and votes moved across jurisdictions.

That transparency matters because regional systems often become politically visible only during failure.

A good regional institution should be understandable before the crisis.

## Local knowledge is real

Centralizers often talk about local control as though it were merely a polite name for obstruction.

Sometimes it is obstruction.

Sometimes it is information.

The people who operate a water system know which valve maps are fiction. A bus operator knows where a timetable fails in real traffic. A maintenance supervisor knows which equipment model causes recurring trouble. A neighborhood may know that a drainage design shown as empty land is actually where children walk to school.

Plans made at higher levels routinely omit such facts because the facts are difficult to encode.

Digital systems can make local knowledge easier to collect, retrieve, and preserve. They cannot make the knowledge unnecessary.

A federalism that can build needs feedback channels that are specific enough to improve a project and bounded enough not to become endless relitigation of whether anything should be built at all.

That means participation earlier, when designs can change cheaply, and firmer decisions later, when uncertainty has narrowed.

It means distinguishing a claim that a project will flood a particular street from a general preference that no project occur. Both can be expressed. They do not require the same remedy.

It also means respecting operation.

A beautiful capital plan can fail because nobody asked the agency that will maintain it. Local operators should enter design before procurement, not inherit assets after ribbon cutting.

This is subsidiarity in a practical sense: decide as close to the work as possible, but no closer than the externalities allow.

## Compacts for things that cross lines

American federalism has long used compacts, authorities, districts, commissions, and joint agreements to govern systems that do not fit ordinary boundaries.

The institutional forms can be ungainly. They can also be indispensable.

The AI-era building agenda will need more of this connective tissue.

Transmission corridors can require multiple states to agree on cost and benefit. River systems require upstream and downstream coordination. Large industrial sites can affect housing, roads, schools, water, and power across several communities. Wildfire does not respect the line between federal forest, state land, utility territory, and private property.

A compact should not become a new veto chamber.

Its purpose is to close gaps between jurisdictions, not add another layer through which every project must pass.

The design test is simple: does the institution have a defined problem, clear authority, a decision rule, transparent financing, and someone accountable for completion?

If the answer to the last question is no, the compact may be a meeting rather than a government.

Shared project records and models can improve situational awareness across these bodies, expose conflicting plans, and show how one jurisdiction shifts costs to another.

But technology will not decide how costs should be divided.

That remains political.

## Emergency federalism exposes the seams

Disasters compress the normal handoffs of government.

A local government needs help immediately. The state coordinates resources. Federal agencies provide money, technical support, logistics, or direct response. Utilities and private contractors act at the same time. Rules designed for normal conditions meet destroyed roads and missing records.

Emergency response works best when relationships were built before the emergency.

Mutual-aid agreements. Preapproved reimbursement rules. Common damage-assessment formats. Interoperable communications. Known points of contact. Standing contracts. Clear thresholds for requesting assistance.

Computational tools can help reconcile damage reports and match resources across levels of government.

The machine should not become another federal portal a local official has to learn while the town is underwater.

Emergency systems are a test of whether ordinary federalism created usable connections.

If the first act of disaster response is discovering who has authority, the design failed before the storm.

## A new bargain for grants

Federal grants often try to achieve many policy goals at once.

The impulse is understandable. A large infrastructure program is an opportunity to improve labor standards, environmental performance, domestic industry, equity, community participation, resilience, and other public purposes.

The problem appears when each purpose becomes a separate procedural layer attached to each project, regardless of whether another policy instrument would work better.

The result can be a Christmas tree of compliance whose ornaments are individually defensible and collectively immobilizing.

A better bargain would distinguish outcome conditions from process prescriptions.

The federal government can say what public money must accomplish: meet safety and accessibility standards, protect civil rights, account for environmental harms, pay labor according to law, report costs honestly, maintain the asset, and deliver measurable service.

Within those boundaries, states and localities should have room to choose methods.

That room should come with transparency.

If an agency receives flexibility, its schedule, cost, scope changes, procurement outcomes, and operating results should be visible. Autonomy and measurement belong together.

Poor performers can receive technical assistance, narrower discretion, or different delivery arrangements. Strong performers should not be forced through additional paperwork merely because another grantee failed elsewhere.

This moves federal oversight from ritual compliance toward demonstrated capacity.

Shared project histories can then reveal recurring causes of delay and performance differences across jurisdictions. The purpose is not a national league table that humiliates slow towns. It is institutional learning at a scale no single city can achieve.

## Maintenance conditions belong in capital grants

Federal money often arrives for construction while operation remains local.

This can produce an unhealthy bargain.

The national government gets a ribbon cutting. The local government inherits decades of maintenance.

Some recipients can sustain the obligation easily. Others build an asset larger than their future budget can support because the upfront grant made construction appear cheap.

Capital programs should therefore require a credible maintenance and operating plan before award.

That does not mean every poor community must prove it can self-finance forever.

Some systems deserve continuing intergovernmental support because the national public interest exceeds local fiscal capacity.

The point is to make the long-term responsibility explicit.

Operating-cost models can compare expected obligations with local revenue capacity before the grant turns into concrete.

The grant should not create a liability disguised as a gift.

## Capacity should compound

One of the strangest features of American project finance is how often temporary money creates temporary organizations.

A grant arrives. Consultants are hired. A project office forms. The asset is built. The team dissolves. Five years later a similar project begins with new people who reconstruct the same knowledge.

Private firms try to preserve organizational memory because it has economic value.

Public institutions should too.

A building federalism would reward reusable capacity.

Design standards should improve from project to project. Contract templates should accumulate lessons. Staff who deliver one rail extension should be able to deliver the next. Digital models should become maintenance records rather than disappearing into an archive. Community relationships should persist beyond a single hearing.

This is where federal money can have a leverage effect larger than the project it directly funds.

A dollar that buys one engineering report is useful once. A dollar that helps an agency build a competent engineering function can improve a decade of projects.

The distinction is difficult to capture in grant scoring because capacity is less photogenic than a finished structure.

But the structure depends on it.

## Federal technical assistance should feel like a utility

Small governments often encounter federal expertise as a document library.

The guidance exists somewhere. The applicant has to find it.

AI creates an opportunity for a different model.

A county could ask a source-grounded federal or state assistant which programs fit a water project, what environmental steps are likely, which standard clauses apply, and where a question requires human consultation. The answer could link to current authority and transfer the case to an expert when needed.

This is not a substitute for local consultants or professional judgment.

It makes public expertise easier to reach.

The service should feel more like a utility than a scavenger hunt.

Shared technical capacity is one of the few ways a federal system can preserve local ownership while reducing the penalty for being small.

## Competition among governments

Federalism allows experimentation.

That is one of its strengths.

States can try different permitting systems. Cities can test street designs. Utilities can use different rate structures. Agencies can compare project-delivery models. When results are measured honestly, variation produces information.

Competition becomes destructive when governments compete by exporting cost.

A state can attract an industrial facility with tax concessions while neighboring jurisdictions absorb housing pressure. A town can block housing while relying on another town to house workers. A utility can defer transmission investment while depending on imports during stress. A city can underfund maintenance while assuming future grants will rescue the asset.

The federal role is partly to prevent this race toward hidden liabilities.

National floors, regional cost sharing, transparent accounting, and maintenance requirements can keep experimentation from becoming free riding.

Then variation becomes useful again.

The most successful public systems rarely emerge because one level of government wins a philosophical argument. They work because responsibilities are divided clearly enough that each level can become competent at its part.

## Every project needs an authority graph

A project schedule usually maps tasks.

A federalism of completion also needs to map authority.

Who may approve the environmental finding? Who controls the road closure? Who can commit utility funds? Who owns the parcel? Who can settle a dispute over a design standard? Who accepts the completed asset? Who can change the operating budget after handoff?

These are not merely stakeholders.

They are decision nodes.

The distinction matters because a stakeholder can be consulted without holding a veto, while an authority can stop a project even if it never attends the coordination meeting.

An authority graph makes two pathologies easier to see.

The first is the **orphan decision**: a necessary choice that everyone assumes somebody else can make. The project waits because the responsibility exists but the authority does not.

The second is the **stacked veto**: several institutions can independently stop the same decision while none is accountable for producing a timely resolution. Each veto may protect a legitimate interest. The combined structure can still create indefinite uncertainty.

The answer is not to eliminate every veto.

It is to know which ones exist, what question each is allowed to answer, how conflicting decisions are reconciled, and when the process ends.

This is where machine assistance is unusually useful without being politically sovereign. A system can track dependencies, identify an unresolved authority edge, show which decision is on the critical path, and preserve the record of who acted when.

It cannot decide who should possess the authority in the first place.

That is constitutional and statutory design.

If a project cannot draw its authority graph, it probably does not yet understand its governance risk.

## Build where authority already exists

Institutional reform can itself become an excuse for delay.

There is always a better governance chart available on paper.

The country should not wait for perfect federalism before replacing a transformer or repairing a bridge.

Many projects can proceed under existing authority if leaders use it. Agencies can standardize contracts now. States can preempt exclusionary rules in targeted areas now. Utilities can improve interconnection data now. Cities can inventory assets now. Federal agencies can simplify grant requirements now.

Structural reform should focus where existing authority repeatedly fails to match the scale of the problem.

This is another lesson AI should reinforce rather than obscure.

The technology encourages redesign because software makes organizational diagrams feel editable. Yet institutions are not applications. They contain law, legitimacy, habits, careers, property, and accumulated trust.

Often the fastest route is not replacing the institution but making its handoffs legible.

Who decides? Who pays? Who permits? Who operates? Who maintains? Who bears risk? Who has to agree, and by when?

If those answers are visible, coordination becomes a tractable problem.

When they are hidden, every project rediscovers government from first principles.

## A country of handoffs

The American state is a chain of handoffs.

Congress to agencies. Agencies to states. States to regions. Regions to cities. Cities to utilities. Owners to contractors. Contractors to subcontractors. Builders to operators. Operators to maintainers.

Failure often appears at the seam.

The grant was approved but the match was missing. The road was designed but the utility relocation was not. The station opened but the zoning stayed frozen. The data center arrived but the transmission upgrade did not. The federal standard changed but the local software could not represent it.

AI is unusually good at seams when the information exists. It can reconcile records, detect mismatches, track dependencies, draft notices, compare requirements, and keep a project model current across organizations.

That makes it a tool for federalism in the literal sense: a tool for federating information without requiring every institution to become one institution.

The political work remains human.

Someone still has to decide whose preference yields when scales conflict. Someone still has to authorize money. Someone still has to tell a community that a necessary project has costs. Someone still has to say no to a company asking for a subsidy that exceeds its public value.

No model removes those choices.

A competent system makes the choices visible and then carries them through.

The output of federalism is not a coordination diagram.

It is the bridge that opens, the line that connects, the homes that can be built, the water system that can be maintained, and the public service that works where a person actually lives.

That is the federalism the building age requires: not centralized, not fragmented, but connected enough to finish.
