# Chapter 4: The Physical Economy Returns

The cloud has foundations.

That sentence became a cliché because it remains useful. A service that appears on a phone is supported by buildings, chips, cooling equipment, power systems, fiber, backup generation, roads, warehouses, mines, factories, and people who maintain all of them.

For years, the most glamorous parts of the economy trained investors and policymakers to look upward in the stack. Software margins were high. Digital products could be copied at low marginal cost. Capital could flow toward companies that scaled without adding factories in proportion to revenue.

Artificial intelligence intensifies the value of software while also pushing attention back down the stack.

The models are digital. The expansion is physical.

This does not mean the United States is returning to an economy dominated by smokestacks. Services will remain enormous. Software will remain central. It means that the marginal constraints on growth can move toward things that cannot be copied by calling an API.

Power. Land. Buildings. Equipment. Materials. Construction. Logistics. Skilled field work.

The physical economy returns not because it disappeared, but because cheap intelligence makes its stubbornness harder to ignore.

## Atoms have lead times

Software can change between breakfast and lunch.

A transformer cannot.

A utility orders equipment from a manufacturer with a production schedule. A substation needs land, engineering, permits, foundations, switchgear, protection systems, cable, testing, and crews. A transmission line crosses property and jurisdiction. A factory requires site work before machinery arrives. A water plant upgrade may need temporary operating arrangements because service cannot simply stop during construction.

Physical systems contain sequence.

Excavation precedes foundation. Foundation precedes equipment installation. Equipment must arrive before commissioning. Concrete cures on its own schedule. Weather changes productivity. A road closure has to be coordinated with the people who still need the road.

AI can compress parts of the sequence by improving design and coordination.

It cannot abolish sequence itself.

That matters economically because rapid growth in digital demand can collide with slow supply response in the physical layer. Prices rise. Queues form. Firms reserve capacity earlier. Projects that look viable on a spreadsheet compete for the same electricians, transformers, parcels, interconnection studies, or construction managers.

The scarce input may not be money.

It may be calendar time inside a supply chain.

## The second factory problem

A company that has built one factory has knowledge a new entrant lacks.

It knows which contractors perform, which drawings created field problems, how long equipment actually took to arrive, what inspectors cared about, which commissioning tests failed, and where the utility schedule proved optimistic.

That knowledge compounds.

The first factory is an engineering project. The second is partly an organizational memory problem.

American industrial policy often focuses on the financial barrier to the first plant: grants, tax credits, loans, guarantees, or procurement commitments. Those tools can matter. They do not automatically create the capability to repeat construction at lower cost.

A mature industrial system learns across projects.

Standard details improve. Suppliers invest because demand is credible. Workers carry experience from one site to the next. Permitting agencies become familiar with the technology. Utilities know the load profile. Owners learn which requirements were useful and which were decorative.

AI can accelerate this learning if project records remain available and comparable.

Change orders from the first facility can become warnings in the design review for the second. Procurement histories can reveal which component specifications narrowed competition unnecessarily. Commissioning reports can become searchable operating knowledge rather than PDFs nobody reads again.

The productivity gain does not come from asking a model to “design a factory.”

It comes from making the second factory less like the first one all over again.

## Construction is manufacturing's missing complement

A country can announce an industrial strategy without having enough capacity to construct the industry.

This is easy to miss because manufacturing statistics begin after the facility opens.

Before production comes site preparation, concrete, steel, electrical work, mechanical systems, controls, clean rooms in some industries, water treatment in others, fire protection, roads, substations, and commissioning. Specialized facilities compete for specialized contractors.

If several sectors expand at once, the same construction market can become a bottleneck for all of them.

Semiconductor plants, battery factories, pharmaceutical facilities, data centers, transmission projects, and public infrastructure do not use identical inputs, but their supply chains overlap. They need engineers, project managers, electricians, switchgear, concrete, heavy equipment, and regional logistics.

An industrial boom can therefore raise the cost of public works, and a public-works boom can raise the cost of industrial projects, unless productivity and supply expand with demand.

This is why construction productivity belongs inside industrial strategy.

Permitting reform alone is not enough. Subsidies alone are not enough. Training alone is not enough.

The construction system has to learn to produce more.

## Materials are political before they are scarce

Concrete looks local until cement supply tightens.

Steel looks interchangeable until specifications, trade rules, mill capacity, delivery schedules, or specialized grades matter. Aggregate looks abundant until the permitted quarry is far from the project and trucking cost dominates. Copper becomes an energy-transition issue because wires connect everything policymakers want to electrify.

Materials are physical constraints embedded in political systems.

Domestic-content requirements can support industrial capacity and political coalitions for investment. They can also increase near-term cost or delay when domestic supply is insufficient. Tariffs can protect producers and raise input prices for downstream users. Environmental rules can internalize real harms while making new extraction or processing slower.

There is no universal answer.

The important discipline is to stop treating material supply as an afterthought.

If a country wants to build transmission, housing, factories, water systems, and data centers simultaneously, it should ask what materials those plans share and how quickly supply can respond.

AI can help with demand forecasting, design optimization, and substitution analysis.

It cannot turn a mineral deposit into refined metal without mines, processing plants, permits, energy, transport, capital, and labor.

The physical economy keeps reappearing one layer lower.

## Land is not merely acreage

A map can show empty space that is economically unavailable.

The parcel may lack transmission. It may be far from a workforce. It may sit in a floodplain. It may have no sewer capacity. It may be divided among owners. It may be subject to zoning that excludes the proposed use. It may have environmental conditions that make development expensive or inappropriate.

Useful land is a bundle of location and permission.

That bundle matters for AI infrastructure because computing facilities often seek combinations that are geographically specific: large power availability, fiber connectivity, acceptable hazard exposure, access to construction markets, and local approval.

The same principle applies to factories and housing.

A city may contain substantial theoretical development capacity while very little land is actually buildable at current prices and under current rules. An industrial site can be advertised for years because the expensive utility upgrades were never completed.

One of the highest-return public investments can therefore be making sites real.

That may mean pre-permitting, utility extensions, environmental remediation, parcel assembly, transportation access, or simply publishing reliable information about available capacity.

The goal is not to choose winners parcel by parcel.

It is to reduce the gap between land that exists on a map and land on which something can actually happen.

## The warehouse teaches the lesson

Modern logistics looks digital because routing, inventory, forecasting, and purchasing are software-intensive.

Then the truck reaches the dock.

A warehouse has doors, turning radii, racks, floors, fire systems, workers, conveyors, power, and neighbors. Freight moves through road and rail networks that become congested at particular places. A delayed bridge closure can matter more to a shipment than a better forecasting model.

E-commerce demonstrated a general principle before generative AI arrived: digital demand can increase physical movement.

Making a purchase easier did not eliminate boxes. It reorganized warehouses and delivery networks.

AI may have similar second-order effects.

Cheaper design can produce more products. Better prediction can make more specialized inventory economical. Automated purchasing agents could increase transaction volume. Robotics can make warehouse operations denser and faster, which can increase the value of electrical capacity and transportation access.

Digital efficiency does not always shrink the physical footprint.

Sometimes it raises utilization until the physical system becomes the new constraint.

## The factory is becoming a computer with consequences

Modern industrial facilities already contain layers of software.

Sensors track temperature, vibration, flow, pressure, position, and quality. Controls coordinate equipment. Manufacturing execution systems track work. Maintenance software records failures. Computer vision inspects products. Digital models support design and simulation.

AI can deepen this integration.

A model can help diagnose equipment faults, optimize process settings, detect visual defects, schedule maintenance, or translate a technician's question into the relevant manual procedure.

The benefit can be large because physical downtime is expensive.

A server process can be restarted. A production line containing hot materials, chemical reactions, moving machinery, or sterile processes may require careful shutdown and restart.

This makes reliability a form of productivity.

It also creates cybersecurity and control risks. An AI system that merely suggests an answer is different from one allowed to change operating parameters. A plant that depends on cloud connectivity for basic function has a different failure surface from one that can operate safely in isolation.

The physical economy becomes more intelligent without becoming less physical.

That means software engineering and industrial safety increasingly meet in the same control room.

## Capital has a body too

Financial discussions often make capital sound like a quantity of money.

Physical capital is equipment somebody must design, manufacture, install, and maintain.

A billion dollars cannot instantly become a billion dollars of transformers if transformer factories are full. Cheap financing cannot make a construction market absorb unlimited projects at the same moment. A tax credit can change project economics while leaving the supply chain unchanged for several years.

This is why spending authorization and productive capacity should not be confused.

When money arrives faster than supply can expand, part of the program appears as higher prices.

That does not mean public investment should wait until every market is slack.

It means programs should include measures that increase supply: standardized designs, predictable pipelines, training, manufacturing capacity, procurement reform, and sequencing that avoids forcing every project into the same narrow window.

The best infrastructure policy does not only buy capacity.

It helps create the industry capable of supplying capacity.

## The economics of repetition

The United States often treats public projects as bespoke events.

A new school gets a new design. A station gets a new specification. A municipal building begins another procurement. A utility writes requirements in its own format.

Some variation reflects legitimate local needs.

Some is expensive habit.

Manufacturing productivity depends heavily on repetition and learning. Construction can capture more of that benefit when owners standardize components, contract terms, data formats, and families of designs.

AI makes variation cheaper to manage, which creates an interesting possibility.

Standardization no longer has to mean identical form.

A common structural system can adapt to different sites. A family of station components can fit different platforms. Housing plans can vary in facade and unit mix while sharing underlying dimensional rules. A standard procurement package can generate project-specific schedules and clauses without starting from an empty document.

The objective is mass customization rather than either extreme: bespoke everything or one rigid template everywhere.

This is a place where software can make the physical economy more productive without pretending the physical world is software.

## Depreciation is the bill from the future

A new asset begins aging before the ribbon is cut.

Paint weathers. Roof membranes move. bearings wear. Software becomes unsupported. Pumps accumulate hours. Pavement cracks. Sensors fail. Batteries degrade. Vegetation grows into rights-of-way.

The accounting term *depreciation* can make this sound abstract.

Operationally, it is a stream of future work.

An AI-era building boom that ignores this will repeat a familiar cycle: generous capital spending followed by inadequate maintenance, then another political campaign to “rebuild” assets that were never properly cared for.

Physical capital requires institutions with memory.

Asset inventories should survive leadership changes. Maintenance schedules should be funded. Design choices should account for who can obtain replacement parts twenty years later. Digital systems should export their data rather than trapping operating history inside a vendor that may disappear.

Abundance built on deferred maintenance is temporary abundance.

## Supply chains need slack somewhere

The pursuit of efficiency often compresses inventory and redundancy.

That can lower cost when conditions are stable.

Physical systems eventually encounter disruptions.

A storm closes a port. A factory fire removes a specialized component. A geopolitical conflict changes trade. A strike slows transport. A supplier exits a small market because demand had become too irregular to justify capacity.

The answer is not to duplicate everything domestically.

That would be expensive and, in many cases, impossible.

The useful question is where failure would stop an important system and how quickly alternatives could be activated.

For some components, strategic inventories make sense. For others, multiple qualified suppliers matter more. Standardized interfaces can allow substitution. Long-term purchase commitments can keep manufacturing capacity alive. Repair capability can reduce dependence on replacement.

AI can map supply networks and identify concentration more easily.

The policy judgment remains: which dependencies justify paying for resilience before the disruption arrives?

The physical economy teaches the value of options.

## Permits are part of production

A permit is not a machine tool, but it affects whether the machine tool is installed.

That makes regulatory capacity part of the production system.

This framing can make environmental advocates uncomfortable because it risks treating review as mere friction. It can make deregulators uncomfortable because it recognizes that competent review creates public value.

Both reactions are useful warnings.

Permitting should identify consequences that markets would otherwise ignore and ensure projects meet democratically chosen standards. It should also produce decisions on a timeline compatible with investment.

A process whose duration is unpredictable raises the cost of capital and favors large incumbents able to carry projects through uncertainty.

Small firms feel the burden more.

This is why administrative reform can be pro-competition.

Clear rules, complete application standards, parallel review, shared data, and decision deadlines reduce a fixed cost of entering the physical economy.

AI can support each of those steps, but only if the rules themselves can be represented coherently.

## The service economy still needs places

A false version of the physical-economy argument pits manufacturing against services.

That misses how intertwined they are.

Hospitals are service institutions full of physical equipment. Restaurants depend on buildings, water, gas or electricity, refrigeration, streets, and waste systems. Universities operate campuses and laboratories. Cloud companies operate data centers. Retail requires logistics. Financial districts require transit and power reliability.

Even remote work has a physical address.

The worker needs housing, electricity, broadband, heating or cooling, and some place where daily life occurs.

As AI changes service work, the quality and cost of these physical complements can shape who benefits.

A remote worker may gain geographic flexibility, but not if every affordable region lacks reliable infrastructure. A small medical practice may gain an AI assistant, but still operate inside a building whose electrical service cannot support new equipment.

The physical economy is not a sector beside the digital economy.

It is the substrate that many sectors share.

## Do not worship factories either

Physical production is politically attractive because it is visible.

That visibility can become its own distortion.

Governments may subsidize a factory because the building is easy to photograph while overlooking higher-value service industries, research, software, care work, or small firms. Communities may compete for a famous manufacturer with tax incentives that exceed realistic public benefit.

The physical-economy argument should not become industrial theater.

A plant is valuable when its output, employment, learning, supply relationships, tax contribution, strategic role, or spillovers justify the resources committed to it.

Some factories will not.

A good public-works strategy builds general capacity rather than arranging every investment around a favored corporate announcement.

Reliable power helps many users. Housing helps many employers recruit. Freight improvements serve multiple firms. Workforce programs with portable skills outlast one plant. Standard permits and transparent infrastructure data lower barriers for entrants as well as incumbents.

The best industrial policy often looks like a good place to build almost anything.

## The price signal can be useful

Scarcity produces political pressure to suppress prices.

Sometimes that is necessary to protect households from shocks.

But high prices also carry information.

Congestion prices signal a constrained road or port. High industrial power prices may reflect expensive generation, transmission limits, or fuel exposure. Rising construction bids can reveal an overloaded contractor market. High land prices can indicate that a region is restricting access to valuable locations.

The policy response should ask what scarcity the price is revealing.

Subsidizing the buyer without expanding supply can make the scarcity worse.

That is especially relevant to AI-era growth. If public policy subsidizes new demand for power, land, or construction while failing to expand those inputs, incumbent owners capture part of the subsidy through higher prices.

Supply-side capacity is therefore distribution policy.

Build more of the constrained complement and the gains spread differently.

## A larger economy needs a larger maintenance economy

Growth adds assets.

Assets add maintenance.

This seems obvious, yet budgets often treat maintenance as a residual after expansion plans are announced.

An AI boom could make this mistake at enormous scale. New data centers require grid upgrades. New factories require roads and utilities. New housing adds water, sewer, schools, parks, and streets. More electrification adds equipment that must be inspected and replaced.

Every increment of physical abundance creates future work.

That should not be treated as a reason not to build.

It is a reason to design the operating institution at the same time as the asset.

Who owns it? Who inspects it? What data will be retained? What is the expected replacement cycle? Which parts are standardized? What revenue pays for operation? What happens when the vendor is gone?

A society that asks these questions early gets compounding value from physical investment.

A society that asks them after failure pays twice.

## The physical dividend

If AI raises productivity, some gains will appear as better software, lower administrative cost, higher profits, and new services.

A portion can appear as physical abundance too.

More housing for a given income. More reliable electricity. Cleaner water. Faster project delivery. Safer roads. Better public buildings. Industrial capacity that can respond when demand changes. Parks and streets designed for hotter climates. Infrastructure maintained before it fails.

This is not the automatic result of technological progress.

It is a choice about complements.

The physical economy returns because intelligence can lower the cost of coordinating it, designing it, and operating it while simultaneously increasing demand for what it produces.

That combination creates an opening.

America can use cheaper intelligence to get better at the expensive work of moving atoms.

The opportunity will be wasted if policy keeps treating the software layer as the whole economy.

The model is new.

The country it must act through still has weight.
