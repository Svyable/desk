# Chapter 18: Who Gets the Productivity Dividend

Productivity sounds like a machine word.

Output per hour. Output per worker. More from less.

The political meaning is human.

Who gets the more?

When a new technology allows the same output with fewer hours, the gain can become higher profit, lower prices, higher wages, shorter working time, more output, higher land values, or tax revenue. Usually it becomes some mixture.

There is no natural law determining the shares.

Institutions do.

This is the most important fact to remember as AI moves from novelty into ordinary production. The technology can create a productivity dividend without specifying who receives it.

Public works are one way to convert part of that dividend into common wealth.

A park is a dividend people can enter. A reliable grid is a dividend that powers everyone attached to it. A water system is a dividend delivered through a pipe. Housing abundance can turn productivity into lower shelter costs rather than land scarcity. Transit can convert economic capacity into access.

None of these replaces income.

They change what income can buy and how much private spending is required to live a decent life.

That distinction becomes more important if AI weakens the traditional connection between labor hours and economic output.

## Turn the flow into a stock

A productivity gain first appears as a flow.

A firm spends less this quarter. A worker produces more this year. A customer pays a lower price. A government office processes more cases with the same staff. Tax receipts change. Profits change. Time is saved.

Those gains can disappear into ordinary consumption or the next budget cycle.

Some can also be converted into assets and institutions that continue producing value after the original gain has faded into the baseline.

That is the public-works opportunity.

A temporary surge in fiscal capacity can repair a water system that lasts for decades. A recurring reduction in administrative cost can finance a maintenance backlog. Growth in a region's tax base can be turned into housing infrastructure, grid upgrades, parks, or transportation that remain useful even if the company that triggered the boom later shrinks.

This is how a flow becomes a stock.

The conversion should not be automatic. Not every productivity gain belongs to government, and not every capital project is a wise use of public money. Households should keep gains. Firms need returns to invest. Workers should capture value. Lower prices are themselves prosperity.

The narrower principle is that a society experiencing unusually rapid productivity growth should ask whether part of the gain can leave behind durable common capacity rather than only higher current income or asset prices.

Chapter 2 described public works as an intergenerational argument about time.

Here is the distributional version of the same argument.

Prosperity is more durable when some of today's surplus becomes tomorrow's lower cost, wider access, and greater resilience.

## The wage channel

For most households, prosperity arrives through wages.

If productivity rises and workers become more valuable, employers compete for them and pay rises. This is the familiar growth story.

AI complicates it because the technology may complement some workers and substitute for others at the same time.

A programmer with AI tools may produce more and earn more. A routine content worker may face lower demand. A field technician may become more valuable because physical execution becomes the bottleneck. A manager may supervise a larger operation with fewer administrative staff.

Average productivity can rise while individual experiences diverge sharply.

That is why aggregate statistics will not settle the political question.

If GDP rises 10 percent and a group of workers loses 30 percent of its income, telling them about the average is not an answer.

The wage channel needs institutions that help workers capture complementarity where it exists: training, mobility, bargaining, competition among employers, credential portability, and labor standards.

Public works can support these institutions through apprenticeship and demand for skilled labor, but infrastructure cannot be the entire wage policy.

A bridge should not carry every social obligation merely because it receives federal money.

The more conditions loaded onto each project, the harder the bridge becomes to build.

Better to design direct labor policy well and let infrastructure focus on infrastructure.

## The bargaining channel

Wages do not reflect productivity mechanically.

They reflect alternatives.

A worker with several employers bidding for a scarce skill can capture more of a productivity gain than a worker in a market dominated by one employer. A contractor with a full pipeline can negotiate better terms than one desperate for the next job. A public employee whose classification has not changed in fifteen years may become far more productive without the salary schedule noticing.

AI can widen or narrow these alternatives.

Remote work can expand the set of employers for some occupations. Platforms can also concentrate access to work. Automation can make certain skills less scarce while making adjacent physical or managerial skills more scarce.

Public infrastructure affects bargaining indirectly by affecting mobility.

Housing abundance makes it easier to move to opportunity. Transportation expands the set of reachable employers. Broadband expands remote options. Child care and reliable public services reduce the penalty for changing jobs.

A worker's outside option is partly built.

This is another way public works distribute productivity without appearing on a pay stub.

## The price channel

Productivity can benefit consumers through lower prices.

This channel receives less political recognition because savings are diffuse.

If AI lowers the cost of legal drafting, software, translation, design, customer support, or administration, companies may compete some of the saving away. Consumers get more for the same money.

This is genuine prosperity.

The effect depends on market structure.

In a competitive market, productivity gains are more likely to reach buyers. In a concentrated market with strong lock-in, firms can retain more of the gain as margin.

Public works influence market structure indirectly.

Abundant electricity lowers a common input cost. Good freight infrastructure expands the geographic range of suppliers. Broadband enables competition in services. Housing supply expands the labor pool available to employers.

Infrastructure can make private markets more competitive by reducing bottlenecks.

The reverse is also true. Scarce infrastructure creates rents.

A company with the only feasible grid connection gains power. A landowner near the only permitted housing site captures scarcity. A vendor controlling proprietary public data can raise prices.

The public-works agenda should therefore treat abundance as competition policy.

Build enough shared capacity that access is not rationed through monopoly positions.

## Consumer surplus is real and politically quiet

Some of the largest technology gains arrive as capabilities people receive without a new line on their income statement.

A navigation app improves a trip. Search finds information that once required professional help. Translation becomes easier. Software features that once cost thousands become bundled into ordinary tools.

Economists call part of this consumer surplus.

It matters.

It can also coexist with household stress if the goods becoming cheaper are not the goods consuming the household budget.

Free digital services do little to offset a severe housing shortage. Cheap generated text does not lower a medical bill. Better online shopping does not make electricity or child care affordable.

This is why the composition of abundance matters.

AI may create enormous surplus in information goods while physical bottlenecks continue to dominate daily expense.

Public works can help move abundance toward the stubborn categories.

Housing. Energy. Transportation. Water. Public space. Reliable services.

A richer digital life is not the same thing as a lower cost of living.

## The land channel

Economic growth often arrives in property values before it arrives in public budgets.

A new transit station opens. Nearby land becomes more valuable. A city allows more jobs downtown. Rents rise. A university expands. Housing demand increases. A new factory makes surrounding property more attractive.

Public investment creates private land gains because location is scarce.

There is nothing inherently wrong with that. Homeownership has been a major source of wealth for American households. Rising values can reflect genuinely improved places.

The distribution becomes problematic when public investment is financed broadly while benefits are captured narrowly through land scarcity.

Housing policy is the first defense.

If more homes can be built near new infrastructure, the value of access is shared across more households and some land gain becomes additional supply. If construction is prohibited, incumbent owners capture more of the benefit.

Tax policy can also reclaim a portion of publicly created land value through ordinary property taxes, special assessments, tax-increment mechanisms, or other tools. These instruments must be designed carefully because property taxes can burden cash-poor owners and speculative revenue forecasts can finance bad projects.

The larger principle is simple.

When public works create location value, some of that value should help pay for public works.

AI can improve land-value analysis, but again the counterfactual is uncertain. Models should inform policy rather than manufacture false precision.

## Scarcity can intercept every subsidy

A government can transfer money to households and still fail to improve living standards if supply cannot respond.

Housing is the clearest example.

Subsidize demand in a market where construction is tightly constrained and part of the benefit can become higher rent or higher land value. A clean-energy subsidy can run into transformer, permitting, or installer shortages. Education aid can encounter a fixed supply of seats. Health benefits can encounter limited provider capacity.

This does not mean demand-side support is useless.

It means supply conditions determine incidence.

Public works are therefore part of transfer policy even when the transfer itself is cash.

Build the complement.

Allow the homes. Expand the grid. Train the workforce. Improve the transit network. Increase treatment capacity.

The more elastic the supply of necessities, the more likely a household subsidy reaches the household rather than the bottleneck owner.

AI can help identify where the bottleneck is absorbing the gain.

The political system has to be willing to expand it.

## The ownership channel

AI may produce enormous returns to capital.

Models, chips, data centers, intellectual property, platforms, and energy assets require investment. If those assets become more productive, owners benefit.

The distribution of ownership therefore matters.

American households own capital indirectly through retirement accounts, pensions, mutual funds, and home equity, but ownership is highly unequal. Public pension funds and university endowments also participate in capital markets. Governments sometimes own utilities, land, or infrastructure assets directly.

A public-works strategy can broaden capital benefits without nationalizing technology companies.

Public utilities can retain returns from infrastructure for customers. Municipal land can be leased rather than sold where long-term value justifies it. Public pension funds can invest prudently in infrastructure. Community-benefit arrangements can ensure local residents receive more than temporary disruption from large projects.

None of these mechanisms is automatically good.

Public ownership can be poorly managed. Pension funds can take inappropriate risk. Land leases can become political favors.

The relevant question is not public versus private in the abstract.

It is whether the ownership structure aligns long-term incentives with the people bearing the cost and risk.

## Public wealth funds are one possible bridge

If AI creates unusually large returns to capital, some governments may consider ways to hold capital on behalf of the public.

The concept can take several forms.

A sovereign or state investment fund. Public pension ownership. Revenue from natural resources or public land invested for future generations. Equity or warrants received in exchange for certain public subsidies. Returns from publicly owned utilities or infrastructure reinvested rather than spent immediately.

These arrangements can broaden ownership.

They can also create political risk, poor investment incentives, favoritism, and dangerous concentration of public money.

A public wealth fund is not free money.

It is an institution that must decide what to own, who governs it, how political interference is limited, when returns are distributed, and how risk is borne.

This book does not need to endorse one model.

The distribution question deserves a place in the conversation because ownership is one of the channels through which AI gains will arrive.

Public works already provide simpler forms of collective ownership.

A park, library, road, utility, or water system is wealth held in common use rather than in a brokerage account.

## The fiscal channel

Higher productivity expands the tax base if the tax system reaches the income and activity being created.

That revenue can finance public goods.

This is the most conventional route from private growth to public capacity, and it is more fragile than it sounds.

Companies can locate profits across jurisdictions. States compete with tax incentives. Property-tax abatements reduce local revenue from capital-intensive facilities. AI may shift value toward intangible assets that are difficult for local governments to tax.

At the same time, public infrastructure costs are intensely local.

A town may need a new substation, road, water main, or fire capacity to serve a facility whose broader profits are taxed elsewhere.

This creates vertical imbalance.

Economic activity should contribute to the infrastructure systems it directly burdens, independent of broader corporate tax policy. Connection fees, utility tariffs, development charges, road-impact arrangements, and negotiated infrastructure contributions can do this when structured transparently.

The fiscal bargain should be durable beyond the press release.

A ten-year tax abatement on a facility with a twenty-year infrastructure obligation deserves scrutiny.

AI can model scenarios, but political officials need the courage to walk away from deals that socialize cost while privatizing upside.

Competition between jurisdictions makes that difficult.

Regional standards can help prevent a race to the bottom.

## There is no single AI tax base

The phrase *AI dividend* can create the impression that a new pool of money will appear in one identifiable industry, ready to be taxed and redistributed.

That is unlikely to be how productivity arrives.

AI can raise the value of a software company, lower the operating cost of a hospital, increase the output of an engineer, reduce the staffing need of a call center, improve a utility's maintenance, or make a contractor more productive. The gain is dispersed through profits, wages, prices, property values, investment returns, and public-sector cost savings.

Trying to design the whole social contract around a narrow technology label can therefore be brittle.

The label changes faster than the tax base.

A broader strategy is to let ordinary fiscal institutions reach the economic income, property, consumption, and transactions they are meant to reach; charge directly for infrastructure burdens where a project imposes them; and make sure verified productivity savings inside public systems have a rule for reinvestment as well as eventual fiscal return.

There may still be cases for technology-specific fees or rules where a specific external cost justifies them.

The larger dividend does not depend on finding one meter attached to "AI."

What matters is whether the institutions surrounding growth can capture a reasonable public return without choking off the investment that creates the gain.

## Tax incidence matters more than the statutory label

A tax can be legally imposed on one party and economically borne by another.

A utility charge paid by a data center may eventually affect customers, shareholders, or prices. A property tax can be capitalized into land values. A payroll tax can affect wages. A development fee can reduce land value, raise sale prices, lower developer returns, or some mixture depending on market conditions.

The same is true of tax cuts and subsidies.

The statutory recipient is not necessarily the final beneficiary.

AI can improve empirical analysis by combining transaction, labor, land, and project data.

It cannot make incidence simple.

Public debate should be modest about claims such as “the company pays” or “the homeowner pays.” The real question is how behavior and prices adjust.

This matters for the productivity dividend because governments can intend to capture gains and discover that scarcity or market structure redirects them.

Distribution policy should be evaluated after the adjustment, not only at enactment.

## Public goods are an income policy

A household cares about disposable life, not only disposable income.

How much does housing cost? How much time disappears into commuting? Is child care available? Does the family need two cars? How high are electricity bills? Can children attend a decent public school? Is the park free and safe? Does a medical appointment require a day off work?

Public goods change the private cost of ordinary life.

This is why infrastructure belongs inside distribution policy.

A $1,000 increase in annual income can be erased by a rent increase. A reliable bus route can eliminate a car payment. Efficient public buildings can reduce tax pressure. Abundant housing can do more for young households than a one-time subsidy that is capitalized into prices.

The most egalitarian public works are often those that increase supply of necessities.

Housing. Energy. Water. Transportation. Public space. Communications.

Abundance reduces the rent extracted by scarcity.

This does not make universal public services a substitute for cash transfers. Poor households still need money. People have different needs that public goods cannot anticipate.

The point is portfolio.

A resilient social contract distributes prosperity through wages, ownership, transfers, and common infrastructure rather than relying on one channel.

If AI destabilizes one channel—labor income—the value of the others rises.

## Progressive infrastructure is partly about who saves

A public investment can be universal in access and unequal in benefit.

A transit line may create large value for households without cars. A park may matter most to residents without private yards. Reliable broadband can matter more to a small business or rural household than to a large firm with redundant connections. Flood protection can preserve modest homes as well as valuable commercial property.

Distribution analysis should therefore ask who avoids private spending because the public system works.

The household that can give up a second car experiences a large gain. The renter who no longer needs a generator during frequent outages gains differently from the homeowner with solar and batteries. The child who can study in a safe public library receives a benefit that never appears as household income.

AI can help model these incidence patterns by combining geography, service use, and household characteristics.

The analysis should inform project design without turning every public good into a means-tested program.

Universality has civic value too.

## Universal basic income is not a sewer system

Automation debates often jump quickly to universal basic income.

The concept is attractive because it addresses the distribution problem directly. If machines produce more and labor is less central, distribute income so people can purchase what they need.

There are strong arguments for and against different forms of basic income, negative income tax, child allowances, or cash supports. This book does not need to settle them.

It needs to draw one boundary.

Cash cannot substitute for scarce infrastructure.

Give every household more money in a city that refuses to build housing and part of the benefit may appear in higher rents. Give people income where electricity is constrained and bills still rise. Give commuters cash without transportation alternatives and they remain in traffic.

Supply matters.

This is why public works and income support are complements.

A social dividend from AI can help households participate in prosperity. A physical dividend ensures there are enough useful things for the money to buy at reasonable prices.

A country that distributes cash while preserving artificial scarcity will discover that landlords, monopolies, and bottlenecks are excellent at intercepting generosity.

## Universal services have their own limits

The opposite mistake is to imagine public services can replace income entirely.

They cannot.

People have different preferences and obligations. One household needs disability equipment. Another needs to support relatives abroad. Another wants education, travel, clothing, food, or a service government cannot anticipate well.

Cash preserves choice.

The useful social contract is mixed.

Public systems handle goods with large spillovers, natural-monopoly characteristics, strong network effects, or basic civic value. Markets and household income handle enormous ranges of individualized consumption. Transfers support people whose market income is insufficient.

AI does not erase the boundary.

It may shift it at the margin by making some services cheaper to provide publicly and some private services cheaper to purchase.

The portfolio should be judged by outcomes rather than ideology.

## Public capacity as social insurance

Infrastructure also insures households against risk.

A levee reduces flood exposure. A reliable grid reduces outage costs. Clean water prevents health risk. Transit provides mobility when a car fails. Public libraries provide information access without a subscription. Parks provide recreation without a private membership.

These benefits are difficult to compare with insurance checks because they prevent events rather than compensate afterward.

The AI era may increase economic volatility as firms and occupations adjust quickly.

Strong public capacity reduces the amount of private precaution households need to purchase individually.

A person changing jobs is less vulnerable if health care, transportation, education, broadband, and public services are reliable. A family can tolerate income shocks better if housing is not consuming half of earnings.

This is the distributional importance of boring competence.

A well-maintained public realm lowers the risk premium of ordinary life.

## Debt can distribute the dividend across generations

Public works often last longer than the taxpayers who finance them.

Borrowing can therefore be fair.

A bridge, water plant, grid upgrade, or transit line used for decades can reasonably be paid for across decades. Future users share both the benefit and the debt service.

Borrowing can also become a way to consume tomorrow's tax base without leaving equivalent value.

The intergenerational test is simple to state and difficult to measure.

Does the asset handed forward justify the obligation handed forward?

AI can improve life-cycle models and maintenance forecasts. It cannot guarantee future political stewardship.

A productivity boom may make debt easier to service if the economy grows. It may also tempt governments to assume future abundance will solve current overcommitment.

Public works should use expected future productivity as a reason for confidence, not an excuse for fantasy.

The future should inherit capacity with the liability.

## The time dividend

One of the least measured forms of inequality is waiting.

Poorer households often spend more time navigating broken systems: long transit trips, multiple benefit offices, unreliable service, difficult repairs, inconvenient work schedules, distant clinics, bureaucratic queues.

Wealth buys ways around waiting.

A car bypasses a bad bus. A private contractor bypasses a municipal repair backlog. A lawyer navigates permits. A concierge medical practice reduces scheduling friction.

AI can create a public time dividend if government uses it to reduce queues and make systems easier to navigate.

A resident should not spend three hours discovering which department owns a problem. A small contractor should not wait months for an invoice that can be verified in days. A permit applicant should know what is missing before entering the queue.

Time saved is real income in another form.

This may be one of the most egalitarian benefits of public-sector AI because administrative burden falls hardest on people with the least flexible time.

But digital convenience must not become digital exclusion.

People who cannot or do not want to use AI interfaces need human channels. Accessibility, language, disability, privacy, and trust matter.

The public service should become easier, not mandatory through one interface.

## Data can be a public asset without becoming public exposure

AI systems generate and depend on data.

Public works generate enormous amounts of operational knowledge: traffic patterns, asset failures, utility performance, project costs, permitting histories, maintenance records.

Used carefully, these data can create public value beyond the original transaction.

Contractors can benchmark costs. Researchers can identify failure patterns. Small governments can learn from larger ones. New vendors can compete without owning decades of proprietary history.

The distributional opportunity is a public knowledge commons.

The privacy and security risks are real.

Detailed infrastructure maps can be sensitive. Utility records can reveal household behavior. Mobility data can become surveillance. Procurement records can contain confidential business information.

The answer is not “open everything.”

It is deliberate data governance: aggregation, de-identification where meaningful, tiered access, security controls, retention limits, and clear public-purpose rules.

A public asset can be shared in forms that do not expose the people inside it.

## The civic dividend

Public works distribute something beyond material benefit.

They create shared experience.

A park, library, plaza, riverwalk, school, pool, or transit system places people who did not choose one another in the same civic world. Private prosperity can become socially isolating when every need is met through individualized services behind gates and screens.

AI could intensify that tendency.

Entertainment becomes personalized. Shopping arrives at home. Work can become remote. Education can be individually tutored. Social interaction can be mediated by synthetic companions.

The more individualized the digital economy becomes, the more valuable physical commons may become.

A public-works agenda should include places whose economic return is difficult to measure precisely because their purpose is social presence.

Not every acre needs to maximize tax yield.

Not every building needs to be a transaction.

A wealthy AI society should be able to afford spaces where people are citizens rather than customers.

## Distribution before resentment

Technological transitions become politically dangerous when their gains are abstract and their costs are local.

A displaced worker sees the lost paycheck. The shareholder sees a portfolio increase through an app. A town sees a data center consuming power. The national economy records higher productivity.

These experiences do not automatically reconcile.

Public works can make the gain visible in the same communities absorbing change.

If AI investment requires a new transmission corridor, build it to strengthen regional reliability rather than only one campus. If a town hosts major facilities, use the tax base for schools, parks, water, and housing infrastructure rather than disappearing it into opaque incentive deals. If construction technology saves money, use some savings to clear maintenance backlogs.

The principle is reciprocity.

Growth should leave capacity behind.

This does not require every project to compensate every grievance. It requires a credible story connecting technological abundance to ordinary life.

## Regional transfers are part of one national market

The geography of AI gains will be uneven.

Some regions will host capital-intensive facilities and collect property taxes. Some will supply electricity. Some will concentrate headquarters and high salaries. Other regions may lose routine work or bear infrastructure costs without capturing much ownership income.

Federal fiscal systems already redistribute across geography through taxes, benefits, grants, and procurement.

The AI era may make that function more important.

National productivity gains can support infrastructure in places that do not sit at the center of the boom, provided transfers are connected to useful capacity rather than permanent dependency.

This is not charity between regions.

The national economy relies on shared systems. Food, energy, logistics, military installations, water resources, labor, and supply chains cross state lines continuously.

Public investment can help keep the national market politically coherent when private gains cluster strongly.

The transfer is most durable when it leaves an asset or capability that strengthens the recipient region's own future choices.

## Measure the dividend where people live

National productivity statistics are important and incomplete.

A household experiences prosperity through wages, prices, housing costs, travel time, service reliability, taxes, leisure, and risk.

A region can post strong output growth while ordinary residents experience worsening affordability. Another can have modest income growth while public services improve enough that life becomes easier.

The AI dividend should therefore be measured across several channels.

Real wages. Housing burden. Energy costs. Public-service reliability. Access to jobs. Travel time. Household wealth. Working hours. Fiscal capacity. Local environmental quality.

No single dashboard can compress a social contract.

The purpose of measurement is to notice when aggregate growth is failing to become broad improvement.

AI makes the measurement easier.

Politics decides whether the result is acceptable.

## The dividend is a choice

There will be no single AI productivity dividend check waiting in a vault.

The gain will appear in millions of transactions and institutional decisions.

A firm reduces administrative staff. A utility optimizes a network. A contractor completes a project faster. A hospital automates paperwork. A city processes permits in half the time. A developer designs with a smaller team.

Each gain creates a small surplus of money, time, or capacity.

The social outcome depends on what happens next.

This is why arguments about AI and inequality cannot be resolved by capability forecasts alone. Even perfect knowledge of what the technology can do would not tell us who benefits.

Distribution is governance.

Public works offer a practical way to participate in that governance without trying to control the entire economy.

Let competitive markets pass gains through lower prices where they can. Let workers bargain for more where their contribution becomes more valuable. Let firms retain returns that justify investment. Let direct transfers address income shortfalls more honestly than artificial task preservation.

Then use ordinary fiscal institutions, fair infrastructure charges, and public-sector productivity savings to convert some of the remaining flow into shared stock.

Build abundant necessities. Preserve public ownership where it creates durable value. Make markets more competitive through shared capacity. Reduce the private cost and risk of ordinary life.

Then let productivity be productivity.

The purpose is not to stop machines from making things cheaper.

It is to make sure a richer economy eventually leaves something durable behind.

Something people can use.

Something that lowers tomorrow's cost.

Something visible from the sidewalk.
