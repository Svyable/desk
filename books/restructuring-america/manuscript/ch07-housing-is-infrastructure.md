# Chapter 7: Housing Is Infrastructure

A labor market has an address.

Economists can describe a metropolitan region as a set of wages, skills, firms, and commuting patterns. A person experiences it as a search for somewhere to live.

That difference matters in an AI boom.

If new technology raises productivity in a region but the region cannot add housing, part of the gain is converted into scarcity. Employers bid for workers. Workers bid for homes. Landowners capture more value. People who already own property may become wealthier while people trying to enter the opportunity face higher rents or longer commutes.

The economy can grow while access to the economy narrows.

Housing belongs in a public-works book because homes are connected to systems that make urban life possible: streets, water, sewer, power, transit, schools, parks, drainage, fire protection, and the legal infrastructure of land use. Most housing is and should be privately built. The capacity to build it is deeply public.

Calling housing infrastructure does not require government to become the universal developer.

It requires government to stop treating shelter capacity as a side effect of planning.

## Opportunity without room

A productive city attracts people.

That is one of the benefits of becoming productive.

If the housing stock can grow, people move in, employers find workers, new firms form, and some of the regional gain appears as additional population and output.

If housing supply is rigid, the adjustment moves into prices.

The city still becomes more valuable. Fewer people can afford to access that value.

This is why housing scarcity can function like a toll on economic mobility.

A nurse offered a job in a booming region compares salary with rent. A teacher asks whether the commute is tolerable. A young engineer decides whether the apparent wage premium survives housing cost. A contractor tries to staff projects while skilled workers live farther away.

The macroeconomic effect is distributed through personal decisions that never appear on a zoning agenda.

AI could make this more pronounced if high-value work clusters around particular research institutions, companies, data infrastructure, or industrial ecosystems. It could also reduce clustering if remote work becomes more capable.

Both futures benefit from housing flexibility.

A region should be able to add homes when people want to live there.

## Zoning is a capacity decision

A zoning map looks like a legal document.

Economically, it is a capacity map.

It determines where apartments are allowed, how many homes can fit on a parcel, how tall buildings can be, how much parking is required, which uses may mix, and which projects face discretionary review.

Those decisions accumulate.

A single height limit may seem minor. A parking requirement may be defended as neighborhood management. A setback may protect light. A discretionary hearing may provide public input. When many constraints overlap across most valuable land, the result can be a region that theoretically welcomes growth while practically refusing it.

The public argument around zoning is difficult because the benefits of restriction are concentrated and visible.

A homeowner sees the building next door. The people who would have lived in homes never built are harder to identify. They may live in another state. They may not have been born when the rule was adopted.

This creates a representation problem.

Future residents do not attend the hearing.

Infrastructure planning already accepts that future users have interests. Housing planning should too.

## Local control has externalities

Land-use authority is often local for good reasons.

Municipal officials know street conditions, neighborhood patterns, utility limitations, school capacity, and local hazards. Residents possess knowledge that a state legislature does not.

The problem appears when local decisions impose costs outside the locality.

A town can restrict housing while relying on a regional labor market. Workers then live elsewhere and commute through other jurisdictions. A suburb can benefit from a nearby job center while refusing housing that job center creates demand for. A municipality can protect its tax base by favoring commercial development while neighboring communities carry the residential-service costs.

These are classic spillovers.

They justify some state or regional role without requiring every zoning decision to move upward.

States can establish minimum housing capacity, require jurisdictions to plan for growth, legalize certain housing types broadly, or tie infrastructure funds to land-use outcomes. Regions can align transit investment with housing capacity.

Local governments can retain control over design, streets, utilities, and neighborhood implementation inside a larger obligation to make room.

Federalism works when the scale of authority matches the scale of the effect.

## Infrastructure can be the real limit

Not every claim of infrastructure constraint is a pretext.

A neighborhood may genuinely have an undersized sewer. A water system may lack pressure. A school district may need facilities. A road may already be unsafe. A power distribution network may require upgrades before a large building can connect.

Upzoning a map does not pour a larger pipe.

The abundance response is not to freeze development forever.

It is to connect land-use permission with capital planning.

If a city expects more homes in an area, its water, sewer, streets, transit, parks, and schools should reflect that expectation. Development fees can fund some expansion, but charges should be tied to actual impacts rather than used to make new residents finance unrelated backlogs.

Existing residents benefit from infrastructure too. New residents should not be treated as the sole cause of every deferred investment.

Growth planning should distinguish expansion cost from maintenance debt.

That accounting is difficult and worth doing.

## The permit is part of the rent

Time has a price in housing development.

A developer acquires land or an option, hires architects and engineers, arranges financing, and carries those costs while approvals proceed. The longer and less predictable the process, the more return the project must eventually earn to be viable.

Delay therefore selects projects.

A large luxury development may survive carrying costs that kill a smaller infill project. An experienced developer with lawyers and consultants can navigate discretionary review more easily than a first-time builder. A nonprofit affordable-housing project can lose grant timing because another approval slips.

Regulatory uncertainty acts as a fixed cost of entry.

This does not mean approval should be automatic everywhere.

It means rules should be clear enough that a compliant project can know it is compliant.

By-right development under democratically chosen standards is one of the most important forms of administrative capacity a city can create.

Save discretionary judgment for cases where genuine discretion is needed.

## Small housing is a scaling technology

Housing debates often jump from detached houses to large apartment buildings.

Much of the physical city can grow through smaller increments.

Accessory units. Duplexes. Triplexes. small apartment buildings. Townhouses. Conversions of underused commercial space where the structure permits it. Additional stories in suitable locations. Homes above shops.

These forms matter because they can distribute construction across many parcels and many builders.

A market composed only of large projects becomes dependent on large developers, complex finance, and lengthy approvals. Allowing smaller projects broadens the set of people who can add supply.

AI can lower some of the professional costs around small development.

Standard plan sets can be adapted more easily. Code requirements can be explained. Preliminary feasibility can be checked before an owner hires a full team. Permit applications can be reviewed for completeness automatically.

The safeguard is professional responsibility where safety requires it.

The benefit is lowering the fixed cost of building one more home.

Abundance is often incremental before it becomes visible in the skyline.

## Construction cost still matters

Zoning reform is not a magic wand.

A project can be legal and still not pencil out.

Land, labor, materials, financing, insurance, building codes, utility fees, taxes, and expected rents all affect feasibility. High interest rates can freeze development even after regulatory reform. Construction markets can be too busy. Specialized urban sites can require expensive foundations or fire systems.

This is why housing policy should resist monocausal stories.

In some places, land-use constraints are dominant. In others, weak demand means new construction is not financially viable. In many, several constraints interact.

A useful diagnostic asks what would happen if one constraint disappeared.

If zoning changed tomorrow, are there builders, sites, and financing ready to respond? If financing became cheaper, would approvals still take years? If material cost fell, is utility capacity available?

AI can help model projects across these variables.

It cannot make the economics work by declaration.

## Building codes are different from zoning

Public arguments often lump every housing rule together.

That is a mistake.

Building codes primarily govern safety and performance: structure, fire, egress, electrical systems, accessibility, energy, and other conditions of the building itself. Zoning primarily governs what may be built where and at what scale.

Both can affect cost.

Their policy logic differs.

A code requirement should correspond to a safety or performance objective and evolve as materials and methods change. A zoning rule should correspond to a land-use objective and account for regional housing need.

AI can make codes easier to navigate, but simplification should not mean weakening life safety.

Automated code checking may eventually catch many routine issues earlier. The best use is to make compliance more predictable while preserving professional review of unusual conditions.

A safer building is a public good.

An incomprehensible compliance process is not.

## Parking is housing policy

A parking space consumes land and construction.

In places where parking is cheap to provide and useful, builders will often provide it voluntarily because residents value it.

A mandatory minimum is different.

It requires the cost even when a project sits beside high-capacity transit, serves residents with low vehicle ownership, or is built on a parcel where structured parking is extremely expensive.

The requirement can reduce the number of homes feasible on a site or raise rents needed to finance the building.

Eliminating a parking minimum does not ban parking.

It changes who decides how much is worth building.

Cities still need curb policy, loading zones, disability access, emergency access, and management of spillover where street parking is scarce.

This is a recurring pattern in abundance policy.

Removing one rigid rule creates a need for better management of the shared resource underneath it.

Freedom to build and competent public operations are complements.

## Transit and housing must agree

A rail station represents a large public investment in access.

The value of that access depends partly on how many people can live and work near it.

When a jurisdiction spends heavily on transit and then limits surrounding land to low-density uses, it underuses the asset.

This is not only an urbanist aesthetic preference.

It is capital efficiency.

High-capacity transportation becomes more useful when more trips can begin or end within a short distance. Housing near transit can reduce the need for every new household to add a car trip at peak time. Retail and services gain foot traffic.

The appropriate density varies by station type and local conditions.

The principle is harder to dispute: land-use policy should not sabotage the capacity public infrastructure was built to provide.

States and transit agencies can make this alignment explicit in funding agreements.

A community receiving expensive regional access accepts some obligation to let people use it.

## Housing is climate adaptation

Climate risk will change which homes are expensive to insure, cool, protect, or rebuild.

Some areas face rising flood, fire, heat, or water stress. Others may become relatively more attractive.

Housing supply needs to respond to that geography.

If safer regions restrict construction, people remain exposed longer or bid up the limited safe housing. If disaster recovery rebuilds the same vulnerability without changes in design or land use, public money can repeat risk.

This does not imply mass retreat from every hazardous place.

Cities contain enormous sunk value and social networks. Many risks can be reduced through infrastructure and building standards.

It does mean housing policy should incorporate future hazard rather than assume the historical map remains fixed.

AI can improve parcel-level risk analysis, but the ethical danger is obvious: a model can turn uncertainty into a score that banks, insurers, or governments treat as destiny.

Risk information should support adaptation choices, not become an unappealable machine verdict on a neighborhood.

## Water decides where homes go

In dry regions, housing debates eventually become water debates.

The relationship is more complicated than slogans suggest.

Urban water use, agricultural use, industrial use, conservation, reuse, groundwater, surface rights, and infrastructure vary dramatically by region. A new home does not have one universal water footprint.

The relevant planning question is marginal supply.

What water source serves the next increment of growth? What infrastructure is required? How reliable is the supply under drought? What conservation assumptions are embedded in the plan?

Cities can grow while using water more efficiently if technology, pricing, landscaping, fixtures, reuse, and system management improve.

They can also outrun sustainable supply if growth plans are detached from hydrology.

Housing abundance cannot be built by ignoring physical limits.

It is built by increasing useful capacity inside them.

## Public housing is a capacity question too

Market-rate supply matters, but some households will not be served adequately by market construction alone.

People with very low incomes, disabilities, unstable earnings, or other constraints can face housing costs beyond what supply reform quickly solves.

Public, nonprofit, and subsidized housing therefore remain part of the system.

The operational lesson from infrastructure applies here as well.

Building the unit is not the end of the obligation.

Housing requires maintenance, management, security, repairs, capital reserves, and responsive service. A program that finances construction while underfunding operations creates deterioration that eventually becomes more expensive than steady care.

AI may help property managers triage work orders, detect recurring failures, translate resident requests, or forecast capital needs.

The dignity test remains human: does the resident live in a home that works?

A public housing asset should be maintained as seriously as a bridge.

## The homeless system sees the bottom of the market

At the extreme end of housing scarcity, people lose housing entirely.

Homelessness has many causes: income shocks, disability, mental illness, substance use, family breakdown, domestic violence, institutional discharge, and local service capacity all matter.

Housing cost and availability are part of that ecology.

A region with abundant inexpensive units has more margin for a household experiencing a setback. A region where every low-cost unit is contested turns small shocks into housing crises more easily.

This is why emergency shelters and supportive services, though essential, cannot substitute for a functioning housing supply.

The system at the bottom cannot absorb endless pressure from scarcity above it.

Public works policy should see homelessness not only as a social-service issue but as one of the places where land, housing, health, and administrative capacity meet.

No single intervention resolves it.

That is a reason for specificity, not fatalism.

## AI can lower the paperwork cost of building

Housing development produces documents.

Site plans. Code analyses. engineering reports. permit forms. environmental documents. financing packages. contracts. inspection records.

Much of this information is necessary.

The cost of producing and reviewing it can fall.

A city can publish machine-readable zoning and code information. Applicants can check obvious violations before filing. Staff can compare revisions and identify missing documents. Standard conditions can be generated consistently. Inspection scheduling can respond to actual project progress.

These are modest changes compared with replacing a zoning code.

They matter because small fixed costs compound across thousands of units.

The danger is digitizing bad rules and congratulating the portal.

The software should sit on top of a policy that permits enough housing in the first place.

AI can make a closed door easier to understand.

It cannot make the door open.

## Incumbent wealth and entrant opportunity

Housing politics is difficult because scarcity produces real winners.

A homeowner who bought decades ago may hold most household wealth in the property. Rising values can fund retirement or family security. A new building can feel like a threat to an asset accumulated over a lifetime.

A renter experiences the same scarcity differently.

Rising values appear as rising rent or a longer commute. A young family trying to buy enters the market after appreciation has already occurred.

Housing policy therefore mediates between incumbent wealth and entrant opportunity.

Pretending one side has no legitimate interests leads to bad politics.

Homeowners deserve predictable rules, compensation when property is taken, and neighborhoods with functioning infrastructure. They do not have an unlimited right to prevent future residents from sharing a high-opportunity place.

The public objective should be stable abundance rather than engineered scarcity.

Property can remain valuable because a city is desirable, not because construction is artificially forbidden.

## Property tax can reward the wrong thing

Local fiscal systems influence land-use decisions.

A municipality that receives more revenue from certain commercial uses than from housing may rationally prefer the tax base while relying on another jurisdiction to house workers. A town concerned about school costs may restrict family housing. Infrastructure finance can encourage sprawling development if new roads and utilities appear cheap upfront while maintenance arrives later.

These incentives are often invisible in zoning debates.

Reform therefore has to include local finance.

States can redesign revenue sharing, school funding, infrastructure aid, or other systems so communities do not face a fiscal penalty for allowing homes.

Impact fees should reflect actual marginal infrastructure cost rather than becoming a politically convenient tax on newcomers.

The goal is to align the municipal balance sheet with regional economic reality.

People cross borders more easily than tax systems do.

## A housing pipeline needs several clocks

Large buildings take time.

Land assembly, design, financing, permits, construction, and lease-up each have their own schedule. A zoning reform passed today may not produce completed units for years, especially if market conditions are weak.

That lag can make reform look ineffective before it has had time to work.

It also means policy should maintain a pipeline rather than respond only when rents spike.

By the time scarcity becomes politically unbearable, the construction that would relieve it should already have been underway.

This is the same timing problem the electric grid faces.

Capacity has to be planned before certainty.

Cities can monitor permit volumes, land prices, vacancy, rent burdens, project cancellations, and infrastructure constraints to understand where the pipeline is failing.

AI can help detect patterns. The political response remains deciding whether more building is acceptable.

## The construction productivity dividend

Housing becomes more affordable not only when land rules change but when building becomes more productive.

Standardized components, modular systems, better scheduling, digital layout, prefabricated mechanical assemblies, automated inspection, and improved procurement can reduce cost or time in suitable projects.

The gains will vary. Construction is not manufacturing on an empty floor.

Yet housing has unusually strong opportunities for repetition.

Bathrooms, kitchens, wall systems, mechanical rooms, structural bays, and utility connections recur. Building codes create common requirements. Large owners and developers can learn across portfolios.

Public housing agencies and nonprofit developers can help create demand for tested systems while avoiding lock-in to one proprietary supplier.

The objective is an ecosystem where several manufacturers can compete around compatible components.

Cheaper construction expands what land-use reform can actually deliver.

## The home as an energy asset

Housing and the grid are becoming more connected.

Heat pumps, electric vehicles, rooftop solar, batteries, smart thermostats, and flexible appliances can turn a home from a passive load into a participant in the energy system.

This creates opportunity and complexity.

A well-insulated home needs less peak heating and cooling. A vehicle can charge when power is abundant. A battery can support a household during outages and, under appropriate rules, provide grid services.

But adding equipment to a bad building envelope is not always the most efficient sequence. Renters may pay utility bills while landlords control capital upgrades. Low-income households can be excluded from technologies requiring upfront investment.

Housing policy, building efficiency, and utility policy increasingly intersect.

A public-works agenda should coordinate them rather than distributing separate rebates and hoping the pieces assemble themselves.

## Abundance without sameness

One objection to aggressive housing construction is aesthetic and cultural.

People fear that abundant building will produce identical towers, erase neighborhood character, or replace local businesses with generic development.

That outcome is possible.

It is not required by abundance.

Rules can govern street frontage, setbacks, trees, historic structures, materials in sensitive areas, and the relationship between buildings and public space without fixing the number of households at scarcity levels.

Design review works best when standards are objective enough to be predictable and focused enough to protect genuine public qualities.

A city can care about beauty and still allow growth.

In fact, a growing tax base and larger construction market can support better public space if governments choose to invest in it.

The false choice is between a frozen beautiful city and an abundant ugly one.

Cities have always changed.

The question is whether change is governed well.

## Housing is the distribution layer

AI productivity will be distributed partly through wages and prices.

Housing determines how much of that gain households keep.

If rent consumes the raise, the landlord receives part of the productivity dividend. If scarce land absorbs demand from new firms, owners of that land gain. If construction responds, more of the gain can appear as additional homes and population instead of only higher prices.

This makes housing supply a form of anti-rent-seeking policy in the literal economic sense.

It expands the quantity of a scarce complement so owners cannot capture all new demand through price.

The same argument will recur with power, transmission, transportation, and other networks.

Abundance changes distribution without requiring government to calculate every transfer afterward.

## Build room for the future

Housing policy is often conducted as though a community is complete.

It is not.

Children grow up. Families form and separate. Workers move. Refugees arrive. Students become adults. Older residents need smaller or more accessible homes. New industries appear. Climate risk changes geography.

A functioning housing system keeps room for these transitions.

That room is physical, legal, and institutional.

Land must be buildable. Infrastructure must have capacity. Rules must be legible. Builders must be able to finance and construct. Public programs must serve people the private market does not reach.

AI can help each institution operate with better information.

The central constraint remains political permission to let the place change.

A productive country cannot promise opportunity while making entry physically impossible.

Housing is where the promise gets an address.
