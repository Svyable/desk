Artificial intelligence can make cognition cheaper without making land, electricity, housing, water, transmission, permitting, skilled field work, or institutional coordination cheap. In that world, the bottlenecks move.

*Restructuring America* argues that an AI productivity boom should be met with a public-works renaissance: not a nostalgia project, not a make-work program, and not an excuse to freeze old jobs in place, but a deliberate effort to convert cheaper intelligence into more abundant physical capacity and a public realm that visibly improves.

The book begins with the historical purpose of public works and the changing economics of intelligence. It then moves through the systems that become binding when software gets better faster than the physical country: power, the grid, housing, water, climate resilience, transportation, maintenance, permits, procurement, construction technology, and skilled human work. The final chapters ask where AI investment lands geographically, who receives the productivity dividend, how American federalism can coordinate systems that cross jurisdictional lines, and what it would mean for a richer economy to look richer from the sidewalk.

The governing principle is complementarity. The book does not depend on a precise AGI forecast. If AI progress slows, the country still benefits from stronger infrastructure and more competent institutions. If AI progress accelerates, those complements become more valuable.

## Editorial principles

1. **Build the complements.** Ask what becomes scarce when intelligence, drafting, prediction, and administration become cheaper.
2. **Prefer physical consequences to technological theater.** The test is whether systems work better, not whether an agency can say it uses AI.
3. **Separate productivity policy from employment preservation.** Useful automation should reduce costs; worker security should be addressed directly rather than by protecting unnecessary tasks.
4. **Treat maintenance as capital stewardship.** A functioning inherited asset is an accomplishment, not the absence of one.
5. **Match authority to scale.** Local knowledge matters, but regional and national spillovers require institutions large enough to represent them.
6. **Distinguish deliberation from delay.** Review should find consequences, improve projects, and produce accountable decisions—not make duration itself a proxy for rigor.
7. **Keep claims falsifiable.** Every chapter's research trail records evidence, boundary conditions, and what would make the argument weaker or incomplete.
8. **Judge abundance at human scale.** Productivity gains matter when they reach wages, prices, time, resilience, public goods, and ordinary places.

## Continuity motifs

- **The bottleneck moves.** Cheaper cognition moves value toward electricity, land, materials, field execution, permission, coordination, and trust.
- **Software terminates in physics.** Digital systems eventually consume power, occupy land, move atoms, or depend on people who do.
- **Abundance is competition policy.** Shared capacity reduces the rents created by artificial scarcity and monopoly access to bottlenecks.
- **Maintenance is prediction.** Mature infrastructure policy intervenes before failure becomes visible.
- **Time is a public good.** Queues, commutes, administrative burden, and avoidable delay are distributional costs.
- **AI should improve the handoff.** Much institutional failure happens between agencies, jurisdictions, contractors, operators, and maintainers.
- **Growth should leave capacity behind.** Places absorbing the costs of technological expansion should gain durable infrastructure rather than only temporary activity.
- **The sidewalk is a balance sheet.** A society's wealth is partly visible in whether ordinary physical systems are reliable, accessible, affordable, and cared for.
