## Claims these sources constrain

The expanded chapter distinguishes five different interventions that should not be collapsed into one claim about “queue reform”:

1. **Interconnection process reform** — readiness requirements, cluster studies, deposits, withdrawal discipline, and faster study production can reduce administrative churn.
2. **Long-term transmission planning** — recurring queue congestion can be evidence that the shared network is undersized and that project-by-project upgrades are an inefficient substitute for anticipatory planning.
3. **Better use of existing corridors** — dynamic line ratings, power-flow controls, advanced conductors, reconductoring, and related technologies can increase usable transfer capacity in some locations without creating a new right-of-way.
4. **Physical supply expansion** — grid-enhancing technologies are complements, not universal substitutes, for new transmission, substations, equipment, and construction labor where underlying capacity is insufficient.
5. **Differentiated connection service** — flexible load, curtailment, storage, and staged service can sometimes reduce the amount of firm network capacity required, but only when performance is contracted, measured, and operationally credible.

## Falsification and boundary conditions

- A larger queue is not equivalent to a larger set of economically viable projects. Multiple applications may represent the same developer option set, and many projects will withdraw.
- Faster studies do not prove that total connection time will fall if siting, equipment, construction labor, transmission expansion, financing, or litigation becomes the binding constraint.
- Grid-enhancing technologies have location-specific value. A successful pilot or high percentage increase on one line should not be generalized to every corridor.
- Reconductoring or right-sizing an existing corridor can be superior to a new route in some cases, but structural limits, substations, protection systems, stability, easements, or long-run load growth can still require new transmission.
- Flexible or interruptible service should not be counted as firm capacity unless performance has been demonstrated under the conditions in which the grid plans to rely on it.
- Long-term regional planning creates a framework for evaluating future needs; it does not eliminate disputes over benefits, cost allocation, siting, state authority, or local impacts.

The chapter's durable claim is therefore narrower than “AI will require a particular amount of transmission.” It is that a grid facing simultaneous uncertainty in generation and large-load growth needs institutions that convert noisy project queues into better planning, better use of existing assets, credible commitments, and timely physical expansion.
