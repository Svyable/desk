# Research Brief — Restructuring America

## Purpose

This book asks what happens to the physical and institutional economy when machine intelligence becomes cheaper faster than land, electricity, materials, skilled field work, public permission, and coordination. Its thesis is deliberately robust to uncertainty about AI capability: infrastructure and state capacity remain useful if AI progress slows, and become more valuable complements if AI progress accelerates.

The research trail therefore has two jobs. First, anchor concrete claims about infrastructure, labor, regulation, and technology in checkable sources. Second, keep the argument from outrunning the evidence by recording boundary conditions and plausible ways each chapter could be incomplete.

## Evidence hierarchy

Prefer, in order:

1. Statutes, regulations, agency rules, official datasets, and primary public records.
2. Government technical reports and national-laboratory research.
3. Peer-reviewed research and transparent working papers with identifiable methods.
4. Standards bodies and professional technical guidance.
5. High-quality secondary reporting for context when a primary source is unavailable.

Forecasts are treated as scenarios, not facts. A projection about AI capability, electricity demand, employment, construction automation, or migration should be labeled as such in later revisions. Where a chapter makes an institutional argument rather than an empirical estimate, sources establish the underlying constraint; they do not pretend to prove the normative conclusion.

## Chapter 1 source trail — The Country Under the Software

- U.S. Energy Information Administration, “Fossil generation could rise with faster-than-expected growth in data center power demand” (2026). Documents renewed U.S. electricity-load growth and the role of data centers. https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=67344
- U.S. Department of Energy, “National Transmission Needs Study” (2026 draft page). Frames new load, including hyperscale AI data centers and manufacturing, as a transmission-planning problem. https://www.energy.gov/oe/national-transmission-needs-study
- U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, “EPA's 7th Drinking Water Infrastructure Needs Survey and Assessment.” Documents large, long-horizon drinking-water infrastructure needs. https://www.epa.gov/dwsrf/epas-7th-drinking-water-infrastructure-needs-survey-and-assessment
- Federal Highway Administration, “Transportation Asset Management Plans.” Describes lifecycle management of existing public assets. https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/asset/plans.cfm

### Falsification note

The chapter should not imply that every important AI bottleneck is physical. Model quality, chips, capital, data, security, market structure, software integration, and organizational learning may remain binding. Nor does a rising infrastructure constraint prove that public ownership is the best answer. The narrower claim is that digital productivity still depends on physical systems whose expansion and maintenance have different timelines and institutions.

## Chapter 2 source trail — What Public Works Were For

- Library of Congress, “The New Deal.” Primary-source teaching collection covering the Public Works Administration, Works Progress Administration, and related programs. https://www.loc.gov/classroom-materials/new-deal/
- Library of Congress, “Today in History — April 8.” Summarizes the WPA's creation and its work on roads, bridges, schools, parks, and other public structures. https://www.loc.gov/item/today-in-history/april-08/
- Library of Congress, “President Franklin Delano Roosevelt and the New Deal.” Places public works inside the broader institutional response to the Great Depression. https://www.loc.gov/classroom-materials/united-states-history-primary-source-timeline/great-depression-and-world-war-ii-1929-1945/franklin-delano-roosevelt-and-the-new-deal/

### Falsification note

New Deal public works should not be romanticized as uniformly efficient, equitable, or sufficient to end the Depression. Programs differed, discriminatory institutions shaped access and implementation, and historians dispute macroeconomic effects. The relevant inheritance is the use of public investment to create durable capacity, not a claim that 1930s institutions can simply be restored.

## Chapter 3 source trail — When Intelligence Gets Cheap

- Dillon, Jaffe, Immorlica, and Stanton, NBER Working Paper 33795, “Shifting Work Patterns with Generative AI” (2025, revised 2025). Field experiment on how an integrated generative-AI tool changed knowledge-worker time use. https://www.nber.org/papers/w33795
- Dell'Acqua et al., NBER Working Paper 33641, “The Cybernetic Teammate” (2025). Field experiment on AI, expertise, and performance in product-development work. https://www.nber.org/papers/w33641
- Cruces et al., NBER Working Paper 34851, “Does Generative AI Narrow Education-Based Productivity Gaps?” (2026). Randomized experiment examining heterogeneous productivity effects and persistence without AI. https://www.nber.org/papers/w34851
- Benzell and Myers, NBER Working Paper 34668, “Automation Experiments and Inequality” (2026). Formalizes why automation can affect workers differently as technology quality changes. https://www.nber.org/papers/w34668

### Falsification note

Current generative-AI experiments are early, task-specific, and often conducted in selected firms or online settings. Short-run productivity gains do not establish economy-wide productivity growth, long-run employment effects, or the arrival of generally capable systems. The chapter's argument depends only on some cognitive tasks becoming materially cheaper, not on a specific AGI date.

## Chapter 4 source trail — The Physical Economy Returns

- U.S. Energy Information Administration, “Data center server energy use grows across the commercial building stock” (2026). Long-run scenarios for server electricity consumption. https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=67704
- U.S. Department of Energy, “Clean Energy Resources to Meet Data Center Electricity Demand.” Discusses generation, flexibility, grid modernization, and data-center load growth. https://www.energy.gov/oe/clean-energy-resources-meet-data-center-electricity-demand
- Bureau of Labor Statistics, “Construction Labor Productivity.” Current BLS productivity measures for selected construction industries. https://www.bls.gov/productivity/highlights/construction-labor-productivity.htm

### Falsification note

A return of physical constraints does not mean software or services shrink as shares of value. Better algorithms can reduce compute, logistics, design, or material requirements. Substitution can soften constraints. The chapter should be read as a bottleneck argument: when one input gets cheaper quickly, slower-moving complements can become more economically important at the margin.

## Chapter 5 source trail — Power Before Everything

- U.S. Energy Information Administration, “Fossil generation could rise with faster-than-expected growth in data center power demand” (2026). https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=67344
- U.S. Department of Energy, “Clean Energy Resources to Meet Data Center Electricity Demand.” https://www.energy.gov/oe/clean-energy-resources-meet-data-center-electricity-demand
- U.S. Department of Energy, “National Transmission Planning Study.” Reviews transmission expansion under multiple power-sector futures. https://www.energy.gov/oe/national-transmission-planning-study-0

### Falsification note

Electricity demand from AI is highly uncertain and depends on hardware efficiency, model architectures, utilization, cooling, siting, and the pace of deployment. Data centers are not the only source of load growth. The chapter should not convert a high-demand scenario into a forecast or assume one generation technology is universally optimal.

## Chapter 6 source trail — The Grid Is a Queue

- Federal Energy Regulatory Commission, “Fact Sheet — Improvements to Generator Interconnection Procedures and Agreements,” Order No. 2023 (2023). https://www.ferc.gov/news-events/news/fact-sheet-improvements-generator-interconnection-procedures-and-agreements
- Federal Energy Regulatory Commission, “Generator Interconnection.” Rules and reference materials for large and small generator interconnection. https://www.ferc.gov/electric-transmission/generator-interconnection
- Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, “Queued Up: Characteristics of Power Plants Seeking Transmission Interconnection As of the End of 2023” (2024). https://emp.lbl.gov/queued-characteristics-power-plants-seeking-transmission-interconnection-end-2023
- U.S. Department of Energy, “National Transmission Needs Study.” https://www.energy.gov/oe/national-transmission-needs-study

### Falsification note

Interconnection queues are not equivalent to projects that will be built; many applications withdraw. Queue volume measures developer interest and process burden more directly than future installed capacity. Reforms can also shift bottlenecks into equipment, siting, transmission construction, or financing rather than eliminate them.

## Chapter 7 source trail — Housing Is Infrastructure

- U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development, “Eliminating Regulatory Barriers to Affordable Housing: Federal, State, Local, and Tribal Opportunities” (2021). https://www.huduser.gov/portal/publications/eliminating-regulatory-barriers-to-affordable-housing.html
- U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development, “Worst Case Housing Needs: 2025 Report to Congress.” https://www.huduser.gov/portal/publications/Worst-Case-Housing-Needs-2025-Report-to-Congress.html
- U.S. Census Bureau, Building Permits Survey. Primary data series for permitted residential construction. https://www.census.gov/construction/bps/

### Falsification note

Regulation is only one component of housing cost. Interest rates, construction labor, materials, land, infrastructure, insurance, household formation, credit, and local demand all matter. Zoning reform can increase feasible supply without guaranteeing rapid construction or affordability at every income level.

## Chapter 8 source trail — The Water We Inherited

- U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, “EPA's 7th Drinking Water Infrastructure Needs Survey and Assessment.” https://www.epa.gov/dwsrf/epas-7th-drinking-water-infrastructure-needs-survey-and-assessment
- U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, “Lead Service Lines.” Current federal information and replacement resources. https://www.epa.gov/ground-water-and-drinking-water/lead-service-lines
- U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, “Planning and Developing a Service Line Inventory.” https://www.epa.gov/ground-water-and-drinking-water/planning-and-developing-service-line-inventory

### Falsification note

National need estimates aggregate systems with very different conditions, governance, source water, treatment processes, and rate bases. Replacing old infrastructure is not automatically superior to rehabilitation, monitoring, or targeted upgrades. Water investment must remain system-specific and health claims must follow drinking-water evidence rather than visual condition alone.

## Chapter 9 source trail — Heat, Fire, and Flood

- NOAA National Centers for Environmental Information, “Billion-Dollar Weather and Climate Disasters.” Historical U.S. disaster-loss dataset. https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/billions/
- FEMA, National Risk Index resources and community risk information. https://hazards.fema.gov/nri/
- U.S. Department of Energy, “State and Tribal Grid Resilience and Transmission Planning Resource Hub.” Includes resilience planning and utility wildfire-mitigation resources. https://www.energy.gov/oe/state-and-tribal-grid-resilience-and-transmission-planning-resource-hub

### Falsification note

Disaster-loss series are affected by exposure, development patterns, wealth, inflation adjustments, reporting, and hazard frequency; rising losses do not by themselves isolate the effect of climate change. Adaptation also has limits and can create maladaptation. The chapter's narrower case is for planning around observed and plausible physical risk rather than treating historical climate as a fixed design condition.

## Chapter 10 source trail — Transportation After the Commute

- Federal Highway Administration, “Chapter 6: Infrastructure Conditions,” Conditions and Performance report. Covers highway and transit asset conditions. https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/policy/23cpr/chap6.cfm
- Federal Highway Administration, “Transportation Asset Management Plans.” https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/asset/plans.cfm
- U.S. Census Bureau, American Community Survey commuting and journey-to-work data. https://www.census.gov/topics/employment/commuting.html

### Falsification note

Remote and hybrid work do not eliminate commuting, and transportation networks serve freight, care work, school, services, and non-office workers as well as downtown office trips. Travel behavior can rebound when capacity or time is freed. The chapter should avoid treating any one post-pandemic work pattern as permanent.

## Chapter 11 source trail — The Maintenance State

- Federal Highway Administration, “What Is Transportation Asset Management?” https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/asset/if08008/amo_02.cfm
- Federal Highway Administration, “Transportation Asset Management Plans.” https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/asset/plans.cfm
- Federal Highway Administration, “Asset Management for Operations.” https://ops.fhwa.dot.gov/program_areas/ops-asset-mgmt.htm
- U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, “EPA's 7th Drinking Water Infrastructure Needs Survey and Assessment.” https://www.epa.gov/dwsrf/epas-7th-drinking-water-infrastructure-needs-survey-and-assessment

### Falsification note

Preventive maintenance is not always cheaper than replacement, especially for obsolete or badly designed assets. Condition models can create false confidence when inventories are incomplete. The strongest claim is that lifecycle cost and service reliability should be visible in capital decisions, not that every inherited asset deserves preservation.

## Chapter 12 source trail — The Permit State

- Federal Infrastructure Permitting Dashboard, “About the Federal Infrastructure Permitting Dashboard.” https://www.permits.performance.gov/about/federal-infrastructure-permitting-dashboard
- Federal Infrastructure Permitting Dashboard, “All Projects.” Public project and review tracking. https://www.permits.performance.gov/projects
- U.S. Department of Energy, “National Transmission Needs Study.” Documents siting, planning, and interregional transmission needs. https://www.energy.gov/oe/national-transmission-needs-study

### Falsification note

A long approval timeline does not prove that environmental review caused the delay, and fast review is not automatically good review. Financing, design changes, litigation, utility conflicts, land acquisition, sponsor readiness, and procurement can all dominate schedules. The reform target is unnecessary uncertainty and serial handoffs while preserving substantive environmental and public-interest review.

## Chapter 13 source trail — Procurement Is a Technology

- Acquisition.gov, FAR 1.102, “Statement of guiding principles for the Federal Acquisition System.” https://www.acquisition.gov/far/1.102
- Acquisition.gov, FAR Subpart 37.6, “Performance-Based Acquisition.” https://www.acquisition.gov/far/subpart-37.6
- Acquisition.gov, FAR Part 34, “Major System Acquisition.” https://www.acquisition.gov/far/part-34
- U.S. Government Accountability Office, “Federal Acquisitions: Congress and the Executive Branch Have Taken Steps to Address Key Issues, but Challenges Endure,” GAO-18-627. https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-18-627

### Falsification note

Federal procurement rules are not identical to state, local, utility, or private construction procurement. Competition can lower price but can also reward unrealistic bids when scope and risk allocation are poor. Alternative delivery methods are not universally superior. Procurement quality should be judged by lifecycle value, competition, integrity, performance, and the market it creates.

## Chapter 14 source trail — Machines on the Jobsite

- NIOSH, “Transforming Construction: Automation and Robotics for a Safer Future” (2024). https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/bulletin/2024/construction-robotics.html
- NIOSH, “Robotics in the Workplace: An Overview.” https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/robotics/index.html
- Bureau of Labor Statistics, “Construction Labor Productivity.” https://www.bls.gov/productivity/highlights/construction-labor-productivity.htm

### Falsification note

Construction sites are variable environments and are harder to automate than controlled factories. Demonstrations do not establish economical deployment at scale. Automation can introduce new safety hazards, integration costs, and maintenance demands. The chapter's argument is for allowing productivity-improving tools to compete, not for assuming construction becomes fully autonomous.

## Chapter 15 source trail — The Human Work That Remains

- Bureau of Labor Statistics, Occupational Outlook Handbook, “Electricians” (2026 update). https://www.bls.gov/ooh/construction-and-extraction/electricians.htm
- Bureau of Labor Statistics, Occupational Outlook Handbook, Construction and Extraction Occupations. https://www.bls.gov/ooh/construction-and-extraction/home.htm
- NIOSH, “Transforming Construction: Automation and Robotics for a Safer Future.” https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/bulletin/2024/construction-robotics.html

### Falsification note

Occupational projections are conditional forecasts, not guaranteed shortages. Wages, migration, apprenticeship capacity, technology, union rules, licensing, project pipelines, and regional demand can change labor supply. “Human work that remains” is not a fixed category; tasks can move between people and machines as systems improve.

## Chapter 16 source trail — Public Works Without Make-Work

- Library of Congress, “The New Deal.” Historical context for public works as employment and infrastructure policy. https://www.loc.gov/classroom-materials/new-deal/
- NBER, “Shifting Work Patterns with Generative AI.” Evidence that AI can save worker time without immediately transforming every coordinated work process. https://www.nber.org/papers/w33795
- NBER, “Automation Experiments and Inequality.” Framework for heterogeneous distributional effects of automation. https://www.nber.org/papers/w34668

### Falsification note

During severe recessions, employment creation can itself be a legitimate macroeconomic objective. The chapter's objection is narrower: long-run infrastructure design should not preserve inefficient production methods merely to protect task counts. Transitional labor-market policy and countercyclical policy remain separate questions.

## Chapter 17 source trail — The Geography of the AI Boom

- U.S. Energy Information Administration, “Fossil generation could rise with faster-than-expected growth in data center power demand.” Regional load-growth discussion. https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=67344
- U.S. Department of Energy, “National Transmission Needs Study.” Regional and interregional transmission needs under new load growth. https://www.energy.gov/oe/national-transmission-needs-study
- U.S. Census Bureau, Population Estimates and migration resources. https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/popest.html
- U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis, Regional Economic Accounts. https://www.bea.gov/data/gdp/gdp-county-metro-and-other-areas

### Falsification note

Data-center siting is not a complete map of AI's economic geography. Talent, firms, research institutions, chip supply, finance, tax policy, latency, land, water, electricity, and network connectivity interact. A facility can add large capital investment with relatively modest permanent employment. Regional benefit must be measured rather than assumed from project size.

## Chapter 18 source trail — Who Gets the Productivity Dividend

- NBER, “Does Generative AI Narrow Education-Based Productivity Gaps?” https://www.nber.org/papers/w34851
- NBER, “Automation Experiments and Inequality.” https://www.nber.org/papers/w34668
- HUD, “Worst Case Housing Needs: 2025 Report to Congress.” Illustrates how high shelter costs can absorb household resources even in a productive economy. https://www.huduser.gov/portal/publications/Worst-Case-Housing-Needs-2025-Report-to-Congress.html
- Federal Highway Administration, “Transportation Asset Management Plans.” Public assets as long-lived service capacity. https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/asset/plans.cfm

### Falsification note

The chapter does not establish the optimal tax system, transfer system, ownership model, or universal-basic-income design. Productivity can reach households through wages, prices, profits, public revenue, and new products in combinations that vary over time. Public works are one distribution channel and one supply-side complement, not a complete inequality policy.

## Chapter 19 source trail — Federalism That Can Build

- U.S. Government Accountability Office, “Grants Management: Efforts to Address Challenges Through Government-wide Collaboration,” GAO-26-108283 (2026). Documents variation and coordination challenges across federal grant programs. https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-108283
- Federal Infrastructure Permitting Dashboard. Cross-agency tracking for major federal infrastructure reviews and authorizations. https://www.permits.performance.gov/about/federal-infrastructure-permitting-dashboard
- U.S. Department of Energy, “National Transmission Needs Study.” A concrete example of infrastructure whose relevant geography crosses state and utility boundaries. https://www.energy.gov/oe/national-transmission-needs-study
- Federal Highway Administration, “Transportation Asset Management Plans.” Illustrates nationally defined requirements implemented through state asset-management systems. https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/asset/plans.cfm

### Falsification note

A larger geographic institution can solve spillovers while creating distance, bureaucracy, or weaker accountability. Regional bodies can become additional veto points. National standards can suppress useful variation. The chapter's claim is not that authority should always move upward, but that decision scale should match externalities while operational choices remain as close to the work as practical.

## Chapter 20 source trail — The Visible Republic

- Federal Highway Administration, “Transportation Asset Management Plans.” Lifecycle stewardship and state-of-good-repair discipline. https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/asset/plans.cfm
- U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, “EPA's 7th Drinking Water Infrastructure Needs Survey and Assessment.” The scale and invisibility of basic system upkeep. https://www.epa.gov/dwsrf/epas-7th-drinking-water-infrastructure-needs-survey-and-assessment
- Acquisition.gov, FAR 1.102. States the federal acquisition system's goals of timely best value, public trust, competition, integrity, and lower administrative cost. https://www.acquisition.gov/far/1.102
- NIOSH, “Transforming Construction: Automation and Robotics for a Safer Future.” Evidence for both productivity promise and safety constraints in physical automation. https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/bulletin/2024/construction-robotics.html

### Falsification note

Visible capital projects can become political theater, and a well-governed society also depends on invisible institutions, transfers, regulation, care, research, justice, and services. Public trust cannot be reduced to infrastructure delivery. The chapter's concluding test is intentionally partial: shared physical competence is one way national prosperity becomes legible in ordinary life, not the sole measure of a good republic.

## Cross-book research cautions

- **Forecast date matters.** AI capabilities, electricity forecasts, labor projections, and infrastructure programs are moving targets. Re-check current values before publication.
- **Avoid causal compression.** A project delay, housing cost, outage, or labor shortage rarely has one cause.
- **Separate stock from flow.** A large project queue is not completed capacity; a large infrastructure-need estimate is not annual spending; a job opening is not a permanent shortage.
- **Name the level of government.** Federal rules, state law, local zoning, regional planning, utility regulation, and private ownership are not interchangeable.
- **Distinguish scenarios from commitments.** DOE, EIA, BLS, and other forward-looking exercises are useful for stress-testing the thesis, not certainties about the future.
- **Do not use AI as a magic implementation step.** Machine assistance can improve retrieval, prediction, drafting, and coordination while leaving authority, incentives, data quality, field verification, and accountability unresolved.
