# Revision Pass 02 — Physical Bottlenecks and Project Learning

## Scope

This pass strengthens the manuscript after the complete-draft merge without reopening the whole architecture at once.

Primary target: Chapter 4, **The Physical Economy Returns**.

## Editorial objective

Turn Chapter 4 into the manuscript's explicit bottleneck-migration chapter: when one input becomes cheaper and faster, slower complements become more economically important at the margin.

The pass should:

- sharpen the distinction between money, authorization, and actual productive capacity;
- add concrete evidence about transformer lead times and over-customization;
- treat standardization as a form of productive capacity rather than aesthetic uniformity;
- strengthen the argument that project data and repetition create institutional learning;
- preserve Chapter 5 for the specific economics and governance of electric power;
- avoid treating factories as intrinsically superior to services;
- keep the thesis robust to uncertainty about AI capability and electricity demand.

## Evidence added

- U.S. Department of Energy, Office of Electricity, **Supply Chain and Market Analysis / Distribution Transformers**. DOE reports that distribution-transformer lead times increased from roughly 3–6 months in 2019 to 12–30 months in 2023 and describes more than 80,000 transformer varieties across U.S. utilities. The policy lesson is not merely "make more transformers"; it is that specification fragmentation can itself consume manufacturing capacity.
- U.S. Department of Energy, **Distribution Transformer Convening Webinar** (2026). DOE describes continued long lead times, sharply higher demand since 2019, and multi-year lead times for some larger transformers.
- U.S. Energy Information Administration, **Annual Energy Outlook 2026 / data center server energy use**. EIA scenarios reinforce the chapter's treatment of digital growth as a source of physical load while preserving uncertainty about the magnitude.
- Bureau of Labor Statistics, **Construction Labor Productivity**. BLS's construction productivity series is retained as the empirical anchor for the claim that construction productivity belongs inside industrial strategy.

## Falsification discipline

The chapter must not imply that every bottleneck becomes physical, that standardization is always superior to customization, or that supply constraints justify indiscriminate subsidy. Algorithmic efficiency, substitution, imports, demand response, modularity, and organizational redesign can all relax physical constraints. The argument is narrower: productive capacity is a system, and spending cannot purchase what the system is not yet capable of supplying on the required timeline.
