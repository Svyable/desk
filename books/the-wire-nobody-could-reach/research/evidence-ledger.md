# Evidence Ledger

This ledger separates what the book can establish from what it can responsibly infer. The categories are revisable as archives open or claims fail.

## Class 1 — verified, declassified, documented, or otherwise strongly established

- Ronald W. Pelton worked for the National Security Agency, decided in January 1980 to sell classified information, contacted Soviet officials, met Soviet agents, compromised classified NSA operations, received more than $35,000, was arrested in 1985, convicted in 1986, and had his conviction upheld. Primary judicial anchor: *United States v. Pelton*, 835 F.2d 1067 (4th Cir. 1987): https://m.openjurist.org/835/f2d/1067/united-states-v-pelton
- The FBI’s official “Year of the Spy” history identifies Pelton as an NSA communications specialist who sold classified U.S. collection-program information to the Soviet Union for years: https://www.fbi.gov/history/cases-and-criminals/year-of-the-spy-1985
- Pelton’s initial calls arranging Soviet contact were intercepted under foreign-intelligence surveillance but not attributed to him at the time, according to the appellate record.
- The codeword Ivy Bells entered public reporting around Pelton’s legal proceedings in 1986, and major contemporary reporting associated it with a highly sensitive Navy undersea communications-intelligence operation.
- CIA Director William Casey pressed the Justice Department to consider action against NBC after the network reported on Ivy Bells and Pelton, establishing the extraordinary sensitivity of the reporting without validating every technical detail in the broadcast.
- USS *Halibut* was a real nuclear submarine whose mission changed substantially after the Regulus era; its later service included unusually sensitive deep-ocean work and exceptional commendations, although exact public mission attribution is incomplete.
- Modern NATO and European Union programs explicitly treat submarine communications and energy links as critical infrastructure requiring prevention, surveillance, resilience, repair and deterrence.

## Class 2 — credible but incomplete reconstruction or testimony

- A U.S. submarine operation in the early 1970s entered the Sea of Okhotsk and covertly instrumented a Soviet undersea naval communications cable. This is the historical core commonly identified as Operation Ivy Bells.
- Serious narrative histories and participant-oriented accounts identify USS *Halibut* with the 1971 initial operation and later specialized submarines with continued servicing or related undersea collection.
- Public reconstructions describe the target at roughly 400 feet depth and the collection hardware as a non-destructive or minimally intrusive inductive tap placed around the cable rather than a conventional cut-and-splice interception.
- The tap reportedly recorded traffic locally for periodic recovery and processing by NSA.
- Repeated accounts state that significant Soviet traffic on the protected line was unencrypted or unusually candid because users trusted the cable’s physical isolation. The broad claim is plausible and important; exact proportions and traffic categories remain poorly documented publicly.
- The Soviet Union recovered U.S. tap hardware after receiving information that narrowed the search. Public accounts tie this directly to Pelton’s disclosure, but the complete primary operational chronology remains classified or unavailable.
- *Halibut* is also widely reported to have recovered fragments of Soviet missile-test hardware from the seabed. This fits known deep-ocean capabilities but needs stronger primary-document reconstruction before every technical claim is treated as closed.

## Class 3 — disputed, over-specific, insufficiently corroborated, or frequently laundered through repetition

- Exact dimensions, mass, power system and component list of each generation of Ivy Bells tap.
- Exact servicing intervals and number of successful retrieval missions across all phases.
- Claims that every later U.S. special-mission submarine cable operation was formally part of Ivy Bells.
- Exact contents of the intercepted Soviet traffic and specific downstream policy or weapons decisions attributed to individual recordings.
- A single clean story for how the Okhotsk cable route was originally identified; the famous cable-warning-sign account is plausible and often repeated but should remain attributed until primary documentation is found.
- Exact missile types, quantities and intelligence conclusions from the reported seabed-debris recovery mission without primary exploitation reports.
- Modern allegations that particular current cable failures were covert state interception or sabotage merely because capable states possess relevant undersea platforms.

## Class 4 — speculative synthesis and open conjecture

- The Soviet security architecture effectively allowed geography to perform part of the job that cryptography should have performed.
- The U.S. security architecture effectively allowed compartmentation to perform part of the job that revocable control and long-horizon personnel security could not perform.
- The operation and its compromise form a mirrored case of hard-perimeter thinking: the Soviets lost confidentiality when the physical perimeter was crossed; the Americans lost the source when an informational perimeter was crossed.
- Repeated servicing is an important empirical discriminator between a one-off secret demonstration and a mature hidden capability because maintenance creates infrastructure, tacit knowledge, schedules and personnel lineage.
- Known secret programs can be used as controls for evaluating contested hidden-program allegations: long-lived, physically consequential programs should tend to create some mixture of logistics, facilities, platform lineage, legal seams, artifacts, personnel memories and downstream technical effects.
- Modern undersea infrastructure should be governed as an adversarial system in which access is assumed possible and confidentiality, availability, detection, repair and attribution are independent problems rather than one perimeter problem.

## Claim-handling rule

The manuscript should never promote a Class 2 narrative detail into Class 1 merely because multiple later books repeat the same source. A Class 3 detail can still be useful as a hypothesis if labeled. Class 4 arguments must remain defeasible and should generate comparative predictions rather than becoming morals that absorb all evidence.
