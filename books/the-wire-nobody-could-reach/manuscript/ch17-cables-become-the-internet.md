# Cables Become the Internet

The cable changed its contents faster than the ocean changed its character.

The Soviet line targeted by Ivy Bells carried military communications through a Cold War network built for a different information economy. Modern submarine fiber carries civilian life at planetary scale: cloud traffic, financial transactions, government communications, video calls, industrial coordination, software, messages, scientific data and the quiet synchronization that makes global systems feel local.

The strategic concentration is larger now.

The technical analogy is not.

That distinction has to be kept clean.

A copper communications cable carrying electrical signals can, under some conditions, be monitored through electromagnetic coupling from outside the conductor. Modern optical fiber carries information as light through glass. The physical problem of covert interception is different. Fiber can be bent or split under particular conditions, amplifiers and repeaters create other opportunities, landing stations are often more accessible than deep-ocean segments, and end-to-end encryption can reduce the intelligence value of captured traffic dramatically.

“Ivy Bells, but fiber” is not an engineering description.

It is a question about concentration.

Where does modern information become physically narrow enough that geography matters again?

The answer includes the seabed.

The European Commission’s 2025 cable-security strategy says submarine cables carry about 99 percent of intercontinental internet traffic. NATO officials use slightly different formulations depending on what they are counting, but the point is the same: the internet is not airborne metaphor. It is glass on the bottom.

This is one of the century’s funniest linguistic accidents. We moved computing into “the cloud” while laying more of the cloud’s nervous system under oceans.

Abstraction grew as physical dependency deepened.

Most users never think about a landing station. They open a page and geography disappears. Network engineering has succeeded when distance becomes boring.

That success produces the same psychological condition that made the Soviet cable interesting: infrastructure fades from awareness as it becomes reliable.

The difference is that modern global networks generally assume failure more explicitly. Submarine cables break every year from fishing, anchors, earthquakes, equipment faults and other causes. Operators route traffic around damage. Repair ships locate faults, raise cable from depth, splice fiber and restore service. Route diversity and spare capacity exist because no one serious believes a cable is untouchable.

Reliability moved from prevention toward resilience.

That is progress.

It is not immunity.

Physical concentration still matters where alternate routes are limited, where islands or remote regions depend on a small number of links, where landing stations cluster, or where repair capacity is constrained. A network can be globally redundant and locally brittle.

This is why cable maps matter strategically. They show not just where information travels but where redundancy narrows.

The old intelligence question returns in a new form: which parts of the network believe geography is someone else’s problem?

Modern cables also carry something Ivy Bells could only hint at: economic sovereignty.

A state may not own the platforms on which its citizens’ data travels. A private consortium may operate routes critical to national communications. A cloud provider may depend on multiple cables it does not fully control. A landing station can sit inside one legal jurisdiction while connecting systems governed by many others.

Security crosses ownership boundaries.

That makes coordination harder than in a military cable system and resilience more distributed.

Who is responsible for detection?

Who owns repair vessels?

Who pays for route diversity that may never be used?

Who decides whether an outage is accident or sabotage?

Who shares intelligence with a private operator during a suspected state attack?

Who protects a cable outside territorial waters?

The technology moved from military infrastructure to international political economy.

The old lesson—geography is not encryption—now has a companion: ownership is not sovereignty.

A government can depend on privately owned infrastructure it cannot command instantly. A company can own infrastructure it cannot defend militarily. An alliance can patrol waters containing cables belonging to firms from many countries. Repair capacity may be commercial while the threat model is strategic.

The seabed becomes a governance problem before it becomes a shooting problem.

This is one reason modern cable security emphasizes partnerships. NATO’s Baltic Sentry initiative combines naval presence, surveillance and new technology with cooperation around critical infrastructure. The European Union’s cable plan spans mapping, prevention, detection, response, repair and deterrence rather than pretending military patrol alone can solve the problem.

That architecture is the mature descendant of the Ivy Bells lesson, even if nobody designed it as such.

Assume access is possible.

Protect content separately.

Detect abnormal interaction.

Maintain redundancy.

Repair quickly.

Attribute cautiously.

Preserve options after failure.

This is defense in depth applied to geography.

Encryption deserves special attention because it changes the intelligence economics. A collector who acquires ciphertext may still gain metadata, timing, endpoint or traffic-volume information depending on the system. Weak key management can reopen content. Compromised endpoints can make perfect cable-layer encryption irrelevant. But strong end-to-end cryptography can transform a physical tap from an intelligence bonanza into expensive noise.

This is exactly what layered security is supposed to do.

The layer closest to the valuable information should not assume the outer layer will always hold.

The Soviet line appears, in public reconstruction, to have placed too much confidence in the outer layer.

Modern designers know better in principle.

Practice remains messy.

Legacy systems persist. Encryption terminates somewhere. Operators need visibility to troubleshoot networks. Lawful interception regimes create controlled access points. Metadata remains operationally important. Misconfiguration can defeat excellent protocols. Nation states attack endpoints, vendors and credentials when the channel itself is inconvenient.

The attack moves.

This is the part of the Ivy Bells analogy I trust most. Security improvement does not end adversarial interest. It changes the least expensive path.

If fiber is hard to tap invisibly, compromise a landing station.

If the landing station is hardened, target management software.

If management software is hardened, steal credentials.

If credentials are protected, target a supplier.

If content is encrypted, collect metadata.

If metadata are minimized, attack an endpoint where plaintext must eventually exist.

The adversary follows the information backward until cost exceeds value.

Ivy Bells was a case in which the United States followed information physically backward to a wire.

The modern equivalent may never involve a diver.

That is why the historical operation should not be used as tactical instruction for contemporary cable interception. Its relevance is architectural.

It reminds us that every global abstraction has a material layer somewhere.

Packets have routes.

Routes have fibers.

Fibers have landing points.

Landing points have people.

People have credentials.

Credentials have recovery procedures.

The chain can be protected well.

It cannot be wished away.

There is one more continuity worth preserving. The attacker’s objective may be silence.

A saboteur wants disruption.

An intelligence collector often wants normal service.

Modern cable security discussions accelerated after visible damage because outages generate political attention. A quiet interception, if technically feasible and operationally valuable, would create far less public evidence.

We should not infer from that that quiet interception is widespread. That would be the conspiracy error again.

We should infer that availability monitoring and confidentiality monitoring solve different problems.

A green dashboard cannot tell you the network has no unauthorized listener.

That sentence was true when tape recorders sat on the seafloor.

It remains true when light crosses oceans.
