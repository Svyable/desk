# The Seabed Is a Battlefield Again

The seabed did not become strategic in the 2020s. It became visible to people who had stopped noticing it.

Navies never forgot completely. Telecommunications companies could not. Oil and gas operators built entire industries around subsea inspection, intervention and repair. Oceanographers laid instruments across the bottom. Intelligence services retained reasons to care about cables, sensors, wrecks and routes.

What changed was the density of civilian dependence and the frequency with which damage became politically legible.

The explosions that damaged Nord Stream in 2022, later incidents involving communications and energy links in the Baltic, and broader concern over Russian and Chinese seabed capabilities pushed critical undersea infrastructure into ordinary security language. By January 2025 NATO had launched Baltic Sentry, increasing naval presence and integrating frigates, maritime patrol aircraft, drones and other surveillance around infrastructure protection. The European Union released a cable-security action framework the next month. In 2026 NATO allies were still accelerating autonomous and AI-enabled systems for Baltic situational awareness.

The new language is detection and resilience.

That tells us what has changed since Ivy Bells.

During the Cold War operation, the target cable was interesting partly because its users appear to have trusted physical remoteness. Modern planners begin from a more pessimistic premise: infrastructure on the seabed can be reached.

The uncertainty has moved from access to attribution.

A cable is damaged. Was it an anchor?

If an anchor, was it dragged accidentally?

If deliberately, who directed the ship?

If a state was involved, can that involvement be demonstrated publicly?

If the evidence is classified, how much can be revealed without exposing collection?

If attribution is uncertain, how should deterrence work?

The questions sound political because the engineering has already conceded physical vulnerability.

This is an important evolution.

No serious Baltic cable-security plan can treat the bottom as a safe. The sea contains merchant shipping, fishing, survey vessels, naval platforms, autonomous vehicles and infrastructure crews. Some actors have legitimate reasons to work near cables. Others may use legitimate activity as cover.

The defensive problem is therefore behavioral.

Which movement is normal?

Which anchoring pattern is anomalous?

Which vessel history matters?

Which cable fault signature suggests external force?

Which acoustic or optical sensors can observe interaction close enough to matter?

How much surveillance is affordable across thousands of kilometers?

The geometry is large and the object is thin.

Ivy Bells solved that problem offensively by narrowing the search to a target route and sending a specialized platform. Modern defenders face the inverse problem: watch enough route to notice a specialized platform before it does something consequential.

This is difficult even with satellites, automatic identification systems, seabed sensors and autonomous vehicles because not every actor broadcasts honestly and not every useful activity occurs where surface systems can see it.

The ocean still offers concealment.

That continuity explains why autonomous underwater systems matter. A crewed submarine is scarce and expensive. Uncrewed vehicles can inspect, patrol, map and revisit areas at lower marginal cost. Distributed sensors can turn temporary patrol into persistent awareness. Machine-learning systems can flag changes in vessel behavior or acoustic patterns for human review.

The temptation is to call this total surveillance.

It is not.

Persistent sensing creates more data and new blind spots. Autonomous vehicles fail. Batteries end. Communications are constrained underwater. Sensors produce false alarms. Algorithms inherit training bias. Adversaries observe patrol patterns and adapt. Seabed topology creates shadows. Commercial operators may not share data seamlessly with navies.

The same old bottleneck appears downstream: collection is not comprehension.

Pelton’s phone call was collected without immediate identification.

A modern defender can collect vessel tracks without knowing intent.

The problem changes medium and retains structure.

This is why attribution demands multiple channels. A suspicious vessel track means more when aligned with cable telemetry, acoustic evidence, seabed imagery, recovered anchor marks, crew records and intelligence about tasking. None of those sources is perfect. Convergence matters.

The method resembles the evidence discipline used throughout this book.

One sensor is an observation.

Independent channels can become a case.

That principle also keeps politics from outrunning physics. States have incentives to frame ambiguous incidents strategically. Governments may want to deter adversaries, reassure allies or avoid escalation. Companies may minimize vulnerability. Ship owners may describe damage as accident. Intelligence services may know more than they can reveal.

Every actor has an incentive surface.

The historian and analyst should track it explicitly.

Ivy Bells was an intelligence operation whose success depended on the Soviet side not attributing any anomaly to American access. Modern seabed competition includes the reverse incentive: ambiguous damage can produce strategic effect without requiring public confession.

An adversary may benefit if everyone believes it could have cut the cable even when evidence remains incomplete.

Capability can deter through uncertainty.

It can also generate paranoia.

That is why resilience may be more reliable than perfect attribution. If a network can reroute, repair and continue operating, the strategic payoff from ambiguous sabotage declines. If one cable failure isolates a region or disables a critical service, attribution becomes politically urgent because the attacker has found a lever.

Engineering can alter deterrence.

Redundancy is not only reliability. It changes an adversary’s expected return.

Repair capacity is not only maintenance. It shortens the duration of coercive effect.

Encryption is not only privacy. It can reduce intelligence value even when physical access occurs.

Monitoring is not only awareness. It raises the chance a covert action becomes attributable.

The undersea contest is therefore not a duel between submarine and cable. It is a system contest across architecture, sensing, cryptography, commercial logistics, alliance coordination and law.

This is where the phrase “seabed warfare” can become too theatrical. Much of the decisive work happens before conflict and outside navies.

A cable consortium chooses route diversity.

A regulator requires incident reporting.

A port authority records anchor movement.

A repair ship crew maintains specialized skill.

A cloud provider buys capacity on multiple routes.

An alliance builds a common operational picture.

A telecom operator terminates encryption correctly.

A boring procurement decision may do more for resilience than a dramatic patrol.

Ivy Bells already taught this lesson from the offensive side. The operation succeeded because a web of mundane systems supported the extraordinary moment on the bottom.

Modern defense needs the same seriousness about the boring layer.

There is another reason to resist military spectacle. Most cable damage is not sabotage. The world has enough anchors, fishing gear, earthquakes and equipment failures to produce a large natural background of incidents. A security architecture that treats every fault as enemy action will exhaust itself.

This is the base-rate problem on the seabed.

Rare hostile acts occur inside a common population of accidents.

Good detection needs to understand both.

The intelligence community faces this problem constantly. Anomalies are interesting because they are unusual. Unusual does not mean hostile. A ship slowing near a cable can be suspicious or broken. A route deviation can reflect tasking or weather. A cable fault can be cut or fatigue.

The defender has to preserve uncertainty long enough to investigate.

Political media cycles reward the opposite.

The modern seabed is therefore a laboratory for disciplined attribution.

That may be Ivy Bells’ most useful afterlife. The old operation proves that sophisticated states have both the capability and incentive to interact secretly with adversary undersea infrastructure. It does not tell us who caused a modern incident.

History establishes possibility.

Evidence establishes event.

Confusing those two is how a true story becomes a machine for producing false ones.
