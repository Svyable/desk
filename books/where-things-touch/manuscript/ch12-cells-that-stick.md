# Cells That Stick

A human body is not a bag of cells.

It is an architecture of cells attached to cells, cells attached to extracellular matrix, tissues attached across specialized junctions, and organs held in mechanically organized relationships. Remove adhesion and the body does not merely become less sturdy. Its signaling, development, repair, immune behavior, and tissue organization change.

At the cellular scale, contact is identity and instruction.

Two families of adhesion receptors make this especially visible. Cadherins are central to many cell-cell adhesions. Integrins connect cells to components of the extracellular matrix. Both connect, through larger protein assemblies, to the cytoskeleton. Both participate in signaling. Both can respond to mechanical force.

A cell does not just stick.

It feels what it is stuck to.

This is a major conceptual difference between biological adhesion and ordinary glue. A cured adhesive joint is usually judged by whether it holds. A living adhesion can strengthen, weaken, migrate, assemble, disassemble, recruit proteins, alter gene expression, and change the cell's behavior in response to mechanical and biochemical cues.

The contact is an active organelle-like system.

Integrin-based focal adhesions illustrate the idea. Integrins span the cell membrane, binding extracellular matrix outside and connecting through adaptor proteins to actin cytoskeleton inside. Force generated by actomyosin contraction can be transmitted through these sites. Mechanical properties of the matrix influence adhesion organization and signaling. Cells pull on their environment and receive information from how the environment pulls back.

Touch becomes mechanotransduction.

The word sounds technical because it needs to distinguish a remarkable event: a mechanical input becomes a biochemical signal.

A substrate is stiff. A tissue is stretched. A junction bears tension. The cell changes signaling pathways, cytoskeletal organization, migration, differentiation, or gene expression. The force does not need to enter a brain to become information.

Cells interpret mechanics locally.

Cadherins perform related work at cell-cell contacts. They mediate adhesion between neighboring cells and connect to the actin cytoskeleton through catenins and associated proteins. Research over recent decades has shown that cadherin junctions are not static rivets. They respond to tension and participate in force transmission across tissues.

A tissue is a network of loaded contacts.

This makes developmental biology a contact story in a deep sense. Embryonic tissues fold, elongate, invaginate, separate, and rearrange. Cells change neighbors. Adhesions form and release. Forces generated locally propagate through sheets of cells. Geometry and signaling interact.

The organism is constructed by controlled sticking and unsticking.

If adhesion were permanent everywhere, development would freeze. If adhesion were weak everywhere, tissues would fall apart. The biological problem is selective, regulated, force-sensitive contact.

We have met this problem before in tape, seals, robot grippers, and bearings, but biology makes it dynamic at molecular scale.

Cells can change the number, affinity, clustering, and mechanical linkage of receptors. They can remodel the matrix they attach to. They can secrete enzymes that degrade it. They can deposit new matrix. They can move by forming adhesions at the front and releasing them at the rear.

The boundary has agency in the literal biological sense that metabolism maintains and changes it.

This is why cancer invasion involves adhesion. A malignant cell's ability to detach, migrate, interact with matrix, enter and leave blood vessels, and colonize new tissue depends partly on changes in adhesion and mechanobiology. It would be simplistic to call cancer an adhesion disease, but it would be equally wrong to treat adhesion as mere structural background.

Where cells touch affects what cells can become and where they can go.

Wound healing makes the relationship more familiar. Cells migrate into injured regions. Matrix is deposited and remodeled. Mechanical tension changes over time. Scar tissue develops. The repaired interface may never reproduce the original architecture exactly.

Healing is contact reconstruction.

This helps explain why biomaterials cannot be evaluated only by bulk strength. Implant a material and cells encounter a surface already conditioned by proteins from biological fluids. The surface chemistry and topography influence which proteins adsorb, how they orient, and how cells subsequently attach. Cells sense the effective interface created by material, adsorbed molecules, and mechanical environment.

The factory surface survives only briefly as a biological fact.

This is a critical point in biomaterials. A titanium implant may be described by alloy composition and roughness, but the body does not inspect its certificate. Water, ions, proteins, cells, microbes, and immune components arrive. The surface becomes part of a living interphase.

Contact changes the object.

This resembles the battery solid-electrolyte interphase for a reason at the level of general structure, not mechanism. In both cases an initially unstable or foreign contact evolves into a new boundary layer that controls future transport and response. The chemistries are entirely different. The design challenge rhymes: engineer what forms after first contact, not merely what leaves the factory.

First contact is manufacturing by the environment.

Cells also teach us that stiffness can be information.

Classic experiments in mechanobiology showed that cell behavior and lineage-related responses can depend on substrate mechanics under certain culture conditions. The field has since become more nuanced, with researchers examining viscoelasticity, ligand density, stress relaxation, geometry, three-dimensional matrices, and other factors. The broad principle remains: cells respond to mechanical properties of their surroundings, not just soluble chemical signals.

The substrate is part of the message.

This matters in tissue engineering. A scaffold is not merely a frame that holds cells in a shape. Its stiffness, porosity, degradation, surface chemistry, architecture, and ligand presentation influence how cells attach and remodel the environment. A material can be biologically active without releasing a drug because mechanics and geometry themselves can guide behavior.

Structure becomes signal at contact.

The same is true in ordinary tissues. Bone, cartilage, brain, tendon, muscle, and skin present cells with radically different mechanical environments. Cells possess specialized adhesion and cytoskeletal systems suited to those contexts.

There is no universal biological surface.

That fact complicates the dream of a perfectly “biocompatible” material. Compatible with what tissue, for what duration, under what load, with what immune state, in which patient, at which anatomical site?

Biocompatibility is a relationship.

This mirrors friction. There is no coefficient that describes a material independent of the pair and conditions. A material that works beautifully as a bone implant may be inappropriate for a blood-contacting surface. A surface that encourages cell adhesion may also encourage unwanted bacterial attachment. A roughness helpful for osseointegration can create concerns if exposed to regions where plaque or biofilm accumulation matters.

The interface has multiple constituencies.

Designers often want opposite biological responses at different parts of the same device. A dental implant may benefit from strong bone integration below the gum line while needing a cleanable, healthy soft-tissue transition and low microbial burden in exposed regions. A vascular graft wants blood compatibility on its lumen while integrating with surrounding tissue externally. A transcutaneous device must connect mechanically to tissue while crossing a barrier that normally excludes microbes.

One device can require several surfaces.

This is a powerful reason to separate bulk design from surface design. The same structural material can be textured, coated, patterned, functionalized, or polished differently by region to create different contact behaviors.

A part can have multiple identities at its edges.

Cell adhesion also introduces feedback through force. Integrins and cadherins are linked to the cytoskeleton, which is not passive scaffolding. Myosin motors generate tension. Adhesions respond. The cell changes its contractile state. Forces redistribute across neighboring contacts.

The boundary and interior co-regulate each other.

This is another recurring architecture. A tire's deformation changes contact stress, which changes forces on suspension. A thermal joint's temperature changes pressure and material properties, which changes conductance. A seal's friction changes temperature, which changes film thickness. A cell's contractility changes adhesion, which changes signaling, which changes contractility.

Interfaces create loops.

Loops are where systems become nonlinear.

A small change in adhesion can alter force distribution enough to recruit more adhesion molecules. Or it can trigger detachment. A small change in matrix stiffness can alter spreading and contractility. A local tissue injury can change mechanical load on neighboring regions.

Linear intuition weakens near thresholds.

This matters in disease. Fibrosis stiffens tissue, and increased stiffness can influence cellular signaling in ways that help sustain fibrotic behavior. Tumor microenvironments change mechanically and chemically. Vascular endothelial cells respond to shear stress from blood flow. Bone remodels under mechanical loading.

Biology is constantly measuring contact conditions and rewriting itself.

That capacity makes life resilient and vulnerable. A machine surface does not usually decide to grow thicker because load increased. Bone can. A bearing does not recruit repair cells. Tendon can remodel. But adaptive systems can maladapt. Scar tissue can impair function. Chronic inflammation can persist. Bone can resorb around implants under unfavorable conditions.

Adaptation is not optimization.

It is response under evolutionary and physiological constraints.

This distinction protects us from romanticizing biological interfaces. Nature has many bad contacts: arthritic joints, pressure ulcers, kidney stones, scar adhesions, dental plaque, thrombi, calcified valves, infected implants. Evolution produces workable systems, not frictionless perfection.

The body also pays maintenance costs machines often hide. Cells consume energy. Proteins turn over. Matrix is remodeled. Immune surveillance operates continuously. Healing can take weeks or months. A living boundary is expensive precisely because it can repair and adapt.

Self-healing is not free.

Engineers exploring self-healing materials confront the same accounting. A coating that repairs cracks needs stored reactants, reversible bonds, heat, light, or another resource. A robot skin that heals cuts needs material mobility or replacement. A battery interphase that continuously reforms consumes electrolyte and active lithium.

Repair consumes something.

The body budgets this through metabolism.

Cell-cell contacts also show how local boundaries scale into global form. No individual adhesion “knows” the shape of a tissue. Yet millions of local attachments, forces, polarity cues, divisions, and rearrangements produce organs with reproducible architecture.

Form emerges from contact rules.

This is one reason developmental biology is relevant to robotics and materials science in a non-metaphorical way. Engineers increasingly build modular systems from many elements that interact locally: swarm robots, self-assembling materials, cellular manufacturing, distributed sensor networks. The challenge is to choose local connection rules that produce desired global behavior.

Biology has been solving versions of this for a very long time.

The lesson is not to copy cadherins into computers. It is to recognize that connection rules can be computational.

A cell adhesion site transmits force and information. It participates in decisions about movement, growth, and organization. The boundary is part of the control architecture.

This becomes literal in immune cells. Adhesion molecules help leukocytes slow, roll, arrest, and migrate through blood-vessel walls. Contact with other cells shapes activation. T cells form specialized contact structures with antigen-presenting cells. Mechanical and biochemical interactions are intertwined.

A meeting between cells can change the fate of an organism.

At our scale, tissue seems continuous. At cellular scale, it is negotiated constantly.

That continuity is not a property of the cells alone.

It is a property of how they touch.
