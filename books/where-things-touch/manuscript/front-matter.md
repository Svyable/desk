# Where Things Touch

*The Hidden Science of Contact That Holds the World Together*

Sven Hardy Benson

A car can weigh two tons and carry more computing power than the spacecraft that first crossed the Moon. It can know its position to within a few feet, see lane markings, brake each wheel independently, and calculate a safer path faster than a driver can blink.

None of that moves the car one inch unless four patches of rubber can negotiate with the road.

That is the wager of this book. We are accustomed to explaining systems by looking at their impressive interiors: the engine, the processor, the battery chemistry, the nervous system, the institution. But action has to cross a boundary. Force has to leave a tire. Heat has to cross a joint. Current has to enter a semiconductor. Blood has to tolerate a foreign surface. Bone has to decide whether a metal implant belongs. A robot can identify a glass perfectly and still drop it if it does not understand what is happening beneath its fingertips.

The boundary is often small enough to ignore until it fails.

Engineers have names for pieces of this problem. Tribology studies friction, wear, and lubrication. Contact mechanics studies what happens when solids press against one another. Heat-transfer specialists study thermal contact conductance. Electrical engineers worry about contact resistance and fretting. Biomaterials researchers study the first proteins that settle onto an implant. Cell biologists study adhesions. Electrochemists live at electrode-electrolyte interfaces. Semiconductor engineers spend careers trying to persuade electrons across junctions that occupy almost no visible space.

These are real disciplines with their own equations, instruments, controversies, and histories. This book does not collapse them into one science. It follows a recurring physical fact through all of them: large systems frequently depend on very small regions where unlike things become coupled.

The coupling is never perfect. Two polished solids do not meet everywhere. A lubricant that separates surfaces also has to transmit load. A seal must touch enough to stop leakage without generating destructive heat. An electrical connector needs intimate metallic contact while surviving oxidation, vibration, contamination, and repeated mating. An implant surface has to become biologically legible without becoming a home for microbes. A battery interface must let ions pass while restraining the chemistry that would consume the cell.

Contact is a negotiation with contradiction.

That makes it a useful place to look. The bulk of a system tells us what it is made of. The boundary often tells us whether it works.

This is a book about those boundaries.
