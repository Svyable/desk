# The Age We Think We Are

The easiest fact about your age is the number.

It is also the fact most likely to be asked for when somebody wants to know something else.

An insurer wants risk. An employer wants experience. A doctor wants biological context. A government wants eligibility. A lender wants a repayment horizon. A dating app wants a filter. A sports league wants a category. A retirement system wants a trigger. A marketer wants a segment. A child wants to know whether you count as old.

The number answers none of those questions by itself.

It answers how much time has passed since birth.

That is a useful piece of information. It is not a complete theory of a person.

Chronological age became an extraordinary administrative technology because it is cheap, stable, legible, and difficult to argue with. A birth date can be written on a form, stored in a database, compared with a threshold, and applied to millions of people without inspecting their bodies, skills, preferences, responsibilities, or circumstances. Bureaucracies love variables like this. They turn continuous human variation into manageable categories.

Under eighteen. Over twenty-one. Eligible at sixty-two. Full retirement age. Senior discount. Age band. Age-rated premium. Youth fare. Masters division. Mandatory retirement where it still exists. Age of consent. Age of candidacy. Age of majority.

Some age rules are sensible because the alternative would be intrusive or impossible. A society cannot administer every right and restriction through a personalized neurological examination. Bright lines can protect people and make rules enforceable.

But once a variable becomes administratively convenient, it tends to travel.

Age begins standing in for things it was never designed to measure.

This matters in a longer-lived society because the gap between chronological age and the characteristics we actually care about can widen.

Two seventy-year-olds can differ enormously in mobility, cognition, health, wealth, caregiving burden, employment, technological fluency, social connection, and appetite for risk. This was always true. What longevity does is increase the number of people living inside those later decades and increase the social and economic importance of getting the distinctions right.

A category that once described a relatively brief final stage can become a thirty-year container.

“Older adult” can include somebody running a company, somebody caring full-time for a spouse, somebody training for a race, somebody with advanced dementia, somebody raising a grandchild, somebody beginning university, somebody working because retirement is unaffordable, and somebody traveling because retirement finally is.

The category is not false. It is too large to do all the work assigned to it.

We can see the problem by noticing how people answer a question that bureaucracy rarely asks: how old do you feel?

Research on subjective age has found that many adults, particularly beyond early adulthood, report feeling younger than their chronological age. The size and meaning of that gap vary across studies and individuals, and “feeling younger” is not a medical measure. It can reflect health, culture, identity, social comparison, or the fact that our mental image of an age was formed before we arrived there.

The last explanation deserves more respect than it gets.

A forty-year-old knows what forty looked like when he was ten. A seventy-year-old remembers what seventy looked like in his parents’ or grandparents’ generation. Clothing, work, disease, smoking, dentistry, physical activity, technology, social roles, retirement, and expectations all change. The age remains numerically stable while its visible expression moves.

We carry outdated pictures forward.

That creates a recurring surprise: I do not feel the age I thought this age would feel.

Sometimes the surprise is vanity. Sometimes it is evidence that the social meaning of age is changing faster than the number.

Consider photographs of adults from several generations ago. It is easy to look at a forty-five-year-old from the middle of the twentieth century and conclude that people “looked older.” Part of that perception comes from hairstyles, clothing, film, smoking, sun exposure, dental care, and the visual codes we associate with our own grandparents. Some of it may reflect real health differences. The photograph is not a biological assay. Yet the reaction tells us something important: age is partly interpreted through cultural signals.

The same thing happens at work.

A person described as “late career” at fifty-five may have a decade or more before a traditional retirement age and longer if she wants or needs to continue. The label compresses the horizon. It can influence whether managers invest in training, whether the worker volunteers for difficult new projects, and whether colleagues imagine her as part of the organization’s future.

The label can become a forecast, and the forecast can change behavior.

Economists have documented age discrimination in hiring through field experiments in which otherwise similar applications receive different responses depending on age signals. The details vary by occupation and study, and discrimination is not the only reason older applicants face different outcomes. But the pattern is strong enough to make one point unavoidable: employers do not merely observe age. They infer from it.

They may infer shorter tenure. Higher compensation expectations. Less technological adaptability. More health cost. Lower energy. Overqualification. Resistance to management. A near retirement date.

Some of these inferences can occasionally be true for an individual. That does not make age a good substitute for asking the relevant question.

If the job requires learning a new system, test whether the applicant can learn it.

If physical capacity matters, measure capacity.

If tenure matters, ask about intentions rather than assuming a fifty-eight-year-old will leave sooner than a twenty-eight-year-old who may change jobs in eighteen months.

If compensation is the issue, discuss compensation.

A long-lived labor market will eventually be forced toward this specificity because age becomes a poorer shortcut as work lives become more varied.

Medicine faces the same problem from the opposite direction.

Chronological age is clinically useful. Disease prevalence changes with age. Drug metabolism can change. fertility changes. recovery changes. risk changes. There is no serious argument for pretending that a birthday contains no biological information.

Yet medicine has also become increasingly interested in measures that try to capture functional or biological aging rather than elapsed time alone: frailty indices, grip strength, gait speed, cognitive measures, biomarkers, epigenetic clocks, and other attempts to describe how an organism is aging.

The science is uneven. Some measures are useful predictors in defined contexts. Others are marketed ahead of their clinical meaning. A biological-age result with a decimal point can create the illusion that aging has been measured more precisely than the evidence supports.

Still, the effort exists because chronological age is an incomplete variable.

The body does not age as one synchronized clock.

Cardiovascular fitness, bone density, muscle, cognition, immune function, hearing, vision, fertility, kidney function, and disease risk can change at different rates. A person can be exceptionally strong and have poor hearing. Cognitively sharp and metabolically unwell. Chronologically young and chronically ill. Chronologically old and functionally independent.

The temptation is to replace one number with a better number.

That would repeat the mistake.

The more useful move is to ask what dimension matters for the decision.

This is a broader principle for the longevity moment: substitute measurement of the relevant capacity for age whenever doing so is practical and fair.

That sounds simple until institutions try it.

Age thresholds are attractive because they are objective in a narrow sense. A capacity test can introduce bias, cost, gaming, humiliation, and dispute. Who designs it? Who appeals? Does it disadvantage people with disabilities unrelated to the task? Can a wealthy person train for it? Does passing once mean passing indefinitely? Does it turn every entitlement into an examination?

There are good reasons not to individualize everything.

The question is where the convenience of age is worth the error it creates.

Driving offers a useful example because public safety, autonomy, and age stereotypes collide directly. Older adults as a group may face changes in vision, reaction time, cognition, and physical function that affect driving. But a rule based purely on chronological age can remove autonomy from capable drivers while missing impaired drivers below the threshold. Many jurisdictions respond with different combinations of renewal periods, vision tests, medical reporting, road tests, and clinician involvement.

The policy debate is not really about whether aging affects driving.

It is about whether birthday-based rules are the best way to manage the relevant risk.

The same debate will spread.

As healthy longevity becomes more variable, age will increasingly separate from role. The sixty-eight-year-old may be the caregiver rather than the cared-for. The seventy-two-year-old may be the employee and the forty-eight-year-old the retiree. A thirty-five-year-old with a chronic condition may need accommodations culturally associated with old age. A sixty-year-old may be supporting adult children and ninety-year-old parents simultaneously.

The life course stops lining up neatly with the form.

This is already visible in family structure. Longer survival means more generations can be alive at once. That sounds like a sentimental gain until the logistics appear. A person in late middle age may be a grandparent, employee, spouse, adult child, and caregiver to two directions of the family at the same time. Age alone does not describe the load.

It can even mislead us about dependency.

We tend to picture dependency as children at the beginning and frail elders at the end, supported by working-age adults in the middle. Real households are more complicated. Grandparents provide childcare. Retired parents help adult children with housing. Adult children manage parents’ medical care. Older spouses care for one another. Young adults contribute income. Multigenerational households pool money and labor. Dependency is a network, not an age bracket.

Public policy often has to use brackets anyway.

That is where the longevity moment becomes a problem of institutional humility.

A rule can be administratively necessary without being ontologically true.

Sixty-five can be a useful eligibility age without meaning that everybody becomes old at sixty-five.

Eighteen can be a necessary legal boundary without meaning that every capacity appears overnight.

A senior category can make a benefit easy to administer without describing the recipient’s identity.

Trouble begins when institutions forget the distinction between a threshold and a truth.

Individuals forget it too.

People internalize age categories. They decide something is “not for people my age” before testing whether the constraint is physical, financial, social, or imaginary. They stop applying. stop learning, stop dating, stop dressing a certain way, stop traveling alone, stop asking for responsibility, or stop imagining a future that would make the activity sensible.

Sometimes that restraint is wisdom. Bodies change and denial has costs.

Sometimes it is anticipatory obedience to a category.

The difference is difficult to see from inside because age norms rarely arrive as explicit commands. They arrive as raised eyebrows, interfaces, default settings, jokes, advertisements, casting choices, HR language, insurance forms, and the age distribution of the people already in the room.

A seventy-year-old walking into a coding course does not need anyone to tell her she is unusual if every other student appears to be twenty-three.

A fifty-year-old intern does not need a policy against him if the role has been culturally coded as a temporary apprenticeship for the young.

A widower in his eighties does not need a rule against dating if everyone around him treats romantic interest as faintly comic.

Ageism is often ambient before it is explicit.

The World Health Organization’s global work on ageism emphasizes how stereotypes, prejudice, and discrimination based on age affect people across the life course. Longevity makes this more than an ethical concern. If societies expect more people to remain engaged for longer, then age stereotypes become an economic design flaw.

You cannot build a sixty-year working life while culturally depreciating workers after forty-five.

You cannot tell people to retrain while treating the campus as youth territory.

You cannot ask households to finance longer retirements while assuming anybody who continues working after a certain birthday has somehow failed retirement.

You cannot celebrate longer lives while making later life socially smaller.

There is another mistake hiding in the opposite direction.

The longevity industry can become ageist by treating age itself as a defect to be defeated.

Language about “reversing” age, defeating decline, staying young, and refusing to grow old can smuggle in the idea that youth is the only desirable form of adulthood. The result is a strange promise: you may live longer, provided you spend the extra years trying not to resemble an older person.

That is not liberation from age categories. It is submission to the most flattering one.

A more useful longevity culture would permit age to mean more, not less.

Experience can be real without guaranteeing wisdom. Physical limits can matter without defining total capacity. A person can be old and ambitious, old and dependent, old and strong, old and foolish, old and learning, old and grieving, old and beginning. The adjective should add context, not close the sentence.

That requires a different kind of measurement.

Instead of asking only, How old are you?, institutions will increasingly need to ask questions such as: What can you do? What support do you need? What responsibilities do you carry? What time horizon are you planning for? What risks actually matter here? What have you learned? What are you willing to learn? What is reversible if we are wrong?

Those questions are harder to put on a form.

They are also closer to the decisions we claim to be making.

The administrative challenge will be deciding where richer measurement is worth the complexity. Not every senior discount needs a functional assessment. Not every age-based entitlement should become means-tested or capacity-tested. Simplicity has value. Privacy has value. Universality has value. Bright lines can protect against arbitrary discretion.

The goal is not to abolish chronological age.

It is to stop asking it to carry information it does not contain.

This distinction becomes especially important when people begin to live through ages that previous institutions treated as residual.

A society with many active adults in their seventies and eighties will discover that “old” is not one market, one medical state, one work status, one housing preference, or one political interest. Companies will segment more finely because money forces them to notice variation. Governments will eventually follow because budgets do the same. Families will have to follow because caregiving already demands it.

The cultural change may lag longest.

Culture prefers archetypes. Young people begin. Middle-aged people maintain. Old people remember.

Longer lives make the script visibly false.

The age we think we are then starts to separate from the age on the form.

That separation can produce delusion. A person who “feels forty” at seventy cannot negotiate with bone density by confidence alone. It can also produce freedom. Feeling a future in front of you may be one of the conditions required to invest in it.

The challenge is to keep those two truths together.

Age is real.

Age categories are made.

Longevity changes the first slowly and the second all at once, because the moment an old category stops predicting what people can do, it becomes available for revision.

The number stays.

The meaning moves.
