# Interlude — The Stranger Who Gets Your Pension

Your future self is legally you and psychologically negotiable.

That is one of the strangest facts in personal finance.

The person who receives money from your retirement account at eighty-five has your name, your Social Security number, your memories in some altered form, and whatever remains of your body. You can make binding decisions for that person decades in advance.

You can also feel almost nothing for them.

Behavioral scientist Hal Hershfield has spent years studying what he calls future self-continuity: the degree to which people experience a future version of themselves as connected to who they are now. His work suggests that when the future self feels more continuous, vivid, and personally real, people can become more willing to make choices that benefit that future person.

The research is not a complete theory of saving. Income, defaults, debt, employer plans, financial knowledge, housing, health, family, and public policy all matter. A person can feel profound continuity with an eighty-year-old self and still have no money left after rent.

But the idea clarifies something the actuarial model misses.

A long horizon contains an identity problem.

The farther away a future self becomes, the less obvious it is that the present self should sacrifice for that person.

This problem grows with longevity because the person being financed gets farther away.

A twenty-five-year-old saving for sixty-five is already transferring resources forty years forward.

Saving for ninety-five extends the distance to seventy years.

The instruction is rational in one sense and bizarre in another. Give up something now for someone you cannot imagine living in a world you cannot imagine.

Financial planning tries to solve the problem with numbers.

Target balance.

Replacement rate.

Expected return.

Withdrawal rate.

Longevity assumption.

The math is necessary.

It does not make the recipient emotionally real.

This is why the future self often appears only when an event pulls them closer.

A parent becomes frail.

A colleague retires.

A friend has a stroke.

A pension statement arrives with a projected monthly income.

A first grandchild is born.

A person sees a photograph of herself from twenty years earlier and realizes how much continuity has already survived.

The distant age becomes inhabited.

The same process can work in reverse.

A person can reach fifty and feel little continuity with the twenty-five-year-old who chose the career. The old self becomes another stranger.

That stranger signed contracts.

Took debt.

Married.

Moved.

Bought the house.

Selected the profession.

The present self is living inside the earlier self’s forecasts.

Longevity therefore creates obligations in both directions.

The present owes something to the future.

The future inherits something from the past.

Most life planning focuses on the first obligation.

Save for old age.

Protect your health.

Build skills.

Buy insurance.

The second obligation is less discussed: how much authority should the past self retain once the person receiving the consequences has changed?

That is the identity version of the one-way door.

A twenty-eight-year-old can choose a career rationally. The sixty-year-old inheriting the choice should not be required to honor it merely because both people share a continuous legal identity.

This sounds contradictory.

If future selves deserve protection, why should past decisions lose authority?

Because continuity is not sameness.

That sentence may be the psychological heart of a long life.

The person at eighty deserves resources because she is you.

The person at fifty deserves the ability to revise because she is not identical to the person who made the earlier plan.

A long-life system has to respect both facts.

This changes how we think about commitment.

A commitment is a transfer of authority across time.

The present self decides that a future self will do something: make a payment, remain responsible for a child, share property with a spouse, continue obeying a contract, preserve an institution, repay a debt.

That transfer can be valuable because it protects long projects from temporary moods.

If every future self could disown every past promise, trust would collapse.

But if every past self could control every future self completely, adaptation would collapse.

Commitment works because society limits both forms of power.

Contracts can be enforced.

They can also expire, be renegotiated, discharged, appealed, or terminated under defined conditions.

Marriage creates obligations.

Divorce exists.

A mortgage binds the future.

The property can be sold.

A profession creates sunk costs.

The worker can leave.

A pension locks money away.

Rules eventually permit access.

These are identity bridges.

They allow one version of a person to make decisions for another without making the authority absolute.

Longevity changes the appropriate bridge length.

A decision made for five years asks less of future identity than one made for fifty.

The longer the contract, the more likely preferences, health, technology, family, and economic conditions change.

This does not make long contracts bad.

It makes revision mechanisms more valuable.

The future-self idea also helps explain why longevity planning can become emotionally unequal.

Some people can picture old age because they have older people in their lives who resemble futures they want.

A grandparent who remains funny, engaged, and independent can make eighty-five feel like a life stage.

A family history dominated by early death, painful illness, or financial insecurity can produce a different imagination.

Subjective survival research captures part of this numerically.

Future-self continuity captures another part emotionally.

The person does not merely estimate whether old age will occur.

She imagines whether the person there is someone worth sacrificing for.

This is why cultural representation matters more than marketing departments admit.

If later life is represented mainly as decline, the future self becomes a liability.

Save so you can afford the bad years.

Exercise so you can avoid becoming that person.

Buy this product so you remain unlike other old people.

The future is framed as defense.

A more complete picture includes capability, intimacy, learning, work, leisure, dependence, care, disability, humor, boredom, status loss, freedom, and ordinary days.

The future self does not need to be idealized to become real.

In fact, idealization can make continuity harder.

If the only acceptable eighty-year-old self is exceptionally healthy, wealthy, thin, traveled, cognitively sharp, and surrounded by family, then an ordinary older person can feel like failure.

The present self may be saving for a fantasy rather than a person.

A robust future image has room for support.

That matters because long-life planning requires decisions about vulnerability.

Who can act for you if cognition changes?

Where can you live if mobility changes?

What happens if a spouse dies?

How much care can you afford?

Which relationships need maintenance before they become emergency contacts?

These questions are unpleasant partly because they ask the current self to identify with a dependent future self.

The longevity industry often avoids this by selling control.

Optimize enough and perhaps dependency can be postponed indefinitely.

The promise is emotionally effective because it preserves continuity with the competent present self.

You will still be you because you will still be able to do what you do now.

That is not a safe assumption.

A future self can remain you after capacity changes.

This seems like philosophy until someone has to design a power of attorney.

Then identity becomes administration.

The document decides when another person can act, what authority they have, and how continuity is maintained after the current decision-maker cannot perform every function.

The same issue appears in advance directives, trusts, beneficiary designations, guardianship, digital legacy, and business succession.

These systems exist because the self is continuous enough to own obligations and discontinuous enough to require representation.

Longevity increases the period over which these documents can matter.

It also increases the danger of creating them once and forgetting them.

A health proxy selected at forty may no longer be the right person at seventy.

A beneficiary designation can preserve a relationship that ended decades earlier.

A will can reflect a family structure that no longer exists.

The document is the frozen intention of a past self.

Review is how the present reclaims authorship.

This gives us another argument for periodic review points.

They are not merely administrative hygiene.

They are identity reconciliation.

Does the person who signed this still represent the person living now?

The question can apply to careers, housing, finances, and relationships as much as legal documents.

This is why the forty-year-old problem felt so central earlier in the book. Around midlife, a person can become conscious of being governed by earlier selves while still having a large population of future selves to protect.

The person is simultaneously heir and ancestor.

That is a peculiar position.

We usually reserve the word ancestor for family history.

In a long life, you become an ancestor to your own future.

Your choices shape a person you will eventually become but do not yet know.

That framing can make long-term investment feel less like self-denial and more like stewardship.

It can also make flexibility feel less selfish.

A past self does not deserve loyalty simply because she acted first.

She deserves gratitude for useful capital and forgiveness for forecasts made with less information.

The future self deserves resources and room.

This may be the right balance for longevity planning.

Do not sacrifice everything for a stranger at ninety.

Do not spend everything because the stranger feels unreal.

Do not obey the twenty-five-year-old forever.

Do not mock the twenty-five-year-old for failing to predict the world.

Leave assets.

Leave options.

Review the promises.

Keep the ones that still deserve to bind.

This is how a person can remain continuous without becoming trapped by continuity.

The stranger who gets your pension is not actually a stranger.

That is why you save for her.

She is not exactly you either.

That is why you should leave her a door.
