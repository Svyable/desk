# The Age Rule

Age rules are efficient because they are lazy.

That is not an insult.

Administration requires laziness of a disciplined kind.

A system serving millions of people cannot investigate every case as though it were the first. It needs variables that are easy to verify, hard to manipulate, and sufficiently related to the thing being decided.

Age is nearly perfect for this purpose.

Everyone has one.

It changes predictably.

It can be documented.

It is correlated with many capacities and risks.

That is why age appears everywhere.

School entry.

Driving.

Voting.

Military service.

Alcohol.

Insurance.

Retirement.

Pensions.

Health coverage.

Sports leagues.

Employment law.

Public office.

Benefits.

The age rule is one of civilization's favorite shortcuts.

Longevity does not make the shortcut useless.

It makes its error term larger in some domains.

This is a subtle problem because a rule can remain statistically sensible while becoming individually absurd.

An older population can have higher average disease risk while containing more healthy seventy-year-olds than any previous generation. A retirement age can remain fiscally useful while poorly describing who can or should work. A driving renewal rule can correctly target a higher-risk group while capturing capable drivers and missing impaired younger ones.

The problem is not whether age correlates with something.

It usually does.

The problem is what the rule is trying to accomplish and what happens when the correlation becomes too crude for the consequence.

This gives us a way to classify age rules without pretending they are all the same.

Some age rules are developmental.

They protect children or establish legal adulthood because society needs a practical threshold even though maturity is gradual.

Some are risk rules.

They use age because health, accident, or mortality probabilities change with age.

Some are allocation rules.

They determine when benefits or obligations begin.

Some are category rules.

They organize participation into age bands for sports, education, or services.

Some are historical fossils.

They remain because the institution around them has not been redesigned.

Longevity puts the last two groups under particular pressure.

An age rule can begin as a practical proxy and end as a cultural truth nobody remembers choosing.

Retirement is the clearest case because the rule affects identity as well as money.

Chapter Twelve examined sixty-five as the famous threshold. The broader issue is what happens when several institutions coordinate around the same number.

Health coverage begins.

Pension eligibility changes.

Employer expectations shift.

Financial plans mature.

Discounts appear.

The person enters a marketing category.

One administrative age becomes a social operating system.

That is powerful because coordination is useful.

Shared thresholds make planning easier. Employers know when people may leave. Households know when benefits begin. Financial products can be designed around common ages.

The cost is lock-in.

Changing one rule can destabilize others.

A government raises pension age, but employers still stop hiring at sixty.

A worker is told to remain economically active without being given a labor market that imagines her as active.

The threshold moves in law and stays fixed in culture.

Longevity policy therefore needs more than updated ages.

It needs synchronized expectations.

The same issue appears in education.

There is rarely a formal rule saying university belongs to the young.

The age rule is embedded in design.

Admissions language assumes recent schooling.

Housing assumes no children.

Recruiting assumes first employment.

Campus life assumes control over weekdays.

Financial aid interacts with family structure in complicated ways.

The institution does not ban a fifty-year-old.

It presents a system whose defaults describe somebody else.

That is a soft age rule.

Soft rules are harder to challenge because no one can point to the sentence that excludes.

They live in expectations.

The labor market has many of them.

“High potential” often means young enough to have a long runway.

“Culture fit” can include unspoken age codes.

“Digital native” converts a historical accident of birth timing into a presumed competence.

“Overqualified” can become a respectable way to reject someone whose experience exceeds the role.

The Age Discrimination in Employment Act prohibits discrimination against workers forty and older in covered employment contexts in the United States. The existence of legal protection does not eliminate soft rules because inference can happen without explicit policy.

A hiring manager does not have to say old.

She can say expensive.

Slow.

Not a fit.

Will leave soon.

The law can police actions more easily than private forecasts.

Longevity makes those forecasts more costly because they can contradict actual horizon.

A sixty-year-old may have ten or fifteen years of work ahead.

A thirty-year-old may leave next year.

Age is being used as a proxy for tenure in a labor market where tenure itself is not distributed the way the stereotype assumes.

This suggests a principle for age rules: whenever the underlying variable can be measured directly at reasonable cost, ask whether the proxy is still justified.

If the concern is ability to perform a job, assess ability.

If the concern is safe driving, assess relevant driving capacity where feasible.

If the concern is contribution years, count them.

If the concern is financial need, measure need.

This sounds like an argument for replacing age with individualized testing everywhere.

That would be a mistake.

Direct measurement has costs and risks.

Tests can be biased.

They can be invasive.

They can be expensive.

They can be gamed.

They can produce false precision.

A capacity test can become a gatekeeping industry more arbitrary than the age rule it replaced.

Bright lines also create dignity because they reduce discretionary judgment.

A universal benefit at an age threshold does not require a person to prove frailty, poverty, or incapacity.

That can be a feature.

A society can choose to provide something because reaching a stage is enough.

The problem is therefore not age rules versus no age rules.

It is rule fit.

What error is acceptable for the decision?

A five-percent discount at a museum can tolerate a crude age threshold.

The loss of a livelihood requires more care.

A legal right may need a bright line for legitimacy.

A medical decision may need individualized evidence.

An insurance price may use population statistics but should not pretend they are a moral judgment.

The stakes determine how much laziness the system can afford.

Longevity changes the stakes by extending the duration of classification.

Call someone old at sixty and the label may follow for thirty years.

Treat someone as late-career at fifty and the category may shape twenty years of investment.

Design a community around retirees as passive consumers and a large population may live inside the expectation for decades.

A short classification can be a convenience.

A long classification can become destiny.

This is why later life needs more internal stages even if we resist naming them all.

Marketers will invent categories because they need specificity. Medicine already distinguishes functional states. Families intuitively know the difference between a vigorous seventy-year-old and a frail one.

Public policy often remains broad because universalism has virtues.

The challenge is to preserve those virtues while avoiding the fiction that all people above a threshold are interchangeable.

There is another path: keep age-based rights and reduce age-based restrictions.

This asymmetry is worth considering.

A benefit can begin at a certain age without requiring a person to leave work.

A transit discount can be universal without implying incapacity.

Health coverage can begin without labeling the recipient retired.

Eligibility can expand while participation remains voluntary.

Age can open a door without closing another.

Many bad age rules do both.

They grant something and silently withdraw expectation elsewhere.

You gain the pension.

You lose the imagined career.

You gain the discount.

You become invisible to the premium brand.

You qualify for the senior program.

You stop being invited to the general one.

The rule changes not only access but social role.

A longer-lived society should become suspicious of that coupling.

The age rule also interacts with politics in uncomfortable ways.

Older people vote at high rates in many democracies. Longer life can increase the duration over which a person participates politically and holds property. That can be stabilizing because experience remains represented.

It can also create fears of gerontocracy.

The obvious reaction is to ask whether leaders can be too old.

This is the age-rule temptation at its most visible.

A public official’s chronological age can be relevant to health, cognitive risk, energy, succession, and the time horizon over which policy consequences will be lived. It can also become a lazy substitute for evidence about actual capacity.

A seventy-nine-year-old can be capable.

A fifty-five-year-old can be impaired.

Voters and institutions need information about function, not only the year of birth.

At the same time, public office raises legitimate concerns that ordinary employment does not. Power can hide impairment. Partisans can deny visible decline. Terms can outlast the ability to perform. Succession matters.

The solution cannot be pretending age is irrelevant.

It also should not be assuming a birthday resolves the question.

This is what makes age rules politically attractive: they remove judgment from contested cases.

A mandatory limit is legible.

A capacity process is messy.

But legibility can be purchased with unjustified exclusion.

The same tension exists in mandatory retirement for certain occupations. Safety-sensitive roles may justify stricter rules because the cost of failure is high and some age-related risks are difficult to assess perfectly.

Yet any mandatory age should be understood as a risk-management choice, not proof that everyone crossing the threshold becomes incapable.

The distinction protects both safety and dignity.

It also keeps the rule revisable as evidence and technology change.

This is an important longevity principle: age rules should have expiration dates of their own.

Not automatic repeal.

Review.

If a threshold was chosen under one demographic, technological, or medical regime, it should not be assumed correct forever.

What was the original purpose?

Does age still predict the relevant variable well enough?

Has direct measurement improved?

Have the consequences of error changed?

Do adjacent institutions still align?

Who bears the false positives and false negatives?

A rule that survives those questions earns durability.

A rule that survives because nobody remembers why it exists is administrative sediment.

Longevity will produce more sediment unless review becomes normal.

This is especially true because age thresholds are politically sticky.

People plan around them.

Industries grow around them.

Changing them creates winners and losers by cohort. A person one month on one side of a new rule can have a different entitlement from someone one month on the other.

Gradual transitions can reduce unfairness but extend complexity.

The politics encourages postponement.

Then demographic change accumulates until reform becomes urgent.

This is another version of the one-way door.

Institutions delay small revisions until only large revisions remain.

A long-life society should prefer continuous calibration where possible.

Not because people enjoy policy churn.

Because slowly changing realities are easiest to absorb through slowly changing rules.

The same philosophy applies culturally.

Age norms can be recalibrated without pretending age disappears.

A seventy-year-old beginner should stop being surprising.

A fifty-year-old trainee should stop being comic.

A thirty-year-old caregiver should not be considered off-schedule.

An eighty-year-old needing substantial help should not be treated as a failed version of active aging.

The range of legitimate states widens.

That is what longevity does at its best.

It does not remove chronology.

It removes some of chronology's monopoly over meaning.

The age rule will remain because institutions need shortcuts.

The goal is not to defeat the shortcut.

It is to make every shortcut remember that it is one.
