# A Longer Life Has a Price Today

A longer life is expensive before it happens.

The bill arrives in the present.

Save more. Work longer. Buy insurance. Delay claiming. Stay healthy. Maintain the house. Keep skills current. Do not spend too quickly. Do not spend so slowly that the life you are trying to protect becomes smaller than necessary. Plan for care. Plan for inflation. Plan for a spouse living longer than you. Plan for yourself living longer than your spouse. Plan for a future in which the plan is wrong.

Longevity is often marketed as a gift of time. Financially, it is also a demand for capital.

The simplest retirement arithmetic makes this obvious. If somebody stops earning at sixty-five and dies at seventy-five, accumulated savings must support roughly ten years. If the same person lives to ninety-five, the period triples. Real planning is much more complicated because investment returns, Social Security, pensions, housing, taxes, health costs, inflation, part-time work, family transfers, and spending patterns all matter. But the direction is not complicated. More years without earnings require either more resources, lower spending, later retirement, higher returns, more support from others, or some combination of them.

The uncertainty creates a peculiar problem.

You do not know your own number.

An insurer can pool mortality across thousands of people. An individual cannot. You get one life. If you die earlier than expected, excessive saving can mean unused consumption, deferred experiences, and assets transferred to heirs. If you live longer than expected, inadequate saving can mean dependence, financial anxiety, reduced care options, and a forced contraction late in life.

The person planning retirement therefore has to make a decision under one of the most consequential uncertainties available: how many future selves must the present self finance?

Economists call one part of this longevity risk. The phrase is cold enough to be useful. Living longer is not the risk in the ordinary human sense. Outliving the resources set aside for living longer is.

That risk explains why annuities exist. In its simplest form, an annuity converts a pool of money into income that continues while the purchaser remains alive. The customer gives up some liquidity and bequest potential in exchange for insurance against extreme survival. The product is conceptually elegant because it turns an individual problem back into a pooled one.

Yet voluntary annuity demand has often been lower than simple economic models would predict. Researchers have spent decades studying the so-called annuity puzzle. Explanations include bequest motives, complexity, distrust, pricing, existing annuitized income such as Social Security, desire for liquidity, health uncertainty, and the fact that people may underestimate their chances of living to advanced ages.

That last explanation belongs at the center of this book.

If a person expects to die earlier than actuarial tables suggest, a product that pays for very old age looks less valuable. The same pessimism can reduce saving, increase early claiming of retirement benefits, or make late-life investments appear irrational. Subjective survival beliefs are not merely opinions about death. They can become discount rates applied to the self.

The future self has to be believed in before she can be funded.

This is an unusual form of empathy. The eighty-five-year-old you is a stranger with your legal name.

She may live in a house you have never seen, use technologies that do not exist, care about people you have not met, and face medical needs you cannot predict. Every dollar you save for her is a transfer across time. Every skill you maintain for her is another. Every friendship you preserve, accessible home you choose, exercise habit you sustain, or document you organize is an investment in somebody you cannot ask whether she appreciates it.

The difficulty is that the present self is not imaginary.

She has rent. Children. A trip she wants to take while her knees work. A parent who needs help now. A business idea. A divorce. A tuition bill. A leaking roof. A year when the market fell. A year when work was intolerable. An afternoon that will not come back.

Longevity planning can therefore become morally backward when it treats current life as a staging area for old age.

The purpose of saving is not to win a contest for the largest unspent balance at death.

The purpose is to make time less coercive.

Money changes what can be refused. A person with savings can leave a bad job, retrain, take care of a parent, recover from illness, wait for better work, move out of an unsafe home, retire when work becomes physically unsustainable, or absorb a market shock without converting it into catastrophe. In a longer life, this option value may matter as much as the terminal retirement balance.

That changes how we should think about the price of longevity.

The standard financial model divides life into accumulation and decumulation. Working years build assets. Retirement spends them down. The model is useful because cash flows do change when work stops. But a multistage life weakens the boundary. A person may accumulate, draw down, retrain, earn again, care for family, work part time, sell a house, start a business, claim benefits, and continue earning in various combinations.

The financial plan becomes less like a reservoir and more like a series of bridges.

Bridge from an old career to a new one. Bridge through caregiving. Bridge through illness. Bridge from full-time work to partial work. Bridge between homes. Bridge through a late education. Bridge to a pension age. Bridge between the death of one spouse and the financial reorganization that follows.

The value of liquidity rises when the path is uncertain.

This can conflict with systems that reward commitment. Retirement accounts often offer tax advantages in exchange for restrictions. Mortgages convert income into an illiquid asset. Pensions reward tenure. Insurance protects against defined risks. These are not bad designs. They solve real problems. But a person whose life course changes repeatedly can become asset-rich and option-poor.

The classic example is housing.

A household can accumulate substantial equity while having little cash. The house may be financially successful and practically constraining. Moving can trigger transaction costs, taxes, higher interest rates, renovation, loss of community, or a change in care access. An owner may have enough wealth on paper to appear secure and too little flexible capital to change the structure of daily life.

Longevity increases the chance that this mismatch persists for years.

The same issue appears in education debt, professional licenses, small businesses, and deferred compensation. Long-term commitments can compound wealth and expertise. They can also reduce maneuverability.

That is why “save more” is an incomplete longevity strategy.

The question is what kind of future the savings make possible.

A person who expects a long life may rationally allocate more to retirement. She may also rationally spend money at forty-five to buy a credential that changes the next twenty years, reduce work hours while children are young, move closer to parents, make a house accessible before a crisis, or take a sabbatical that prevents a permanent exit from work.

These expenditures look different if the only financial objective is maximizing assets at retirement.

They look like investments if the objective is preserving a workable life across decades.

This is where longevity and discounting meet.

A dollar today is generally worth more than a dollar in the future because it can be invested, because inflation changes purchasing power, and because immediate consumption has value. Finance formalizes this through discount rates. Human beings have psychological discount rates too. We value present rewards more heavily than distant ones, sometimes far more heavily.

Longer expected lives complicate the intuition. More future years can make distant outcomes more relevant. They can also make the future feel so far away that urgency disappears.

A twenty-five-year-old told to save for age ninety may hear the same practical instruction as somebody told to prepare for a lunar colony. The horizon is too distant to motivate. A sixty-year-old who begins to believe ninety is plausible may suddenly find the same age concrete.

Planning changes when a future date crosses from abstract to imaginable.

This is one reason lived examples matter. An eighty-eight-year-old parent can make late life more real than a retirement calculator. So can caring for someone whose savings ran short, watching a colleague work happily into the seventies, or seeing a friend become unable to work earlier than expected.

Experience updates the private forecast.

Markets update it too, sometimes noisily. A booming longevity industry can make extreme lifespan claims culturally salient long before science supports them. Retirement calculators routinely default to planning ages in the nineties. Financial advisers increasingly discuss “longevity planning” rather than a fixed retirement horizon. Stanford’s work on financial advice in an age of longevity argues for planning around a longer, less linear life course.

The useful contribution is not a new magic number for death.

It is recognition that uncertainty belongs in the plan.

A plan built around one precise lifespan is fragile because precision is unavailable. A more robust plan asks what happens across a range. What if work ends at fifty-eight? What if it continues to seventy-two? What if one spouse lives twenty years after the other? What if the house must be changed? What if care is needed? What if no major health shock arrives and the person remains active much longer than expected?

Scenario planning sounds corporate until the scenarios are your own life.

The purpose is not to make every future safe. That is impossible. It is to avoid plans that succeed only in the narrow future we happened to imagine first.

The same principle applies to time itself.

A person with no financial margin may have no temporal margin. Every weekday is sold. Every transition threatens income. Every error must be corrected immediately. Optionality becomes a luxury because the person cannot afford the interval between paths.

That is why longevity advice can become cruel when it ignores wealth.

“Work longer” sounds reasonable in aggregate. It sounds different to a sixty-seven-year-old roofer than to a sixty-seven-year-old professor. “Delay Social Security” can improve lifetime benefits for many people who can afford to wait; it is not available in the same way to someone who needs income now. “Keep learning” is easier with tuition, time, broadband, childcare, and a job that rewards the learning. “Move somewhere more age-friendly” assumes a movable life.

The future is not equally purchasable.

This inequality is not only about how long people live. It is about how much freedom they have while planning for the possibility.

A wealthy household can overprepare and still enjoy the present. It can hold cash, buy insurance, own a home, fund retirement accounts, help children, hire care, take a sabbatical, and invest in health. A lower-income household may face a forced tradeoff between each pair. The advice can be identical while the feasible set is completely different.

A society serious about longer lives therefore has to care about the financial architecture beneath individual planning.

Portable benefits matter because careers may be less linear. Affordable education matters because retraining may recur. Housing supply matters because older households need options other than staying put or moving far away. Health coverage matters because a career transition should not require gambling with medical bankruptcy. Care infrastructure matters because longevity can increase the years in which families support older relatives. Retirement rules matter because longer work should be possible without becoming compulsory for people whose bodies cannot sustain it.

These are not traditionally grouped under longevity.

They should be.

The cultural version of longevity is obsessed with adding years at the end. The economic version has to ask what conditions make the middle adaptable enough to reach those years without exhaustion.

There is also a less obvious cost to expecting a longer life: commitment can be delayed.

If a person believes there is always more time, she can keep options open too long. Save the career decision for later. Save marriage for later. Save children for later. Save the difficult conversation for later. Save the creative work for later. Longevity can become an excuse for procrastination dressed as flexibility.

This is not merely individual weakness. Markets can profit from indefinite deferral. Subscription businesses, rentals, temporary work, revolving credit, and products designed for continuous upgrading all make it easier to avoid final choices. Some flexibility is valuable. Some is expensive uncertainty rented month by month.

The future can be overvalued just as easily as it can be discounted.

The right financial response to longevity is therefore not maximal preservation of capital or maximal preservation of options.

It is staged commitment.

Commit enough to let things compound. Preserve enough flexibility to revise when the facts change.

That balance is familiar in investing. A portfolio contains assets with different risk, return, liquidity, and time horizons because one asset cannot do every job. A long life may need the same kind of temporal diversification: periods for deep commitment, periods for exploration, assets that compound, reserves that remain liquid, relationships worth staying inside, and doors that do not need to be welded shut merely to prove seriousness.

The analogy should not be pushed too far. Children are not asset classes. Marriage is not a call option. A community is not a liquidity instrument. Human obligations acquire moral weight that markets do not capture.

But the central financial insight survives the translation.

Time changes the value of reversibility.

When the expected horizon lengthens, the present price of a bad lock-in rises. So does the present value of a good long-term investment.

That is the tension longevity places on money.

Save because the future may be long.

Spend because the present is not a rehearsal.

Invest in commitments that deserve decades.

Keep enough freedom to escape the ones that do not.

No calculator can solve that allocation.

But every calculator is already pricing the longer life today.
