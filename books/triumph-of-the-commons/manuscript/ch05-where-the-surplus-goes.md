# Where the Surplus Goes

Whenever a new technology creates abundance, society eventually fights over the dividend.

The steam engine created a productivity dividend.

Electricity created an energy dividend.

Computers created an information-processing dividend.

AI creates a cognitive dividend.

There are at least five ways to spend it.

A company uses AI to reduce cost or increase output and keeps the gain as profit.

This is the default market mechanism. It will finance enormous innovation. It is not sufficient as a social strategy.

Competition drives prices down or quality up. Consumers receive better products for less money.

This is also powerful. Cheap translation, design, tutoring, coding, entertainment, and planning can improve daily life dramatically.

But consumer benefits flow through markets, and markets serve purchasing power rather than need.

Productivity gains become shorter working hours, higher wages, better tools, or more meaningful jobs.

Historically, this dividend is contested rather than automatic.

A nurse whose paperwork is automated might receive more time with patients—or a larger patient load.

A teacher whose grading is automated might receive smaller classes—or more classes.

Technology does not decide.

Governments use AI to deliver better services at lower cost: faster permits, better fraud detection, more responsive tax administration, smarter infrastructure maintenance, improved emergency response.

This can enlarge effective state capacity without proportionally enlarging bureaucracy.

The risk is surveillance. A state that can help more efficiently can also monitor more efficiently. The same capacity requires stronger rights.

The most neglected dividend is capability that becomes freely reusable by everyone.

Open models.

Open scientific tools.

Public datasets.

Civic agents.

Open educational systems.

Shared safety evaluations.

Reusable medical translation infrastructure.

Open agricultural advisory systems.

Disaster-response agents.

Public-interest software.

The commons dividend is what prevents every generation from paying again for knowledge society already purchased.

It is the cognitive equivalent of a public park: value remains available without a transaction at the gate.

These dividends are not mutually exclusive.

A healthy AI economy likely needs all five.

Profit funds risk-taking.

Consumers benefit from competition.

Workers deserve a share of productivity.

States need improved capacity.

The commons ensures that some portion of machine intelligence becomes shared inheritance.

The danger is allowing the first dividend to swallow the other four.

A society obsessed only with firm-level productivity may become richer while leaving public systems cognitively starved.

This has happened before.

Private software became extraordinary while public software often remained painful.

Private logistics became astonishing while some public services still required paper forms.

Private personalization became sophisticated enough to predict what advertisement you might click while public education still struggled to personalize instruction.

The mismatch was not because public problems were less important.

It was because public value is harder to capture privately.

AI gives us a chance to correct the imbalance by explicitly budgeting for the commons dividend.

Governments could require a small percentage of publicly subsidized compute to support open civic applications.

Universities could treat production-ready public tools as scholarly contributions.

Corporations could donate models, compute, evaluations, or engineering time as part of social-impact commitments.

Foundations could fund maintenance rather than endless pilots.

Procurement systems could prefer interoperable solutions that return improvements to shared infrastructure.

The question would become routine:

**What portion of this new intelligence becomes common wealth?**

That single question could shape the century.

There is a quiet reason many technological gifts fail.

The receiving institution cannot absorb them.

A philanthropist donates computers to a school with unreliable electricity.

A startup gives a hospital an AI tool that cannot integrate with its records.

A government buys analytics software but never trains staff.

A research model is released openly but requires hardware unavailable to the communities it was meant to help.

A pilot succeeds because five brilliant people babysit it, then collapses when the grant ends.

The lesson is uncomfortable: capability is not impact.

Impact requires absorption.

If AI is to contribute to global well-being at scale, we need institutions capable of receiving machine intelligence without being destabilized by it.

This means redesigning organizations around five capacities.

AI systems can be wrong with confidence. Any institution using them in consequential settings needs mechanisms for checking claims, tracing sources, escalating uncertainty, and detecting systematic failure.

The goal is not perfect accuracy. Human institutions are not perfectly accurate either. The goal is bounded failure with visible pathways for correction.

No citizen should be trapped by an automated decision with no human path of appeal.

The more AI is used to interpret rules, allocate services, or flag risk, the more important procedural rights become.

Automation should make appeal cheaper, not disappear it.

An AI system can help assemble the evidence for its own challenge.

Organizations need logs of what models did, which versions were used, what decisions followed, what incidents occurred, and how policies changed.

Otherwise every failure becomes anecdote and every improvement evaporates after staff turnover.

When machines save time, institutions should decide explicitly where saved human attention goes.

If a social worker saves three hours of documentation each week, the organization should not allow those hours to vanish invisibly into higher caseloads. It should choose: deeper client contact, more prevention, more complex cases, more training, or shorter working hours.

The surplus needs a destination.

Communities should be able to adapt systems to local conditions without begging distant vendors for permission.

Language, law, culture, infrastructure, and risk vary.

A health agent designed for Boston cannot simply be dropped into Bamako.

A farming system trained on industrial agriculture may fail smallholders.

A legal assistant designed around one jurisdiction can become dangerous elsewhere.

The commons model therefore requires modularity: shared core capabilities with local governance over adaptation.

Technologists love launches.

Communities need maintenance.

A public-interest AI tool is not a gift if nobody funds updates, security patches, changing regulations, model evaluations, user support, translation review, and incident response.

The moral center of infrastructure is maintenance because maintenance is a promise across time.

To maintain something is to say: the people who depend on this tomorrow matter too.

This is where governments and philanthropy often underperform. Funding systems prefer novelty because novelty photographs well. Maintenance looks like overhead.

But the triumph of the commons will be built by maintainers.

The heroes will be the people who keep the medical ontology updated, who review edge cases in minority languages, who reconcile public records, who investigate model failures, who write boring interoperability standards, who keep old devices supported, who document every change.

Civilization is not what we invent.

Civilization is what still works on Tuesday.

Every productivity revolution creates a distribution problem after it creates a production opportunity.

The steam engine did not decide who owned the factory.

Electricity did not decide who received the gains from mechanization.

The internet did not decide whether lower communication costs would appear as consumer surplus, corporate profit, new labor markets, surveillance advertising, or public knowledge.

Technology changes what is possible. Institutions divide the result.

AI will be no different, except that the object being divided includes time and attention.

This makes the surplus unusually hard to see.

When a factory produces more physical goods, output appears in inventory and sales. When software saves a lawyer twenty minutes, the gain may vanish into the rest of the afternoon. It can become another client call, an earlier trip home, a higher margin, a lower bill, or simply twenty minutes of less frantic work. Accounting systems rarely label it "cognitive surplus."

They should.

An institution that cannot see the surplus cannot govern it deliberately.

Suppose a hospital introduces systems that reduce documentation time. The narrow business case estimates labor savings. The broader civic case asks a second question: what fraction of those saved hours becomes patient care, clinician recovery, lower cost, additional capacity, or profit?

There is no universal correct split. A hospital with terrible margins may need financial relief to remain open. A burned-out workforce may need time more than throughput. A waiting list may make extra capacity the priority.

The point is to make the choice explicit.

This is what I mean by a commons dividend.

It is not necessarily a tax. It is a habit of asking where productivity gains go and reserving some share for capabilities that benefit people beyond the immediate owner of the machine.

The dividend can be paid in money.

It can be paid in time.

It can be paid in access.

It can be paid in open knowledge.

It can be paid in lower prices.

It can be paid in stronger public infrastructure.

The form should match the surplus.

A software company that benefits from a public dataset might fund its maintenance.

A hospital that saves documentation time might expand free care navigation.

A university that uses AI to accelerate research might publish more reusable tools, protocols, or negative results.

A government that reduces administrative cost might improve human escalation instead of simply cutting staff.

A company that automates routine internal expertise might provide employees with some of the saved time for learning or reduced hours.

The most important dividend may be the worker dividend because it determines whether people experience AI as liberation or extraction.

If every minute saved by a tool becomes a new expectation, automation creates no felt abundance. The worker becomes an accessory to a faster machine.

This dynamic already exists without AI. Email accelerated communication and then filled the space it saved. Smartphones reduced coordination friction and made employees reachable everywhere. Office software eliminated clerical work and generated new clerical work in the form of reporting, slides, tracking, and self-service administration.

Efficiency has a way of eating its own dividend.

The intelligence age will require deliberate friction against that tendency.

A firm might declare that a portion of verified time savings cannot be converted into higher quotas during the first year of a deployment.

A hospital might track after-hours documentation and commit to reducing it.

A public agency might measure citizen wait time rather than cases per employee.

A school might evaluate whether teachers spend more time with students, not merely whether they produce more materials.

These are governance choices inside organizations, not grand national policies. They can change the emotional meaning of the technology.

The consumer dividend is more familiar. Competition can drive the cost of intelligence down. Services once sold by the hour may become bundled into subscriptions or included in other products. Basic tax help, design, translation, coding, and research assistance may become dramatically cheaper.

That is real welfare even when it never appears as wages.

But consumer surplus can coexist with concentrated power. Search engines were extraordinarily useful while concentrating advertising. Social networks gave away communication while accumulating behavioral data. Cheap intelligence can enrich daily life and still create dependency on a few providers.

The state dividend appears when governments use the same technology to improve public services or lower administrative cost. It may also appear as tax revenue from new industries.

The danger is that states receive the surveillance dividend instead.

A system that can detect fraud can often monitor innocent behavior. A system that can personalize service can often profile citizens. A system that can coordinate across agencies can often centralize power. The same integration that makes bureaucracy less frustrating can make the state much harder to escape.

The commons dividend therefore cannot be defined merely as "government gets more capability." Public capability has to remain bounded by rights.

The profit dividend will be enormous in some places and deserved in many of them. Building reliable systems costs capital and talent. Companies that create valuable tools should be able to earn returns. A theory of the commons that treats profit as contamination will fail because it misunderstands the productive role of private risk-taking.

The question is not whether profit exists.

It is whether all surplus is automatically assigned to ownership simply because ownership was first in line.

Modern corporations already make more complicated distributions. They pay wages, invest in research, lower prices, buy back shares, pay dividends, donate, comply with regulation, contribute to standards, sponsor open-source projects, and build infrastructure that others use. The mix is contested but plural.

AI increases the size of the mix.

That gives organizations room to create explicit contribution policies.

Consider a company that deploys an internal coding agent. It might promise that a percentage of maintenance improvements developed through the system will be contributed upstream to open-source dependencies when legally and commercially feasible. This is not charity in the old sense. It strengthens the software commons the company itself relies on.

A logistics firm might publish anonymized emergency routing tools developed during a disaster.

A manufacturer might contribute safety datasets or failure taxonomies.

A legal technology company might maintain public explainers for common procedures while selling premium services to professionals.

A cloud provider might offer a standing compute reserve to qualified disaster-response organizations.

The best contributions often align with the contributor's competence. They are not detached acts of guilt after profit. They are extensions of what the organization already knows how to do.

This is why the commons dividend should be designed near the source of the surplus.

Traditional philanthropy often moves money from a successful activity into an unrelated charitable activity. That can be valuable. But machine intelligence enables another route: turn a capability created inside the successful activity into a reusable public capability.

A model that improves supply-chain forecasting can generate tools for food banks.

A translation system built for global commerce can support public-interest language access.

A cybersecurity system built for large enterprises can offer a reduced, automated tier to small nonprofits.

A scientific model built for proprietary drug discovery can contribute selected methods or predictions to neglected-disease research.

The company still has to protect legitimate trade secrets, privacy, security, and competitive advantage. The contribution does not require opening everything. It asks what can travel without destroying the reason the organization exists.

This is the portfolio view of surplus.

Some goes to owners because owners funded the risk.

Some goes to workers because productivity without worker benefit becomes politically unstable and morally thin.

Some goes to customers through lower prices and better products.

Some goes to the state through taxes and public procurement.

Some goes to the commons because every firm operates inside a world it did not build alone.

That last statement is easy to make sentimentally. It is stronger when made literally.

Companies rely on public roads, standards, currencies, courts, educated workers, scientific knowledge, internet protocols, emergency services, stable institutions, and generations of accumulated research. AI companies in particular stand on enormous layers of publicly accessible language, code, science, and culture.

This does not settle every copyright or compensation dispute. It does make the fiction of complete self-creation hard to sustain.

The commons dividend is a way to acknowledge interdependence without pretending that every input is collectively owned.

It can also reduce a dangerous political pattern.

When a technology creates obvious private winners and diffuse public losses, backlash arrives after the system is entrenched. Regulation becomes a fight over taking something away. Everyone hardens.

If contribution is built in earlier, the politics can be different.

A community sees a new data center and also sees a local grid investment, a compute allocation for schools, workforce training, waste-heat reuse where practical, transparent water reporting, or a research partnership.

A worker sees an agent arrive and also sees a reduced administrative burden, protected learning time, and a share of productivity gains.

A citizen sees a government AI system and also receives source-grounded explanations, faster service, privacy protections, and guaranteed access to a human.

The technology is still disruptive. But the dividend is visible.

Visible reciprocity matters because trust is not generated by aggregate statistics. People judge systems through the distribution they can see.

A country can grow richer while a town feels abandoned.

A company can become more productive while an employee feels squeezed.

A hospital can improve average throughput while a patient feels less heard.

The commons dividend is an attempt to keep aggregate gain connected to lived gain.

That connection will not emerge from a universal formula. Different industries and institutions will require different bargains.

But one question should become routine in every AI deployment:

What are we doing with the surplus?

If the answer is unknown, that is not neutrality.

It means the default is deciding for us.

There is one more dividend that ordinary accounting misses: option value.

When intelligence becomes cheaper, an institution can afford to attempt work whose expected return was previously too small or uncertain.

A town can model three infrastructure alternatives instead of commissioning one.

A school can test several explanations for a difficult concept.

A researcher can explore a neglected hypothesis before committing laboratory resources.

A small organization can investigate a suspected problem rather than ignoring it because expert help is expensive.

The benefit is not only the final answer. It is the enlarged set of choices.

This matters most for people and institutions that historically operate with almost no slack. Wealth purchases optionality. A large company can hire consultants, run pilots, commission studies, and abandon approaches that fail. A poor municipality may have one chance to get a procurement right.

Cheap cognition can democratize experimentation.

That is a commons dividend because experimentation produces knowledge even when the first option is rejected.

A city that models a transit change and publishes the analysis gives the next city a head start.

A nonprofit that tests three outreach methods can contribute the failure modes.

A school that discovers a tutoring pattern does not have to keep the insight local.

The surplus becomes a larger search space for society.

That may be one of AI's most optimistic effects: not merely doing known things faster, but allowing more people to investigate what might be possible before committing scarce money, land, materials, or human lives.

Optionality is difficult to measure.

It is still real.

A civilization with more affordable second opinions is harder to trap in first ideas.

A commons dividend can also be negotiated at the product-design level.

Imagine a commercial tutor with millions of users.

The company may reasonably keep its model, interface, and premium curriculum proprietary. It could still publish anonymized error taxonomies showing which concepts students commonly misunderstand, contribute accessibility improvements to shared standards, or provide a free public tier through libraries.

A legal platform can sell advanced workflow tools while maintaining current plain-language explanations of basic public procedure.

A scientific company can protect proprietary candidates while contributing evaluation methods.

The point is to stop treating openness as binary.

Organizations can choose which layer to return.

This makes contribution more compatible with sustainable business because it preserves scarce complements while sharing non-rival improvements.

The best dividend is often the layer whose public availability enlarges the market rather than destroys it.

Better standards create more users.

Better security creates more trust.

Better public data creates more applications.

Better education creates more capable customers and workers.

The commons can be upstream economic development.

This is one reason the generosity-versus-growth debate is poorly framed.

Some contributions are not sacrifices against growth.

They are investments in the environment that makes future growth possible.
