# The Altruism Market

Markets are magnificent at answering one question:

What will someone pay for?

Civilization also depends on another question:

What should exist even when nobody can pay enough?

The gap between those questions contains much of the commons.

Clean air.

Basic science.

Pandemic surveillance.

Open standards.

Public-interest journalism.

Disease eradication.

Biodiversity records.

Safe streets.

Translation into languages with small commercial markets.

Maintenance of software used everywhere but purchased by almost nobody.

Traditional economics calls many of these public goods because it is difficult to exclude non-payers and because one person's benefit may not significantly reduce another's.

AI creates a new opportunity: we can make public-good production more legible, modular, and contractible.

We can build markets not for virtue, but for verified contribution.

Philanthropy and public procurement often pay for activity.

Run the program.

Hire the staff.

Publish the report.

Hold the workshop.

Open the center.

These may be necessary, but they are proxies for what we actually care about.

Did more children learn to read?

Did fewer people die from the disease?

Did the river become cleaner?

Did the software vulnerability disappear from deployed systems?

Did small farmers receive more accurate forecasts?

Did court backlogs fall without reducing due process?

AI can lower the cost of measuring some outcomes, comparing interventions, and detecting unintended effects. That makes new forms of public-good purchasing possible.

A city might post a challenge: reduce average permit confusion by half while preserving legal protections. Competing teams—companies, nonprofits, universities, civic hackers, agent collectives—could propose systems. Payment would depend partly on independently measured improvement.

A global health fund might reward verified reductions in diagnostic delay for a neglected disease rather than merely purchasing reports.

An open-source security fund might pay maintainers for verified removal of vulnerabilities from widely used dependencies.

The unit of exchange becomes contribution.

This idea can go wrong quickly.

If every helpful act receives a price, generosity can become performative optimization. People may stop doing unmeasured good. Communities may contort themselves around indicators. The best storytellers may outcompete the best maintainers. A new class of impact financiers may capture the very surplus the system was supposed to distribute.

We should not build a Bloomberg terminal for human suffering and assume morality has been solved.

Markets are tools for discovery and coordination. They are not complete moral systems.

So an altruism market requires boundaries.

Some goods should be funded as unconditional capabilities: libraries, baseline education, emergency response, due process.

Some outcomes are too difficult or dangerous to monetize directly.

Some communities should be able to reject outside optimization altogether.

Some work deserves stable salary rather than tournament-style reward.

Some forms of care lose something important when converted into a transaction.

The design goal is therefore a mixed economy of contribution.

Grants for exploration.

Prizes for breakthroughs.

Contracts for measurable services.

Public salaries for essential maintenance.

Commons dividends for reusable infrastructure.

Volunteerism for gifts that people want to remain gifts.

AI introduces another strange participant: the productive agent that can earn resources without personally needing most of them.

Imagine a network of software agents maintained by a cooperative. They perform paid commercial tasks—coding, analysis, design, translation, logistics planning. A fraction of their revenue automatically funds public-interest tasks chosen by the cooperative's members.

Ten thousand commercial jobs finance a million free translations of public-health documents.

A profitable scientific model allocates idle cycles to neglected-disease research.

An accounting agent network funds tax clinics for low-income workers.

A design system used by large firms maintains an accessibility toolkit for public websites.

The agent becomes not merely a worker but an endowment.

This is one of the genuinely new economic structures made possible by machine labor.

Human endowments traditionally begin with capital. A wealthy donor gives assets; investment returns finance a mission.

An agent endowment can begin with capability.

Give the system useful skills, permission to perform bounded commercial work, transparent rules for allocating surplus, and a governance structure that cannot easily be captured. Its contribution can continue as long as its services remain valuable.

The machine works so that someone else need not pay.

Software platforms contain primitives: login, payment, search, storage, messaging.

A commons civilization should add another:

**giveback**.

Every productive system should be able to route a chosen fraction of surplus—money, compute, generated knowledge, validated improvements, spare capacity, training material, or human time saved—toward a commons.

A company might choose one percent.

A public institution might require ten percent of publicly financed research tooling to be reusable.

An individual might route the first hour of weekly agent time toward a neighborhood project.

A university might dedicate unused nighttime compute to an open scientific queue.

A municipality might return anonymized process improvements to a civic commons.

The precise percentage matters less than the existence of the mechanism.

At present, giving is often administratively separate from producing. You earn, then later decide whether to donate.

The giveback primitive integrates contribution into production itself.

Altruism becomes an output channel.

When enough systems contain that channel, the commons acquires something it has rarely possessed: automatic growth.

Civilizations are shaped by defaults more than declarations.

Most people do not read the terms. They do not customize every setting. They do not reconsider every recurring payment, privacy permission, retirement allocation, organ-donation rule, software update, or institutional procedure.

They live inside choices that were made in advance.

This makes the default one of the most powerful political objects in an AI-mediated world.

If the default is enclosure, abundance concentrates.

If the default is contribution, abundance leaks outward.

Every useful AI system creates some form of surplus.

Time is saved.

Errors are reduced.

Knowledge is discovered.

Processes are simplified.

Labor is displaced or redirected.

Revenue increases.

Capacity sits idle between peak periods.

The critical design decision is what happens next.

Today the default in most organizations is straightforward: the surplus accrues upward. Saved labor becomes margin. Improved efficiency becomes profit. New knowledge becomes proprietary advantage. Spare capacity remains private. Institutional learning stays trapped in the institution.

This is not evil. It is simply the default logic of the firm.

But a society can choose other defaults in selected domains.

Publicly funded projects can default to open publication.

Government software can default to reusable modules when security permits.

Scientific workflows can default to machine-readable methods.

Agent systems can default to preserving provenance.

Productivity programs can default to sharing a fraction of verified gains with workers.

Public data systems can default to privacy-preserving interoperability rather than departmental silos.

Consumer agents can default to asking whether unused subscription value, loyalty points, or compute credits should be donated.

Defaults turn moral aspiration into routine behavior.

Most citizens will never read a hundred-page AI governance framework.

They will experience governance in thirty-second moments.

The tutor says, “I don't know.”

The benefits agent says, “You can appeal this decision.”

The medical assistant says, “This is urgent; speak to a clinician.”

The civic system says, “Here is why I recommended this option.”

The workplace agent says, “This action affects your colleague's access rights.”

The public procurement model says, “This bidder's score depends heavily on a disputed assumption.”

Constitutional principles become real only when they survive compression into interface behavior.

A commons civilization therefore needs a thirty-second constitution: a handful of default behaviors embedded in systems people actually touch.

Show provenance when it matters.

State uncertainty.

Preserve appeal.

Do not silently convert advice into authority.

Offer a low-bandwidth path.

Prefer reversible actions when consequences are unclear.

Protect the non-user affected by the user's request.

Return reusable improvements when rights permit.

These are not slogans for conference stages. They are product requirements.

A global commons does not require global uniformity.

One city may choose that its civic AI defaults to Spanish and English, another to Swahili and English, another to three Indigenous languages. One health system may require physician review for a class of decisions that another safely automates. One community may opt out of data sharing that another embraces.

Pluralism belongs in the default layer too.

The most dangerous phrase in technology is not “move fast and break things.”

It may be “one size fits all.”

A triumph of the commons should make local customization cheap without making interoperability impossible.

Standards can define what must be communicated while communities decide how it is expressed.

Protocols can define the handshake while cultures decide the conversation.

This is how the internet became globally interoperable without every website becoming identical.

The same principle can govern public intelligence.

There will be enormous pressure to set defaults for extraction.

Default data retention.

Default proprietary formats.

Default upselling.

Default behavioral manipulation.

Default dependence on one provider.

Default replacement of workers without sharing gains.

Default silence about uncertainty.

Default optimization for the paying user even when others bear the cost.

The defense is not merely regulation after harm.

It is building better defaults early enough that people experience them as normal.

Imagine a generation that grows up assuming every public decision can be explained, every automated denial can be appealed, every school child can ask for tutoring, every government form can be translated, every publicly funded discovery leaves a reusable trace, and every major AI deployment reports where its productivity dividend went.

That generation would find many current institutions barbarically opaque.

Progress often works this way.

Yesterday's luxury becomes today's expectation, then tomorrow's right.

The battle for the commons is therefore partly a battle over what people come to regard as ordinary.

We should make generosity boring.

We should make contribution automatic enough that nobody writes a press release about it.

We should make the preservation of shared capability feel as unremarkable as backing up a database.

The triumph will be secure when it stops feeling heroic.

The phrase "altruism market" sounds like a contradiction because we use altruism to name acts that are not performed for price.

The contradiction disappears if the market is understood as a matching system.

There are needs.

There are capabilities.

There are funders.

There are contributors.

The difficult problem is often connecting them with enough information to know whether useful work occurred.

Philanthropy already contains markets of a kind. Foundations solicit proposals. Charities compete for donations. Governments contract for social services. Donors compare ratings. Impact investors seek measurable outcomes.

AI can make these systems more granular and more dangerous.

More granular because needs can be described, matched, and monitored at lower cost.

More dangerous because whatever is measured can be gamed at machine speed.

Imagine a global fund that pays for verified translations of essential health material into low-resource languages.

The need can be decomposed.

Which materials?

Which languages?

Which regions?

Which documents are current?

Which require specialist review?

Machine systems can draft translations.

Human reviewers can validate samples or high-risk passages.

Usage and error reports can feed back into the resource.

Payment can be tied to maintained, reusable coverage rather than sheer word count.

This is a market for a public good.

The same pattern could fund accessibility remediation, open-source maintenance, scientific replication, cybersecurity improvements for nonprofits, public-data cleaning, or legal-information updates.

The unit of purchase is not "altruism."

It is a verified contribution.

That phrase needs defense too.

Verification can easily become a bureaucracy worse than the problem.

If a volunteer has to fill out six forms to prove a thirty-minute contribution, the system destroys the generosity it was meant to amplify.

Machine intelligence can reduce verification cost.

It can compare the submitted work with the source.

Run tests.

Check whether a software patch was accepted.

Confirm that a dataset changed.

Sample translation quality.

Detect duplicate claims.

Record provenance.

The contribution can be verified in the workflow rather than through a report written afterward.

This creates the possibility of outcome contracts that were previously too expensive to administer.

A municipality could pay for potholes correctly mapped and repaired rather than hours billed.

A health fund could pay for verified appointment completion among hard-to-reach patients rather than messages sent.

A research program could pay for successful reproduction packages rather than pages of documentation.

The danger is obvious: outcomes are never as simple as the metric.

A service provider can avoid difficult patients to improve completion rates.

A translator can optimize benchmark text.

A maintenance contractor can focus on visible bugs.

A tutor can teach to the test.

An altruism market can become a machine for selecting easy beneficiaries.

This is why public-good markets need obligation alongside incentive.

Serve the hard cases.

Report uncertainty.

Accept random audit.

Preserve appeal.

Publish negative outcomes.

Prevent cherry-picking where equity is part of the purpose.

The market should not pretend that price can encode every value.

This is one reason I prefer plural mechanisms.

Some public goods are suitable for contracts.

Some for grants.

Some for prizes.

Some for vouchers.

Some for direct public provision.

Some for volunteer communities.

Some for professional institutions with stable budgets.

AI can improve all of them without turning all of them into markets.

The contribution ledger belongs in this chapter because ledgers are seductive.

Imagine a system that records every useful thing a person or agent contributes to the commons.

Open-source fixes.

Mentoring.

Scientific datasets.

Civic translation.

Disaster volunteering.

Public explanations.

The ledger could make invisible work visible. It could help funders find reliable contributors. It could create reputation that travels across institutions.

It could also become monstrous.

A universal moral score would punish private forms of care that are hard to measure.

It would advantage people whose work produces digital traces.

It would invite performative generosity.

It could become a basis for employment, credit, or political status.

It could turn altruism into a compulsory public performance.

The contribution ledger should therefore be narrow, voluntary where possible, domain-specific, and attached to artifacts rather than souls.

A software project can record that a contributor fixed a vulnerability.

A translation commons can record reviewed work.

A scientific repository can record dataset stewardship.

These reputations need not merge into one number.

The contributor should be able to have no global altruism score at all.

This is a larger principle for identity in the intelligence age: do not aggregate merely because aggregation is technically possible.

Local reputation is often healthier than universal reputation.

The same caution applies to agent cooperatives.

As agents become able to perform economic work, groups of people may pool agents for shared purposes.

A neighborhood can operate a planning assistant.

A union can maintain a contract-analysis agent.

A professional association can share a research system.

A group of small businesses can run a procurement agent.

A patient community can maintain a literature-monitoring service.

The cooperative model is attractive because the users govern the capability they depend on.

But ownership alone does not guarantee good governance.

A cooperative can exclude outsiders.

A local group can adopt discriminatory rules.

A majority can misuse minority data.

The commons needs rights that survive local democracy.

Still, agent cooperatives offer an important alternative to two extremes: every person buying isolated commercial subscriptions, or one central government providing intelligence to everyone.

Plural ownership can make capability more contestable.

The giveback primitive is a simpler idea.

Whenever a system solves a problem, ask whether some part of the solution can safely improve the shared layer.

An agent fixes an obscure software incompatibility.

Can the patch go upstream?

A public servant explains a confusing rule.

Can the explanation improve the official knowledge base?

A researcher cleans a dataset.

Can the cleaning script be published?

A teacher creates a useful adaptation.

Can it enter a curriculum commons?

A company detects a new fraud pattern.

Can a privacy-preserving indicator help others defend themselves?

This can be built into tools as a prompt, not a mandate.

"You appear to have created something reusable. Would you like to contribute a generalized version?"

The machine can prepare the version.

The human or institution decides whether it has the right to give.

That small interaction could generate a surprising amount of public value because useful artifacts are often lost through inattention, not secrecy.

The altruism market is ultimately less about buying virtue than lowering transaction cost around contribution.

Find the need.

Find the capable contributor.

Make the work easy to verify.

Pay when payment helps.

Preserve volunteer space.

Return reusable value.

Avoid collapsing human worth into a score.

Markets are good at some things.

Commons are good at others.

The interesting future is the architecture that knows the difference.

Prizes offer a particularly useful mechanism when the desired outcome is clear but the method is not.

A government can say: produce a validated tool that makes this public dataset accessible to screen-reader users.

A foundation can say: create high-quality translation coverage for these emergency phrases in these languages.

A scientific funder can say: reproduce this class of results with openly documented workflows.

Multiple teams can try different approaches.

The funder buys success rather than specifying the contractor's method.

AI lowers the cost of participation because small teams can access capabilities once available only to large organizations.

That can widen the pool of solvers.

But prizes should not be romanticized.

They shift risk to participants.

They can privilege teams that can afford to work before payment.

They can underfund maintenance after the award.

They can produce prototypes that are impressive and institutionally orphaned.

A commons prize should therefore include an adoption path.

Who maintains the winning artifact?

Under what rights?

Who hosts it?

How are users supported?

What happens when the underlying model changes?

Does the winner receive ongoing stewardship funding?

The point is not to discover a clever object.

It is to create a durable capability.

This is the difference between innovation theater and public infrastructure.

Vouchers create another path when the public objective is access but users need choice.

Instead of building one national tutoring system, a government can give eligible students access credits usable across approved services.

Instead of choosing one legal-navigation provider, a public program can fund the user and require providers to meet interoperability, privacy, and quality standards.

The user gains some market power.

The public buyer still shapes the floor.

This only works when comparison is realistic. If services are too complex to evaluate, choice can become burden.

AI can help users compare, but then the comparison agent itself needs independence from the providers being ranked.

The market design has to include the meta-layer.

Who helps the buyer choose?

Who verifies claims?

Who resolves failure?

A voucher without trustworthy navigation can simply move procurement complexity from government to the person least equipped to manage it.

Choice is a capability when support makes it usable.
