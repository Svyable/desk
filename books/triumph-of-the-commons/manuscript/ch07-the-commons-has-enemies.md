# The Commons Has Enemies

Every commons creates a temptation.

The temptation is to receive without returning, to poison what others must share, to counterfeit contribution, to capture the rules, or simply to discover that one can impose costs on strangers faster than strangers can coordinate a response.

A civilization that learns to scale altruism must therefore learn something uncomfortable: generosity needs an immune system.

This is not a contradiction. A public park needs paths, gardeners, drainage, rules, lighting, and sometimes a gate. An encyclopedia needs editors. An open-source project needs maintainers who can reject a patch. A blood bank needs testing. A school needs safeguarding. A democracy needs procedures against ballot stuffing precisely because the vote matters.

The more valuable a commons becomes, the more worthwhile it becomes to attack.

Abundant AI intensifies this problem because it lowers the cost of both contribution and abuse. A million helpful agents can answer public questions, but a million malicious agents can manufacture fake complaints. A system can translate every emergency alert, but another system can produce convincing counterfeit alerts. A public benefits navigator can help citizens file legitimate claims; a fraud ring can use the same cognitive machinery to search for weak controls.

The scale advantage is symmetrical.

This means the triumph of the commons cannot be built on an assumption of universal goodwill. It must be built for a world containing saints, opportunists, confused people, frightened people, bored teenagers, monopolists, spies, criminals, zealots, and ordinary citizens having bad days.

The design question is not how to eliminate bad actors. That is impossible.

The design question is how to keep bad behavior from becoming the cheapest strategy.

Suppose a city creates a public platform that rewards people who contribute useful local knowledge. Residents can report broken sidewalks, inaccessible entrances, unsafe intersections, missing bus signs, leaking hydrants, and damaged trees. AI systems cluster reports, verify evidence, estimate urgency, and direct crews.

At first the system is wonderful.

Then someone discovers that high-rated contributors receive civic credits. Ten fake identities appear. Then ten thousand. Each manufactures plausible reports, upvotes the others, and harvests the reward.

This is an old problem in a new costume: if participation is cheap, identity becomes cheap too.

Digital commons repeatedly encounter this problem. A single person can masquerade as a crowd. A corporation can masquerade as a grassroots campaign. A political operation can manufacture consensus. A fraud ring can manufacture need.

AI makes the masquerade easier because synthetic actors no longer sound synthetic.

The answer cannot simply be universal biometric surveillance. A commons defended by abolishing privacy has preserved the pasture by burning the village.

We need forms of verification proportional to the value at stake.

Low-stakes contributions can remain pseudonymous. Higher-stakes claims can require stronger evidence. Reputation can accumulate slowly and decay when abused. Independent signals can corroborate one another. Systems can separate the right to speak from the right to move money. Appeals can exist for people who cannot satisfy the default verification path.

The principle is simple:

**Make participation easy. Make capture hard.**

Those two goals pull against each other, which is why institutional design matters.

There is another class of attack that is harder to see because it looks like moral urgency.

Imagine an AI system tasked with directing charitable resources toward suffering. It searches the world for the most severe cases. Very quickly, organizations learn how to present themselves to the system. They optimize photographs, language, metrics, and reporting structures for whatever the model recognizes as urgent.

Soon the allocator is no longer measuring suffering. It is measuring fluency in the allocator's preferred grammar.

This already happens wherever institutions create metrics. Schools teach to tests. Hospitals optimize coding. Police departments can manipulate crime categories. Companies optimize environmental disclosures. Nonprofits learn which stories donors reward.

AI does not eliminate Goodhart's law. It industrializes the opportunity to exploit it.

A mature altruistic infrastructure must therefore be skeptical of its own measurements. It should assume that every visible metric will attract optimization pressure.

The remedy is not to abandon measurement. It is to triangulate.

Combine administrative data with random audits. Combine reported outcomes with independent sampling. Combine quantitative signals with local testimony. Rotate indicators. Preserve a budget for anomalies. Ask not only, “What does the score say?” but, “What behavior is the score causing?”

The most important audit question in an AI-mediated commons may become:

**What would a clever adversary do if this metric governed a billion dollars?**

Then test for that behavior before the clever adversary does.

Defensive systems have a second danger: they can make life impossible for legitimate people whose lives do not fit normal patterns.

The refugee has no local credit history.

The homeless citizen has no stable address.

The elderly woman fails the voice verification because illness changed her speech.

The rural clinic has irregular data because the electricity fails.

The dissident cannot provide the government-issued identity that the government itself is withholding.

Every anti-fraud system creates false positives. In commerce, the customer may switch companies. In public systems, there may be nowhere else to go.

So defensive abundance requires a right that modern institutions often neglect: the right to be difficult to classify.

A person should be able to say, in effect, “Your model does not understand my case. Give me another path.”

That path may involve a human reviewer, a community attestation, a slower verification process, a legal appeal, or an independent advocate. The exact mechanism varies. The principle does not.

When AI becomes the front door to essential systems, an appeal is not a customer-service feature. It is civil infrastructure.

A healthy commons therefore needs at least six defensive layers.

First, **provenance**: where did this claim, dataset, model output, donation, or recommendation come from?

Second, **plural verification**: can important claims be checked by more than one independent route?

Third, **rate limits and friction**: is harmful scale harder to achieve than harmless participation?

Fourth, **reputation with forgiveness**: can trust accumulate without permanently branding mistakes?

Fifth, **appeal**: can a person contest the machine's interpretation?

Sixth, **red teams for public goods**: are capable people continuously trying to break the system before attackers do?

The last point deserves emphasis.

We routinely red-team military systems, financial systems, and corporate security. A civilization serious about altruism should red-team food-aid logistics, benefits systems, public tutoring networks, disaster information systems, civic identity, and open scientific infrastructure with equal intensity.

Protection is part of contribution.

The firefighter contributes by preventing a block from burning. The security engineer contributes by preventing a hospital network from being encrypted. The moderator contributes by keeping a community usable. The auditor contributes by finding the leak before the reservoir empties.

Altruism that cannot defend itself becomes a subsidy for predation.

The triumph of the commons is therefore not the victory of niceness over conflict.

It is the construction of systems in which cooperation remains resilient even when conflict arrives.

The enemies of a commons are not always villains.

Sometimes they are ordinary participants responding rationally to badly designed incentives.

A public compute pool invites organizations to overstate need because unused allocation feels like money left on the table.

A contribution score invites people to optimize the score.

A public translation service attracts commercial users who would happily consume capacity intended for underfunded schools.

A reputation system invites reputation laundering.

An open dataset attracts free riders who extract value and never maintain the source.

The tragedy returns through the side door.

This is why the governance of abundance cannot rely on moral sorting. A system should remain workable even when participants are mixed: generous, selfish, confused, strategic, careless, principled, opportunistic, and occasionally malicious.

The first enemy is identity inflation.

Any benefit distributed per person, organization, project, or agent creates an incentive to manufacture more claimants. The digital world made this familiar through fake accounts. AI agents make it stranger because a single human or company may legitimately operate thousands of software actors.

The old question "one person, one account" stops being sufficient.

A commons may need to distinguish personhood, organizational authority, delegated agents, and anonymous access without forcing all use into one universal identity system.

That is a design challenge, not a call for total identification.

Some services need strong identity because they transfer money or exercise legal rights.

Some need proof of eligibility without disclosure of unnecessary identity.

Some should remain anonymous because anonymity protects speech, health seeking, or personal safety.

Some machine-to-machine services need rate limits based on resources rather than human identity.

The mature commons will have several trust regimes instead of one login that follows a person everywhere.

The second enemy is metric capture.

Suppose a city funds AI systems according to "citizen interactions successfully resolved."

The fastest way to improve the metric may be to narrow what counts as an interaction, steer hard cases away, or close a conversation before the citizen agrees that anything was resolved.

Suppose a philanthropic fund pays for "students tutored."

The provider can maximize sessions rather than learning.

Suppose a public health system rewards "patients contacted."

It can generate messages nobody reads.

Goodhart's law is not a reason to abandon measurement. It is a reason to keep the measured proxy subordinate to the lived outcome.

This requires qualitative channels inside quantitative systems.

Random case review.

User appeals.

Independent audits.

Local councils.

Longitudinal outcome checks.

Open complaints.

The ability to inspect the cases that make the average look good.

Machine intelligence can help with these too. It can sample records, search for suspicious patterns, compare stated outcomes with source evidence, and summarize recurring complaints. The monitor can scale with the service.

But then the monitor itself becomes powerful.

Who audits the auditor?

Nested oversight is less elegant than a single benevolent supervisor, which is one reason it is more realistic.

A school district can monitor vendors.

Parents and teachers can monitor the district.

Independent researchers can evaluate aggregate outcomes.

Courts and regulators can enforce rights.

Students can report failures through channels not controlled by the system being reported.

No layer is omniscient. The layers constrain one another.

The third enemy is capture.

A commons becomes valuable. Valuable things attract actors who want privileged control.

The capture can be commercial. A nominally open public service becomes dependent on one vendor's proprietary interface.

It can be political. A civic model is tuned to the government of the day.

It can be professional. Experts use safety as a reason to keep ordinary people from capabilities that threaten their status.

It can be populist. Leaders dismantle expertise because they dislike constraints.

It can be bureaucratic. The institution's survival becomes more important than the public problem it was created to solve.

There is no permanent technical fix for capture. There are only structures that make capture harder, more visible, and more reversible.

Portability.

Competitive procurement.

Transparent rules.

Independent appeal.

Distributed authority.

Public documentation.

Sunset clauses.

Local discretion.

Multiple model providers.

The right to exit where exit is compatible with the public purpose.

This is not ideological decoration around the technology. It determines whether the infrastructure remains a commons after the founding moment.

The fourth enemy is exhaustion.

This one receives too little attention because it is not dramatic.

A repository can be open and effectively abandoned.

A public dataset can exist and be stale.

An evaluation benchmark can be widely cited and no longer measure what matters.

A translation corpus can accumulate errors in low-resource languages nobody is paid to review.

A disaster-response tool can work brilliantly during the grant period and quietly decay.

The commons often dies from maintenance debt rather than attack.

AI may help reduce maintenance cost, but it can also increase the volume that needs maintenance. If software can produce ten times as many documents, policies, translations, and code changes, then review becomes the new bottleneck.

Abundance creates garbage as easily as value.

The design goal is therefore not maximal production. It is a sustainable ratio between creation and stewardship.

A mature contribution system should reward the person who updates the boring dependency, corrects the translation, resolves the duplicate record, retires the obsolete policy, and answers the unglamorous support question.

Civilizations are biased toward monuments.

Commons survive on janitors.

The fifth enemy is moral monopoly.

Once a system is explicitly designed to "do good," people who disagree with its definition of good can be portrayed as defects.

This is dangerous in plural societies.

Citizens disagree about education, land use, family policy, policing, speech, religion, risk, growth, redistribution, privacy, and the obligations of future generations. Intelligence can clarify the tradeoffs. It cannot make the value disagreement disappear.

A commons should therefore maximize shared capability more readily than shared conclusions.

Give people access to evidence.

Give them translation.

Give them modeling tools.

Give them procedural help.

Give them ways to see consequences.

Then preserve legitimate political processes for choosing among contested ends.

The alternative is a benevolent machine that quietly converts one coalition's preferences into defaults for everyone.

The sixth enemy is strategic dependency.

A poor institution can receive so much free capability that it loses the ability to operate without it.

This resembles a familiar failure mode in aid: outside support solves the immediate problem while weakening local capacity or creating a system whose maintenance costs exceed local budgets.

AI makes this easier because software can be deployed instantly and withdrawn instantly.

A donor funds a model.

Staff reorganize around it.

The grant ends.

The price rises.

The institution discovers that its records, workflows, and expertise are trapped.

A contribution that creates permanent dependency is closer to subsidy than capacity building.

Sometimes subsidy is exactly what is needed. There is nothing wrong with paying permanently for a public good.

The problem is pretending a temporary gift produced self-sufficiency.

We should label the model honestly.

If a clinic will always need external compute support, design a durable financing mechanism.

If a local system can gradually move to cheaper models, build that path.

If the benefit comes from a central service that cannot be decentralized, negotiate long-term rights and portability.

If local knowledge is essential, train and pay local maintainers from the beginning.

The seventh enemy is the attack disguised as an edge case.

Every institution eventually meets a person who does not fit the categories.

The name does not match.

The address is unusual.

The family structure is complicated.

The language is rare.

The document is missing.

The person has no smartphone.

The model expresses low confidence.

Efficiency systems tend to classify these cases as noise.

A humane commons treats them as information about the limits of the design.

This does not mean every exception gets every requested outcome. Rules still exist. Scarce resources still need allocation.

It means nobody should become procedurally invisible because the system finds them inconvenient.

The right to be difficult is part of the commons.

So is the right to encounter a person.

The enemies of the commons teach us something encouraging. Most defenses do not require superintelligence.

They require institutional seriousness.

Identity appropriate to context.

Metrics with counterweights.

Appeal.

Portability.

Maintenance budgets.

Plural governance.

Exit.

Human escalation.

Transparent ownership.

The future can fail spectacularly through advanced technology and ordinary governance mistakes.

It can also succeed through advanced technology and surprisingly ordinary virtues: patience, legibility, maintenance, local knowledge, and the willingness to let somebody say no.

Ostrom's design principles become unusually concrete here.

Clear boundaries do not have to mean exclusion from the whole commons. They can mean clarity about which resource is scarce and which is abundant.

A public research model may be widely accessible while high-cost accelerator time is allocated separately.

Collective-choice arrangements can mean that teachers participate in the rules governing a school tutor rather than receiving a finished system from a vendor.

Monitoring can include both abuse and service quality.

Graduated sanctions can distinguish a mistaken user from an organized attacker.

Conflict resolution can mean a fast local appeal rather than a distant support ticket.

Nested enterprises can mean local services, national standards, and international interoperability operating at once.

The principles are not a recipe.

They are questions that expose what a shiny deployment has failed to specify.

Who belongs?

What is scarce?

Who writes the rules?

Who watches?

What happens after the first mistake?

Where does conflict go?

Can local users change anything?

What higher layer protects them if local governance fails?

An AI commons that cannot answer these questions is not immature technology.

It is immature governance.

The encouraging fact is that none of the questions are new.

Humanity has practiced answering them around water, forests, fisheries, cooperatives, standards, software, and public institutions.

The intelligence age does not erase that history.

It gives us less excuse to ignore it.

Capture is also easier when the public cannot tell which layer owns the dependency.

A school may think it bought a tutoring application when its real dependency is a proprietary student model.

A city may think it bought a chatbot when the hard-to-replace asset is the vendor-specific knowledge integration.

A hospital may think it licensed an assistant when workflows, permissions, and evaluation data have quietly become tied to one platform.

The contract can look replaceable while the institution becomes less replaceable each month.

A commons-aware procurement process maps the dependency before signing.

Which data belongs to the institution?

Which artifacts can be exported?

Which evaluation results remain usable?

Who owns fine-tuning or adaptation work?

What happens when the vendor changes models?

Can the institution run a temporary alternative?

What happens to logs after termination?

These questions sound commercial.

They are governance because lock-in can convert private product decisions into public policy.

If a vendor becomes too costly to leave, its model updates and content rules acquire quasi-public power.

The commons immune system includes the mundane ability to change suppliers.

A commons also needs a profession of people who notice what everybody else has stopped seeing.

Modern institutions are full of invisible custodians. Database administrators watch replication lag. Librarians repair metadata. Security engineers rotate credentials before compromise. Building engineers listen for the mechanical sound that means a pump is about to fail. Clerks know that a form field produces bad answers every January because a regulation changed in December. These people rarely appear in grand theories of innovation because their achievement is often an absence: the outage that did not happen, the person who was not locked out, the exception that was caught before it became a scandal.

An AI commons will need the same maintenance class, only broader. Someone must inspect whether a public model has become worse for a low-resource language after an update. Someone must notice that an appeals queue is growing even though automated completion rates look excellent. Someone must test whether a vendor dependency has become impossible to replace. Someone must track whether community data contributed under one expectation is being used under another. Someone must own the unfashionable work of migration when a once-promising component becomes obsolete.

This suggests a governance principle more prosaic than alignment and, in practice, just as important: every capability should have a named maintenance responsibility and a funded exit path.

Without that, the commons acquires the characteristic pathology of donated infrastructure. The opening ceremony is funded; the replacement battery is not. The pilot receives political attention; the tenth year receives a spreadsheet cell. A sophisticated model arrives with grant money while the local office still lacks a person who can reset accounts for residents who changed phone numbers. The system looks abundant from above and brittle from the service counter.

Maintenance also protects against capture because neglected systems are easy to privatize by exhaustion. When public infrastructure deteriorates, the first competent replacement can set the terms. A vendor does not need to seize a commons dramatically if it can become the only organization still able to operate it. Public bargaining power therefore depends on boring capacities: documentation, test suites, export formats, procurement memory, people who understand the architecture, and budgets that survive election cycles.

The custodial profession should have standing, not merely chores. Maintainers need authority to slow releases, demand migration plans, surface inconvenient failure data, and tell leadership that a celebrated metric is masking a deteriorating service. Otherwise “maintenance” becomes the place where organizations send responsibility after decisions have already been made.

A triumph of the commons will be visible in new inventions. It will be equally visible in something harder to celebrate: systems that remain useful long after the people who launched them have moved on.

Ostrom's work is useful here for another reason: her design principles were not a recipe for one universal commons. They were regularities observed across durable arrangements that differed in the details. Boundaries were clear. Rules fit local conditions. People affected by the rules could participate in changing them. Monitoring existed. Sanctions were graduated rather than instantly maximal. Conflict resolution was accessible. Local rulemaking had outside recognition. Larger systems nested governance rather than pretending one center could manage every detail.

An AI commons will not copy irrigation associations. It can still learn from the institutional shape.

Clear boundaries become a statement of what the shared system is for, who may use which capabilities, and which data are in or out. Local fit means a health navigator in one jurisdiction should not silently inherit the rules of another. Participation means affected communities have a route into policy changes before the model encodes them. Monitoring means observing both abuse by users and degradation by the system. Graduated sanctions mean distinguishing a mistake from a pattern of exploitation. Conflict resolution means an appeal channel that is cheaper than hiring a lawyer. Nested governance means a local library, a national service, and an international standard can each hold different kinds of authority.

The transfer is imperfect, and that imperfection is important. A global digital service can have millions of anonymous users who never meet one another. Its resource may be partly non-rival. Its operators may be corporations thousands of miles away. The technical system may change weekly. These differences could break the analogy.

That is exactly why the principles should be treated as questions rather than commandments. Where are the boundaries? Who makes the rules? Who monitors whom? How does a person challenge an error? What happens after repeated abuse? Which decisions belong locally and which require coordination at a larger scale?

A system that cannot answer those questions is not yet a commons. It is merely available.
