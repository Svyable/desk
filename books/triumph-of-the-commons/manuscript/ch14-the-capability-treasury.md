# The Capability Treasury

Nations maintain treasuries because money is a general-purpose resource.

A treasury does not know in advance whether the next crisis will require bridges, vaccines, firefighters, food purchases, debt service, or emergency housing. It holds a medium that can be converted into many kinds of action.

In an AI-rich civilization, useful intelligence becomes a second general-purpose resource.

This creates a new institutional possibility: the **capability treasury**.

A capability treasury is not a vault full of models. It is a public reserve of problem-solving capacity: compute, verified tools, public datasets, translation systems, simulation environments, educational agents, scientific infrastructure, cybersecurity assistance, accessibility services, and the human expertise required to govern them responsibly.

The purpose is simple.

When a community encounters a problem, it should not need to become a technology company before it can obtain serious cognitive help.

Today, that barrier is everywhere.

A small municipality may need to replace lead service lines but lack engineers who can reconcile old maps, resident reports, property records, and streetwork schedules. A rural hospital may need to redesign staffing but lack operations analysts. A school district may need to understand absenteeism but have data trapped across incompatible systems. A conservation group may have years of wildlife observations and no ability to model them. A legal-aid office may be drowning in documents while clients wait months.

Each problem is soluble in principle.

The limiting factor is access to organized capability.

We already accept the idea that some infrastructure should be available before any one user knows exactly how it will be used. Roads are built before every journey is planned. Libraries buy books before every question is asked. Fire departments exist before the fire. Statistical agencies collect data before the policy debate. Weather services operate continuously because society benefits from a shared picture of the atmosphere.

A capability treasury extends that logic to intelligence.

Its basic unit is not cash but *the ability to get something difficult done*.

Imagine a county with twenty thousand residents receiving access to a public stack that can:

- translate official material into the languages residents actually use;
- audit forms for readability and accessibility;
- identify duplicated administrative work;
- model traffic and land-use changes;
- help teachers create individualized practice material;
- search for grants without paying a consultant a percentage of the award;
- examine procurement contracts for suspicious clauses;
- help local businesses understand regulations;
- assist emergency managers in planning scenarios;
- give residents clear explanations of public decisions;
- preserve institutional memory when staff change.

None of these functions is glamorous.

That is exactly why they matter.

Civilization is held together by unglamorous cognition.

The pothole database.

The vaccination reminder.

The translation of a zoning notice.

The reconciliation of two spreadsheets that describe the same bridge differently.

The detection of a duplicate benefit application before a family is accused of fraud.

The warning that a procurement deadline falls on a holiday nobody noticed.

Markets often underprovide this work because its value is diffuse. A company can sell a luxury concierge to one affluent customer more easily than it can capture the social value of making a city permit legible to everyone.

That is the economic reason a capability treasury matters.

The most socially valuable uses of intelligence are not always the most privately monetizable.

If all high-quality AI capacity is allocated by willingness to pay, then intelligence abundance may simply deepen old gradients. The organizations that already have lawyers, engineers, analysts, and consultants will obtain even more leverage. The institutions with the greatest unmet need will remain too administratively weak to purchase the tools that could strengthen them.

Abundance can coexist with exclusion.

There may be enough intelligence for everyone and still no institution responsible for making sure everyone can reach it.

A capability treasury solves the access problem by treating some baseline of machine intelligence as civic infrastructure.

The analogy is the public library, not the command economy.

Public libraries did not abolish bookstores. They created a floor beneath information access. Public schools did not abolish private tutoring. Public weather data did not abolish commercial forecasting. The existence of a public option often expands the private ecosystem because businesses can build on shared foundations.

The same could happen with intelligence.

A public capability layer can handle the boring, universal, rights-protecting functions while private systems compete in specialized services.

The architecture could be plural rather than monolithic.

Cities might operate local models for sensitive administrative tasks.

Universities might host scientific agents.

Libraries might provide citizen-facing interfaces.

National institutions might negotiate compute capacity and maintain security standards.

International organizations might support low-income countries with multilingual public models and scientific infrastructure.

Open-source communities might maintain critical components.

Private companies might donate capacity, sell premium extensions, or receive contracts to maintain systems under public rules.

The treasury is therefore not one machine. It is a financing and governance concept.

Its core principle is that a society should deliberately reserve some portion of its expanding cognitive capacity for purposes whose benefits cannot be fully captured by a buyer.

How large should that portion be?

There is no universal number.

But we can borrow a principle from ecology: harvest without destroying regeneration.

When an AI system creates large productivity gains, some part of that surplus can be reinvested into the conditions that made the gain possible: education, open knowledge, public data, scientific research, safety infrastructure, energy systems, legal clarity, and the communities whose information trained or improved the system.

This is not punishment for success.

It is maintenance of the substrate.

A company that relies on roads pays taxes that help maintain roads. A bank relies on courts, currency, identity systems, and public stability. A technology company relies on decades of publicly funded research, global communications standards, educated workers, and legal institutions.

Intelligence is never produced in a vacuum.

The capability treasury recognizes that productive systems stand on inherited commons.

The question is how to make the return flow visible and useful.

One mechanism is a **public capability dividend**.

Instead of treating every benefit of AI as higher profits or lower prices, governments and institutions could negotiate for in-kind contributions: compute hours for public-interest research, model access for schools, security tooling for hospitals, translation capacity for courts, scientific inference for neglected diseases, accessibility features for disabled users, or technical assistance for municipalities.

A second mechanism is the **public twin requirement**.

When public money funds an AI-enabled system, a reusable version of the non-sensitive tools, documentation, schemas, prompts, evaluation methods, or lessons should return to a common repository whenever legally and technically possible.

A city should not pay five vendors in ten years to rediscover the same permit workflow.

A hospital network should not solve a scheduling problem with public money and then lose every lesson when the contract ends.

A ministry should not fund a crop advisory system whose underlying knowledge disappears when a donor leaves.

Public spending should purchase not only a service, but institutional memory.

A third mechanism is **capability matching**.

Philanthropy already matches donors to causes. A capability treasury could match idle or donated computational resources, technical volunteers, validated agents, and public datasets to institutions with high social returns but low purchasing power.

The matching problem is itself ideal for AI.

Which rural clinic could benefit most from this diagnostic workflow?

Which school system has the data quality required for this intervention?

Which conservation project can use spare satellite-analysis capacity?

Which legal-aid organization has an urgent document backlog compatible with this privacy-preserving tool?

A well-governed system can continuously search for these opportunities.

This is where altruism begins to look less like fundraising and more like logistics.

The history of humanitarianism contains a painful lesson: generosity is not enough. Aid can arrive at the wrong place, in the wrong form, at the wrong time. Warehouses can fill with donated goods nobody requested while essential items remain scarce. Projects can optimize for donor visibility instead of local usefulness.

Capability philanthropy could repeat those mistakes at digital speed.

A town does not need a chatbot because somebody has a chatbot to give away.

It needs help with the problem it actually has.

The capability treasury must therefore begin with demand, not supply.

Ask communities what burdens consume their scarce attention.

Ask teachers what work prevents them from teaching.

Ask nurses what forms keep them from patients.

Ask scientists what analysis they cannot afford.

Ask disabled people which interfaces exclude them.

Ask small businesses which administrative obstacles consume days.

Ask civil servants which process everybody knows is absurd but nobody has the capacity to repair.

Then deploy intelligence against those burdens.

The treasury is not a cathedral to AI.

It is a public works department for cognitive friction.

Its success should be measured by mundane outcomes.

Hours returned to nurses.

Forms eliminated.

Languages supported.

Waiting times reduced.

Fraud prevented without harassing innocent people.

Students receiving useful feedback.

Scientific hypotheses tested.

Small towns acquiring capabilities once limited to capitals.

Disabled citizens completing processes without assistance.

Public records becoming understandable.

Security vulnerabilities fixed before they are exploited.

This is the political genius of capability infrastructure: it can make the benefits of AI tangible without requiring citizens to admire AI.

People do not need to love sewage systems. They appreciate not having cholera.

They do not need to understand packet routing. They appreciate that messages arrive.

They should not need to become connoisseurs of model architecture to benefit from abundant intelligence.

If the technology is truly transformative, its highest achievement will be to disappear into ordinary competence.

The permit is clearer.

The clinic calls sooner.

The child gets help.

The bridge is inspected.

The translation is available.

The public defender finds the precedent.

The town learns from the town on the other side of the world that already solved the same problem.

That is what a capability treasury buys.

Not machines.

Civic competence.

Imagine that every useful AI interaction carried a tiny optional instinct:

**Can one percent of the value created here be returned to the commons?**

Not necessarily money.

One percent of compute.

One percent of engineering time.

One percent of the new dataset, after privacy protections.

One percent of the generated educational material.

One percent of the saved administrative capacity.

One percent of the insight, converted into a reusable public note.

One percent of the product surface, reserved for accessibility.

One percent of the model's idle capacity, allocated to scientific or humanitarian queues.

One percent sounds almost insultingly small.

That is its power.

Civilizations are changed by defaults that are small enough to survive contact with ordinary incentives.

A heroic program may attract attention and then collapse. A tiny automatic contribution can persist quietly for decades.

The industrial economy contains countless examples of microscopic fractions becoming enormous at scale. A small tax funds a national system. A few basis points transform investment returns over time. A fraction of each paycheck becomes retirement savings. A tiny reduction in defect rates saves millions of units in mass production.

When intelligence is deployed trillions of times, a small contribution rate can create staggering public capacity.

This is not a proposal for a universal one-percent tax.

It is a design intuition: **build contribution into flow rather than waiting for virtue after the fact.**

The twentieth century often treated philanthropy as something successful people did after accumulation. First create wealth. Then decide whether to give some away.

That model produced extraordinary institutions, but it also separated contribution from production.

The AI economy offers a chance to reconnect them.

What if every major productive system had an upstream channel?

A coding agent fixes a bug in proprietary software. If the bug originates in an open-source dependency and the fix can safely be generalized, it prepares an upstream patch.

A logistics model finds a more fuel-efficient routing pattern. If the insight is non-sensitive, it contributes the general method to a public transport optimization library.

A pharmaceutical research system discovers a failed molecular pathway. The failure data, often commercially useless but scientifically valuable, is contributed to a shared negative-results repository under appropriate rules.

A company develops an accessibility adaptation for its own users. The underlying technique becomes part of an open standard.

A municipality pays for a new benefits workflow. The generic forms and integration lessons return to a national civic-technology commons.

Contribution becomes a side effect of doing the work.

This is the social equivalent of regenerative braking.

A hybrid car recaptures energy that would otherwise disappear as heat. A commons-oriented institution recaptures knowledge and capability that would otherwise disappear as private residue.

The deeper opportunity lies in **surplus attention**.

AI will save time unevenly. Some jobs will become faster. Some organizations will need fewer hours to perform the same work. The obvious economic response is to convert every saved minute into more output.

But that is a choice, not a law of physics.

A civilization can decide that some productivity gain should become slack.

Slack is not laziness.

In complex systems, slack is resilience.

A nurse with eight free minutes can explain something properly.

A teacher with an unfilled hour can call a parent before a problem becomes a crisis.

An engineer with a quiet afternoon can fix the maintenance issue that has been postponed for three years.

A scientist with discretionary time can pursue the strange result nobody funded.

A civil servant with breathing room can redesign the absurd form everybody hates.

A parent who finishes work earlier can sit beside a child.

If AI merely increases throughput, it may create richer institutions that feel more frantic.

If some productivity becomes discretionary human time, it can increase the capacity for care.

The one-percent civilization therefore protects not only financial surplus but temporal surplus.

A hospital might commit a fraction of AI-generated efficiency gains to staff learning and patient communication.

A company might reserve a small share of saved engineering time for maintaining open-source dependencies.

A school might convert administrative automation into smaller feedback loops rather than larger class loads.

A government agency might use reduced paperwork to provide real assistance instead of simply shrinking headcount until queues return.

This is a subtle political question.

Who owns the productivity dividend?

The shareholder?

The worker?

The customer?

The state?

The public?

There is no single answer. Different institutions will bargain differently.

But if the entire dividend is captured by whoever owns the machines, then scalable altruism will remain peripheral no matter how capable the machines become.

The triumph of the commons requires a social norm that asks a simple question whenever productivity rises:

**What portion of this new capability should become shared capability?**

The answer can be negotiated, local, experimental, and plural.

The question must become normal.

Consider what happened with corporate cybersecurity.

For years, many organizations treated security as an optional cost center. Then the networked world made insecurity contagious. A weak vendor could expose a strong customer. A compromised device could become part of an attack on someone else. Gradually, security became part of ordinary engineering culture: patching, disclosure, audits, incident response, minimum standards.

Contribution may follow a similar path.

At first, organizations that return useful knowledge to the commons will look unusually generous.

Then ecosystems may discover that contribution improves resilience, recruitment, interoperability, reputation, and innovation.

It may become professional etiquette.

A mature engineer asks whether a dependency can be improved upstream.

A mature AI institution may ask whether a solved problem can be improved upstream too.

This cultural shift matters because laws cannot enumerate every possible contribution.

The commons grows partly through habit.

Cite the source.

Share the fix.

Document the edge case.

Translate the guide.

Publish the benchmark.

Report the failure.

Leave a trail.

Make the next person's job easier.

These gestures look small because they are small.

So are cells.

At scale they become a body.

The one-percent civilization is not built by mandatory sainthood.

It is built by systems in which ordinary self-interested activity leaves behind a little public fertility.

That is a more realistic ambition than asking eight billion people to become morally exceptional.

Humanity does not need everyone to spend their life giving.

It needs the machinery of ordinary life to give a little as it moves.

A capability treasury is easiest to understand by comparing it with money and equipment.

Governments maintain strategic petroleum reserves because energy shocks can become national emergencies.

Hospitals stock critical supplies.

Militaries maintain reserve capacity.

Central banks provide liquidity when private markets seize.

The shared principle is that some capabilities are too important to depend entirely on spot-market availability.

Cognitive capacity is joining that category.

During ordinary times, a school, health department, small town, public-interest newsroom, or research lab may be able to purchase commercial AI services as needed.

During a crisis, demand can surge.

Prices can change.

A provider can ration usage.

A foreign dependency can become politically sensitive.

A security incident can make a service unavailable.

A public institution can also be too small to negotiate decent terms even when the market is functioning normally.

A capability treasury would pool purchasing, infrastructure, and technical competence so essential users do not face the market alone.

The treasury does not have to own the largest data center in the country.

It can hold a portfolio.

Long-term contracts.

Reserved cloud capacity.

Publicly operated clusters.

Local inference hardware.

Access to open models.

Emergency credits.

Evaluation suites.

Specialist staff.

Portable workflows.

The portfolio reduces dependence on any one supplier or architecture.

This is financially sensible for the same reason insurance pools risk.

The usage of many public institutions is not perfectly correlated in ordinary times. A shared compute bank can buy capacity at scale and allocate it dynamically.

The model also allows deliberate cross-subsidy.

A large agency can buy efficiently.

A tiny rural clinic cannot.

Pooled procurement spreads fixed costs and bargaining power.

The treasury becomes more interesting when it stores more than compute.

It can store verified public prompts and workflows.

Language resources.

Safety evaluations.

Accessibility components.

Open datasets.

Agent permissions templates.

Incident reports.

Procurement clauses.

Model cards written for public-service contexts.

The goal is not merely a warehouse of GPU hours.

It is accumulated operational competence.

This is the same distinction that separates cash from capacity.

An organization can receive a grant to buy AI and still make a poor deployment because it lacks the people to evaluate vendors, redesign workflows, protect data, and maintain the system.

A capability treasury can offer shared technical staff in the same way some governments offer shared legal, procurement, or cybersecurity services.

Small institutions gain access to expertise without each building a full department.

The treasury can also become a public investor.

Some capabilities will not be supplied adequately by the market because demand is fragmented or unprofitable.

Minority-language models.

Assistive interfaces.

Tools for neglected diseases.

Public-record search.

Local-government software.

Open evaluation infrastructure.

The treasury can finance these as public assets, then contract with private or nonprofit builders.

This is a different posture from buying licenses after the market decides what exists.

It allows society to express demand for public capability before a product has a conventional payer.

The difficult issue is governance.

A capability treasury can become a patronage machine.

Who receives subsidized compute?

Which model developers receive contracts?

Which public-interest projects count?

How do we avoid a technically sophisticated agency becoming a gatekeeper over the rest of society?

Allocation should therefore combine clear categories with plural decision making.

Some capacity can be formula-based.

Schools receive allocations by enrollment.

Public hospitals by patient load.

Universities by research programs.

Some can be competitive.

Researchers or nonprofits apply for additional capacity.

Some should be emergency reserve.

Some should be available through vouchers that local institutions control.

The treasury should publish allocation rules, aggregate usage, evaluation criteria, and conflicts of interest.

It should be possible to appeal.

This is one place where contribution accounting can help without becoming a social-credit system.

Projects that receive public capability can be asked what reusable outputs they will return.

A dataset.

A safety evaluation.

A translation resource.

A protocol.

A software component.

A documented lesson.

Not every project will produce a public artifact. Some work is sensitive. Some fails.

Failure should be reportable without punishment when it is honest, because negative results are part of the commons.

The bargain is not "free compute in exchange for success."

It is "public capability in exchange for responsible learning and, where possible, reusable value."

The treasury should also measure opportunity cost.

Compute spent on one task cannot be spent on another.

This prevents the rhetoric of non-rival intelligence from hiding physical scarcity.

A public model call may be cheap, but millions of calls are not costless.

The allocation system can therefore price internal consumption even when the end user pays nothing.

Agencies see the resource cost.

Citizens do not face a toll for basic service.

This is similar to how institutions account for electricity or staff time internally.

Scarcity remains visible to managers without being imposed on every individual transaction.

The capability treasury is one answer to the question of how altruism becomes infrastructure.

It converts charitable exception into standing capacity.

Instead of hoping a company donates credits during the next emergency, society has a reserve.

Instead of every small institution begging for technical help, there is a shared service.

Instead of public-interest AI being whatever commercial systems happen to make cheap, there is a buyer capable of creating demand for neglected capability.

The treasury does not replace markets.

It gives the public bargaining power inside them.

That is a recurring theme of the commons.

The alternative to monopoly is not necessarily public ownership of everything.

It is enough independent capability that no single owner gets to define the possible.
