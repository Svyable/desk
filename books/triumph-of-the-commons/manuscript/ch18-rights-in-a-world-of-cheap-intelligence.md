# Rights in a World of Cheap Intelligence

Every major expansion of productive capacity eventually creates a rights question.

Printing made censorship newly consequential because ideas could travel farther.

Industrialization made labor protections urgent because factories concentrated power over workers.

Mass databases made privacy a civil issue because institutions could remember more than communities ever had.

AI makes cognitive access itself a rights question.

When high-quality explanation, translation, navigation, tutoring, and assistance become cheap enough to provide universally, denying them can begin to resemble denying literacy in an age of books.

This does not mean every person is entitled to unlimited computation.

No society can promise infinity.

It means we should identify a baseline of **cognitive civil infrastructure** below which people are unnecessarily excluded from modern life.

A person should be able to understand an official decision that affects them.

A person should be able to access public information in a usable language and format.

A child should be able to receive basic educational explanation even when a teacher is unavailable at that moment.

A disabled person should not be blocked from public services because an interface was designed for one kind of body.

A citizen should have practical help navigating rights that exist on paper.

A patient should be able to understand routine medical instructions, while serious medical judgment remains appropriately human and accountable.

A worker should be able to learn what an automated system is doing when it materially affects employment.

A person facing a consequential automated decision should have a path to explanation and appeal.

These are not rights to a particular product.

They are rights to meaningful participation in institutions whose complexity would otherwise become exclusionary.

Call this the **right to legibility**.

Modern societies are full of formally available rights that are functionally inaccessible because the procedures are too complicated.

You may have the right to appeal, but the deadline is hidden in page nine.

You may qualify for assistance, but eligibility spans three agencies.

You may challenge a fee, but the relevant evidence is scattered across documents you do not know how to request.

You may vote on a bond issue, but the fiscal explanation assumes professional fluency in municipal finance.

The right exists.

The interface fails.

AI can make institutions legible at scale.

That capability should change what citizens are entitled to expect.

There is a broader principle here.

Technological feasibility often precedes moral expectation.

Before elevators, nobody spoke of elevator access. Once tall buildings became ordinary, access became a question of disability rights. Before digital networks, immediate access to public records was impractical. Once records could be published cheaply, secrecy required stronger justification.

As cognitive assistance becomes abundant, complexity itself becomes less defensible as a barrier.

"The process is complicated" will stop sounding like an explanation.

It will sound like a design bug.

A second emerging right is the **right to human escalation**.

AI may become the first interface for billions of institutional interactions. That can be convenient and humane when the system works. But no person should become permanently trapped inside an automated layer when the matter is important and the system is wrong.

The right to human escalation does not mean a human must personally handle every transaction.

It means there is a known threshold where judgment, accountability, and exception handling become reachable.

This right protects against a peculiar cruelty: systems that are infinitely available and infinitely unable to understand the one fact that matters.

A third is the **right to cognitive privacy**.

As AI becomes a thinking companion, people will reveal drafts, fears, plans, questions, confusions, and half-formed beliefs they would never publish. These interactions can become the most intimate informational layer in ordinary life.

A society that treats this layer casually will create unprecedented opportunities for manipulation.

A person must be able to think with a machine without automatically converting every thought into an asset for someone else.

The ability to explore an idea privately is part of intellectual freedom.

Libraries understood this before algorithms did.

A good library does not ask you to justify why you read the book.

A good cognitive infrastructure should inherit that restraint.

A fourth is the **right to non-dependency**.

This may sound paradoxical in a book advocating universal access to AI.

But help that destroys the recipient's ability to function without the helper can become a form of control.

An educational agent should sometimes teach the learner how to solve the problem alone.

A small business tool should make records exportable rather than trapping the owner in one platform.

A public agency should preserve human-readable procedures rather than allowing institutional knowledge to exist only inside one vendor's model.

A country receiving technical assistance should gain local capacity, not permanent dependence on a foreign system.

The altruistic test is not merely "Did we help?"

It is "Did the help expand the recipient's future agency?"

This is the difference between a crutch and a prosthesis.

Both support.

One may be temporary; the other becomes integrated capability.

The fifth right is the **right to know when persuasion is synthetic**.

AI will make tailored persuasion extraordinarily cheap. A political campaign, employer, seller, ideological movement, or scammer can generate arguments shaped to a person's fears, history, vocabulary, and social context.

A commons civilization cannot ban persuasion.

Civilization is persuasion.

Teachers persuade students to care. Public-health workers persuade people to vaccinate. Parents persuade children to sleep. Citizens persuade one another to vote differently.

But there is a moral difference between encountering another person's argument and being invisibly subjected to a machine that can test thousands of rhetorical variants against your predicted vulnerabilities.

People should have meaningful ways to know when they are interacting with systems designed to influence them at scale.

The sixth right is the **right to remain inefficiently human**.

You may write the letter yourself.

You may take the slow route.

You may draw badly.

You may learn arithmetic without a calculator.

You may ask a human clerk the question the machine already answered.

You may perform a craft whose automated equivalent is cheaper.

A civilization that worships efficiency will regard these choices as waste.

A civilization that values agency will recognize them as forms of human authorship.

The point of abundant intelligence is to expand the space of possible human lives.

Not to narrow life to the behavior a system finds optimal.

Rights are the walls that keep benevolence from becoming domination.

They are not obstacles to the triumph of the commons.

They are what makes the triumph worth having.

Every dream of large-scale altruism eventually encounters a dangerous sentence:

**We need a metric.**

The sentence is usually correct.

It is also how many disasters begin.

If a system is going to allocate billions of machine-hours, recommend interventions, compare programs, or decide which public problem receives attention first, it needs feedback. Did the tutoring help? Did the clinic reminder increase attendance? Did the translation reduce errors? Did the housing intervention reduce homelessness? Did the disaster-warning system reach the people in danger?

Without measurement, altruism becomes theater.

With bad measurement, altruism becomes coercion.

This tension will become central as AI allows institutions to optimize social systems with unprecedented speed.

The danger is obvious: once something can be measured, institutions may begin treating the measure as the person.

A child becomes a test score.

A patient becomes a risk category.

A neighborhood becomes a crime probability.

A worker becomes a productivity index.

A citizen becomes an engagement metric.

The machine does not need to hate anyone for this to become dehumanizing. It only needs to optimize a proxy with enough authority.

The altruistic civilization therefore requires a discipline of **measurement without ownership**.

Measure outcomes, but do not claim total knowledge of the people producing them.

Use indicators, but preserve the right of human beings to exceed the categories assigned to them.

Optimize systems, but do not turn every life into a permanently scored object.

This sounds philosophical until a concrete decision must be made.

Suppose a city has enough housing-support capacity to help ten thousand families and fifteen thousand are at risk of eviction. An AI system can estimate which families are most likely to become homeless without intervention.

Should the city use it?

Probably, under some conditions.

But the answer changes depending on what the model is allowed to see, how errors are appealed, whether certain neighborhoods have historically distorted data, whether landlords can access the score, whether the system deprioritizes people who are harder to predict, and whether the prediction becomes a label that follows a family for years.

The same tool can be an instrument of compassion or an architecture of stigma.

The difference lies in governance.

The first rule is that **a measurement system should be narrower than the person**.

If the goal is to predict eviction risk, the resulting score should not quietly become a general measure of responsibility, employability, parental fitness, creditworthiness, or civic worth.

Institutional databases love to metastasize. A field collected for one reason becomes tempting for another. AI makes cross-linking easier, which means restraint must become more explicit.

The second rule is **use the least invasive signal that achieves the legitimate goal**.

A school trying to identify students who need tutoring does not need a psychological profile of every household if recent coursework is enough. A transit agency optimizing routes does not need to know the identities of every passenger if aggregate flows suffice. A hospital planning staffing may not need a permanent behavioral score attached to each clinician.

Capability creates temptation. Ethical design is often the refusal to use all available capability.

The third rule is **measure the system as aggressively as you measure the individual**.

This is one of the great asymmetries of bureaucracy. Institutions collect exquisite detail about applicants while remaining vague about their own failures.

A benefits agency may know the date a citizen missed a form but not the average number of contradictory letters the agency sent before that date.

A school may track student attendance by the minute but not how many substitute-teacher vacancies made attendance feel pointless.

A court may score a defendant's risk but not publish how often hearings are delayed because the institution itself cannot schedule efficiently.

AI should reverse this gaze.

Before asking, "Why did this person fail?" ask, "Where did the system create unnecessary failure?"

This is an altruistic use of intelligence because it directs scrutiny toward power.

The fourth rule is **preserve contestability**.

No important social score should be a magic number with no path of appeal.

If an AI denies, ranks, flags, or deprioritizes a person in a consequential setting, the affected person should be able to learn the relevant basis, correct bad information, and reach a human process capable of changing the outcome.

This will be expensive.

That is not an argument against it.

Rights are often expensive because efficiency and justice are not identical objectives.

The promise of AI is that it can reduce the administrative cost of procedural fairness. It can prepare explanations, collect supporting documents, surface inconsistencies, translate appeals, and help reviewers understand the case. Abundant intelligence should make due process more available, not less.

The fifth rule is **plural metrics for plural values**.

There is no single scalar called human well-being that can safely govern civilization.

A good school is not merely the one with the highest test scores. A good hospital is not merely the one with the shortest stays. A good city is not merely the one with the highest property values. A good life is not merely the one with maximal self-reported happiness.

Human flourishing contains tensions: freedom and security, achievement and leisure, belonging and individuality, continuity and change, efficiency and dignity.

A system that optimizes one dimension too hard can damage the others.

So large-scale altruism should use dashboards rather than gods.

Multiple indicators.

Visible tradeoffs.

Local interpretation.

Qualitative evidence.

Periodic review.

Space for people to say, "The numbers improved and life got worse."

The sixth rule is **allow communities to define part of the objective function**.

A remote fishing community, a dense immigrant neighborhood, a university town, and a post-industrial rural county may rank public priorities differently. Some variation is not a bug to be smoothed away by a universal optimizer. It is democracy.

AI can help communities articulate those priorities with unusual precision.

Residents can explore scenarios.

Minority views can be summarized rather than erased.

Tradeoffs can be translated into ordinary language.

Proposals can be simulated.

Feedback can be gathered from people who cannot attend a Tuesday evening meeting.

But the machine should not quietly convert participation into permission.

Consultation is not consent.

A thousand comments summarized by a model do not automatically confer legitimacy on a policy.

Human beings retain the right to govern themselves badly.

This is uncomfortable for technologists because optimization culture treats suboptimal choices as defects.

Yet political freedom includes the freedom to value things an optimizer would not choose.

A town may preserve an inefficient old market because it is the place where generations learned one another's names.

A language community may resist a standardized spelling that would improve machine translation because the variation carries history.

A school may keep music instruction despite weak short-term test effects because adults remember that education is larger than examination.

A humane AI civilization must be capable of understanding value without demanding that all value become machine-legible.

This leads to the deepest rule: **some things should remain unoptimized.**

Friendship.

Play.

Grief.

Religious practice.

Private reflection.

Unstructured childhood.

The aim of scalable altruism is not to place life inside a benevolent control system.

It is to remove needless suffering and needless friction so people have more room to live lives that are not managed by the system.

The successful metric is therefore sometimes absence.

Fewer forms.

Fewer preventable infections.

Fewer hours spent proving eligibility.

Fewer languages excluded.

Fewer days waiting for a simple answer.

Fewer people harmed by an error no one had time to notice.

Then the institution should recede.

This is why the commons needs privacy.

Privacy is not merely an individual preference. It is a shared civic resource. A society in which every action can be remembered, inferred, priced, and optimized produces cautious people. Experimentation becomes dangerous. Dissent becomes expensive. Identity hardens because yesterday's behavior follows you forever.

A forgiving society needs some forgetting.

The irony is that AI can help create privacy as well as destroy it.

It can perform analysis locally rather than centralizing raw data.

It can generate synthetic examples.

It can redact sensitive details.

It can answer statistical questions without exposing individual records.

It can enforce purpose limitations more consistently than hurried humans.

It can warn when a proposed analysis exceeds the consent under which data was collected.

The technology that makes universal surveillance possible can also make data minimization practical at scale.

Again, capability is morally ambiguous. Architecture decides.

The triumph of the commons will fail if it requires every person to become transparent to the machine.

The commons should know enough to help.

Not enough to own.

Cheap intelligence creates rights questions because cognition is becoming a gateway to other rights.

If a person cannot understand the process, she cannot reliably exercise the right.

If an automated system can deny access with no explanation, formal entitlement becomes fragile.

If essential services assume machine access, people who cannot or will not use the machine can be excluded.

The first cognitive civil right is legibility.

People should be able to understand decisions that materially affect them.

This does not mean every model must expose an impossible transcript of internal computation.

It means the institution must provide the applicable rule, the relevant evidence, the decision, and a usable explanation.

"Because the model scored you 0.43" is not an explanation.

The second right is contestability.

A person must be able to say the system is wrong and reach a process capable of changing the outcome.

Appeal should not require technical expertise.

The evidence used should be accessible subject to legitimate privacy and security limits.

A human reviewer should not be forced to rubber-stamp the model.

The third right is to a human world.

Not every interaction needs a person.

People should still have a meaningful way to reach human judgment in high-stakes or exceptional cases.

This right is especially important where the system controls necessities, legal status, health, education, employment, or civic participation.

The fourth right is cognitive privacy.

Questions reveal people.

A person may ask an assistant about debt, illness, immigration status, sexuality, family conflict, political fear, addiction, or a plan to leave an abusive relationship.

The fact that the interface is helpful does not make the conversation ordinary telemetry.

Sensitive cognitive assistance should have strong limits on retention, secondary use, advertising, profiling, and compelled access.

Local processing should be used where it materially improves privacy without destroying service quality.

The fifth right is freedom from compulsory personalization.

A service should not demand a comprehensive profile merely because personalization improves average performance.

A citizen should be able to ask a tax question without giving the state or vendor a permanent memory of every prior question.

A student should be able to receive help without having every uncertainty converted into a lifetime predictive score.

A worker should be able to use productivity tools without consenting to continuous psychological inference.

The sixth right is provenance in high-stakes knowledge.

When a system advises about law, medicine, public safety, or government procedure, the user should be able to see the authoritative source and its date.

Fluency should never be allowed to substitute for jurisdiction.

The seventh right is non-discrimination in access to the cognitive floor.

If public intelligence becomes basic infrastructure, it has to work for people with disabilities, low literacy, uncommon languages, old devices, weak connectivity, and atypical cases.

Universal access will always be imperfect.

The obligation is to design for the edge before the edge becomes invisible.

Rights also constrain measurement.

The commons wants to know whether people are flourishing.

That desire can become invasive.

A government does not need to know a person's mood every hour to know whether a service works.

A school does not need complete behavioral surveillance to evaluate a tutor.

A public-health system does not need to infer every private habit to measure appointment access.

Measure the intervention before measuring the soul.

This principle protects pluralism too.

Flourishing is not one universal scalar.

People value autonomy, security, family, achievement, leisure, community, faith, exploration, privacy, belonging, and other goods differently.

A public AI system should not quietly optimize everyone toward one psychometric model of the good life.

It can help individuals pursue their own legitimate goals.

It can help democratic institutions evaluate public outcomes.

It should be cautious about collapsing the two.

Rights also apply to the humans working beside the systems.

A nurse should know when an algorithm is influencing a recommendation.

A civil servant should be able to challenge an automated workflow.

A teacher should not be evaluated by a model she cannot inspect.

A worker should not lose professional discretion merely because the software produces a confident answer.

The right to disagree with the machine is part of professional responsibility.

This becomes especially important as models improve.

People defer to tools when tools are usually right.

The better the system becomes, the easier it is for human oversight to become ceremonial.

A pilot who always clicks approve is not meaningful oversight.

A doctor who cannot afford the time to reread the record is not meaningful oversight.

A benefits officer whose reversal rate is punished is not meaningful oversight.

Human responsibility requires human authority.

That may mean designing workflows that occasionally require independent reasoning before showing the model output.

It may mean sampling disagreements rather than treating them as inefficiency.

It may mean protecting workers who escalate systemic concerns.

The rights architecture should also include the right to know when one is interacting with a machine in contexts where that distinction matters.

Not every autocomplete needs a warning label.

A synthetic caseworker, therapist-like companion, recruiter, political messenger, or negotiator should not impersonate a human relationship.

Deception about agency distorts consent.

The final right is collective.

Communities should have some authority over high-impact intelligence systems deployed on them.

Local language communities should participate in translation standards.

Workers should have voice in workplace monitoring systems.

Patients should have representation in health-data governance.

Citizens should be able to contest public-sector models through democratic and legal channels.

This returns us to Ostrom.

People affected by the resource regime participate in making the rules.

Cheap intelligence does not make that principle obsolete.

It makes it possible to ignore the principle at much larger scale.

Rights are the boundary that prevents abundance from becoming benevolent domination.

The commons exists for people.

People do not exist to complete the commons.

Rights also need to survive the move from policy documents into interfaces.

A person rarely experiences “contestability” as a principle. She experiences a button, a phone number, a deadline, a reason code, a named office, or the absence of all five. Privacy is experienced as which questions are asked, what can be skipped, how long information is retained, and whether a sensitive fact appears unexpectedly in another context. Transparency is experienced as whether the explanation names the rule that mattered or merely says an automated system reached a result.

This translation from principle to procedure is where many rights quietly weaken. An organization can publish excellent governance language while making the appeal path practically unusable. It can technically disclose automated assistance in a privacy policy nobody sees while presenting the output with the authority of a human decision. It can offer opt-out while making the non-personalized route so degraded that consent becomes ceremonial.

The cognitive civil-rights agenda should therefore be tested through ordinary tasks. Can a person receive a meaningful explanation without technical expertise? Can she correct a record once and have the correction propagate? Can she request a human review without losing her place in line? Can she use the essential service without agreeing to unrelated profiling? Can a journalist or auditor reconstruct the class of system involved after something goes wrong?

The answers will never be perfect. They can still be observable.

Rights that cannot be exercised under stress are decoration.
