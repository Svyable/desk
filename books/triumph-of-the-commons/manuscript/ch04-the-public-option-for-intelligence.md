# The Public Option for Intelligence

In the industrial age, countries built public roads because mobility was too important to leave entirely to private toll roads.

They built public schools because literacy was too important to distribute only through family wealth.

They built libraries because knowledge was too important to sell one book at a time.

They built sanitation systems because disease did not respect property lines.

They built weather services because forecasts become more valuable when everyone can use them.

The AI age will force a similar question:

**Which forms of intelligence are too socially important to distribute only according to purchasing power?**

A market for premium intelligence will emerge naturally. Corporations will purchase powerful models. Wealthy families will hire personalized AI teams. Investors will operate fleets of research agents. Elite schools will integrate sophisticated tutoring. Law firms will automate analysis. Pharmaceutical companies will use models to accelerate discovery.

There is nothing inherently wrong with this. Markets are excellent at finding valuable uses for technology.

But if the highest-capability systems concentrate only where money already concentrates, AI may increase cognitive inequality even while lowering cognitive cost.

This is the paradox of abundance: something can become dramatically cheaper and still remain unevenly distributed.

Clean water is cheap in a modern city. That does not mean everyone on Earth has clean water.

Digital storage is cheap. That does not mean every archive is digitized.

Online education is cheap. That does not mean every student receives good instruction.

Falling unit cost creates possibility, not access.

A public option for intelligence would guarantee some baseline layer of high-quality AI assistance as a civic utility.

Not one government chatbot.

An ecosystem.

Imagine every resident having access to:

- a tutor grounded in the national curriculum but adaptable to individual learning needs;
- a health-information navigator that explains official guidance and helps prepare questions for clinicians;
- a legal-information guide that can explain procedures without pretending to replace counsel;
- a benefits navigator that knows current eligibility rules;
- a multilingual civic interpreter;
- a small-business assistant for licensing, taxes, and compliance;
- an accessibility assistant for people interacting with complex digital systems;
- a public-records explainer that can summarize budgets, legislation, and local decisions with links to source material.

The system should not be designed to make citizens dependent on a central oracle. It should be designed to make institutions legible.

Every answer should be traceable.

Every policy interpretation should show the authoritative source.

Every uncertain conclusion should display uncertainty.

Every consequential action should preserve human appeal.

Every model update should be auditable.

Every community should have a path to contest systematic errors.

The point is not to automate government. The point is to make government understandable at human scale.

The public library offers a useful precedent.

A library does not tell you what to think. It creates broad access to informational capability.

It does not eliminate bookstores. It coexists with a market.

It does not guarantee that every book is true. It develops norms for curation, provenance, and intellectual freedom.

It does not demand that citizens justify their curiosity before entering.

It is one of civilization’s most elegant answers to a commons question: knowledge creates more value when access expands.

The AI equivalent of a library could provide not just information but active cognitive assistance.

A library card might one day represent the right to a certain amount of compute.

Students could run simulations.

Job seekers could rehearse interviews.

Researchers could analyze public datasets.

Inventors could prototype.

Nonprofits could automate routine administration.

Local journalists could inspect government documents.

Citizens could explore policy tradeoffs.

This would be more than free chatbot access. It would be public cognitive infrastructure.

Electricity became transformative when societies stopped treating generators as isolated marvels and built grids.

AI may follow the same path.

The model is the generator. Compute infrastructure is the grid. Public-interest applications are appliances. Standards are the sockets.

A mature society will ask not merely, “Do we have advanced models?” but:

Who can connect?

At what price?

Under what rights?

With what privacy?

Using which public datasets?

For whose benefit?

The answer need not be full state ownership. The internet itself offers a more pluralistic model: public investment, private innovation, open protocols, nonprofit institutions, universities, community networks, and commercial services layered together.

The essential principle is access to capability without mandatory dependence on a single gatekeeper.

A public option for intelligence is how the AI revolution stops being something that happens to citizens and becomes something citizens can use.

Suppose a foundation has ten million dollars.

Traditionally it asks: where should we spend the money?

The AI era adds a second question: **what useful capability can we build once and let everyone reuse?**

This is the altruism multiplier.

The basic equation is:

> **Impact = quality of intervention × number of people reached × frequency of reuse × duration of usefulness**

AI can increase the middle terms dramatically.

A grant that funds one hundred counselors helps the people those counselors can reach.

A grant that helps counselors encode their best educational practices into a safe, evidence-grounded support tool may extend some portion of their expertise to millions—while still reserving human counselors for situations that require human judgment and relationship.

A grant that translates five hundred critical medical protocols by hand is useful.

A grant that builds a verified translation pipeline, terminology system, review process, and local adaptation workflow can keep translating new protocols for years.

A grant that pays experts to answer a thousand questions is useful.

A grant that turns those answers into a continuously improving public knowledge system changes the cost curve of answering the next million.

This shifts philanthropy from purchasing outcomes toward building outcome-producing machinery.

Traditional philanthropy often tries to scale organizations.

A successful nonprofit operating in one city raises money to open offices in ten cities. It hires managers, leases space, recruits staff, builds local partnerships, creates reporting structures, and attempts to preserve culture during expansion.

This is hard because organizations are heavy.

Methods are lighter.

If the core value of the organization can be partially expressed as knowledge, workflow, decision support, training, or coordination, AI can help package the method for reuse by existing institutions.

Instead of becoming a giant organization, the nonprofit can become a protocol.

Its expertise can diffuse.

This does not eliminate local adaptation. It makes adaptation cheaper.

One of the most promising futures for effective nonprofits is not becoming global empires. It is becoming upstream contributors to shared intelligence.

They discover what works locally. AI helps translate the practice. Other communities modify it. Results flow back. The method improves.

The nonprofit becomes part laboratory, part teacher, part open-source maintainer.

The altruism multiplier becomes more powerful when contributions feed one another.

A teacher develops an effective explanation for fractions.

The explanation is added to a public learning commons.

AI systems test variants with different students.

Educators review which variants work for which misconceptions.

The best versions become easier to retrieve.

Translations are created.

New teachers improve them.

Accessibility specialists adapt them.

Students generate new questions.

The system learns where learners struggle.

The original act of teaching becomes a seed for an ecosystem.

This is open source for social knowledge.

Today, much professional knowledge is trapped in private heads, private inboxes, private consulting engagements, or reports nobody reads. AI makes it possible to convert tacit expertise into reusable public capability—if we deliberately capture it.

That “if” matters.

Without contribution norms, AI may simply suck public knowledge into proprietary systems and resell access.

With contribution norms, institutions can require reciprocity.

Publicly funded research can return machine-readable findings to public repositories.

Public contracts can require reusable documentation.

Model evaluations can be shared across agencies.

Safety incident reports can become cross-sector learning resources.

Educational materials can be published under open licenses.

Public-interest prompts, workflows, agent protocols, and benchmark tasks can be maintained like open-source software.

The multiplier is not just a technical property. It is an institutional choice to preserve reusability.

A donation says: use my resources for good.

An AI agent may enable a richer instruction: continuously search for opportunities to use a bounded amount of my resources for measurable public benefit, subject to my values and constraints.

That turns philanthropy into delegation.

Imagine millions of people assigning tiny agents to contribute in ways they would never have time to manage manually.

One person allocates ten dollars a month to whichever local food pantry currently has the greatest documented shortage.

Another donates spare compute to public-interest scientific projects.

Another allows an agent to translate one verified educational resource each week into an underserved language.

A software developer contributes automated testing to nonprofit codebases.

A retired engineer contributes review capacity to open infrastructure designs.

A company donates unused overnight compute to climate modeling.

A household permits its energy-management system to shift demand slightly when doing so helps stabilize a renewable grid.

Each act is tiny.

The network is enormous.

The future of altruism may look less like one heroic benefactor and more like billions of low-friction micro-contributions coordinated by machines.

Altruism, at last, gets an operating system.

The phrase "public option for intelligence" can sound grander than the first version needs to be.

Begin with a library card.

A public library is a modest institution with an extravagant premise: a person should be able to encounter far more knowledge than she can personally afford to purchase. The library does not abolish authors, publishers, bookstores, or private ownership. It creates a floor of access.

The same logic can apply to machine intelligence.

A public intelligence service does not need to be the best model in the world. It needs to be good enough, trustworthy enough, and available enough to make basic cognitive assistance part of civic capacity rather than a luxury product.

That may mean a student can use a tutor without surrendering personal data to an advertising business.

It may mean a small business can understand a regulation through an official service grounded in the current law.

It may mean a citizen can query public records through an interface that shows sources.

It may mean a nonprofit can translate materials into the languages spoken in its community.

It may mean a researcher at a small institution can use literature analysis and coding tools without putting unpublished work into a vendor's training pipeline.

The public option should be judged by the capabilities it guarantees, not by whether government trained the underlying model.

This is important because governments are unlikely to be the best builder of every foundation model, and they do not need to be. A city does not manufacture every bus. A public health system does not invent every drug. A library does not print every book.

Public capacity can be assembled through procurement, open models, shared infrastructure, negotiated access, local deployment, and common standards.

The state has another role that is less visible but perhaps more important: buyer.

Large public systems purchase enormous quantities of software and services. If procurement asks only for a low unit price, vendors will optimize for the cheapest compliant product. If procurement asks for portability, audit logs, open interfaces, source grounding, human escalation, accessibility, privacy, and data export, the market receives a different signal.

A public option can therefore improve private options.

This is one reason public standards matter even when most people ultimately use commercial systems. Minimum accessibility rules changed buildings beyond government offices. Food-safety standards influence private kitchens and factories. Financial disclosure rules shape business behavior far outside the regulator.

A credible public buyer can make interoperability and accountability normal features rather than expensive custom requests.

The analogy to libraries also forces us to confront cost honestly.

Libraries are not free. They are prepaid.

Society pools money so that the transaction at the moment of need can be cheap or invisible to the user. That design is especially valuable for goods whose social return is larger than what the individual can capture.

Education is the obvious example. A child cannot borrow against all the future benefits society receives from her becoming literate. Public schooling solves part of that mismatch through collective financing.

Cognitive assistance has similar spillovers.

A small business that correctly understands tax rules creates less enforcement cost.

A patient who understands discharge instructions is less likely to return to the hospital for preventable reasons.

A citizen who can navigate a permit correctly saves time for the agency.

A local organization that can defend itself against basic cyberattacks reduces risk to partners.

A researcher who can search prior work efficiently is less likely to waste public grant money duplicating a known dead end.

The beneficiary is not only the person receiving the answer.

This provides an economic case for public intelligence that does not depend on sentiment. Some capabilities produce positive externalities. Markets tend to underprovide goods when the buyer cannot capture the full value created by the purchase. Public provision, subsidy, or pooled procurement can make sense for the same reason we fund other infrastructure.

The difficult question is which capabilities belong in the floor.

"Everything" is not a useful answer.

A public system that tries to match every premium entertainment feature, speculative trading tool, marketing service, or luxury concierge will waste money and crowd out experimentation. The civic floor should be narrower and more durable.

Education.

Public-service navigation.

Translation and accessibility.

Basic legal information with clear jurisdictional limits.

Health information and care navigation with strict safety boundaries.

Cybersecurity hygiene.

Scientific and technical search.

Emergency information.

Administrative tools for small public and nonprofit institutions.

Access to public data and records.

These categories share two properties. They enable participation, and the social cost of exclusion is high.

A public option should also be plural.

A single national model creates a tempting point of control. Better architecture may look like a broker: approved models from several sources, common evaluation, common identity and privacy protections, common logging rules, and the ability to route a task according to sensitivity, cost, language, or local policy.

One model may be strong at translation.

Another may run locally.

Another may have better provenance.

Another may be acceptable for routine public information but not health.

The public capability is the routing and governance layer, not the brand of the model.

This matters for resilience. Monocultures fail together.

A vulnerability in one provider should not disable every school and agency. A pricing dispute should not become a national service outage. A political disagreement with a vendor should not require rebuilding every workflow. A model update should not silently change the behavior of thousands of public systems overnight.

Portability is therefore part of public value.

The public option should be able to switch suppliers.

Data should remain usable.

Prompts and workflows should not be trapped in proprietary formats when avoidable.

Evaluation suites should survive model replacement.

Human processes should not be rewritten from zero each time the market changes.

This sounds like ordinary technology management because that is what mature public AI should become. The most important systems will eventually be boring.

The ambition belongs in the coverage, not the interface.

Imagine a person loses a job on a Thursday.

Today, the experience can fragment immediately. Unemployment insurance has one site. Health coverage has another. Training programs have another. Food assistance has another. Childcare support has another. The person is expected to know which agencies exist, how they define a household, which deadlines matter, and what documentation each requires.

A public intelligence layer could begin with a different unit: the person's problem.

"I lost my job. What changes now?"

The system could identify relevant programs, explain tradeoffs, collect information once where legally permissible, generate a checklist, prefill forms for review, schedule appointments, and flag anything uncertain for a human benefits counselor.

The underlying programs have not changed.

The experience of the state has.

That is a profound shift because many bureaucracies are organized around the agency that owns a program, while citizens experience life events that cross agencies.

Intelligence can become the connective tissue.

There is a danger here. A service that knows enough to coordinate across agencies can become a surveillance system with an unusually intimate view of a life. The public option must therefore be built with data minimization, purpose limits, separation of powers, and meaningful alternatives.

Convenience is not consent.

A person should not have to reveal every aspect of her life to obtain a bus pass.

Systems should ask only for what a task requires. Sensitive data should stay local when possible. Access should be logged. Secondary use should be constrained. People should be able to complete essential processes through non-AI channels when needed.

The library analogy helps again. A library can recommend books without demanding the right to sell a lifetime reading history to every interested buyer. Public institutions can provide personalized service without treating personalization as permission for total memory.

The public option also needs a budgetary imagination.

Compute will not fund itself.

One model is direct appropriation, like schools and libraries.

Another is pooled purchasing across agencies and municipalities.

Another is a public compute bank that buys capacity at scale and allocates credits to qualified institutions.

Another is a requirement that publicly funded models or datasets return a reusable capability to the public where rights and safety allow.

Another is a negotiated commons dividend from major deployments that rely heavily on public infrastructure, public research, or public data.

Different countries will choose different mixes.

The larger principle is that access to basic intelligence should be treated as an infrastructure question before it becomes an emergency inequality question.

We have seen the alternative with broadband.

When networks are built primarily around the easiest customers, the hard-to-serve areas arrive later, at higher public cost, after education, commerce, and government have already reorganized around connectivity. Intelligence infrastructure could repeat the pattern faster.

The rural school.

The minority language.

The person with a disability.

The tiny municipality.

The low-margin clinic.

The nonprofit with no procurement department.

These users should be in the architecture from the beginning because systems designed only for lucrative users accumulate assumptions that are expensive to reverse.

This is not an argument for freezing innovation behind public procurement.

It is an argument for a civic floor under a volatile market.

Commercial models can race.

Public capacity should endure.

If the premium system becomes astonishing, wonderful. If a startup invents a better interface, use it. If an open model becomes sufficient, route more work there. If local hardware gets cheaper, push sensitive tasks to the edge.

The public option does not need to win the race.

It needs to make sure everyone is allowed onto the track.

There is a political advantage to defining the public option as a capability floor rather than a state monopoly. It leaves room for excellent private services to exist above the floor and for communities to operate alternatives beside it. The public succeeds when nobody is excluded from basic assistance, not when every citizen uses the same product.
