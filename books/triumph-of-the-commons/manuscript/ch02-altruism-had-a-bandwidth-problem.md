# Altruism Had a Bandwidth Problem

Consider the most generous person you know.

Now give them ten thousand people to help before dinner.

Generosity breaks under scale.

This is not a moral failure. It is physics.

Human altruism evolved in bodies. We carry groceries. We hold hands. We listen. We teach. We donate. We show up. All of these acts require time, energy, attention, or resources. Even saints sleep.

The twentieth century solved some of this scaling problem through institutions. Charities, governments, foundations, hospitals, universities, multilateral organizations, and volunteer networks became amplifiers of individual concern. You could donate to a vaccine program rather than personally vaccinating a village. You could fund a university rather than personally teaching ten thousand students.

But institutions created a second bottleneck: administration.

Every dollar of concern had to travel through forms, meetings, procurement, audits, reports, grant applications, translation layers, legal reviews, evaluation frameworks, donor communications, and coordination among people who often had different incentives.

A philanthropist could care instantly. The institution could take eighteen months.

This gap between moral urgency and operational bandwidth is one of the defining frustrations of modern altruism.

We know children need tutors. We do not have enough tutors.

We know patients need explanation. Clinicians do not have enough minutes.

We know small nonprofits need legal and accounting support. They cannot afford professional rates.

We know governments need better software. The procurement cycle outlives the minister.

We know scientific literature is too large for any researcher to read. We still publish more of it every day.

We know citizens need trustworthy context. Experts cannot personally answer everyone.

The problem is not merely insufficient money. It is insufficient bandwidth.

AI attacks bandwidth directly.

A trained human can produce one excellent explanation. An AI system can produce a million contextualized explanations derived from it.

A lawyer can create a template. An AI can help thousands of people fill it correctly.

A professor can design a course. An AI tutor can make the course conversational for each student.

A physician can define warning signs. An AI can reinforce those signs at the bedside, at home, and in a language the patient understands.

A government can publish a regulation. An AI can become the interactive explanation layer around it.

What was once a fixed amount of care becomes a reusable pattern of care.

This is the most important economic property of machine-mediated altruism: **non-rival helpfulness**.

If I give you my coat, I no longer have the coat.

If I give you a good explanation, I still have it.

If the explanation can be copied and adapted nearly for free, then one act of cognitive generosity can become a public good.

Open-source software taught us this lesson in a narrow domain. One programmer fixes a bug and millions benefit. One engineer writes a library and thousands of companies inherit the capability. One security researcher publishes a patch and the entire ecosystem can improve.

AI generalizes the pattern from code to cognition.

The reusable unit is no longer only a program. It can be a teaching strategy, a diagnostic workflow, a negotiation script, a scientific method, a checklist, a planning heuristic, a bureaucratic navigation path, a piece of institutional memory, or a way of noticing a human need.

In this sense, AI can become an altruism compiler.

Humans specify values, goals, examples, and constraints. Machines compile them into repeated acts of assistance.

The phrase sounds mechanical because it is mechanical. That is the point.

We have romanticized generosity as spontaneous sacrifice. But many of the greatest improvements in human well-being came when care became standardized.

Clean water is standardized care.

Building codes are standardized care.

Vaccination schedules are standardized care.

Seat belts are standardized care.

Universal education is standardized care.

Emergency numbers are standardized care.

Social insurance is standardized care.

The most powerful altruism often becomes invisible because it has been engineered into the environment.

You do not thank the stranger who designed the fire exit sign. You simply leave the burning building.

AI gives us the opportunity to engineer far more intelligence into the environment.

A society can choose to make good advice abundant.

It can choose to make translation abundant.

It can choose to make tutoring abundant.

It can choose to make institutional navigation abundant.

It can choose to make scientific assistance abundant.

It can choose to make basic legal explanation abundant.

It can choose to make administrative competence abundant.

The moral achievement is not the machine saying something kind.

The moral achievement is building a world in which useful help is waiting before desperation begins.

The bandwidth problem becomes easiest to see in places where nobody doubts the moral objective.

Take education. Almost everyone agrees that a student benefits from timely feedback, patient explanation, and practice calibrated to what she actually understands. The disagreement begins when we ask who is going to provide it to every student, every evening, in every subject, in every language. A wealthy family buys tutoring. A well-staffed school builds smaller classes and office hours. Everyone else is told that personalization is important by institutions that cannot afford to personalize.

Medicine has the same shape. A physician may know that a patient needs ten additional minutes to understand how to manage a new diagnosis. The schedule may offer four. Nurses compensate. Family members compensate. Search engines compensate badly. Confusion gets carried home in a plastic bag of medications.

Law has the same shape. Rights can exist on paper while practical access to them depends on whether someone can afford a person who knows where the right is buried.

Government has the same shape. A benefit can be authorized, appropriated, and technically available while uptake remains low because eligibility is confusing, documentation is tedious, or the people who need it most have the least spare attention.

These are not failures of intention. They are failures of service capacity.

That phrase is worth keeping because it moves altruism out of the emotional register and into the operational one. A society can care deeply and still have too little service capacity to express the care. It can pass a generous law and deliver a humiliating experience. It can fund a program and make access to the program a part-time job.

The modern welfare state, hospital, university, court, charity, and multinational aid system all discovered the same uncomfortable truth: once generosity leaves the scale of the household, it becomes administration.

Administration is not a dirty word. It is the machinery by which a promise becomes real. The trouble is that administration consumes a vast amount of scarce cognition.

Someone has to read the application.

Someone has to compare it with the rule.

Someone has to explain what is missing.

Someone has to route the exception.

Someone has to translate the answer.

Someone has to record what happened.

Someone has to notice when the same exception occurs a thousand times and the rule itself may be the problem.

This is the terrain where AI can matter long before it becomes superhuman at anything glamorous.

Suppose a public agency receives five million routine questions a year. Today, the economics push it toward a call center, a website, a frequently asked questions page, and queues. The website is cheapest and least adaptive. The call center is adaptive but expensive. The queue becomes the rationing device.

A capable language system changes the feasible frontier. It can explain the same rule in many ways, ask clarifying questions, translate, retrieve the relevant source, summarize the next step, and prepare a case for a human when the situation does not fit. None of this removes the need for accountable humans. It changes what those humans spend their time on.

The dangerous implementation is easy to imagine: replace the call center, hide the phone number, let the machine confidently improvise, and declare victory because average handling cost fell.

The useful implementation is different: automate the repetitive explanation, preserve human escalation, measure whether people actually reach the service they are entitled to, and reinvest some of the released labor in cases where judgment and empathy matter.

That difference recurs throughout this book. Cost reduction is not yet contribution. The destination of the saved capacity matters.

A school that uses AI to generate worksheets and then increases class size may have automated without expanding educational abundance.

A school that uses AI to handle routine practice while teachers spend more time diagnosing misconceptions, mentoring, coordinating with families, and working with students who are falling behind has converted automation into a care dividend.

A clinic that uses AI documentation to squeeze another three appointments into the day may improve throughput.

A clinic that gives part of the saved time back to patients may improve medicine.

The same technology can produce either outcome because the altruism multiplier is institutional, not automatic.

This is why arguments about whether AI "replaces" workers often begin too late. They ask which tasks can be automated but not who controls the resulting minutes.

Minutes are political.

If a system saves a million hours of professional labor, those hours do not float into the air. They appear somewhere in an income statement, a staffing plan, a waiting list, a family schedule, or a budget. They can become profit, leisure, lower prices, more service, or simply more work.

The case for scalable altruism is therefore not that machines will teach humans to be nicer. It is that machine labor can create a new surplus of usable attention, and societies can choose to route some of that surplus toward needs markets underserve.

This already happens in miniature whenever a person builds a reusable tool.

A spreadsheet template can save thousands of hours.

A clear explainer can prevent thousands of mistakes.

An open-source package can spare generations of programmers from rewriting a solved function.

A standardized medical checklist can help teams perform a complex process more reliably.

The creator's original effort becomes detached from the number of beneficiaries.

AI makes that pattern more general because the reusable object can be interactive. It does not merely sit there waiting to be interpreted. It can ask what you mean.

That interactivity is the crucial step from publishing knowledge to distributing service.

A library gives you the book. A tutor helps you use it.

A legal website gives you the statute. A navigator helps you understand what it means for your situation.

A database gives you the records. An analyst helps you find the pattern.

Historically, the second half of each pair was expensive because it required a person. When some of that service can be generated on demand, institutions can give away something more valuable than information: competent first-pass attention.

We should be careful with the phrase "first pass." It sets the right boundary.

Many needs are not safe to automate end to end. A model can help a patient prepare questions; it should not be allowed to invent a diagnosis and quietly close the case. It can help a tenant understand the structure of a notice; it should not pretend to be a lawyer licensed in a jurisdiction it cannot even identify. It can help a teacher see patterns in student work; it should not become the unquestionable authority on a child's ability.

Scalable altruism works best when machines expand the front door and humans remain available for the rooms where accountability matters.

This suggests a practical doctrine: use abundant cognition to reduce the cost of getting to a competent human decision.

In many systems the human decision itself is not the bottleneck. The bottleneck is everything around it.

A doctor does not need to spend half the visit reconstructing a medication history if the history can be prepared accurately in advance.

A judge does not need to read every page of a routine filing if the record can be organized and the disputed points surfaced, with the original text one click away.

A civil servant does not need to retype the same data into three incompatible systems if software can reconcile them.

A scientist does not need to manually search every journal for adjacent work if a research agent can maintain a living map of the literature.

This is not merely "efficiency." Efficiency is often used as a managerial word that means producing the same output with fewer people. The larger possibility is capability: giving an institution the ability to do something it previously could not do at all.

A school can answer every student's question after class.

A city can explain every permit in plain language.

A rural hospital can review treatment guidance against a larger body of evidence.

A small charity can have competent bookkeeping, grant drafting, translation, and data analysis without building four departments.

A local journalist can inspect a procurement database that once required a specialist team.

Capability is more interesting than efficiency because it changes who gets to participate.

The most unequal societies are unequal not only in money but in backstage support. Powerful people move through the world surrounded by lawyers, assistants, accountants, researchers, schedulers, fixers, advisors, and institutions that absorb complexity on their behalf. Everyone else is expected to encounter the same complexity alone.

An intelligence commons can function as backstage support for ordinary life.

That is a more concrete promise than "AI for good." It gives us something to design and something to measure.

Does the system reduce waiting?

Does it reduce abandonment?

Does it help a person complete the process correctly?

Does it make the source rule visible?

Does it know when the situation exceeds its competence?

Can the person reach a human?

Does it work in the languages people actually speak?

Does it preserve privacy?

Does the institution learn from repeated failure rather than forcing the next person through the same trap?

These questions sound procedural because dignity is procedural.

People experience institutions one interaction at a time. A right that requires twenty phone calls is a weaker right than one that can be exercised. A public benefit that only professionals can navigate is partly privatized by complexity. A school that technically offers help but makes a student wait three weeks is rationing education through time.

The bandwidth problem is not solved when the model can produce an answer.

It is solved when the answer becomes reliable service.

That takes integration, evaluation, governance, and maintenance. It takes humans who are still responsible for the institution. It takes interfaces that allow uncertainty to be visible rather than hidden behind fluent language. It takes budgets that count prevented frustration as a real outcome.

And it takes a cultural shift in how we think about generosity.

We tend to imagine altruism as a transfer from a person who has something to a person who lacks it. Food. Money. Time. Blood. A spare room. Those transfers remain essential.

But a large part of twenty-first-century altruism may be architectural.

Make the form understandable.

Make the expertise callable.

Make the translation automatic.

Make the evidence searchable.

Make the appeal reachable.

Make the successful method reusable.

The person who designs those conditions may never meet the people helped by them.

That does not make the contribution less human.

It means human concern acquired bandwidth.

Social scientists who study administrative burden often separate the cost of public programs into more than money. People have to learn that a program exists and understand whether it applies. They have to comply with documentation and procedure. They also absorb psychological costs: stigma, frustration, loss of autonomy, the feeling that an institution assumes they are lying.

These costs help explain why a benefit can be formally generous and practically inaccessible.

AI can reduce the first two while accidentally worsening the third.

A flawless automated checklist is still humiliating if it interrogates a person with no explanation.

A fast eligibility system is still oppressive if it demands irrelevant personal data.

A chatbot that never gets tired can become an infinitely patient wall.

The service design question is therefore not only "Can the person complete the process?" but "What does the process ask the person to endure?"

This is one place where human-centered digital government offers a useful precedent. The United Kingdom's Government Digital Service built standards around understanding the user's whole problem, making services simple, ensuring people can use them regardless of digital access, and improving services continuously rather than treating launch as the finish line.

Those principles were created before generative AI. They become more important after it.

A conversational interface can disguise a fragmented service instead of fixing it. It can make a broken process feel polite.

The test is whether the user succeeds with less burden.

If the answer is yes, the intelligence contributed.

If the answer is merely that the institution handled the same burden more cheaply, the transformation is incomplete.

This gives us a useful benchmark for every deployment in the book: the machine should remove complexity from the citizen before it removes responsibility from the institution.

The bandwidth argument also changes philanthropy.

Traditional philanthropy often faces a painful ratio between program spending and administration. Donors understandably want more money to reach the mission, but administration is how the mission avoids fraud, measures results, trains staff, protects data, complies with law, and learns.

AI may lower some of those costs, but the correct response is not to demand that administration disappear.

It is to ask which administrative tasks can become shared infrastructure.

Thousands of small charities maintain similar donor systems, accounting workflows, grant calendars, translation needs, safeguarding procedures, and reporting templates.

A nonprofit intelligence cooperative could provide these functions across organizations while leaving program decisions local.

The gain would not come from making every charity identical.

It would come from removing duplicated cognitive overhead.

This is one place where the commons can increase organizational diversity. When basic administrative competence is cheap, a small specialized organization does not need to grow into a large bureaucracy merely to survive.

The same principle applies to civic groups, cooperatives, research teams, and community media.

Cheap shared back-office intelligence can let more institutions remain small enough to know the people they serve.

Scale the support.

Do not automatically scale the organization.

Consider the caregiver who is trying to keep an older parent at home.

The problem rarely arrives as one application. It is a stack of small administrative events: understanding which benefits exist, finding a doctor who accepts a plan, arranging transportation, reconciling a pharmacy change, locating respite care, interpreting a bill, renewing an eligibility document, applying for a property-tax exemption, and learning which piece of equipment is covered under which rule. Each organization sees one transaction. The caregiver sees a second job.

This is where “administrative burden” becomes concrete. The harm is not only the hours. It is that every hour is taken from something else: paid work, sleep, care, recovery, or the patience required for the next problem. People with money purchase coordinators, accountants, lawyers, private nurses, or simply more forgiving options. People without money become their own integration layer.

A public-interest agent could change the experience without changing any underlying entitlement. It could maintain a consented record of what has already been submitted, explain why one program is relevant and another is not, draft forms for review, keep a calendar of renewals, identify contradictions before they become denials, and tell the caregiver when a human professional is needed. The system would not create a new benefit. It would convert fragmented institutional knowledge into continuity.

The important metric would not be how many conversations the agent completed. It would be whether fewer people abandoned valid claims, missed deadlines, made avoidable trips, or spent evenings retyping facts another public body already possessed.

That is a very different conception of AI productivity. The unit of progress is not tokens per second. It is life returned.

Public-administration research gives this problem a useful vocabulary. Administrative burden is commonly divided into learning costs, compliance costs, and psychological costs. A person has to discover that a program exists and understand its rules; then gather documents, fill forms, travel, call, wait, and prove eligibility; then absorb the stress, stigma, loss of autonomy, or repeated uncertainty that can accompany the process. The categories matter because a service can improve one while worsening another. A beautiful website may lower learning cost but still demand impossible documentation. Automation may lower staff workload while making an appeal more psychologically punishing because no person appears to understand what happened.

The political point is sharper. Burden is not just waste. It helps determine who actually receives a right or benefit that nominally exists. Research on safety-net programs has shown that administrative frictions can reduce access among eligible people, and that the burden falls hardest on those with the fewest spare resources. In that sense, process design becomes distribution policy.

This is why the highest-value public AI may sometimes look like burden transfer. The system takes on the learning work: What exists? What applies? What changed? It takes on much of the compliance work: Which records are missing? Which fields can be prefilled? Which deadline matters next? And it can reduce psychological cost by explaining a decision in ordinary language and preserving continuity across interactions.

But burden can also be transferred in the wrong direction. An agency can deploy AI to make it cheaper to demand more documentation, perform more surveillance, or issue more automated challenges. A company can use a chatbot to make customer service cheaper while making resolution harder. The same technology that can carry paperwork for the citizen can carry enforcement paperwork for the institution.

The question is therefore not whether AI reduces administrative cost in aggregate. It is whose cost it reduces.

A humane system moves avoidable burden away from the person with the least ability to bear it and toward the institution with the greatest ability to automate it. That is a design choice, not an automatic property of intelligence.
