# The Commons Stack

Grand visions fail when they cannot be decomposed.

So let us decompose this one.

What would we actually build if we wanted AI to contribute at scale to global well-being?

Not one world model.

Not one benevolent supercomputer.

Not a ministry of goodness.

We would build a stack.

The internet became powerful because layers could evolve independently. Physical networks moved bits. Protocols routed them. Servers hosted services. Browsers rendered pages. Applications created experiences. No single organization needed to own the whole thing.

A cognitive commons needs a similar architecture.

People and institutions need ways to establish relevant facts without exposing everything about themselves.

A person may need to prove she is eligible for a service without revealing unrelated data.

A researcher may need to prove affiliation.

A public agency may need to prove an alert is authentic.

A model may need to prove which version produced an output.

The identity layer should support selective disclosure, pseudonymity where appropriate, organizational credentials, revocation, and recovery.

Its purpose is trust, not universal tracking.

Every important artifact should be able to carry a history.

Where did the data originate?

Which transformations were applied?

Which model produced the analysis?

Which human approved the action?

Which source supports the claim?

What changed between versions?

Provenance is the plumbing of accountability.

Without it, abundant intelligence becomes abundant hearsay.

Helpful systems require access to information, but access should be bounded.

A school tutor may see a student's assignments but not her parents' tax records.

A benefits agent may use income information for eligibility without retaining it for advertising.

A research agent may query hospital data without receiving identifiable patient records.

Permissions must be understandable, revocable, inspectable, and narrow enough that compromise does not expose an entire life.

Laws, regulations, scientific findings, public datasets, educational materials, civic procedures, technical standards, safety guidance, and institutional documentation should be available in machine-readable forms whenever rights and security allow.

This layer is intellectual infrastructure.

It is where a society decides that public knowledge should be usable, not merely technically published.

A scanned PDF buried on a website is disclosure.

A structured, versioned, provenance-rich source is infrastructure.

Different tasks require different intelligence.

Some can run on small local models. Some require specialized systems. Some require frontier-scale reasoning. Some require deterministic software rather than generative models at all.

The commons stack should therefore be model-plural.

Capability can come from public models, open models, commercial providers, universities, local systems, and specialized tools, connected through common interfaces and evaluated against public requirements.

No single model should become synonymous with intelligence itself.

Does the system help?

Does it work equally well across languages and populations?

Does it hallucinate dangerously?

Does it create dependency?

Can users appeal?

Can it withstand abuse?

Does it reduce or increase staff workload?

Does it preserve privacy?

Does the claimed benefit survive independent measurement?

Evaluation must be continuous because systems and environments change.

A model that passed last year's test may fail after a policy revision or distribution shift.

The stack must know where the machine ends.

Teachers.

Nurses.

Lawyers.

Social workers.

Civil servants.

Engineers.

Community leaders.

Judges.

Emergency operators.

The purpose is not to keep humans ceremonially “in the loop.”

It is to route scarce human judgment toward cases that genuinely require it.

Finally, every useful system should be able to give something back.

A bug fix.

A translated resource.

An anonymized process improvement.

A benchmark.

A lesson learned.

A fraction of revenue.

Unused compute.

A safety discovery.

A reusable workflow.

The stack is incomplete without the return path.

Otherwise it is merely a distribution system.

The commons begins when receiving and contributing are connected.

These layers sound technical because they are technical.

But each contains a political choice.

Identity asks who must reveal themselves.

Provenance asks who can be held accountable.

Permission asks who controls information.

Public knowledge asks what belongs to everyone.

Models ask who supplies intelligence.

Evaluation asks what counts as success.

Escalation asks where authority resides.

Contribution asks where surplus flows.

Architecture is governance that programmers can compile.

That is why the commons stack matters.

It converts values into interfaces.

The future does not need another list of principles unless somebody can open a door on Monday morning and begin.

So here are ten institutions that could make the triumph of the commons more likely.

They need not share a logo. They need not be controlled by one country. They can exist as public agencies, international organizations, cooperatives, universities, nonprofits, public-benefit companies, or federations.

What matters is the function.

A pool of subsidized computing capacity for schools, researchers, municipalities, nonprofits, public defenders, small clinics, and other organizations whose missions create public value but whose budgets cannot compete with large firms.

Access would be governed by transparent criteria, audited for abuse, and diversified across providers where possible.

The point is not free compute for everything.

It is preventing the price of cognition from becoming a tollbooth on public agency.

A permanent institution that adapts and evaluates AI systems for public-service tasks.

Not merely a procurement office.

A place where technologists sit beside civil servants, teachers, translators, accessibility experts, security researchers, and residents.

Its output is not one model but tested civic capability: benefits navigation, translation, document explanation, case triage, procurement analysis, meeting accessibility, regulatory search, and institutional memory.

Thousands of languages and dialects remain poorly served by digital systems because commercial incentives follow market size.

A translation commons would fund datasets, evaluation, speech resources, terminology, local review, and models for languages whose speakers should not need to become economically large before becoming computationally visible.

Language access is not a luxury feature.

It is the gateway to every other right mediated through information.

A prestigious career path for people and agents who maintain neglected public digital infrastructure.

Open-source dependencies.

Government software.

Scientific databases.

Accessibility tooling.

Archival formats.

Cybersecurity patches.

Public datasets.

The corps would make maintenance legible, funded, and honorable.

Civilizations decay when everyone wants to invent and nobody wants to maintain.

An independent institution capable of evaluating consequential automated systems used in public life.

It would publish benchmarks, investigate failures, maintain incident databases, test disparate performance, and develop methods for auditing systems without demanding disclosure of every legitimate trade secret.

The observatory would be less like a censor and more like a building inspector.

You may construct many kinds of building.

But when people must enter, society has an interest in whether the stairs collapse.

A standing pool of compute, models, data agreements, multilingual communication tools, mapping capacity, and trained human coordinators that can be activated during emergencies.

It would practice before disasters.

Its systems would work under degraded connectivity.

Its agreements would be negotiated before people are underwater.

The reserve would turn improvisation into preparedness.

A patient capital institution for technologies with large public spillovers and weak short-term business models.

Tools for neglected diseases.

Low-resource-language systems.

Open agricultural models.

Accessibility infrastructure.

Public-interest cybersecurity.

Interoperability.

Archival technology.

Its investments could return money when successful, replenishing the fund, but financial return would be one measure among several.

The fund would invest where value is real but difficult to capture privately.

A public-interest archive for preserving the reasoning, methods, and machine-readable history of important civic systems.

It would help institutions avoid forgetting why rules exist, how infrastructure evolved, what failures occurred, and which lessons were learned.

Privacy and security constraints would be strict.

The object is not to remember everything about citizens.

It is to stop institutions forgetting themselves.

Not necessarily one organization, but a legal and operational institution: in high-stakes domains, people must have a meaningful route from automated systems to accountable human judgment.

The guarantee would define response standards, staffing expectations, appeal rights, and auditing methods.

Automation without escalation is cheap until the first impossible case.

Then it is cruel.

A global, interoperable way to record verified contributions to shared capability without turning morality into a social-credit score.

The ledger would track artifacts and institutions more readily than intimate individual virtue.

This dataset was released.

This vulnerability was fixed.

This translation was contributed.

This public model was improved.

This procurement workflow saved other cities six months.

This company returned compute.

This research project published negative results.

The purpose is to make contribution discoverable and reusable, not to rank human worth.

Ten institutions can sound like ten reasons to wait.

Do not wait.

Build one.

A city can create a civic model foundry.

A university can create a compute bank.

A foundation can finance a translation commons.

A country can establish an algorithm observatory.

A consortium of companies can fund a maintenance corps.

A regional government can create a disaster intelligence reserve.

A software platform can implement the giveback primitive.

The triumph of the commons will not arrive through synchronized global enlightenment.

It will arrive unevenly.

One institution proves a mechanism.

Another copies it.

A standard emerges.

A failure teaches a lesson.

A profession forms.

A budget line becomes permanent.

A generation of citizens begins to expect the capability.

Then the strange idea becomes infrastructure.

That is how civilization usually changes.

A stack is useful only if it helps us notice missing layers.

The web did not become useful because one company built a perfect website. It became useful because protocols, addressing, browsers, servers, security standards, search, identity systems, payment systems, developer tools, and content ecosystems accumulated around one another.

The intelligence commons will be similar.

A model alone is not infrastructure.

The first layer is identity without total surveillance.

The system needs to know enough about the actor to apply the correct rights and limits.

A child may receive different protections from an adult.

A licensed professional may receive access to specialized tools.

A public employee may act under delegated authority.

An anonymous person may still deserve access to general information.

An agent may need to prove which human or organization authorized it without exposing unrelated identity.

There should be no assumption that one global identifier is appropriate for every task.

The second layer is provenance.

Where did the information come from?

When was it updated?

What transformations were applied?

Which model version produced the output?

Which human approved the action?

Can the user see the primary source?

Provenance is how a fluent system remains connected to reality.

The third layer is permission.

An agent can read a calendar but not send invitations.

It can draft a payment but not release funds.

It can suggest a policy change but not enact one.

It can summarize medical information but not silently alter a record.

Permissions should be granular, revocable, logged, and understandable to the people granting them.

The fourth layer is public knowledge.

Laws, regulations, forms, public datasets, emergency information, research outputs, standards, educational materials, geographic data, and institutional guidance need machine-readable forms.

This may sound like a clerical project.

It is one of the highest-leverage investments available.

An AI cannot give a reliable answer from a public rule that is buried in a scanned PDF, contradicted by three outdated pages, and updated through an obscure notice.

Machine-readable government is not government by machine.

It is government whose public knowledge can be inspected, queried, and reused.

The fifth layer is models and tools.

Plural is intentional.

Different tasks have different requirements for cost, capability, privacy, latency, language, safety, and local control.

A public architecture should be able to route among models rather than making every institution dependent on one.

It should also separate language generation from tools that perform actions.

A calculator should calculate.

A database query should query.

A payment system should enforce payment rules.

The model can coordinate these tools without pretending its prose is execution.

The sixth layer is evaluation.

Before a public system is trusted, it should be tested on the actual job.

Does the benefits navigator cite the right rule?

Does the translation preserve legally significant meaning?

Does the tutor work across reading levels?

Does the emergency classifier miss certain neighborhoods?

How does performance change after model updates?

Evaluation is not a one-time gate.

The world moves.

The system needs continuous evidence.

The seventh layer is human escalation.

Every architecture diagram should make the human channel visible rather than placing it in a footnote.

Which cases escalate?

To whom?

With what context?

How quickly?

Can the human override?

Does the user know an override occurred?

Can the worker inspect the source rather than only the model's summary?

The escalation system is where responsibility becomes operational.

The eighth layer is contribution.

What does the deployment give back?

Better public data.

A fixed workflow.

A tested translation.

An evaluation suite.

A documented failure.

An upstream patch.

A reusable safety component.

Institutional memory.

The contribution layer turns isolated deployments into cumulative public capability.

These layers suggest institutions worth building.

A public compute bank gives schools, researchers, nonprofits, and public agencies access to pooled capacity.

A civic model foundry evaluates and adapts models for public tasks.

A global translation commons maintains high-value language resources with local reviewers.

A maintenance corps funds the unglamorous care of public software, datasets, standards, and knowledge bases.

A public algorithm observatory tests high-impact systems and makes failures legible.

A disaster intelligence reserve keeps tools, data, and compute ready before crises.

A commons venture fund finances public capabilities the commercial market will not build early enough.

An institutional memory trust helps organizations preserve decisions, lessons, and provenance across staff turnover.

A human escalation guarantee makes access to accountable people a defined service level in high-stakes systems.

A contribution ledger records domain-specific reusable work without turning human worth into one global score.

None of these institutions needs to be created as one giant ministry.

Some can be nonprofit.

Some can be intergovernmental.

Some can be cooperative.

Some can be university-based.

Some can be private companies operating under public contracts.

Some can be open communities.

Plural institutional form is a feature.

The question is whether the functions exist.

This is where the stack becomes constitutional.

Architecture distributes power.

A system with no export function gives power to the vendor.

A system with no appeal gives power to the first decision.

A system with one identity layer gives power to whoever controls identity.

A system with one model gives power to the model provider.

A system with no provenance gives power to fluent assertion.

A system with no maintenance budget gives power to entropy.

These are political choices encoded as technical defaults.

Engineers often dislike this framing because it makes architecture feel ideological.

The architecture is already ideological in the practical sense that it determines who can act.

A bridge decides which loads can cross.

A protocol decides what systems can communicate.

An access-control list decides who can read.

A data schema decides which distinctions the institution can represent.

Technical systems allocate possibility.

The commons stack should therefore optimize for reversibility and substitution.

Can a component be replaced?

Can data move?

Can local rules override global defaults where legitimate?

Can the system continue in degraded mode?

Can users see which component failed?

Can one community fork a public tool without forking the entire infrastructure?

This is how the stack avoids becoming a new centralized choke point.

Standards are powerful because they create compatibility.

They are dangerous when compatibility becomes compulsory uniformity.

The right balance is thin shared layers and rich local variation above them.

A common interface for model evaluation does not require identical values.

A common format for public-service provenance does not require identical policies.

A common identity credential can prove an attribute without centralizing every service relationship.

A common contribution format can make an artifact reusable without forcing one reputation system.

This is how a global commons can remain plural.

The first version should be small.

One city can create a source-grounded civic assistant with human escalation and publish the reusable components.

One university consortium can build a research compute bank.

One language community can build a maintained translation resource.

One health system can create a reproducibility standard for its clinical guidance.

One disaster network can pre-negotiate an intelligence reserve.

The stack grows through successful interfaces, not declarations.

That is how durable infrastructure often emerges.

A local solution proves useful.

Others copy it.

Differences appear.

A standard is negotiated.

Tooling improves.

Institutions form.

Maintenance becomes professional.

The thing that looked like a project becomes a layer.

The triumph of the commons will probably arrive this way too.

Not as the unveiling of one benevolent machine.

As a thousand pieces that begin to fit.

Interoperability deserves one final defense because it is easy to treat as a technical preference.

Interoperability is bargaining power.

If a school can export student work, evaluation history, and tutor configuration into a standard format, it can replace the provider.

If a city can switch models without rebuilding its knowledge base, it can negotiate price.

If a hospital can move an agent workflow between approved environments, one vendor's outage does not become a clinical outage.

If a community can fork a translation glossary, it can preserve local language decisions.

Exit changes the behavior of providers even when nobody exits.

A market with high switching cost can look competitive while users are effectively captive.

The commons stack should therefore measure exit cost.

How many person-months to migrate?

What data is lost?

Which permissions have to be rebuilt?

Which evaluations transfer?

Which source links survive?

Which users must retrain?

These are public-interest metrics.

A low subscription price attached to a five-year migration trap is not cheap.

A public institution that ignores exit cost is borrowing from its successor.

Good infrastructure makes replacement boring.

That may be the best sign that the stack is working: individual components can become obsolete without taking the public capability with them.

The stack also needs a discovery layer even if it is not numbered separately.

People cannot use a public capability they cannot find.

Institutions repeatedly build overlapping tools because they do not know a reusable component exists.

A commons registry can describe available models, datasets, evaluations, workflows, language resources, public APIs, and maintained components.

Machine agents can search the registry before building something new.

"Does this already exist?"

"Who maintains it?"

"What license applies?"

"What populations has it been tested on?"

"What failed?"

Discovery prevents duplicated generosity.

It also creates pressure for maintenance because abandoned resources become visibly abandoned rather than permanently appearing in search as viable options.

The registry should prefer current stewardship over historical popularity.

A component with a thousand old stars and no maintainer is not healthier than a boring library updated last week.

The commons stack needs a way to know what is alive.

The stack also needs a discovery layer because a commons nobody can find behaves like scarcity.

Public institutions repeatedly build useful things that remain trapped behind local names, procurement portals, abandoned project pages, or undocumented APIs. Another agency facing the same problem cannot reuse what it does not know exists. The predictable result is parallel purchasing: ten organizations commission ten versions of a translation tool, a document classifier, a scheduling system, or a safety evaluation because no trusted registry shows what has already been paid for.

A Commons Registry would be deliberately mundane. It would list reusable models, datasets, evaluation suites, service components, policy templates, accessibility tests, incident reports, and deployment playbooks. Entries would state who maintains the asset, what license or permission applies, what data it was evaluated on, known limitations, dependencies, version history, and whether another organization is actually using it. Dead projects would remain visible as dead rather than disappearing, because knowing what failed is also public knowledge.

Discovery changes the economics of procurement. A buyer can ask why a new custom build is necessary when a reusable component already exists. A small organization can begin with a proven baseline rather than a blank page. Maintainers can see where demand is accumulating and justify investment. Researchers can identify gaps that hundreds of institutions are independently stumbling over.

The registry should not become a mandatory app store with one gatekeeper. Its job is legibility, not control. Multiple hosts can mirror it. Communities can annotate entries. Institutions can publish alternatives. The important property is that useful public work leaves a trail thick enough for the next contributor to find.

There is already a modest institutional precedent for the interoperability ethic. The United Kingdom's Service Standard tells government teams to use and contribute to open standards, common components, and patterns so services do not repeatedly solve the same problems and so organizations retain flexibility rather than becoming locked into one supplier. The idea is less dramatic than a global Commons Stack, which is why it is useful. Reuse becomes normal when procurement, architecture, and service standards reward it.

The same rule should extend to public AI evaluations.

Today an institution can spend heavily testing a model for accessibility, safety, language performance, document extraction, or a specialized public-service workflow, then leave most of what it learned inside a contract file. The next institution pays again. Some evaluation data will be sensitive, and some attacks should not be published in immediately reusable form. But a large share of the method can be common: test definitions, redacted failure patterns, benchmark construction, incident taxonomies, procurement questions, rollback criteria, and lessons about what human review actually caught.

A Public Algorithm Observatory would collect that residue. It would not certify one permanent “safe model.” Models change and deployments differ. It would make institutional learning cumulative. A city considering a new translation system could see where similar systems failed. A school district could find accessibility tests already developed elsewhere. A regulator could identify recurrent failure patterns across vendors without waiting for each problem to become a scandal.

This is how the stack earns the name commons. The shared layer is not only software. It is accumulated judgment about how software behaves in the world.
