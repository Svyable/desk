# The One-Percent Civilization

“Free” is one of civilization's most expensive words.

A public library feels free when a child walks through the door, but the building was purchased, the books acquired, the staff paid, the lights powered, and the catalog maintained. A public road feels free at the moment of use, but asphalt, bridges, drainage, inspection, snow removal, land, bonds, taxes, and decades of maintenance stand behind the sensation.

So it will be with public intelligence.

If every student can have a tutor, someone pays for compute.

If every small town can have a planning assistant, someone pays for models, electricity, security, data stewardship, evaluation, and integration.

If every citizen can have a benefits navigator, someone pays to keep the rules current.

The marginal cost of an answer may become tiny without the total system becoming costless.

This distinction is where many visions of abundance become unserious. They confuse declining unit cost with the disappearance of cost.

A commons civilization needs an economic model for the intelligence it wants everyone to access.

Public AI has at least seven layers of expense.

There is compute: the machines that perform inference and training.

There is energy: the electricity, cooling, storage, transmission, and physical infrastructure that make computation possible.

There is model development: research, engineering, evaluation, fine-tuning, and deployment.

There is data stewardship: collecting, cleaning, licensing, preserving, anonymizing, and updating the knowledge on which systems depend.

There is institutional integration: connecting models to actual school systems, clinics, benefit rules, procurement databases, scientific instruments, and public workflows.

There is safety and security: audits, abuse prevention, incident response, access controls, and red teams.

And there is maintenance: the endless work of keeping the whole arrangement useful after launch day.

The last two are usually underestimated because they are not glamorous.

A demo is cheap compared with a decade.

There is no single correct model, but four financing families are likely to recur.

The first is **general taxation**. Public intelligence can be treated like education, roads, sanitation, courts, or libraries: a shared capability financed because broad access creates broad social value.

The second is **usage pooling**. Institutions—schools, hospitals, cities, universities, cooperatives—can combine purchasing power, negotiating shared access rather than buying fragmented contracts alone.

The third is **commons dividends**. Commercial use of publicly funded research, data, infrastructure, spectrum, natural resources, or publicly supported AI capacity can return a fraction of value to a capability treasury.

The fourth is **cross-subsidy**. Premium commercial services can help finance universal baseline services, much as some communication networks, insurance pools, and public utilities have historically mixed tiers of service.

These models can coexist.

The important thing is to make the financing explicit.

When financing is invisible, public capacity becomes vulnerable to sudden withdrawal. A philanthropic pilot disappears when a donor changes priorities. A corporate gift ends when market conditions tighten. A ministry licenses a tool for three years and loses the institutional memory when the contract ends.

A right that depends on quarterly generosity is not yet a right.

The phrase “public option” is useful because it avoids a false choice between state monopoly and private monopoly.

A public option for intelligence does not require governments to build every model.

It requires that citizens and public institutions have a reliable baseline of useful cognitive capability whose existence does not depend on a single company's business model.

Private systems can compete above it.

Open systems can interoperate with it.

Universities can improve it.

Local governments can adapt it.

Civil society can audit it.

Companies can sell specialized layers.

But the baseline remains.

Imagine a national or transnational service that guarantees every accredited school a tutoring model, every public-interest researcher a compute allowance, every municipality a secure administrative assistant, every clinic a translation and protocol-support system, and every citizen access to a basic civic navigator.

This would be expensive.

So were sewers.

So were universal schools.

So were electrical grids.

The correct comparison is not with zero. It is with the cost of incompetence.

How much do societies already spend because people miss benefits they were entitled to, fail courses for lack of feedback, misunderstand medical instructions, duplicate research, process forms manually, suffer preventable fraud, or pay consultants to rediscover knowledge another agency already possesses?

Public intelligence should be evaluated not only as a new expense but as a machine for converting existing waste into reusable capacity.

Cities budget asphalt. Universities budget laboratory space. Hospitals budget operating rooms. Governments budget spectrum and water.

In an AI-intensive civilization, they may also budget cognition.

A school district could receive an annual compute allocation proportional to enrollment and need.

A local government could receive a civic inference budget for planning, translation, casework assistance, and public communication.

A research group could apply for public compute the way it applies for telescope time or a scientific grant.

An emergency could unlock reserve cognitive capacity, much as disasters unlock reserve funds and personnel.

This sounds technical, but the political question beneath it is ancient:

Who gets to command the society's productive capacity?

In the industrial age, unequal access to capital determined what could be built.

In the AI age, unequal access to high-quality intelligence may determine what can be understood, designed, negotiated, defended, and discovered.

If compute becomes a critical input to agency, then access to compute becomes a question of citizenship.

No account of AI abundance is complete without physical honesty.

Computation consumes energy. Data centers occupy land, use materials, draw water in some configurations, require transmission infrastructure, and depend on supply chains. Intelligence may become cheap without becoming immaterial.

A commons civilization cannot celebrate abundant cognition while externalizing its physical costs onto communities that receive few benefits.

This creates a straightforward rule:

**Every cognitive dividend should carry an ecological ledger.**

Where does the power come from?

What infrastructure does it require?

Who bears the local cost?

What efficiency gains reduce demand?

What forms of computation deserve priority when capacity is constrained?

The purpose of intelligence is not to escape ecology. It is to become better at living within it.

The deepest promise of AI is not infinite computation. It is more value per unit of scarce resource.

A model that helps a city eliminate wasted truck routes may consume electricity while saving fuel, road wear, labor, and emissions. A system that accelerates material discovery may require intensive compute while enabling lower-energy technologies. A diagnostic assistant may use a data center while avoiding unnecessary travel and duplicated testing.

The accounting must be whole.

Abundance becomes credible when it is measured against the entire system, not merely the price of one API call.

The most dangerous sentence in an abundance argument is "the marginal cost is nearly zero."

It is often true at the wrong layer.

The extra copy of a digital file can cost almost nothing.

The system that stores, serves, secures, updates, and governs the file does not.

The extra model response can become cheap.

The data center, accelerator, network, cooling system, grid connection, and engineering organization remain physical.

A serious commons has to pay its bills.

That begins with the cost stack.

There is capital cost: hardware, buildings, networking, storage, and grid connection.

There is operating cost: electricity, bandwidth, staff, maintenance, security, and replacement.

There is model cost: training, licensing, adaptation, evaluation, and serving.

There is institutional cost: procurement, compliance, human review, accessibility, documentation, and appeal.

There is external cost: emissions, water use, land use, supply-chain impact, and the opportunity cost of electricity or public capital.

There is failure cost: fraud, bad decisions, downtime, legal liability, and the burden shifted onto people when the system is wrong.

Cheap inference can hide an expensive institution.

This is why the commons needs a balance sheet.

The One-Percent Civilization is a thought experiment, not a magic percentage.

Imagine that one percent of the economic value created by large-scale machine intelligence were deliberately reinvested in public capability.

Not one percent of revenue.

Not one percent of compute.

Not one percent collected through one universal tax.

One percent of the realized surplus, assembled through many mechanisms.

Some would come from government appropriations.

Some from corporate contribution.

Some from philanthropic capital.

Some from public procurement that requires reusable outputs.

Some from shared savings inside institutions.

Some from new taxes or fees where democratic systems choose them.

Some from voluntary compute or data contributions.

The exact mix matters less than the order of magnitude.

A small fraction of a very large productivity gain can finance a large cognitive commons.

The difficulty is that value appears in different places.

If AI lowers prices, consumers receive value but no organization holds a pot of money labeled "consumer surplus" that can be redirected.

If workers finish tasks faster and go home earlier, the value is time.

If a company earns higher margins, the value is profit.

If a government reduces administrative error, the value may be avoided cost.

If a new scientific discovery prevents disease, the value is diffuse and delayed.

A commons dividend therefore cannot rely on one accounting mechanism.

It needs a portfolio.

Direct public funding is the most straightforward.

Legislatures can fund public compute, digital infrastructure, research tools, translation, and civic services because the activities have public value.

This is ordinary budgeting, and it should not be embarrassed about being ordinary.

A second mechanism is shared savings.

An agency deploys AI and demonstrably reduces a cost. A portion of the savings remains with the agency for service improvement rather than disappearing into the general budget.

This creates an incentive to find efficiency while protecting the public-purpose dividend.

A hospital system that reduces administrative burden can reinvest some of the gain in navigation, nursing, or preventive outreach.

A school district can reinvest saved preparation time or software cost in tutoring and teacher support.

A third mechanism is procurement giveback.

Public contracts can ask vendors to return reusable components, evaluations, documentation, or data standards when the work was publicly financed and the material can be released safely.

This prevents every jurisdiction from paying repeatedly for the same scaffolding.

The public buys the specific service.

The commons receives the generalizable layer.

A fourth mechanism is voluntary contribution tied to business competence.

Companies can maintain open-source dependencies, public safety tools, educational access, emergency reserves, or scientific resources.

This should be encouraged without becoming a substitute for taxes or a way to buy regulatory favor.

Philanthropy is additive, not sovereign.

A fifth mechanism is negotiated local benefit where infrastructure creates concentrated physical costs.

A large compute facility can place demands on electricity, land, water, and transmission.

Communities can negotiate around those real externalities.

Grid upgrades.

Transparent resource reporting.

Local training.

Tax arrangements.

Public research partnerships.

Heat reuse where technically sensible.

Community compute allocations.

The purpose is not to turn every project into a moral shakedown. It is to recognize that the infrastructure of cheap cognition is located somewhere.

The energy honesty clause should be simple: never describe cognitive abundance as immaterial.

Bits arrive through machines.

This matters environmentally and politically.

A system that produces public benefit while destabilizing a local grid has not accounted for the full public outcome.

A system that lowers emissions through better planning may justify substantial energy use.

The relevant comparison is not "AI uses energy, therefore bad" or "AI is digital, therefore negligible."

It is value per unit of scarce physical resource, under credible alternatives.

Public-interest workloads should therefore be cost-aware.

Use the smallest sufficient model.

Cache repeated work.

Run locally when it improves privacy or resilience and is efficient.

Batch where latency does not matter.

Schedule flexible compute when power is cleaner or less constrained.

Measure actual energy and resource use rather than relying on dramatic global estimates detached from the deployment.

A commons should model restraint because public goods do not become virtuous by consuming inefficiently.

Financing also requires protection against austerity logic.

If AI reduces the cost of public service, governments may be tempted to cut the budget by the full amount.

That can erase the dividend.

Some savings should absolutely reduce cost to taxpayers.

But if public systems are already understaffed, part of the gain should repair the service deficit.

A benefits office with a six-month backlog does not become "overstaffed" because automation makes each case faster.

A school with inadequate counseling does not have surplus labor because lesson planning becomes easier.

A legal aid organization with a waiting list should not lose funding because document review improves.

The benchmark is need, not the previous labor input.

This distinction is politically important because productivity gains can otherwise create a ratchet.

Public institutions automate.

Budgets shrink.

Service expands until it hits the new constraint.

Then another automation is demanded.

Workers experience efficiency as permanent pressure and citizens never experience abundance.

The One-Percent Civilization is an argument for breaking that ratchet.

Reserve some gain for slack.

Slack is often treated as waste.

In resilient systems it is capacity waiting for the moment demand spikes.

An empty hospital bed can look inefficient until a pandemic.

A trained public servant with time to improve a process can look underutilized until a crisis.

Compute reserve can look idle until a disaster.

Maintenance capacity can look nonproductive until the failure it prevents.

The commons needs slack because it serves needs markets cannot always forecast.

The financing system should also tolerate failure.

A public capability fund that only backs projects with guaranteed measurable returns will select safe, legible work and abandon novel public goods.

Some translation projects will fail.

Some research agents will be less useful than expected.

Some open datasets will receive little use.

Some communities will reject the tool.

The portfolio can still succeed.

Private venture capital understands this principle well. A portfolio can absorb failure because a few successes create large returns.

Public innovation needs similar permission, with different accountability.

The fund should be able to explain why the experiment was reasonable, what was learned, and whether the lesson was returned to the commons.

Failure becomes waste when it teaches nothing.

The final financing principle is intergenerational.

Do not build a cognitive commons on temporary promotional prices.

Do not commit essential services to a model whose long-term cost is unknowable without an exit plan.

Do not fund pilots with capital budgets and leave maintenance to institutions that cannot afford it.

Do not accept donated systems whose future license terms can change unilaterally.

Count the replacement cycle.

Count the human staff.

Count cybersecurity.

Count evaluation.

Count energy.

Count the right to appeal.

The cheap demo is not the cost of the institution.

Once those costs are visible, the project may still be extraordinarily attractive.

That is the encouraging part.

If machine intelligence truly drives the price of useful cognition down, society does not need to confiscate the entire gain to build a large commons.

It needs the discipline to notice the gain, price the real infrastructure underneath it, and reserve a fraction before the default absorbs everything.

One percent is not sacred.

The habit is.

A balance sheet also forces a conversation about who bears transition cost.

Suppose an agency funds public AI by eliminating hundreds of positions immediately.

The service may improve in the long run.

The workers bear a concentrated short-run loss.

That loss is not erased by future consumer surplus.

A humane financing system treats transition as part of the project cost.

Training.

Income support.

Redeployment.

Pension obligations.

Time for teams to redesign work.

Support for communities where one public employer matters disproportionately.

This is not only compensation.

It improves implementation because experienced workers carry institutional knowledge the new system needs.

The cheapest layoff plan can destroy the tacit expertise required to supervise automation.

The same principle applies internationally.

If wealthy countries use AI to reduce demand for outsourced administrative work, lower-income regions that built service economies around that demand may face concentrated shocks.

A global commons cannot celebrate cheaper cognition while ignoring the workers whose comparative advantage was cognitive labor.

Part of the productivity dividend should fund new capability where displacement is greatest.

Education and transition support are obvious.

More interesting is infrastructure that allows those workers to become users and owners of the same leverage.

Shared agent platforms for small businesses.

Local-language tools.

Public compute.

Exportable digital services.

Research capacity.

The goal is not to preserve every old job.

It is to prevent the ownership of new cognitive capital from being geographically narrower than the labor market it disrupts.

Financing the commons is therefore also financing a transition between economic regimes.

Public financing should also distinguish capital from operating cost.

Politicians enjoy funding visible launches.

Maintenance is quieter.

A public model is announced with a grant.

A translation program receives seed money.

A civic data portal is built.

Three years later the model needs replacement, the language reviewers need pay, and the data has not been updated.

The initial investment becomes a decaying asset.

Infrastructure finance has long struggled with this problem. AI makes it acute because software can appear weightless while demanding continuous renewal.

Every public capability should therefore carry a maintenance forecast.

What must be paid annually?

Which components have replacement cycles?

Which data must be refreshed?

Which people cannot be automated?

What security work is recurring?

What vendor prices are variable?

What happens after grant funding?

A project that cannot answer these questions is a pilot, not infrastructure.

Pilots are useful.

The mistake is calling them permanent gifts.

There is one budget line abundance enthusiasts routinely omit: replacement.

A service can be cheap to launch and expensive to own. Models change. Hardware ages. Security requirements tighten. Accessibility standards improve. Data agreements need renegotiation. Staff turn over. A dependency that was free becomes commercial. A vendor changes an interface. A popular public tool attracts enough demand that its inference bill becomes material. The first-year cost is therefore a poor proxy for the cost of a durable commons.

Public-interest AI should be financed with something closer to total cost of ownership. Before a service is declared free, planners should ask what it costs over a decade to operate, evaluate, secure, migrate, support, and eventually retire. Not every estimate will be accurate. The discipline matters anyway because it turns maintenance from an unpleasant surprise into part of the original promise.

This suggests a simple funding rule: every significant public capability should launch with both an operating budget and a maintenance reserve, or with a credible revenue mechanism that fills those functions. The reserve need not be literal cash sitting untouched. It can be contractual commitments, shared-service funding, endowment income, capacity guarantees, or a recurring appropriation. What matters is that the institution knows how year six gets paid for before celebrating year one.

The same logic applies to donated private capacity. A company can make an extraordinary public contribution by offering compute, models, engineering, or cloud credits. But a temporary grant should not become the invisible foundation of a permanent public obligation unless there is an exit plan. Otherwise generosity creates dependency and dependency later becomes leverage. The public partner needs to know the replacement cost, the export path, and the date on which the gift becomes a procurement decision.

A ten-year view also changes which projects look attractive. A flashy bespoke system may lose to a less glamorous component that many agencies can reuse. A slightly more expensive open standard may beat a cheap proprietary shortcut if migration is likely. Training local staff may dominate repeated consultant contracts. An investment in documentation can produce more resilience than another feature.

This is not an argument for bureaucratic paralysis. It is an argument for counting the whole thing.

The One-Percent Civilization is compelling only if its arithmetic includes the electricity meter, the help desk, the security patch, the migration, the union negotiation, the redundant link, the human appeal, and the person who still remembers why the strange exception exists. A civilization that can afford intelligence should be able to afford stewardship.

Energy makes the accounting unavoidable. The International Energy Agency estimated that data centers consumed about 415 terawatt-hours of electricity globally in 2024, roughly 1.5 percent of world electricity consumption, and projected consumption around 945 terawatt-hours by 2030 in its 2025 base case. The global share remains modest relative to total electricity use, but data centers are geographically concentrated, so local grid effects can be much larger than the global percentage suggests.

Those numbers should cure us of two opposite mistakes.

The first is pretending digital abundance is immaterial. Intelligence served from a data center has a physical address somewhere. It draws power, uses equipment, requires cooling, occupies land, connects to networks, and competes for infrastructure that has other users. Public-interest AI cannot claim moral exemption from those costs merely because its output is beneficial.

The second mistake is treating energy use as proof that broad AI deployment is inherently wasteful. The relevant comparison is not electricity versus zero electricity. It is electricity and hardware versus the activity displaced or improved. A model that reduces unnecessary travel, improves grid operation, speeds scientific discovery, or allows one public system to replace ten duplicative systems can create material savings elsewhere. A frivolous high-compute task can do the opposite.

The commons therefore needs energy accounting good enough to compare uses, not a single moral score for computation. High-value public services may deserve reserved capacity even during scarcity. Some workloads can be scheduled when clean power is abundant or delayed without consequence. Efficiency improvements can be shared instead of captured only as higher usage. Procurement can reward operators that disclose enough information to make the tradeoffs legible.

Abundance is not the absence of a meter. It is the ability to spend scarce resources where the social return is unusually high.
