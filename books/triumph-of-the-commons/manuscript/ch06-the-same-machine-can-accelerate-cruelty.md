# The Same Machine Can Accelerate Cruelty

Any serious argument for AI-enabled altruism must stare directly at the opposite possibility.

AI lowers the cost of cognition.

That includes malicious cognition.

Fraud becomes cheaper.

Propaganda becomes cheaper.

Surveillance becomes cheaper.

Cyberattacks become cheaper.

Manipulation becomes cheaper.

Target selection becomes cheaper.

Bureaucratic repression becomes cheaper.

Weapons design may become cheaper.

The machine that helps a citizen understand the law can help an authoritarian state discover which citizens resist it.

The system that personalizes education can personalize political persuasion.

The agent that navigates bureaucracy can automate denial.

The model that detects corruption can be pointed at dissidents instead.

The danger is not that AI has values. The danger is that it makes existing values more operational.

A benevolent institution becomes more capable of benevolence.

A predatory institution becomes more capable of predation.

This is why capability alone cannot be our progress metric.

Society often treats speed as progress.

Faster discovery.

Faster decisions.

Faster production.

Faster response.

But speed amplifies direction.

A car driving toward a hospital should accelerate.

A car driving toward a cliff should brake.

The defining governance task of the AI era is therefore directional: which objectives are allowed to scale?

This is more difficult than writing a list of banned uses. Objectives hide inside systems.

An engagement algorithm may not be designed to polarize, yet polarization may improve engagement.

A fraud detector may not be designed to discriminate, yet historical patterns may encode discrimination.

A productivity system may not be designed to degrade work, yet management may use it to intensify workloads.

An automated benefits system may not be designed to exclude people, yet every additional documentation requirement may exclude the vulnerable.

We need to govern incentives, not merely intentions.

Before deploying powerful AI into a social system, ask four questions:

**Who gains capability?**

Does the system empower the person with less power, or mostly strengthen the institution already holding it?

**Who bears mistakes?**

If the model fails, is the cost borne by a wealthy organization or by a person who may lose housing, liberty, health, or livelihood?

**Can the affected person contest the outcome?**

A system without appeal transforms error into authority.

**Does the deployment expand the commons or enclose it?**

Are improvements reusable and auditable, or does the system create deeper dependence on a private gatekeeper?

These questions do not eliminate risk. They expose its distribution.

One of the strongest cases for public-interest AI is defensive.

If malicious intelligence becomes cheap, ordinary people need protective intelligence too.

Every person may need a scam detector.

Every small business may need a cybersecurity assistant.

Every journalist may need synthetic-media verification tools.

Every citizen may need privacy agents that negotiate data permissions.

Every nonprofit may need legal and security guidance.

Every child may need systems that identify manipulative content.

Every local government may need tools to detect coordinated disinformation.

Security cannot remain a luxury service for institutions large enough to hire specialists.

The commons needs an immune system.

AI can help provide one.

The arms race is real. The humane response is not to pretend harmful capability will remain scarce. It is to ensure defensive capability becomes abundant faster.

There is a seductive error in conversations about AI and social good: the fantasy of the benevolent machine.

Build a sufficiently wise system, give it the right moral objective, and let it optimize the world.

This is the wrong dream.

The commons does not need a king, even a silicon king.

Human values are plural. Communities disagree about priorities, traditions, risk, justice, family, dignity, privacy, and the meaning of a good life. Many disagreements are not bugs waiting for technical resolution. They are politics.

A system that claims to optimize “global well-being” must choose a definition of well-being. The choice creates power.

Who decides whether a city should prioritize housing affordability or preservation?

Who decides whether a medical system should spend its next dollar on neonatal care or elder care?

Who decides how much privacy to trade for public safety?

Who decides whether educational excellence means standardized achievement, creativity, civic formation, vocational readiness, or some mixture?

There is no neutral objective function waiting to be discovered.

The triumph of the commons therefore requires not a benevolent superintelligence but a **pluralistic ecology of intelligences** operating under human rights, democratic processes, professional norms, markets, community governance, and open technical standards.

The goal is to distribute capability while constraining domination.

AI is most socially legitimate when it expands the quality of human choice.

It explains.

It models options.

It exposes tradeoffs.

It finds precedents.

It detects contradictions.

It proposes.

It translates.

It checks.

It remembers.

It can sometimes execute bounded tasks.

But the more a decision concerns contested values or irreversible human consequences, the stronger the case for preserving accountable human authority.

This is not because humans are magically wiser. Humans are biased, corruptible, inconsistent, and frequently incompetent.

It is because legitimate authority requires responsibility.

A model cannot be voted out.

A model cannot go to prison for corruption.

A model cannot apologize in a morally meaningful sense.

A model cannot represent a constituency.

A model cannot consent to bear the consequences of its decision.

We can delegate computation without delegating sovereignty.

As AI becomes embedded everywhere, people may need new rights.

The right to know when a consequential interaction is automated.

The right to meaningful explanation of automated decisions.

The right to appeal to a human in high-stakes settings.

The right to privacy from pervasive model inference.

The right to access public digital infrastructure without commercial profiling.

The right to use assistive intelligence without surrendering ownership of one’s thoughts, drafts, and personal data.

The right to remain unoptimized in intimate parts of life.

A child should be allowed to draw badly without the system correcting the drawing.

A couple should be allowed to have an inefficient conversation.

A neighborhood should be allowed to preserve a quirky tradition that no optimization model can justify.

A person should be allowed to waste time.

Human flourishing includes inefficiency.

The commons is not a machine for maximizing output.

It is the shared space in which human lives become possible.

AI should enrich that space, not consume it.

The easiest way to become naïve about beneficial AI is to stare only at beneficial use cases.

Every capability that makes help cheaper can make harm cheaper too.

Translation helps a refugee understand a form. It also helps a fraud ring target victims in their own language.

Code generation helps a nonprofit fix a broken website. It also lowers the skill required to probe insecure systems.

Synthetic media can make educational material accessible. It can also impersonate a family member.

Personalization can make public service humane. It can also make propaganda intimate.

Scientific assistance can search for neglected treatments. It can also help an irresponsible actor search dangerous design spaces.

The mirror is not an argument for paralysis. It is an argument against moral bookkeeping that credits the good and treats the bad as somebody else's problem.

The industrial age learned this slowly. A chemical process could produce fertilizer and explosives. A logistics network could deliver food and armies. Aviation connected continents and expanded warfare. Cryptography protects dissidents and criminals. General-purpose capability does not arrive pre-sorted by virtue.

AI compresses the interval between invention and dual use because the same interface can serve both.

This creates a distinctive problem for the commons. A shared capability can increase collective welfare while simultaneously expanding the attack surface of the society sharing it.

Open email was a commons of communication. Spam exploited the openness.

The web democratized publishing. Malware, scams, manipulation, and industrialized misinformation arrived through the same channels.

Open-source software makes powerful infrastructure inspectable and reusable. Attackers can inspect and reuse it too.

The lesson is not to close every commons. It is that successful commons acquire immune systems.

Email gained reputation systems, filtering, authentication protocols, rate limits, abuse teams, and legal enforcement. Software ecosystems gained package signing, vulnerability databases, dependency scanning, maintainers, incident response, and security norms. Financial networks developed identity controls, fraud models, insurance, reversibility, and specialized investigators.

None of these defenses made abuse disappear. They increased the cost of abuse and the resilience of legitimate use.

An intelligence commons needs the same maturation.

The first layer is provenance.

A machine-generated instruction that affects health, money, legal rights, or public safety should not float through the world as anonymous fluent text. The user should be able to tell what sources it relied on, what system produced it, when the relevant data was updated, and whether the output was generated under an official process or copied into a screenshot.

Provenance will not make falsehood impossible. People can forge labels. Systems can cite bad sources. But provenance changes investigation from archaeology into inspection.

The second layer is bounded authority.

A model that can advise is less dangerous than a model that can transact without limits. An agent that can draft a wire transfer is different from one that can execute it. A system that can identify a possible fraud case is different from one that can freeze a person's account.

Capability should expand more slowly than authority.

This principle is familiar in aviation. Autopilot is powerful because it operates inside a dense system of certification, redundancy, monitoring, procedures, and trained human responsibility. We would not celebrate an experimental flight model because it can control an aircraft in a demo and then silently give it authority over passenger jets.

Consumer software has often adopted the opposite culture: ship, observe, patch.

Some AI applications can tolerate that. High-consequence systems cannot.

The third layer is asymmetry in favor of defense.

If machine intelligence makes phishing ten times easier and fraud detection only ten percent better, society loses even if the defense improved.

We should therefore ask where public investment can create defensive abundance.

A small charity cannot employ a twenty-person security team. A shared agent can monitor configuration, patching, suspicious login patterns, and exposed secrets.

An elderly person cannot hire a forensic analyst before answering a call. A trusted device can detect likely impersonation attempts and create a safe verification step.

A local election office cannot maintain the same media-forensics capability as a national intelligence agency. Shared infrastructure can help authenticate official communications and rapidly investigate fabrications.

Defensive systems have a special claim on commons funding because their benefits spill across networks. One compromised organization can become the entry point to many others. Raising the security floor for the weak protects the strong too.

The fourth layer is friction.

Technology culture often treats friction as failure. In safety systems, friction can be the product.

A payment that takes one extra confirmation can prevent a scam.

A model that refuses to produce a dangerous operational sequence can slow misuse.

A high-impact government action that requires human approval can preserve accountability.

A delay before a new agent receives broad permissions can give monitoring time to learn its behavior.

The art is to place friction where the cost of a mistake is asymmetric.

Too much friction can exclude legitimate users and push them toward worse alternatives. Too little friction turns every convenience into an exploit surface.

The fifth layer is redress.

No defensive system will be perfect. That means the right question is not only "How accurate is it?" but "What happens when it is wrong?"

Fraud systems illustrate the problem. A false negative costs money. A false positive can lock an innocent person out of rent, food, or wages. If the system is optimized entirely around fraud loss, the innocent customer becomes a rounding error.

An altruistic intelligence system must count the burden of being wrongly classified.

Can the person appeal?

Can a human see the evidence?

Is there a deadline for resolution?

Does the system restore more than access when it caused downstream harm?

Can repeated false positives change the model or policy?

Redress is often dismissed as administrative overhead. In a machine-mediated society it becomes part of the safety architecture.

There is also a deeper threat: benevolence itself can become a justification for control.

A state can say that comprehensive data collection allows it to target benefits more efficiently.

An employer can say that emotional monitoring helps prevent burnout.

A school can say that pervasive behavioral tracking allows personalized education.

A platform can say that suppressing uncertain information protects users from misinformation.

Each claim can contain a truth. Each can also enlarge an institution's power over people who have less ability to refuse.

Good intentions do not neutralize power.

This is why the strongest version of the commons is not paternalistic. It increases people's capability to act, understand, verify, and appeal. It does not merely optimize them toward a centrally selected definition of flourishing.

A public tutor should help a student learn, not build a permanent behavioral dossier.

A health navigator should help a patient reach care, not quietly become an insurance risk score.

A civic assistant should explain policy, not steer political opinion under the cover of service.

A benefits system should detect abuse, not make poverty synonymous with continuous suspicion.

The distinction can be hard to preserve because the same data often improves both service and control.

The humane response is not to pretend the tradeoff disappears. It is to design data boundaries as seriously as model capability.

Collect less.

Keep more local.

Separate datasets whose combination would create unnecessary power.

Expire data.

Make access visible.

Restrict secondary use.

Allow essential service without maximal personalization.

These are old privacy principles. AI makes them more urgent because inference can extract more from the same record.

The mirror also reaches the book's central claim about altruism.

If helping becomes cheap, fake helping becomes cheap too.

Scammers can create synthetic charities.

Propagandists can simulate grassroots movements.

Companies can wrap extractive products in personalized language of care.

Political systems can manufacture the appearance of consensus.

The moral vocabulary of generosity can itself become an attack surface.

That means contribution needs verification.

Not every claimed public good is one.

A system that reports "lives improved" should expose enough method to test the claim.

An organization that receives subsidized compute for a public purpose should be accountable for use.

A contribution ledger should distinguish a reusable asset from publicity about the asset.

Outcomes should be audited where the stakes justify it.

This is uncomfortable because generosity has traditionally received some protection from suspicion. We do not interrogate every volunteer as if he were laundering a reputation.

At machine scale, we will have to become more discerning without becoming cynical.

The right question is not "Can this be abused?" Almost everything can.

The better questions are: How easily? At what scale? Who detects it? Who pays when detection fails? Can the system recover? Does defense remain available to the weak?

The same machine can accelerate cruelty.

So the architecture of scalable altruism has to accelerate resilience too.

A civilization that makes power abundant without making defense abundant has not built a commons.

It has built a target.

Cybersecurity provides a preview of what defensive abundance could look like because the asymmetry between large and small institutions is already extreme.

A major bank can employ specialized teams, monitor networks continuously, purchase threat intelligence, rehearse incidents, and negotiate with vendors.

A small nonprofit may have one person who resets passwords when something breaks.

Both connect to the same internet.

The weak organization's insecurity is not isolated. Its accounts can be used to attack donors, partners, clients, or larger networks. One compromised supplier can become the route into a much more valuable target.

Security therefore has commons properties.

Raising the floor protects the network.

AI can help by packaging expert routines into shared services: configuration review, patch prioritization, suspicious-email analysis, backup checks, incident playbooks, and plain-language explanations of what happened.

The crucial phrase is "help by."

A defensive agent with broad system access is itself a security risk. It can be manipulated, overprivileged, or wrong. The defense must follow the same bounded-authority rules as the system it protects.

This is why public defensive infrastructure may be a better first investment than public offensive capability.

Defense is easier to justify as a spillover good.

A protected clinic protects patients.

A protected school protects families.

A protected municipality protects tax records and public operations.

The contribution compounds outward.

The same principle can apply to media authentication and fraud prevention. If only wealthy people and corporations can afford strong verification tools, synthetic deception becomes another tax on vulnerability.

Defensive intelligence should be boring, widely available, and harder to turn off than the attacker's latest trick.

The cruelty mirror also includes simple neglect.

A high-performing system deployed only for profitable users can widen inequality without committing any dramatic abuse.

The affluent receive tutors that adapt continuously.

Poor schools receive static software.

Large firms receive defensive agents.

Small organizations remain exposed.

Major languages receive excellent models.

Minority languages receive brittle ones.

Urban hospitals receive clinical copilots.

Rural clinics receive generic chat.

This is not malevolent AI.

It is ordinary market segmentation applied to a technology whose capabilities increasingly mediate participation.

The public-policy response should not be to forbid premium service.

It should be to define a floor where exclusion creates large social harm.

Broadband policy offers a warning. Once schools, businesses, and government reorganize around connectivity, lack of access becomes more damaging than it was before the connected systems existed.

Intelligence can create the same ratchet.

The better the privileged service becomes, the more institutions will assume that everyone has equivalent help.

Forms become more complex because "the assistant can handle it."

Homework becomes more personalized because "the tutor can explain it."

Professional expectations rise because "the agent can draft it."

People without access face not the old baseline but a new world designed around tools they do not have.

The cruelty of abundance can be architectural.

That is why the commons floor must arrive early.

The most important defensive commons may be the least glamorous one: a shared capacity to recognize manipulation before it becomes profitable at population scale.

Fraud has always exploited asymmetry. The scammer can specialize in one story while the recipient encounters it for the first time. Cheap generative systems can deepen that asymmetry by producing more variants, in more languages, with better personalization and better timing. The obvious response is to give every institution a private fraud detector. The better response, where law and privacy allow it, is to let detection improve collectively without requiring every victim to rediscover the same pattern.

Imagine a public-interest clearinghouse that receives carefully minimized reports of new scam structures from banks, consumer-protection offices, messaging services, local governments, and civil-society groups. It does not need the entire private conversation. It needs enough pattern information to identify what is changing: a new impersonation technique, a new payment route, a new forged-document template, a new way of pressuring elderly users, a new multilingual script. Defensive models can then be tested against those patterns and the resulting safeguards distributed widely.

The asymmetry changes. A novel attack may still work once. It should not have to work ten thousand times before the defense learns.

This is also where provenance stops being a philosophical nicety. In an environment filled with plausible synthetic material, institutions need cheap ways to answer humble questions. Did this notice actually come from the tax authority? Was this video published by the emergency-management office? Is this payment request associated with the hospital the message claims to represent? No universal authenticity system will solve every deception problem, and authentication itself can become a tool of exclusion. But reliable channels, signed official communications, transparent provenance, and easy verification can make whole classes of fraud more expensive.

The danger is that defensive infrastructure quietly becomes surveillance infrastructure. A system designed to detect coordinated abuse can be repurposed to detect coordinated dissent. A provenance requirement meant for official notices can creep into ordinary speech. A model that protects vulnerable people from manipulation can be trained to classify unconventional behavior as suspicious. The defense must therefore be bounded by the same rights it is meant to protect: data minimization, explicit purpose, independent audit, expiration, appeal, and the ability to communicate privately without being treated as a threat.

There is a useful test here. A defensive commons should make it easier for a person to prove that an institution is real without making it necessary for the person to prove that every private thought is legitimate.

That distinction is thin enough to lose and important enough to design around. The machine that can accelerate cruelty will not be neutralized by asking it to be kind. It will be constrained when defenders can learn together, when institutions authenticate themselves, when victims have somewhere to appeal, and when the infrastructure of safety is broadly available rather than sold only to whoever can afford the premium tier.

Risk management offers a useful counterweight to the temptation to describe benevolence as a model personality. NIST's AI Risk Management Framework, for example, treats trustworthiness as a collection of properties and practices rather than a single promise. Reliability, safety, security, resilience, accountability, transparency, explainability, privacy, and fairness can conflict with one another in a real deployment. The framework's practical value is precisely that it forces an organization to ask who governs the system, how risks are measured, what is mapped before deployment, and how problems are managed afterward.

A commons needs the same operational sobriety. “Helpful” cannot be a substitute for a threat model. A public agent that is exceptionally willing to comply with a user may be dangerously helpful to a fraudster. A medical system that gives confident answers quickly may be less useful than one that knows when to stop. A disaster agent that shares information freely may violate privacy at exactly the moment people are most vulnerable.

This does not mean public systems should become timid. It means they should distinguish reversible help from irreversible authority. Summarizing a form is not the same as denying a benefit. Suggesting a route is not the same as dispatching an ambulance. Drafting a letter is not the same as filing it under somebody's legal identity. The more difficult an action is to undo, the stronger the requirements for authorization, evidence, review, and logging should become.

That gradient is more practical than a universal demand for “human in the loop.” Humans can become decorative approvers who click through machine output too quickly to add judgment. A serious control specifies which human, with what information, holding what authority, at which moment, and with enough time to intervene.

Cruelty at machine scale often begins with an unexamined shortcut. So does safety. The difference is whether the institution has built a place where the shortcut can be seen.
