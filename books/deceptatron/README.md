*Propaganda in the 2030s*

Propaganda used to have a production problem.

A state, party, company, movement, intelligence service, cult, pressure group, or ambitious individual had to make a message, choose an audience, buy or seize distribution, repeat the message often enough to matter, and hope that the same words could move millions of different minds.

The internet weakened the distribution constraint. Social platforms made targeting, testing, automation, fake consensus, and rapid amplification ordinary features of political communication. The first great era of computational propaganda was therefore an era of scale: more accounts, more posts, more segments, more channels, more measurement.

The 2030s introduce a more unsettling possibility.

The message itself can become adaptive.

A generative system can produce a different argument for every person. A persistent model can infer which version of an issue is likely to matter to a particular target. A synthetic speaker can be made familiar, local, expert, intimate, angry, funny, cautious, wounded, or reassuring. A feed can test not only which headline earns a click but which sequence of facts, omissions, images, voices, and social signals changes a belief over days or months. An AI assistant can become a new gate between a person and the public world, summarizing events, choosing sources, answering objections, remembering preferences, and deciding what deserves attention.

The twentieth-century propagandist wanted the perfect slogan.

The propagandist of the 2030s may want the perfect model of **you**.

This book calls the resulting system a **Deceptatron**: not a single supercomputer and not a science-fiction mind-control ray, but a closed-loop persuasion stack that can observe a person, model them, generate an intervention, deliver it through a trusted channel, measure the response, and adapt the next intervention. The machine does not need to know whether a claim is true. It needs to know whether the claim, framing, omission, messenger, timing, or sequence moves the target toward an objective.

That distinction is the center of the book.

Propaganda in the 2030s is not primarily a content problem. It is a **control problem**.

A false video matters. A fabricated photograph matters. A synthetic voice matters. But a society can learn to distrust individual artifacts and still remain vulnerable to a system that selects from true facts, half-truths, genuine grievances, selective context, emotional timing, real witnesses, fake witnesses, and personalized explanations until each person receives a world optimized to produce a response.

The most effective future propaganda may contain surprisingly few literal lies.

It may simply become very good at deciding which truths never meet.

## Working thesis

Most familiar accounts of propaganda assume a sender, a message, an audience, and a channel. The sender may lie or tell selective truths. The channel may be a poster, newspaper, radio station, television network, rally, schoolbook, bot network, influencer account, or targeted advertisement. The audience receives the message and responds.

Digital platforms complicated that model by making distribution algorithmic and feedback measurable. The same campaign could test variants, segment audiences, buy precision, exploit recommendation systems, manufacture apparent consensus, and use automation to flood a public space. Researchers described much of this territory as computational propaganda.

Generative AI, persistent behavioral models, synthetic media, cheap agents, and conversational interfaces create a further shift. The unit of propaganda can move from **message** to **relationship**.

The persuader no longer has to select one message for a segment. A system can maintain an evolving representation of the target and generate the next move in context. It can remember what did not work. It can change messengers without changing objectives. It can retreat from a claim that creates resistance, approach the same objective through a different grievance, and wait for a moment when the person is more receptive. It can make the persuasion look less like persuasion by embedding it inside assistance, entertainment, companionship, search, customer service, education, or ordinary conversation.

That does not mean such systems will always work. Human beings are not deterministic machines, persuasion effects are often small and unstable, personal data can be wrong, and attempts to manipulate can backfire. The book does not assume an omnipotent operator.

It argues something more modest and, for that reason, more plausible:

> **When persuasion becomes cheap to generate, cheap to personalize, cheap to test, and cheap to repeat, the scarce resource is no longer the message. It is a sufficiently accurate model of the person and a sufficiently trusted path into that person's attention.**

The political consequences follow from that change in economics.

## The book's lane

*Deceptatron* is not a general book about misinformation, deepfakes, social media, election security, or artificial intelligence. Those subjects appear because they are parts of the machinery, but they are not the organizing idea.

The book is about the transition from **broadcast propaganda** to **adaptive propaganda**.

Broadcast propaganda asks: *What message should we send?*

Computational propaganda asks: *How do we distribute, amplify, target, and automate it?*

Adaptive propaganda asks: *What should this person encounter next, given everything the system currently believes about them and everything it has observed about their reaction so far?*

That last question turns persuasion into a feedback loop.

The loop can be commercial. It can sell a product, a financial asset, a subscription, a grievance, or a lifestyle. It can be political. It can recruit, demobilize, radicalize, discourage, distract, exhaust, or simply make collective action feel pointless. It can be geopolitical. It can be used to fracture an adversary's public sphere without persuading anyone to admire the adversary. It can be institutional. A company, agency, movement, or platform can use adaptive communication to reduce resistance to decisions that would look different under a common description.

The technology does not decide the objective.

It changes the cost of pursuing it.

## Prior art and novelty discipline

The book does not claim that propaganda, personalization, computational persuasion, recommender systems, psychographic targeting, influence operations, synthetic media, or conversational persuasion are new.

They are not.

Propaganda studies are more than a century old as a modern field. Advertising and political consulting have long segmented audiences. Social platforms made behavioral targeting and algorithmic distribution routine. Oxford researchers have studied computational propaganda since the 2010s. Security teams have documented state-linked and commercial influence operations using generative AI. Experimental work published in 2025 and 2026 has shown that large language models can produce politically persuasive messages and, in controlled settings, can become more persuasive when given personal information or when allowed to sustain a dialogue.

The original contribution of *Deceptatron* is the synthesis:

> **The next important unit of propaganda is not the post, ad, video, bot, or fake account. It is the adaptive persuasion loop connecting a model of the target to a generator, a delivery surface, behavioral feedback, and another intervention.**

That framing changes the defensive question.

If the threat is a bad artifact, detect or label the artifact.

If the threat is an adaptive loop, the defense must also govern access to personal models, repeated experimentation on individuals, synthetic social proof, persuasive delegation, provenance, rate, timing, and the relationship between an assistant and the interests of the person it serves.

## The reversal

The popular fear about AI propaganda is that nobody will know what is real.

That fear is justified but incomplete.

A more advanced propaganda system does not need to make reality completely unknowable. In many cases that would be counterproductive. People cannot be guided very effectively through a world they regard as random noise.

The better system gives the target enough truth to remain oriented and enough selection to remain steerable.

It may show real crimes committed by one side and omit real crimes committed by another. It may use authentic economic data but choose the comparison year that creates the desired emotional conclusion. It may quote a real expert, strip away the caveat, then surround the quotation with a synthetic chorus of apparently independent agreement. It may give one person a moral argument, another an economic argument, another a security argument, and a fourth no argument at all—only a story about what people like them supposedly already believe.

The lie becomes optional.

The optimization does not.

That is the reversal at the center of the book.

The great propaganda machine of the 2030s may not be the machine that can fabricate anything.

It may be the machine that learns which fragment of reality is enough.
