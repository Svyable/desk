# The Counterfactual Feed

At any moment, there are more true things happening in the world than any person can know.

This is not a technology problem.

It is a condition of being human.

A city contains thousands of arguments, accidents, births, crimes, repairs, meetings, illnesses, deals, firings, friendships, rescues, scams, small acts of competence, and small acts of cruelty before lunch. A nation contains more. The planet produces an impossible volume of potentially relevant reality.

Every information system therefore selects.

A newspaper decides what reaches page one. A television producer chooses which footage opens the broadcast. A teacher chooses which wars fit in a semester. A friend chooses which story from the weekend to tell. A historian decides what belongs in the chapter and what becomes a footnote.

Selection is not evidence of manipulation.

It is unavoidable.

The political problem begins when the selection process is optimized against the individual in ways the individual cannot see.

That is the counterfactual feed.

The phrase is useful because every feed is one path through a much larger set of possible feeds.

You saw this sequence.

You could have seen another.

The difference may have been produced by chronology, editorial judgment, subscription, chance, recommendation, advertising, engagement prediction, or deliberate influence. Usually it is a mixture.

A counterfactual feed is the invisible comparison between the world you were shown and the other plausible worlds assembled from material that also existed.

This is where propaganda can become powerful without fabricating anything.

Imagine two citizens living in the same city for one month.

The first sees sixteen stories about violent crime, six clips of shoplifting, four interviews with frightened shop owners, three stories about police staffing, and a video of a prosecutor being heckled.

The second sees eighteen stories about wage theft, five clips about unsafe working conditions, four interviews with tenants facing eviction, three stories about hospital debt, and a video of a landlord being confronted by residents.

Every story is real.

Every video is authentic.

Every interview is correctly attributed.

At the end of the month, the two citizens may hold radically different intuitions about what kind of city they inhabit.

Neither has been lied to in the narrow sense.

Both have been shown a sample.

Sampling is political because people infer frequency from availability. Events that come easily to mind can feel more common, more representative, and more urgent. The effect is not mechanical. People bring experience, ideology, and skepticism. Still, repeated exposure changes the material available for judgment.

News editors have always known this.

Propagandists have always exploited it.

The 2030s difference is that the sample can be individualized and continuously optimized.

A mass propaganda system has to choose which danger the nation should fear.

An adaptive system can choose which danger **you** should fear.

This creates a new relationship between truth and deception.

The most effective liar in a high-information environment may be the curator.

If enough true material exists to support many emotional conclusions, a system can construct a persuasive world by selecting rather than inventing.

This is not the same as saying all narratives are equally valid.

Some samples are plainly more representative than others. Some claims have better evidence. Some events are more consequential. A newsroom that publishes only shark attacks would distort public understanding even if every shark attack occurred. A government that publicizes every crime committed by an out-group and suppresses crimes committed by an in-group is propagandizing through selection.

The difficulty is measurement.

What is the correct denominator for a feed?

How many stories about crime should a person see?

How many about economic growth?

How much attention should a war receive relative to domestic policy, science, corruption, climate, health, culture, or ordinary competence?

There is no neutral mathematical answer.

That is why the counterfactual-feed problem cannot be solved by requiring every algorithm to be “objective.”

The deeper requirement is contestability.

A person should be able to understand the main forces shaping selection, change them, compare alternatives, and escape optimization loops that have become self-reinforcing.

Self-reinforcement is the central risk.

Suppose a feed notices that you reliably engage with stories about institutional corruption.

It shows more.

You click more because the stories are now a larger share of what you see.

The system becomes more confident that corruption is your interest.

You begin to believe corruption is everywhere because evidence of corruption is everywhere in your feed.

That belief makes you engage even more intensely.

The system has not inferred a preference from a stable self.

It has participated in creating the preference it measures.

This is the feedback problem again.

Prediction and intervention become entangled.

A recommendation system predicts what you will watch, then changes what is available to watch. Your response becomes training data for the next prediction. Over time, the model can help manufacture the behavior it interprets as evidence about you.

This does not mean recommendation systems inevitably radicalize everyone. Causal research on algorithmic feeds has produced more complicated results than the popular story suggests. Large experiments associated with Facebook and Instagram have found that changing feed ranking can substantially change exposure and platform behavior while producing smaller, mixed, or difficult-to-detect changes in political attitudes and polarization over the study periods.

That is important counter-evidence.

A feed is not a remote control for ideology.

People carry politics from family, community, religion, workplace, region, education, and offline experience. They choose sources. They seek congenial information. They encounter politics elsewhere. Some of the apparent relationship between algorithmic media use and extremity reflects the people who choose that media in the first place.

The Deceptatron thesis does not require a recommender to manufacture ideology from nothing.

It requires the recommender to shape exposure enough that an adaptive persuader can work with the resulting environment.

Agenda setting may be easier than conversion.

The feed does not need to make you believe the conclusion.

It can decide which question you spend the week answering.

That distinction deserves more attention.

Political power often lies upstream of opinion.

Before people decide what they think, they decide—usually without noticing—which subjects deserve thought.

A scandal can dominate an election even if voters disagree about its meaning. A policy can disappear because nobody is talking about it. A conflict can feel urgent because it is continuously visible. A humanitarian crisis can become politically irrelevant because it is visually absent.

The counterfactual feed is therefore an agenda machine.

In the 2030s, the agenda may become personal.

One voter experiences the election as an argument about immigration.

Another experiences it as an argument about health care.

Another sees corruption.

Another sees climate.

Another sees culture.

Another sees almost no politics at all, because the system has learned that political content causes disengagement.

The campaign does not need to persuade everybody on one issue if it can determine which issue frames the choice for each person.

This can produce a strange public sphere.

Millions of people participate in the same election without experiencing the same campaign.

They vote on the same candidates but answer different questions.

Democracy has always contained this plurality. Farmers and bankers do not experience the same economy. A parent and a childless retiree can weigh school policy differently. Regional media produce different agendas. Ethnic and religious communities have their own information networks.

The danger is not difference.

The danger is **unobserved personalization of difference**.

A plural public sphere can still argue with itself because groups can see one another's claims.

An individualized public sphere makes comparison harder.

Your political opponent may appear irrational because you do not know what evidence has been made salient in their environment.

They may think the same of you.

Each person sees the other's conclusion without seeing the other's feed.

That gap is fertile ground for propaganda.

An operator does not need to persuade two groups to agree.

It can show each group a version of the other that confirms contempt.

To one side, surface the most offensive protest signs.

To the other, surface the most callous comments.

Do not fabricate anything.

Select the tail of each distribution and present it as the center.

The groups become mirrors held at bad angles.

This tactic is old. Political movements have always highlighted the worst examples of their opponents. What changes is the ability to personalize which worst example will be most activating for each viewer.

A religious conservative receives an anti-religious clip.

A civil libertarian receives an authoritarian clip.

A parent receives a school controversy.

A business owner receives a threat to property.

A young renter receives a clip of a wealthy homeowner dismissing housing costs.

The opponent becomes whatever version is easiest for the target to hate.

That is a form of synthetic identity even when every individual opponent shown is real.

The system constructs an operational model of the other side from selected evidence.

The feed can do the same thing with institutions.

A police department can be represented by its worst officer.

A university by its most absurd seminar.

A corporation by its most exploitative decision.

A union by its most corrupt leader.

A religion by its most extreme adherent.

A city by its most violent block.

A country by its most embarrassing politician.

The target does not need false information to acquire a false model.

This is why media literacy framed as “check whether the story is true” will become increasingly inadequate.

The citizen must also ask: **Why this story? Why now? Compared with what? How representative is it? What am I not seeing?**

Those are harder questions because they require a view of the counterfactual.

Platforms possess some of that view.

They know which stories could have been shown and were not. They know the ranking scores. They know the ad auctions. They know which content categories dominate a person's exposure. They know which interventions changed distribution.

Users see the output.

This asymmetry should become a major governance question.

Algorithmic transparency is often discussed as though publishing code would solve it. It would not. Modern ranking systems are complex, dynamic, and dependent on data. A source-code dump would be useless to almost everyone and could create security and gaming risks.

The more meaningful transparency is experiential.

What forces shaped my feed?

How much came from accounts I chose versus recommendations?

How much was paid?

How much was political?

Which topics were overrepresented relative to my subscriptions?

What changed after I engaged with one story?

Can I reset the learned model?

Can I compare a chronological feed, a subscription-only feed, and a recommendation feed?

Can researchers audit aggregate patterns without receiving private dossiers about individuals?

Those are institutional design questions, not code disclosure questions.

The counterfactual-feed idea also applies to AI assistants.

A user asks, “What happened in Congress today?”

The assistant cannot return everything.

It selects.

A user asks, “What are the strongest arguments against this bill?”

It selects.

A user asks whether a controversy matters.

It selects a frame for importance.

If the assistant has personal memory, it may tailor the answer to the user's background and stated interests.

This can be excellent assistance. A physician might want health policy detail. A small-business owner may care about compliance. A local resident may need the regional effect.

The risk appears when personalization quietly becomes political steering.

An assistant can construct a counterfactual feed in a single paragraph.

It can decide which facts make the summary.

The user may never know what an alternative answer would have contained.

This makes pluralism at the model level important.

A society in which one or two assistants mediate most information encounters concentrates agenda-setting power even if the assistants never produce explicit propaganda. Small defaults become enormous social forces at scale.

Which sources are considered reputable?

Which events count as major?

How much context is enough?

When is a claim described as disputed?

Which uncertainty is foregrounded?

These choices cannot be eliminated.

They can be made contestable.

The public needs ways to compare systems, inspect sources, request alternative framings, and understand when personalization affected the answer.

One of the most important rights of the 2030s may be the right to ask the machine for a **different plausible feed**.

Not because every perspective is equally true.

Because the only way to understand selection is to see that selection occurred.

Imagine a button labeled: “Show me what I would have seen if you knew nothing about me.”

Or: “Show me the strongest evidence this summary omitted.”

Or: “Show me how sources with different editorial judgments covered this event.”

Or: “Reset political personalization for thirty days.”

These features would not create neutrality.

They would expose contingency.

That matters because propaganda benefits when the selected world feels like the world itself.

The counterfactual feed breaks the illusion.

It reminds the user that information arrived through a system making choices.

The strongest propaganda machine of the 2030s may never tell you that two plus two equals five.

It may show you ten thousand true things that make four feel irrelevant.
