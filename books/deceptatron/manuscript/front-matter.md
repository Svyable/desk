# Deceptatron: Propaganda in the 2030s

**Sven Hardy Benson**

*Propaganda in the 2030s*

© 2026 Sven Hardy Benson. All Rights Reserved.

This is a working manuscript on Svyable Desk.

The book examines propaganda, persuasion, influence operations, synthetic media, recommender systems, generative artificial intelligence, personal modeling, political communication, and possible institutional responses. It is descriptive and defensive in purpose. It does not provide an operational manual for covert manipulation, evasion, harassment, or political interference.

The examples in the book distinguish documented historical or contemporary cases from scenarios used to reason about the 2030s. Future-facing scenes are illustrations, not reports of events that have already happened.

A research trail accompanies the manuscript. Concrete empirical claims should be read with the limits of their sources in mind. Persuasion is difficult to measure, effects vary by context, and laboratory findings do not automatically predict population-scale outcomes.

There is a reason to begin with those cautions.

A book about propaganda can easily become propaganda about propaganda.

The subject rewards drama. It invites a writer to describe an all-seeing system, an obedient population, a mastermind with infinite data, a perfectly timed message, and a democracy one clever model away from collapse. That version is cinematic and usually wrong.

People are stubborn. They misunderstand messages. They resist pressure. They hold contradictory beliefs. They change their minds for reasons nobody measured. Political campaigns waste fortunes. Advertisers misread their customers. recommendation systems annoy the people they are supposed to know. Intelligence services mistake noise for signal. A model can infer the wrong person with exquisite confidence.

None of that makes the underlying change unimportant.

The danger of the next propaganda era does not depend on perfect persuasion.

It depends on the economics of repeated attempts.

For most of modern history, a propagandist had to compress. A poster had one image. A radio address had one voice. A newspaper had one edition. A television advertisement had one cut, or perhaps a handful. Even when campaigns segmented by geography, class, language, party, or demographic group, the basic artifact was expensive enough that the sender had to decide what many people would see in common.

The internet reduced the cost of distribution and multiplied the number of versions. Social platforms added measurement. Behavioral advertising added targeting. Recommendation systems added individualized ordering. Automation added volume. Synthetic media began lowering the cost of making the artifact itself.

Generative systems change the shape of the problem because they lower the cost of variation toward zero.

The system does not need one perfect argument.

It can afford ten thousand mediocre ones and keep the few that work.

It does not need one convincing persona.

It can generate a thousand voices and discover which one a particular person treats as familiar.

It does not need to decide whether fear, pride, resentment, humor, belonging, disgust, fairness, nostalgia, status, or simple convenience will move the audience as a whole.

It can test.

And if it is connected to enough behavioral data, it can test in context.

That is the threshold this book is interested in.

The old propaganda problem was largely one of **manufacture and distribution**.

The emerging propaganda problem is one of **feedback**.

A feedback system observes the result of an action and uses the result to change the next action. Thermostats do it. Recommendation systems do it. Ad auctions do it. Industrial control systems do it. Machine-learning systems are built around it.

Persuasion becomes different when the same logic is applied to the relationship between a message and a person.

Suppose a system knows almost nothing about you. It shows three versions of the same political claim. You ignore two and linger on the third. That is already information.

Suppose it knows your approximate age, location, profession, media habits, purchase history, social graph, and the issues that reliably keep your attention. It can begin with a stronger prior.

Suppose it has a persistent conversational history and knows which objections you have raised before. It can avoid repeating failed arguments.

Suppose it can choose among text, audio, video, memes, charts, eyewitness accounts, expert summaries, friends-of-friends, synthetic personalities, and apparently neutral assistants.

Suppose it can observe whether you clicked, shared, searched, asked a follow-up question, donated, stayed home, became angry, became uncertain, or simply spent twenty more minutes inside a particular information environment.

No one of those capabilities is mind control.

Together they form something closer to an adaptive instrument.

The title *Deceptatron* is deliberately a little ridiculous.

Propaganda machines deserve less mystique than we give them. The people who build them are not sorcerers. Their systems crash. Their data is messy. Their campaigns collide with other campaigns. Their objectives contradict one another. The absurdity in the name is a reminder that the machine can be both clumsy and dangerous.

It is also a reminder that deception is only one of its possible inputs.

A Deceptatron can work with lies, but it can also work with accurate facts arranged to produce a false picture. It can work with real grievances. It can work with selective memory. It can work with timing. It can work with the absence of a story. It can work by persuading one group that everybody agrees and another that nobody can be trusted. It can work by making a true event feel enormous or making an enormous event feel distant and technical.

The objective matters more than the truth value of any single sentence.

That is why fact-checking, while necessary, cannot be the entire defense.

If one person receives a stream of individually accurate stories about violent crime and another receives a stream of individually accurate stories about wage theft, both may end the month with distorted estimates of what kind of country they live in. The distortion can be produced without inventing a case. Selection alone can do a great deal.

The same is true of political opponents. A system can show only the most foolish supporter of one side and only the most dignified supporter of the other. Every clip can be authentic. The composite is still propaganda.

By the 2030s, the most consequential influence systems may be less interested in forging evidence than in controlling the sequence in which evidence becomes psychologically available.

Sequence is underrated.

A fact encountered before suspicion feels different from the same fact encountered after suspicion. A correction read before a rumor may inoculate. The same correction arriving after a person has repeated the rumor publicly may threaten identity. A statistic offered by a disliked institution may repel. The same statistic repeated by a trusted acquaintance may pass without resistance. A person who is tired, lonely, frightened, financially stressed, or furious may process a message differently than the same person on a quiet morning.

An adaptive system can wait.

That ability separates it from the poster on the wall.

The poster cannot notice that you looked away.

The radio cannot rewrite yesterday's speech because of your objection.

The television advertisement cannot remember that you were persuaded by the economic argument but irritated by the moral one.

A conversational system can.

An assistant can.

A feed already does, in a primitive way.

The 2030s do not need a technological miracle. They need the pieces to become cheaper, more integrated, and more persistent.

Those pieces are visible now.

Large language models can generate persuasive political messages. Controlled experiments have found cases where personalized model-based debate changes opinions more than human debate. Security teams have documented influence actors using generative systems for content production, translation, persona support, research, and engagement. Synthetic audio and video continue to improve. Content provenance standards are becoming more mature. Recommendation systems already choose which tiny slice of the world's content reaches an individual. AI assistants are becoming a normal interface through which people ask what happened, what matters, and what they should do next.

The uncertain part is not whether all of these technologies exist.

The uncertain part is how they will be combined, governed, resisted, normalized, and commercialized.

That is where the 2030s begin.

The central argument of the book is simple enough to state before the first chapter:

**Propaganda becomes a new kind of social infrastructure when the sender can continuously model the target, generate the next intervention, route it through a trusted interface, observe the response, and adapt faster than institutions can restore a common account of what happened.**

The first half of the book follows the machine as it gains those abilities.

The second half asks what a society can do once it understands that the problem is no longer only false content.

The answer will not be a Ministry of Truth. It will not be universal censorship. It will not be a browser badge that turns every disputed claim red. Those responses mistake epistemic authority for institutional resilience.

A free society has to preserve disagreement.

It also has to preserve enough shared reality for disagreement to mean something.

That is the harder design problem.

And it is the one the next decade is already handing us.
