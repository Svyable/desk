# Friction for Persuasion

Technology has spent half a century declaring war on friction.

One click.

Instant checkout.

Autoplay.

Infinite scroll.

Single sign-on.

One-tap sharing.

Same-day delivery.

Background synchronization.

Personalized defaults.

Ask once and the agent remembers.

Most of this is wonderful.

Friction is often waste wearing the costume of tradition. Nobody misses waiting in line to pay a utility bill. Nobody needs a travel agent to print an airline ticket. A person with a disability can gain enormous autonomy when an assistant removes steps. A small business can compete with a large one when software collapses administrative overhead.

But friction has never been uniformly bad.

A vault door is friction.

A signature is friction.

A second pair of eyes is friction.

A cooling-off period is friction.

A speed bump is friction.

A court warrant is friction.

A bank asking whether you really intended to wire your savings to a new account is friction.

Civilization is partly the art of deciding which actions should be easy and which should require a moment, a record, another person, or a cost.

Adaptive persuasion forces the same question onto information systems.

What should remain cheap?

Political argument should remain cheap.

Citizens should be able to criticize leaders, praise candidates, organize movements, publish satire, distribute pamphlets, create videos, run blogs, argue in group chats, and use AI to express their own political ideas. The fact that generation becomes easier is not itself a democratic problem.

What should become expensive is covert leverage.

That distinction is the policy center of the book.

Do not tax speech.

Tax the hidden machinery that turns private knowledge, fake identity, industrial experimentation, and trusted relationships into undisclosed political power.

This requires selective friction.

The first friction belongs around identity at scale.

An ordinary person should be able to speak pseudonymously. Pseudonymity protects dissidents, whistleblowers, abuse survivors, workers criticizing employers, minorities in hostile communities, and people who simply do not want every political opinion attached to a searchable legal identity.

Industrial persona creation is different.

A system that creates ten thousand apparently independent citizens is not exercising ten thousand people's speech rights.

It is one controller manufacturing social evidence.

Platforms can make that expensive without requiring every user to display a passport. Stronger verification can be triggered by behavior and scale: large political ad purchases, high-volume automated account creation, coordinated management of many political identities, or claims of official status.

The principle is proportional identity assurance.

The more power an account seeks to exercise over public distribution, the stronger the obligation to establish who controls it.

The second friction belongs around sponsorship.

A citizen should not need forensic skills to discover that an apparently independent campaign was funded by an interested party.

Disclosure rules already exist for many political advertisements. Adaptive systems make the old disclaimer insufficient because the persuasive artifact may be generated dynamically and embedded in conversation.

The disclosure has to follow the relationship.

Who is paying this agent?

Whose objective is it optimizing?

Is this answer sponsored?

Did sponsorship affect which source or recommendation appeared?

A tiny label flashed once at onboarding is not meaningful disclosure for a six-month political relationship.

Sponsorship should remain retrievable throughout the interaction.

The third friction belongs around sensitive personal context.

This is where privacy law and persuasion law begin to overlap.

A campaign knowing that a person lives in a district is different from a campaign knowing that the person spent the afternoon discussing a divorce with a private assistant. A public petition signature is different from an inferred mental-health crisis. A voter-registration record is different from a private conversation about debt.

The system may technically possess all of these signals.

Possession should not imply political usability.

Purpose limitation is friction.

Data gathered to provide health, financial, educational, intimate, or personal-assistant services should not quietly become a targeting input for third-party political persuasion.

This is true even if the raw data never leaves the provider.

The sponsor does not need the secret.

The sponsor needs the machine to exploit the secret.

A meaningful rule therefore governs **use**, not merely transfer.

The fourth friction belongs around experimentation.

Commercial platforms run experiments constantly. Experiments improve products. They reveal which interfaces confuse users and which features help. Political campaigns also test messages, and there is nothing inherently illegitimate about discovering which explanation voters understand.

The concern appears when high-scale systems conduct persistent, individualized persuasion experiments using private behavioral feedback without the target knowing that experimentation is occurring.

The ethical vocabulary here is underdeveloped.

We would recognize a problem if a university secretly randomized citizens into emotionally manipulative political interventions without consent. A commercial system can produce functionally similar variation as part of optimization and call it product behavior.

Not every ranking update is a human-subjects experiment. Not every message test requires informed consent. But the absence of a perfect category should not mean the absence of boundaries.

High-risk political experimentation can carry recordkeeping duties.

What objective was optimized?

What target population was included?

Which sensitive features were available?

What outcomes were measured?

How long did the intervention persist?

Was the system designed to reduce turnout, increase distrust, or exploit moments of vulnerability?

These records need not be public at the individual level. They should exist for audit.

The fifth friction belongs around rate.

Scale changes the character of conduct.

One person sending one political message is ordinary speech. One controller sending ten million personalized messages through synthetic identities is infrastructure.

Law already understands thresholds. Financial reporting changes at scale. Environmental permits change with emissions. campaign disclosures change with spending. telecom rules distinguish carriers from callers. Online systems can distinguish ordinary participation from industrial automation.

Rate limits are crude, but the principle is useful: the ability to reach and adapt to millions of people should not be treated as merely millions of isolated acts.

Scale creates externalities.

The sixth friction belongs around memory.

Persistent memory makes assistants useful. It also creates cumulative persuasive leverage.

Political targeting profiles should not become immortal by default.

Users need the ability to inspect, reset, correct, suspend, and expire political personalization. Systems should distinguish a temporary reaction from a durable preference. A person who explored a controversial issue once should not spend five years being treated as a member of an inferred political tribe.

Memory should have half-lives.

That does not mean every datum expires on the same schedule. A user may want an assistant to remember their district permanently. They may not want it to remember a moment of grief as a political targeting signal at all.

The important design idea is intentional persistence.

Storage is cheap. Forgetting therefore has to be designed.

The seventh friction belongs around delegation.

An assistant that can act for a user can create enormous convenience. It can register for events, purchase tickets, schedule appointments, draft messages, and eventually perform civic tasks.

Political action deserves deliberate boundaries.

An agent should not donate, endorse, sign a political petition, publish political speech in the user's name, or change political communication preferences merely because a persuasive conversation made the action seem like the next logical step. Consequential civic actions should require clear user authorization at the point of action.

This is not because politics is uniquely sacred.

It is because delegated political action can be mistaken for the user's own expression.

The machine should not manufacture consent through momentum.

The eighth friction belongs around high-trust assistants.

This may be the most important.

If an assistant represents itself as acting for the user, third parties should not be able to secretly rent its persuasive voice.

The user can choose a partisan assistant. A campaign can offer a campaign chatbot. A newspaper can offer an editorial assistant. A government can offer a public-service agent. Their institutional identity is visible.

A general personal assistant is different.

Its value comes from broad loyalty.

The user asks it about medicine, money, travel, relationships, work, news, and politics because the assistant is supposed to organize information around the user's interests.

Secretly inserting a sponsor's objective into that relationship is not ordinary advertising.

It is conflicted agency.

A loyalty rule would create friction at the most valuable point in the Deceptatron stack.

The ninth friction belongs around synthetic social proof.

Generated media labels are not enough if thousands of generated people can still appear to agree independently.

Platforms need ways to detect and disclose common control.

The relevant unit is not whether each post was generated. A human can operate a fake persona manually. An AI can help a real person write. The social question is whether apparent independent actors are actually coordinated by one controller.

This suggests a concept borrowed from finance: beneficial ownership.

Who ultimately controls the account network?

Political systems may need an analogous notion of **beneficial authorship**.

A thousand accounts can have a thousand names while one organization determines their strategy.

The public deserves to know when apparent plurality is centrally owned.

The tenth friction belongs around auditability.

A system that can influence millions should leave enough records that independent investigators can reconstruct what happened.

Not full surveillance logs of every citizen.

System records.

Campaign objectives.

Targeting categories.

Model versions.

Sponsor identities.

Automation levels.

Aggregate reach.

Major policy changes.

Retention rules.

Incident reports.

Without records, every scandal becomes archaeology.

The company says the behavior was accidental. The campaign says the contractor acted alone. The contractor says the model improvised. The model has no legal agency. The logs are gone.

Responsibility evaporates into architecture.

Audit trails keep responsibility attached to control.

Selective friction will attract predictable objections.

The first is innovation.

Won't these rules slow useful AI?

Some will.

That is what friction does.

The relevant comparison is not friction versus no friction. It is whether the social cost prevented by the friction justifies the burden. We already accept that payment systems should be slower when fraud signals appear, that medicine should be tested, and that large financial transfers should leave records.

The goal is to keep the burden away from ordinary low-risk use.

The second objection is incumbency.

Large companies can afford compliance; small organizations cannot.

This is real. Bad regulation can turn safety into a moat.

Thresholds therefore matter. A neighborhood campaign should not need the compliance department of a national platform. Duties can scale with reach, automation, sensitivity of data, and persistence of the persuasive relationship.

The third objection is political abuse.

A government could define “propaganda” to burden opponents.

Also real.

That is why the rules should target conduct that can be described without ideological judgment: undisclosed sponsorship, impersonation, unauthorized sensitive-data use, automated scale, false claims of independent identity, hidden third-party objectives inside user-serving agents, and failure to maintain required records.

The law should not need to decide whether the message is left-wing, right-wing, patriotic, subversive, wise, stupid, popular, or offensive.

The fourth objection is evasion.

Bad actors will route around the rules.

Of course.

Security is rarely about making abuse impossible. It is about raising cost, reducing scale, improving detection, and preserving accountability.

A burglar can bypass a lock. Locks still change burglary.

The fifth objection is jurisdiction.

A foreign operator may ignore domestic law.

That is why control points matter. Platforms, app stores, payment systems, cloud providers, advertising exchanges, telecom networks, and model providers sit at chokepoints even when the speaker is abroad. Democracies should use those chokepoints carefully because infrastructure power can become censorship power. But refusing to govern infrastructure does not create neutrality. It leaves the rules to private incentives and adversaries.

Friction also has a cultural dimension.

The Deceptatron benefits from our impatience.

We want immediate answers.

Immediate sharing.

Immediate outrage.

Immediate certainty.

Immediate action.

Some resilience comes from recovering the legitimacy of delay.

Read before reposting.

Check the source.

Wait for the count.

Ask whether the clip is representative.

Ask who paid.

Ask why the assistant thinks this matters to you.

These habits cannot carry the whole burden. “Media literacy” often becomes a way for institutions to offload systemic problems onto individuals. A citizen cannot personally audit a billion-parameter recommender or investigate every synthetic persona.

But individual friction complements institutional friction.

A good system makes the careful action easier.

It puts provenance one tap away.

It preserves the full clip.

It shows the sponsor.

It warns when a story is old.

It lets the user disable political personalization.

It makes “show me another framing” a normal command.

It separates an ad from advice.

It does not force the citizen to become a forensic analyst.

The design question is where to place the pause.

Too much friction can become paternalism.

A platform that interrupts every political post with warnings will train users to ignore warnings. A government that requires identity checks for ordinary speech chills dissent. An assistant that refuses every contested topic becomes useless. A provenance system that treats unsigned media as fake harms citizen journalism.

Selective friction has to remain selective.

This is why the Deceptatron architecture is helpful. It tells us where leverage concentrates.

Identity at industrial scale.

Sensitive context.

Persistent memory.

Paid objectives.

Synthetic consensus.

High-trust surfaces.

Automated distribution.

Behavioral feedback.

These are places where a small amount of friction can alter a large amount of influence.

The objective is not a slower internet.

It is a public sphere in which the easiest path is not always the path that hides the most power.

The twentieth-century propagandist paid for printing presses, airtime, organizers, and repetition.

The Deceptatron drives many of those costs toward zero.

A democratic response should not restore scarcity to expression.

It should restore scarcity to deception's unfair advantages.

Make the propagandist pay for the thing democracy actually needs to know.

Who are you?

Who paid you?

Whose interests does this agent serve?

What private knowledge did you use?

How many people did you pretend to be?

How long did you experiment on me?

What did the system remember?

And when the answer is “none of your business,” make that answer expensive.
