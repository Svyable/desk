# The Audience Becomes a Model

A census asks who you are in categories that can fit on a form.

A recommendation system can ask a stranger question.

Who are you likely to become five seconds from now?

Will you keep watching?

Will you click?

Will you skip?

Will you buy?

Will you argue?

Will you leave?

The difference between those questions explains why the audience of the 2030s will not merely be segmented more finely than the audience of the twentieth century.

It will be modeled differently.

Traditional audience categories usually had names. Age. Income. Neighborhood. Religion. Party. Occupation. Education. Language. Gender. Family status. Newspaper readership. Union membership. Veteran status. Home ownership. Race and ethnicity, often with ugly political purposes attached to the classification.

The categories could be crude, prejudiced, administratively convenient, or surprisingly useful. What mattered is that a human being could usually understand the category being invoked.

A campaign might decide that retirees worried about inflation should hear one argument and young parents another. A magazine could sell advertisers access to affluent golfers. A direct-mail list could identify people who had donated to a cause before. A pollster could divide the electorate by education and party identification.

The model existed in language.

Machine learning does not require that courtesy.

A predictive system can use thousands of variables and interactions to estimate a probability without producing a human-readable social type. It can represent a person as a vector of numbers whose dimensions have no natural translation into a sentence. It can cluster people because their behavior is mathematically similar even when no strategist would look at them and say they belong to the same constituency.

The audience becomes less like a list of groups and more like a field of estimates.

That is useful for commerce because prediction does not need biography.

A retailer does not need to understand the meaning of your life to estimate which product you are more likely to buy.

A streaming service does not need a theory of your identity to rank the next show.

A fraud system does not need to know why you are traveling to decide that a transaction looks unusual.

An advertiser does not need to know whether you are anxious in any deep psychological sense if a pattern of behavior predicts response to a message framed around security.

The model only has to be useful for the decision.

This distinction removes some of the mysticism from surveillance.

People often imagine the danger as a file containing the secret truth about them. Somewhere, in a server farm, a corporation or government has assembled the definitive dossier: beliefs, desires, weaknesses, relationships, health, money, fears, habits, private thoughts.

Real systems are usually more fragmented.

One company may have location history. Another has purchases. Another has browsing behavior. Another has a credit file. Another has an advertising identifier. A platform has engagement. A data broker has inferred household characteristics. A model provider may have conversational history. A retailer has returns. A mobile operating system has app activity. A political organization has a voter file enriched by commercial data.

The fragments do not have to become one perfect dossier to matter.

They have to become operational at the moment of decision.

That is the artificial audience.

The person does not appear to the system as a complete self.

They appear as whatever representation helps choose the next action.

This is where propaganda in the 2030s intersects with a broader transformation in identity. A machine-made model of a person can become consequential without being recognized by the person it represents. It can classify, rank, price, authenticate, recommend, or deny. In a persuasion system, it can decide which version of reality is most likely to hold attention or change behavior.

The model may be wrong.

Wrong models can still be profitable.

This sounds contradictory until one remembers that many decisions do not require certainty.

An advertisement can be shown to ten people if two of them are promising. A recommendation system can tolerate many misses if the average ranking improves. A campaign can waste messages on incorrectly inferred targets if the targeting still performs better than random distribution. A fraud model can accept false positives up to the point where customer pain becomes more expensive than fraud prevented.

The economics are statistical.

So is the politics.

A propaganda system does not need to know exactly what will persuade Sven, Maria, Jamal, Chen, or Amina. It may only need to know that one framing has a slightly higher predicted chance of movement than another.

At scale, slight advantages accumulate.

This is why arguments about whether personality can be inferred perfectly from digital traces often miss the operative threshold. Perfect psychological profiling would be frightening, but it is not required. A campaign already gains something if it can avoid sending the least effective message to the wrong people.

The difference between a five-percent and a six-percent response rate can be economically meaningful even though ninety-four percent of attempts still fail.

Propaganda can live in that gap.

The audience model becomes more powerful when it is dynamic.

A voter file is relatively static. A person's address may change. Party registration may change. voting history accumulates. Commercial enrichment adds variables. But much of the record describes a person as a collection of attributes.

A behavioral model can update after every interaction.

This afternoon you look different to the system than you did this morning.

You watched a clip about a strike.

You searched the employer's name.

You opened a local article.

You ignored a national commentator.

You asked an assistant whether the strike could affect deliveries in your city.

Each action can become evidence about what is salient now.

Salience is politically important because persuasion does not occur against a stable person standing outside time.

People move through problems.

A homeowner who never thought about flood insurance becomes highly attentive after water enters the basement. A parent who ignored school-board politics becomes engaged after a child is disciplined. A worker who rarely follows monetary policy begins reading about interest rates when a mortgage resets. A person who dismissed immigration as an abstract issue changes attention when a local employer closes or a family member waits for a visa.

A static profile says what kind of person this is.

A dynamic model asks what has become live.

The difference can make targeting feel like intuition.

The message arrives just when you were thinking about the issue.

Sometimes that timing is coincidence.

Sometimes it is advertising infrastructure.

Sometimes it is the simple fact that your own behavior revealed the concern before you recognized it as a political signal.

The 2030s add conversational data to this environment.

Search queries already reveal intention more directly than many passive traces. A person asking a question is telling the system what they do not know, what they are considering, or what has become important enough to articulate.

A persistent AI assistant can see more.

Not everything, and not necessarily in one centralized database. But a long-running conversation can reveal preferences, recurring worries, unresolved decisions, family context, work context, political assumptions, vocabulary, tolerance for detail, sources the person trusts, sources they dislike, and the kinds of explanations that produce follow-up questions.

That information is valuable because persuasion is partly about objection handling.

A generic advertisement guesses which objection is in the room.

A conversational system may have heard you state it.

If the assistant serves you, that can be useful. It can avoid wasting time on explanations you already understand. It can remember that you are vegetarian, that you hate red-eye flights, that your mother has limited mobility, that you prefer cheaper options, that you do not want meetings before nine.

Memory makes assistance better.

The same memory can make persuasion more precise.

This creates one of the most important institutional questions in the book: **whose objective is allowed to touch the model?**

If a personal assistant builds a rich representation in order to serve the user's stated interests, can an advertiser buy access to the representation indirectly by asking the assistant to recommend a product?

Can a political campaign pay to have an issue explained in a framing selected against the user's profile?

Can the assistant's operator tune responses to favor a commercial partner while still describing the result as personalized help?

Can a government compel access?

Can a malicious extension, connected service, or delegated agent use the memory for a purpose the user never expected?

The problem is larger than privacy because the dangerous event may occur without exporting the data.

A platform can keep the model secret and still use it to choose the intervention.

No dossier has to leave the building.

The targeting can come to the data.

This architecture is familiar in advertising. A company may allow advertisers to select audiences or optimization goals without revealing the identities or raw data of the people being targeted. From a privacy perspective, that can be better than handing over a spreadsheet of names and browsing histories.

From a persuasion perspective, the power remains.

The operator does not need to possess the model if the platform will optimize on the operator's behalf.

This is why transparency based only on data transfer is insufficient.

The key question is what decisions a model is permitted to influence.

A person might reasonably consent to an assistant using health context to remind them about an appointment and still reject a pharmaceutical advertiser using the same inferred concern to choose a persuasive message.

They might want a news service to remember which city they live in but not allow a political campaign to identify the local grievance most likely to move them.

They might welcome a shopping assistant that estimates price sensitivity and object to a retailer using the same estimate to raise the offered price.

The model is not inherently abusive.

The objective changes the ethics.

The Federal Trade Commission's work on surveillance pricing offers a useful commercial analogy. Its 2025 study described intermediaries using detailed behavioral and contextual information—including location, browsing patterns, shopping history, and even interactions on a webpage—to support individualized pricing. The point is not that every company does this or that political persuasion is identical to pricing.

The point is that behavioral observation can already be translated into individualized economic treatment.

Once institutions learn to price the person, they can also learn to frame for the person.

Those two practices may converge.

An insurance message can emphasize risk to a risk-sensitive customer. A financial product can be explained differently to a novice and a sophisticated investor. A public policy can be sold as freedom to one person and protection to another. A platform can rank the evidence differently for each.

Some of this is ordinary communication.

Good teachers personalize explanations. Doctors speak differently to different patients. Friends choose examples that will make sense to the person they know. A democracy would become worse, not better, if every political argument had to be expressed in identical language to everyone.

The problem is not personalization.

The problem is unaccountable optimization.

A human friend has limited memory, limited time, and a relationship that usually contains reciprocal obligations. A campaign model can personalize millions of interactions without friendship, reciprocity, or fatigue.

A teacher is accountable to educational norms. A doctor is bound by professional duties. A lawyer owes duties to a client. A financial adviser may have fiduciary obligations in some contexts.

What duty does an adaptive political model owe the target?

Usually none.

That asymmetry is the political vulnerability.

The person experiences individualized communication.

The system experiences an optimization problem.

The audience model also changes what secrecy means.

In old propaganda, secrecy often concerned sponsorship. Who paid for the pamphlet? Which government funded the newspaper? Which intelligence service ran the radio station? Which company commissioned the front group?

Sponsorship remains important, but adaptive systems introduce another hidden layer: **the reason you received this version**.

A disclosure can tell you who paid for a message and still leave the targeting logic invisible.

“Paid for by Citizens for a Better Tomorrow” tells you almost nothing about why the system chose a frightened parent as the messenger, why it used a local statistic, why it arrived after midnight, or why your neighbor received an entirely different appeal from the same sponsor.

That targeting explanation may be impossible to compress into a label.

It may also be essential to understanding the persuasion.

The same sentence means something different if it was shown to everyone than if it was shown only to people a system inferred were recently bereaved.

The ethics of the message can depend on the selection rule.

This is familiar in discrimination law. A neutral policy can have different significance depending on how it is applied. A price can look ordinary until one learns that a protected class received it systematically. A job advertisement can appear harmless until one learns who was excluded from seeing it.

Persuasion has a similar hidden structure.

The content is only the visible surface.

The audience model is the machinery underneath.

By the 2030s, that machinery may become more valuable than the media itself.

Synthetic video will commoditize. Text generation will be abundant. Voice cloning will spread. Images will be cheap. Anyone with access to capable models will be able to produce attractive persuasive material.

When content is abundant, advantage moves elsewhere.

Who has the best behavioral data?

Who controls the trusted interface?

Who can measure outcomes?

Who can maintain memory?

Who can connect the target model to the generator without exposing the process to scrutiny?

Those are infrastructure questions.

They are also power questions.

The audience used to be the people sitting in front of the television.

Then it became a set of segments in an ad console.

In the 2030s, it may become something more intimate and less human-readable: billions of continuously updated predictions about what each person is likely to notice, believe, ignore, repeat, fear, buy, or do next.

The propagandist does not need to know you.

The propagandist needs a model that is useful enough.
