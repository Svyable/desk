# The Right to a Common Reality

A democracy does not require everyone to agree.

It requires enough people to disagree about the same thing.

That condition is easy to miss because political philosophy tends to begin farther downstream. We debate rights, representation, legitimacy, equality, liberty, majorities, minorities, institutions, constitutions, and procedures. All of those arguments assume a quieter infrastructure beneath them: citizens can point toward some common objects even when they interpret those objects differently.

There was an election.

Here are the ballots.

The legislature passed this text.

Here is the statute.

The official said these words.

Here is the recording.

The bridge collapsed.

Here is the site.

The unemployment series uses this methodology.

Here are the revisions.

The court issued this opinion.

Here is the opinion.

None of these objects ends politics. They begin it.

One citizen can think the law is just and another can think it is monstrous. One can think the speech was courageous and another reckless. One can argue that the economic statistic captures lived reality badly. One can argue that the court misread the constitution. Shared evidence does not create shared judgment.

It gives judgment something to fight over.

The adaptive information environment threatens this condition in a way that the phrase “misinformation crisis” does not fully capture.

The problem is not merely that two citizens may believe different falsehoods.

They may inhabit different **salience structures**.

One person spends a month encountering crime. Another spends the same month encountering medical debt. A third encounters immigration. A fourth encounters corruption. A fifth receives politics mainly as comedy. A sixth has a personal assistant that filters politics almost entirely because previous political content made them disengage.

All six live in the same country.

All six may be accurately informed about the material they saw.

They can still arrive at election day with radically different models of what the election is about.

Plurality is not the problem. A farmer and a software engineer have never experienced the same economy. Regional newspapers have always had regional agendas. Ethnic, religious, professional, and ideological communities sustain distinct conversations. Democracy would be poorer if every citizen consumed one official bulletin.

The danger is personalization that becomes so complete, so invisible, and so responsive that the citizen cannot distinguish their world from **the** world.

That is where a right to common reality begins.

The phrase needs immediate limits.

It cannot mean a right to an official truth.

Governments lie. Majorities can be wrong. Experts can become complacent. institutions protect themselves. Consensus can lag evidence. A ministry empowered to define reality would solve the propaganda problem by becoming the propagandist.

Nor can the right mean that platforms must show everyone the same feed.

That would destroy useful personalization and create enormous editorial power in whoever chose the common feed.

Nor can it mean that controversial claims disappear until institutions approve them.

Democracy depends on challenges to institutions, including challenges that begin as minority claims.

The right is narrower and more structural.

A citizen should have reasonable access to the shared evidentiary layer beneath personalized interpretation.

The ballot count.

The original speech.

The full bill.

The public dataset.

The court record.

The source document.

The provenance chain.

The correction history.

The fact that other citizens are receiving materially different framings.

The right is not to agreement.

It is to **referents and counterfactuals**.

This becomes especially important when AI assistants mediate information. A traditional newspaper places its editorial selection in public. Everyone can buy the issue and see what page one contained. A television broadcast is inspectable after the fact. Even a social-media post has some public existence, though ranking differs by user.

A generated answer can be private and ephemeral.

Ask an assistant, “What matters in the election?” and the response may be produced only for you. Ask again tomorrow and the answer may change. Another user asks the same question and receives a different structure, vocabulary, source mix, and set of omissions.

Personalization can make the answer more useful. It can also eliminate the public artifact that critics traditionally inspect.

What does accountability look like when there is no single front page?

One answer is not to archive every sentence forever. That would create surveillance and an unusable mountain of generated text.

The more promising answer is to expose the **dimensions of selection**.

Was this answer personalized using my history?

Which stored preferences materially affected it?

Did a sponsor influence source selection or ordering?

What would the answer look like with personalization disabled?

Which major sources or arguments were omitted for brevity?

Can I ask for a version optimized for representativeness rather than relevance?

Can I compare the strongest competing interpretations without having the assistant flatten them into false equivalence?

These controls would make personalization visible without pretending to abolish editorial judgment.

A useful analogy is nutrition labeling.

A label does not tell a person what to eat. It exposes dimensions that would otherwise be hidden inside the product. The consumer still decides.

An information system could expose its own ingredients.

Not every model weight. Not a meaningless code dump. Not a claim that the algorithm is neutral.

The ingredients that matter to autonomy.

Personal history used: yes or no.

Paid influence: yes or no.

Political personalization: yes or no.

Primary sources available: yes or no.

Synthetic speakers: disclosed.

Recommendation objective: selected by user, platform, sponsor, or some combination.

The point is legibility.

A person cannot exercise meaningful choice over an influence system they cannot perceive.

The right to common reality also requires public statistics about the personalized environment.

This is difficult because individual privacy matters. A regulator or researcher should not need to read everybody's private conversations to discover that one demographic group is systematically receiving a different political frame.

We need aggregate audit methods.

Researchers can use controlled accounts, consented data donations, privacy-preserving measurement, public ad archives, model evaluations, and platform-provided aggregate statistics. Different methods have weaknesses. Together they can reveal whether personalization creates materially different political worlds.

This is not unprecedented.

Society audits systems without publishing every private record. Banks undergo examinations. clinical trials use aggregate evidence. auditors inspect controls. election systems use samples and recounts. privacy law distinguishes between individual disclosure and accountable processing.

The public sphere needs a similar audit vocabulary.

One metric might be exposure concentration.

How much of a person's political information comes from one topic, source family, or ideological cluster compared with a user-selected baseline?

Another might be personalization distance.

How different is the answer a person receives from a version generated without personal history?

Another might be sponsorship penetration.

How often does third-party paid influence affect ostensibly user-serving recommendations?

Another might be synthetic social density.

How much apparent peer activity around a political topic comes from automated or centrally controlled accounts?

No metric should become a truth score. The purpose is to make the architecture measurable.

The common-reality problem becomes most acute when personalization changes not only content but **facts of social existence**.

How many people support this?

Is this movement growing?

Are people like me defecting?

Is this controversy national or confined to one online community?

Did the candidate really say that?

Is the clip representative?

Is the story everywhere because the event is important or because the ranking system discovered that I cannot stop watching it?

These are questions about the relationship between personal experience and public distribution.

The Deceptatron gains power when the relationship is hidden.

Imagine a political actor wants to suppress turnout among a group unlikely to support it. The system does not need to tell people not to vote. It can make politics feel futile.

Show corruption.

Show hypocrisy.

Show institutional incompetence.

Show both parties breaking promises.

Show people like the target saying they are sitting this one out.

Avoid explicit falsehoods.

Avoid a traceable call to abstain.

Optimize for the belief that participation changes nothing.

The target may arrive at that conclusion independently.

A right to common reality cannot prohibit cynicism. Some cynicism is earned. It can, however, expose when the environment has been deliberately engineered to create a false estimate of everyone else's cynicism.

That is the key distinction.

Democracy cannot guarantee optimism.

It can defend the authenticity of the social signals from which citizens infer whether collective action is possible.

This connects the common-reality right to freedom of association.

A person who falsely believes nobody shares their concern may never discover a movement. A person who falsely believes everyone supports a cause may join without understanding the actual distribution of opinion. Synthetic consensus changes the perceived opportunity to associate.

The right to find real people becomes part of the right to organize.

This is why bot and persona governance is not merely a platform-hygiene issue. Industrially manufactured social proof can alter the perceived topology of society.

Who is around me?

Who agrees?

Who will show up?

Who will punish me?

Who has already given up?

Those beliefs affect political behavior before ideology does.

The common-reality right should therefore protect several layers at once.

First, access to public records and primary evidence.

Second, visibility into material personalization of political information.

Third, the ability to request a nonpersonalized or differently personalized comparison.

Fourth, honest representation of synthetic and coordinated social actors.

Fifth, independent auditing of high-scale political recommendation and persuasion systems.

Sixth, preservation of plural sources of verification so that common reality does not become official reality.

The sixth is the most important safeguard against abuse of the first five.

A government can publish records and still lie. The defense is competing records, courts, journalists, opposition parties, civil society, academics, whistleblowers, local officials, international observers, and citizens with cameras.

Common reality should be **federated**.

The word matters.

A healthy democracy does not have one memory. It has institutions capable of checking one another.

The election office publishes totals. Parties retain observers. Courts can inspect disputes. journalists report. researchers analyze. citizens can obtain records. losing candidates can challenge within procedures. The result becomes legitimate not because one actor says “trust me,” but because multiple actors can inspect the same underlying process.

The Deceptatron tries to break that federation in two ways.

It can isolate citizens into personalized worlds.

Or it can convince citizens that every verifier belongs to one conspiracy.

The first destroys common referents.

The second destroys confidence in the institutions that preserve them.

The defense must avoid mirroring either tactic.

Do not force everyone into one information stream.

Do not demand unconditional trust.

Create inspectable overlap.

This is also a design challenge for AI companies.

An assistant should be able to answer, “Why did you choose these stories?”

It should be able to produce a source-grounded version.

It should distinguish user-requested personalization from platform-imposed personalization.

It should let the user reset or suspend political memory.

It should reveal sponsorship at the point where sponsorship matters.

It should not pretend that a personalized answer is simply what any reasonable person would have received.

And when the user asks a genuinely contested question, the assistant should preserve the dispute without converting disagreement into mush.

Pluralism is not “both sides.”

Sometimes evidence is lopsided.

Sometimes one side is lying.

Sometimes a minority view later proves correct.

The assistant's job is not to manufacture symmetry. It is to make the structure of evidence legible enough that the user can see where confidence comes from.

That is a harder standard than neutrality.

Neutrality can become a pose.

Legibility is testable.

Did the system disclose the primary source?

Did it distinguish fact from inference?

Did it reveal that personalization changed the answer?

Did it preserve relevant uncertainty?

Did it hide a sponsor?

Did it represent a statistical peer group as though it were a real community?

Did it make an important omission because the omission improved engagement?

Those are questions auditors can investigate.

The right to common reality may sound like a grand constitutional phrase. In practice, it is built from boring infrastructure.

Public archives that work.

Records that remain online.

Version histories.

Open meeting minutes.

Authenticated emergency channels.

Accessible court documents.

Election audits.

Source citations.

Ad libraries.

Data portability.

User controls.

Independent research access.

Interoperable provenance.

The boring infrastructure is the point.

Propaganda thrives where verification is expensive and comparison is difficult.

A democracy becomes more resilient when the citizen can cheaply move from a claim to the object underneath it.

That does not guarantee wisdom.

Nothing does.

A citizen can inspect the record and still choose badly. A partisan can reject overwhelming evidence. A conspiracy can survive authentication. A society can possess excellent archives and terrible judgment.

The goal is not to engineer correct citizens.

It is to prevent the information system from quietly removing the conditions under which citizens can correct one another.

That is the democratic stakes of personalization.

The public sphere was never one conversation.

It was a set of overlapping conversations with enough shared objects that arguments could collide.

The 2030s risk is not merely fragmentation.

It is **noncollision**.

Your strongest evidence never meets mine.

My strongest objection never enters your assistant.

Your feed never shows the event that makes my position intelligible.

My social environment tells me everyone agrees.

Yours tells you everyone agrees too.

We arrive at the same polling place carrying different countries in our heads.

A right to common reality does not demand that we leave with the same country.

It demands that somewhere beneath the personalization, there remains a place where the evidence can meet.
