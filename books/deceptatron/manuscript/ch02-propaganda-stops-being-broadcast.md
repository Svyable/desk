# Propaganda Stops Being Broadcast

The loudspeaker was once a symbol of power because power had something the crowd did not.

A channel.

The state had the transmitter. The newspaper owner had the press. The studio had the cameras. The party had the mailing list. The church had the pulpit. The employer had the factory gate. The army had the printing unit. Whoever controlled the channel could speak to many people at once, and the rest of society had to work harder to answer.

This arrangement created a particular style of propaganda.

It favored messages that could survive repetition.

A slogan had to be short enough to remember, broad enough to fit many lives, emotional enough to move without explanation, and simple enough to repeat until familiarity began doing some of the work. The propagandist's craft was compression. A complicated political program became a symbol. A war became a face. An economic problem became an enemy. A coalition became a word.

That is one reason the visual archive of propaganda looks so certain.

Posters are not good at doubt.

A poster cannot spend three paragraphs considering the strongest argument on the other side. A thirty-second radio spot cannot preserve four layers of causal ambiguity. A marching chant does not improve when a footnote is added.

Mass media favored simplification partly because the people making propaganda wanted simplification and partly because the channels themselves rewarded it.

The important point is that propaganda adapted to the infrastructure available to it.

When the infrastructure changes, the propaganda changes too.

This can be difficult to see because new systems often inherit the appearance of old media. The first websites looked like pages. Early online advertising looked like banners. Streaming borrowed the language of channels. Podcasts borrowed radio's intimacy. Social posts borrowed headlines, photographs, letters, diaries, and gossip.

But the infrastructure beneath those familiar forms was different.

A printed newspaper does not rearrange itself after noticing which story you read first.

A television set does not normally decide that your neighbor should see a different version of the evening news because she lingered on a different clip yesterday.

A pamphlet does not report back that the fourth paragraph caused a reader to stop.

A social feed can do all three equivalents.

The shift from broadcast propaganda to computational propaganda began when distribution became programmable.

Researchers at Oxford and elsewhere used the phrase computational propaganda to describe the use of algorithms, automation, and human curation to manipulate public opinion over social networks. The documented tactics included political bots, fake accounts, coordinated posting, trolling, data-driven targeting, hashtag manipulation, and organized networks designed to make certain claims or identities appear more popular than they were.

By 2020, Oxford researchers had identified organized social media manipulation in more than eighty countries. States used it. Political parties used it. Private firms sold it. The details differed across regimes and platforms, but the direction was clear.

Propaganda was no longer only something that entered the network.

The network itself had become part of the persuasive instrument.

This distinction is essential.

A newspaper can print a lie.

A social platform can do something else as well: it can influence whether the lie reaches ten people or ten million, whether it appears beside a correction or an endorsement, whether it is shown to people likely to reject it or people likely to repeat it, whether disagreement is visible, and whether the apparent social reaction looks spontaneous.

The message matters.

The routing matters too.

Once routing becomes computational, the old idea of “the audience” begins to fracture.

Broadcast media had audiences in the plural, of course. Television programmers knew that different shows attracted different viewers. Political campaigns bought different markets. Newspapers had sections. Magazines were built around interests and identities. Direct mail could target astonishingly specific lists long before social media.

The difference was granularity and feedback.

A broadcast campaign might decide that suburban women in a particular region should receive one message and rural men another. A digital system can create thousands of overlapping groups based on behaviors that no campaign strategist would have named in advance.

People who watched most of a video but did not share it.

People who searched for an issue after seeing a post.

People who visit certain pages late at night.

People whose friends respond to a particular kind of grievance.

People who are similar, by some machine-learned measure, to people who donated.

A category no longer needs to make intuitive human sense if it predicts response.

This is one of the quiet revolutions in digital persuasion.

The old propagandist thought in social types.

The new system can think in statistical neighbors.

A person may be targeted not because someone decided “middle-aged accountant” is a politically meaningful identity but because a model found that a pattern across twenty-seven behaviors predicts interest in a particular narrative. The target may never know which behaviors mattered. The campaign operator may not know either.

That kind of targeting makes public analysis harder because the persuasive category becomes invisible.

A journalist can notice that a politician is appealing to farmers, veterans, parents, or union members. Those categories exist in language and public life.

A learned audience segment can exist only inside a model.

The segment may have no name.

It may dissolve next week.

It may be created because the people inside it respond similarly to one measurable cue and share nothing else politically.

That is already a large departure from broadcast politics.

Generative systems push it farther because the message can now dissolve too.

During the first era of digital targeting, a campaign still needed creative assets. Humans wrote the advertisement, shot the video, approved the image, or at least defined a finite set of variants. The targeting system chose among things that already existed.

When generation becomes cheap, targeting and creation can begin to merge.

The category can be temporary.

The message can be temporary.

The messenger can be temporary.

The only durable object may be the objective.

That is the transition this book calls adaptive propaganda.

It is tempting to describe this as “microtargeting plus AI,” but the phrase is too static.

Microtargeting suggests that a campaign knows who you are, chooses a tailored message, and sends it.

Adaptive propaganda can treat the first message as a probe.

The response changes the model.

The next message changes with it.

This feedback loop means the system does not need a perfect profile at the beginning. It can learn from interaction.

That matters because real personal data is messy.

Public debate about political targeting often swings between two fantasies. In one, data brokers and platforms know everything. Every purchase, friendship, insecurity, belief, and private desire has been assembled into a psychological x-ray. In the other, digital targeting is mostly junk: inaccurate categories, stale lists, and marketers pretending to know more than they do.

Both pictures contain truth.

Data can be extraordinarily detailed and still produce wrong inferences. A model can know where someone slept, what device they use, what they bought, which pages they visited, and which accounts they follow while completely misunderstanding why any of it happened.

Adaptive systems reduce the need to settle that uncertainty in advance.

They can test the hypothesis.

Maybe the person is worried about crime.

Show one story.

No response.

Maybe the person is worried about taxes.

Show another.

A click.

Maybe the click reflects curiosity rather than agreement.

Try a related message tomorrow.

The system is not reading the soul.

It is conducting an experiment.

This is a less glamorous description than psychographic mind reading, but it may be more consequential because experimentation works with partial knowledge.

A/B testing transformed commercial digital design for the same reason. A company did not need a complete theory of human motivation to learn that one button produced more purchases than another. It needed traffic, variants, a measurable outcome, and time.

Political persuasion is harder than button design. The outcomes are noisier. Ethics matter more. Causal attribution is worse. People receive competing messages. A vote occurs months later. A belief may not be observable at all.

Still, many intermediate outcomes are visible.

Attention.

Sharing.

Search.

Donation.

Volunteer sign-up.

Petition signature.

Event attendance.

Unsubscribe.

Complaint.

Follower growth.

Sentiment in a conversation.

Whether the person asks the assistant for more information.

Whether the person repeats a phrase in another setting.

Those signals can become optimization targets even when the ultimate political effect remains uncertain.

The broadcast propagandist had to rely on polling, circulation, audience ratings, field reports, and intuition to infer whether a message was working.

The adaptive propagandist can receive a stream of behavioral traces.

That stream changes the tempo of influence.

Political communication has traditionally had rhythms. The speech is written. The ad is produced. The mail piece goes out. The poll returns. The strategy meeting happens. The campaign adjusts.

Software compresses the loop until “campaign” begins to sound like the wrong unit.

The system is always campaigning.

It does not need an election to be six weeks away.

A foreign influence operation can spend years making accounts look ordinary. A commercial actor can continuously learn which anxieties sell. A political organization can maintain issue-specific communities between elections. A movement can treat every controversy as fresh training data about which stories activate which people.

The result is not one permanent persuasive machine controlling society.

The result is many systems learning in parallel.

They compete.

One side tries to frighten you. Another tries to reassure you. A company wants you to buy. A government wants you to comply. A movement wants you to march. A platform wants you to remain engaged. An influencer wants attention. Your friends want recognition. Your assistant may be trying to satisfy your stated preference for brevity while quietly ranking away information you would have found important.

This competition is one of the strongest arguments against simplistic mind-control stories.

People do not live inside one propaganda system.

They live at the intersection of many optimization systems with conflicting objectives.

The confusion can itself become politically useful.

If no narrative can dominate, an operator may aim for exhaustion.

If persuasion is difficult, demobilization may be easier.

If a positive story about the operator is unbelievable, a flood of incompatible stories about everybody else may reduce the cost of being distrusted.

This has historical precedent. Propaganda has never required universal belief. Authoritarian systems have often benefited when citizens concluded not that the state was honest but that truth was inaccessible, politics was dangerous, and public participation was pointless.

Digital abundance gives that strategy new tools.

A broadcast ministry could issue one official version.

A networked influence system can issue twenty contradictory versions through apparently unrelated voices.

One account says the event never happened.

Another says it happened but was staged.

Another says it happened and was justified.

Another says the controversy is a distraction from a larger scandal.

Another attacks the person who first documented it.

Another posts a joke.

The goal does not have to be coherence.

Coherence is a burden if the objective is to break confidence in everybody else's account.

Generative systems reduce the cost of this strategy because contradiction is cheap.

That is another difference from broadcast propaganda.

The poster had to choose.

The adaptive network can diversify.

One of the most important defensive mistakes of the next decade would therefore be to measure propaganda only by consistency. Analysts often look for repeated talking points, copied phrases, synchronized hashtags, reused images, or shared infrastructure. Those clues remain useful, especially for identifying coordination.

But the more capable system will learn that repetition is detectable.

It can coordinate objectives while varying language.

It can tell different stories to different people.

It can maintain separate identities with separate tones.

It can make convergence visible only at the level of behavior it is trying to produce.

That makes the objective harder to infer from the content.

Suppose ten thousand accounts never repeat the same claim, but all of them increase the perceived social cost of supporting one policy. Some mock. Some express disappointment. Some raise procedural questions. Some tell personal stories. Some change the subject. Some ask whether the policy is worth the division. Some say they once supported it but no longer can.

The content is heterogeneous.

The direction is not.

Broadcast propaganda was legible because it was centralized enough to look like propaganda.

Adaptive propaganda can become less legible as it becomes more coordinated.

This does not mean the old forms disappear.

States will still give speeches. Parties will still make television ads. movements will still print signs. Propaganda accumulates media rather than replacing them. Radio survived television. Posters survived radio. Email survived social feeds. The new system will use old channels whenever they work.

But the center of gravity can move.

The loudspeaker loses some of its privilege when the whisper can scale.

That phrase sounds contradictory.

A whisper is intimate because it is scarce. One person leans toward another. The words feel directed. The speaker notices the listener's face. If the listener looks confused, the speaker explains. If the listener becomes angry, the speaker may retreat. If the listener reveals something personal, the conversation changes.

Mass media traded that responsiveness for reach.

The 2030s may reduce the trade.

A generated system can speak to a million people separately.

It can maintain the appearance of conversation while serving an objective defined elsewhere.

It can remember enough to make the next message feel connected to the last one.

The propaganda stops being broadcast not because broadcasting disappears.

It stops being broadcast because the persuasive unit no longer has to be shared.

The crowd can be addressed one person at a time.

There is another inheritance from broadcasting worth preserving because it reveals what is being lost.

Broadcast messages were public in a peculiar way even when the institutions behind them were not transparent.

A radio speech could be heard by opponents.

A newspaper advertisement could be clipped.

A television spot shown in one market could be recorded and replayed somewhere else.

A leaflet intended for one neighborhood could be handed to a reporter.

The same scale that made broadcast propaganda powerful also made at least part of it observable.

The message had to survive being seen by people it was not designed to persuade.

That constraint imposed a kind of accidental discipline.

A politician might emphasize different issues before different audiences, but a sentence delivered to a hall full of people could escape the hall. A national advertisement could be criticized nationally. An official newspaper had to live with yesterday's headline. The archive did not make propaganda honest, but it created a common object around which disagreement could form.

Targeted digital advertising weakened that property.

Adaptive generation can weaken it further.

The problem is sometimes described as opacity, but opacity is only part of it. A black box is at least one box. Investigators can imagine opening it.

Adaptive persuasion creates a counterfactual problem.

To understand why one person received a message, an investigator may need to know what the system could have shown instead.

The persuasive meaning lives partly in the alternatives.

A story about factory closures has one meaning if everybody in a district sees it. It has another if only people inferred to be economically insecure receive it. A calm explanation of election administration has one meaning if it is a general civic message. It has another if it is selectively withheld from people whose disengagement benefits the sponsor.

The artifact can be identical.

The politics can differ because the selection rule differs.

Broadcast archives preserve what was said.

Adaptive accountability also has to preserve enough information about what was not said.

That is difficult because the space of possible messages can be enormous.

Nobody should expect a campaign to archive every sentence a model might theoretically have generated. That would be meaningless volume. The useful record is higher level: the objectives, constraints, targeting features, model versions, classes of intervention, sponsor, distribution surfaces, and measurements that governed the system.

Those records let investigators ask whether the variation was ordinary tailoring or something more consequential.

Did the system use a person's private financial concern to choose political content?

Did it treat inferred grief, illness, or family conflict as a persuasive opportunity?

Did different groups receive materially contradictory representations of the same policy?

Did the system optimize for informed support, raw engagement, donation, demobilization, or some proxy nobody outside the vendor understood?

Did it discover a pattern and continue exploiting it after internal testing showed that the pattern depended on false or misleading material?

These are governance questions, not requests for a ministry of copy approval.

The distinction matters because a response modeled on broadcasting can easily overreach.

If the law assumes the problem is merely that too many customized messages exist, the apparent solution is to force everyone back toward identical messages.

That would be a mistake.

Democratic persuasion benefits from translation.

A disability-rights group should be able to explain a proposal in terms relevant to people with disabilities. A farm organization can discuss a trade policy through crop prices. A veterans group can discuss it through military readiness. A local candidate should be able to talk about the local bridge rather than recite a national script.

Difference is not deception.

Personalization becomes troubling when difference combines with hidden leverage and unobservable experimentation.

The broadcast era bundled several properties together: one-to-many distribution, common creative, limited feedback, expensive production, and public visibility. Digital systems unbundle them.

We can keep some benefits without restoring the old infrastructure.

Public visibility can be designed without requiring one message for everybody.

Sponsorship can remain visible even when the wording varies.

Targeting categories can be audited without publishing personal dossiers.

Systems can record the objective they optimized without exposing every private interaction.

Platforms can make large political campaigns legible as campaigns even when the campaign speaks through many generated forms.

The general principle is simple enough: personalization should not erase accountability.

This becomes more important as political communication migrates into services that do not look like media.

A person knows when a campaign advertisement interrupts a television program.

The category is obvious.

A generated answer inside search, navigation, shopping, education, or a personal assistant can be harder to classify. The persuasive act may appear as advice, ranking, summary, or omission rather than as a recognizable advertisement.

Broadcast regulation often depended on knowing where the broadcast was.

Adaptive persuasion may travel through any interface that can observe, choose, and respond.

That shifts attention from media categories to relationships and functions.

Instead of asking only whether a communication occurred on television, social media, or a chatbot, the more durable questions are these:

Was a third party paying for an objective?

Was personal information used to increase persuasive effectiveness?

Was the interaction political in purpose even if it looked informational in form?

Was the system representing itself as loyal to the user while optimizing for somebody else?

Could the user discover that conflict?

Could an independent investigator reconstruct it later?

These questions follow the power rather than the format.

That is useful because formats change quickly.

The broadcast era lasted long enough for institutions to build rules around familiar objects: stations, newspapers, networks, advertisements, airtime, circulation.

The adaptive era will not offer that comfort.

A conversational layer can sit on top of search this year and inside a vehicle next year. A model can produce text, voice, video, summaries, rankings, or actions without changing the underlying objective. The interface can change while the persuasion loop remains the same.

The defensive vocabulary therefore needs to become architectural.

Who observes?

Who models?

Who chooses?

Who generates?

Who delivers?

Who measures?

Who remembers?

Who benefits?

Those questions are less glamorous than arguments about the latest synthetic-media format. They are also harder for technology to make obsolete.

There is a final reason broadcasting remains relevant.

Shared messages create shared criticism.

When millions of people see the same claim, they may disagree fiercely about it, but they can at least point to the same sentence. A newspaper can rebut it. A comedian can parody it. An opponent can quote it. A teacher can discuss it. Friends can compare what they heard.

Individualized persuasion weakens that civic multiplier.

If every person receives a different version, criticism becomes harder to generalize.

One person's screenshot may be dismissed as atypical.

Another person may never have seen the disputed claim.

A campaign can say the model improvised.

The vendor can say the sponsor set the objective.

The sponsor can say the platform controlled delivery.

Responsibility diffuses at the same moment the message becomes more intimate.

This is not an argument for nostalgia.

Broadcast systems concentrated enormous power. They excluded voices, flattened difference, and let a small number of institutions define what counted as national attention. The internet broke open that bottleneck, and much of that opening should be protected.

The task is narrower.

Preserve abundance while rebuilding legibility.

Let people speak differently to different people.

Let small organizations use generative tools.

Let citizens receive explanations that fit their circumstances.

But when persuasion becomes industrial, paid, adaptive, and hidden inside trusted systems, require enough public trace that the campaign can still be treated as a public act.

The loudspeaker was visible because everyone could see who held it.

The million-person whisper is harder.

Democracy does not need to make it loud again.

It needs to make the hand on the microphone visible.
