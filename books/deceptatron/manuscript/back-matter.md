# Back Matter

*Deceptatron: Propaganda in the 2030s* is a work of forward-looking narrative nonfiction. Its central concept—the Deceptatron—is not the name of a known deployed system. It is a way to reason about what happens when capabilities that already exist in partial form become connected: personal modeling, generative communication, recommendation, synthetic media, persistent memory, automated distribution, behavioral measurement, and repeated optimization toward an influence objective.

The distinction matters. This book does not claim that an all-powerful machine currently controls political opinion, that artificial intelligence can reliably determine a person's private psychological weaknesses, or that every personalized information system is propaganda. Human persuasion remains uncertain. Political attitudes are often stubborn. Models make bad inferences. Operators optimize the wrong metrics. Synthetic content can backfire. Institutions adapt. People compare notes.

The argument is about cost and architecture.

When the cost of producing another persuasive variation approaches zero, the scarce resources change. Trust matters more. Access matters more. Memory matters more. Measurement matters more. The ability to choose what a particular person encounters next can matter more than the ability to invent a spectacular lie.

That is why the book spends relatively little time imagining perfect deepfakes and more time on ordinary infrastructure.

A second attempt.

The research trail for the manuscript is maintained in the accompanying research directory. Sources are selected with a preference for peer-reviewed experiments, primary technical standards, original threat reports, government studies, statutes and regulations, and established research programs. Experimental persuasion findings are treated as evidence of capability under specified conditions, not as proof of real-world electoral control.

Three strands of evidence are particularly important to the book's thesis.

First, research on computational propaganda established well before the current generative-AI wave that political actors could combine automation, algorithms, fake identities, audience targeting and organized social-media manipulation at international scale. *Deceptatron* treats that literature as prior art, not as something AI suddenly invented.

Second, peer-reviewed experiments published in 2025 demonstrated that large language models can produce politically persuasive messages, that conversational AI can shift measured political preferences in controlled settings, and that access to personal information can increase persuasive performance in at least some debate conditions. These studies are significant without being magical. Their limits—short exposure, experimental settings, immediate attitude measures, and uncertain external validity—are part of the book's argument.

Third, technical provenance standards such as C2PA provide a concrete example of both the promise and the limits of authenticity infrastructure. Provenance can help establish where digital material came from and whether associated provenance records remain intact. It does not establish that a claim is true, representative, fair, or contextually honest. That boundary is explicit in the standard itself.

The policy proposals in the book are therefore deliberately architectural.

Protect the user's relationship with a trusted agent.

Separate private memory from undisclosed third-party persuasion.

Make industrial synthetic consensus more attributable.

Preserve public records and primary evidence.

Create useful system-level transparency for adaptive political campaigns.

Allow researchers to investigate aggregate effects without converting private life into an open dataset.

Use provenance to answer provenance questions.

Keep government out of the business of declaring a single authorized reality.

And place friction where hidden power concentrates rather than where ordinary people speak.

The most important normative proposition can be stated simply:

**A person should not become easier to manipulate merely because they trusted a system enough to let it know them.**

The most important empirical proposition is more conditional:

If personalized generation, persistent memory, trusted assistants, synthetic social proof, behavioral feedback, and cheap experimentation continue to converge, then propaganda in the 2030s will increasingly be understood not as a collection of messages but as a closed-loop relationship between a target model and a system that keeps choosing what the target should encounter next.

If those capabilities fail to converge, if personalization gains prove weak, if users reject persistent assistants, or if institutions successfully separate personal modeling from political optimization, the Deceptatron will remain a warning rather than a defining communications architecture.

That is a possibility worth rooting for.

## Selected research trail

Oxford Internet Institute researchers Philip N. Howard, Samuel Woolley, Samantha Bradshaw and collaborators developed a major body of work on computational propaganda and organized social-media manipulation. Their global inventories documented the international growth and professionalization of these practices before generative AI became the center of public debate.

Francesco Salvi, Manoel Horta Ribeiro, Riccardo Gallotti, Robert West and collaborators reported a preregistered study in *Nature Human Behaviour* in 2025 examining GPT-4 in short multiround debates, including conditions where opponents received sociodemographic information about participants. The personalized AI condition produced the study's strongest persuasive result. The manuscript treats the finding as evidence that personalization can matter under controlled conditions, not as evidence of deterministic manipulation.

Hui Bai, Jan G. Voelkel, Shane Muldowney, Johannes Eichstaedt, Robb Willer and collaborators reported three preregistered experiments in *Nature Communications* in 2025, with a combined sample of 4,829, finding that LLM-generated messages could shift attitudes on tested public-policy issues relative to neutral controls and were broadly comparable with lay-human persuasive messages.

Research published in *Nature* in 2025 examined AI dialogues in the context of the 2024 United States presidential election, the 2025 Canadian federal election and the 2025 Polish presidential election. The experiments found measurable effects on candidate preference and emphasized the role of relevant facts and evidence in the model's persuasive behavior. The result strengthens one of this book's central reversals: effective machine persuasion need not depend on exotic psychological tricks or wholesale fabrication.

The U.S. Federal Trade Commission's surveillance-pricing work provides evidence from another domain that granular behavioral and contextual data can be operationalized in individualized automated decisions. The manuscript uses this as an architectural analogy, not as evidence that the same intermediaries are conducting political persuasion.

The Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity's Content Credentials specifications and harms guidance are used to distinguish provenance from truth. Their explicit boundary is important: validated provenance information can establish useful facts about an asset's history without determining whether the asset's substantive message is true.

## A note on terminology

“Propaganda” is used broadly in the manuscript to describe organized influence directed toward political beliefs, expectations, legitimacy, participation or behavior, especially where sponsorship, identity, selection or optimization is materially asymmetric. The term should not be read as a synonym for political speech the author dislikes.

“Adaptive propaganda” refers to influence in which some element of the intervention can change in response to information about the target or the target's behavior.

“Deceptatron” refers to the closed-loop architecture, not to one model, company, state, platform, or product.

“Common reality” does not mean common opinion. It means enough shared referents, inspectable records and plural verification that citizens can disagree about the same public world.
