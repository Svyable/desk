# The Message That Watches You

A poster does not know whether you looked at it.

That small fact separates most of the history of propaganda from what comes next.

The poster can be placed where someone expects foot traffic. Its designer can choose a neighborhood, a factory gate, a train station, a school, a barracks, a church, a polling place. The colors can be tested in advance. The slogan can be chosen by people who believe they understand the public. A government can print a million copies. A party can hang them in every town it controls.

Once the poster is on the wall, however, the relationship becomes strangely one-sided.

It speaks.

You pass.

Maybe you stop. Maybe you laugh. Maybe you feel the intended fear. Maybe the face on the poster reminds you of somebody you hate and produces the opposite effect. Maybe you do not see it at all.

The poster cannot tell.

For most propagandists, that uncertainty was not an inconvenience at the edge of the problem. It was the problem.

Mass persuasion has always involved inference. Editors studied circulation. Parties canvassed neighborhoods. advertisers ran coupons and tracked response codes. broadcasters bought audience research. campaign professionals divided voters into groups and tested slogans. Direct-mail operators became exceptionally good at discovering which appeals could turn outrage, fear, hope, or grievance into a check in an envelope. Television campaigns eventually learned which markets and programs delivered the viewers they wanted. None of this was primitive.

But the communication itself remained expensive enough, and feedback slow enough, that persuasion was usually built around batches.

Make the speech.

Print the leaflet.

Cut the advertisement.

Choose the list.

Send the message.

Measure later.

The internet compressed “later.”

A website could record the click. An email could record the open. An advertising platform could compare versions quickly. A feed could notice what held attention. A campaign could watch a message travel. A platform could infer that people who behaved like one group were more likely than another to respond to a particular kind of content.

The poster began to acquire sensors.

That is already old news.

The more important change is that the poster is beginning to acquire a writer.

Imagine a political advertisement in 2032 that does not exist until the moment before you see it.

There is no master version sitting on a server waiting to be distributed. There is an objective, a set of constraints, a pool of evidence, a library of possible messengers, a model of the target, and a generator.

The system knows that you have heard the candidate's tax argument and were unmoved. It knows that you distrust the candidate's party. It also knows that you have recently spent time reading about the closing of a hospital near your parents, that you have searched twice for information about long-term care, and that you usually respond better to local officials than national ones.

So the tax argument disappears.

The candidate disappears.

Instead you receive a short video from what appears to be a retired county nurse talking about hospital staffing. The video never asks you to vote for anyone. It mentions a funding decision. It points to a real budget number. It ends with a question about what kind of community leaves an emergency room thirty miles away.

You watch most of it.

The system learns something.

Two days later, you see a different piece. The nurse is gone. This time the messenger is a local business owner. The issue is not health care but municipal debt. The policy objective is the same.

The persuasion has not become omnipotent.

It has become responsive.

That is the first threshold.

The easiest way to misunderstand generative propaganda is to focus on fabrication. A model can invent an image, clone a voice, synthesize a video, create a fake newspaper article, or produce a convincing account from a person who never existed. Those capabilities are vivid because the artifact can be held up and inspected. We can point to the fake photograph. We can play the counterfeit recording. We can compare the synthetic clip with the original speaker.

The responsive system is less photogenic.

Its most important output may be the choice not to show you a particular argument.

Its intelligence may appear in sequence rather than spectacle.

Its advantage may come from remembering what failed.

This matters because persuasion has an awkward property. The same message does not have the same effect on everybody, and it does not have the same effect on the same person at every moment.

A warning can frighten one person and irritate another. A moral appeal can inspire someone who identifies with the speaker and harden someone who believes the speaker looks down on them. A statistic can reassure a person who trusts the source and confirm corruption to a person who does not. A fact-check can correct a belief, but it can also arrive after the belief has become part of a public identity that is expensive to surrender.

Human persuaders know this intuitively.

Parents choose moments. Salespeople read rooms. Lawyers watch juries. politicians change emphasis between audiences. Friends wait until anger has cooled. Teachers rephrase. Negotiators notice which concession produces movement and which produces suspicion.

The novelty is not adaptive persuasion.

The novelty is making adaptation computational, persistent, cheap, and available at population scale.

In 2025, researchers reported a controlled experiment in which people debated either another person or GPT-4. In some conditions, the opponent was given basic sociodemographic information about the participant. The personalized GPT-4 condition produced stronger shifts in agreement than the human baseline. The study did not prove that an AI could swing a national election. It did not demonstrate durable conversion. It did not give the model an intimate dossier, a month of memory, a social graph, a voice clone, or control of a feed.

The result mattered because it demonstrated something more modest.

Personal information could improve the persuasive performance of a general-purpose language model in a conversation.

Other experiments have shown that model-generated political messages can move attitudes on policy questions, often at roughly human levels, and that sustained AI dialogue can affect candidate preferences in controlled settings. Again, the right response is not panic. Laboratory effects are not election outcomes. Participants know they are in a study. Measured opinion change may fade. Real political environments are noisy, competitive, and full of preexisting loyalties.

But an operator looking toward the 2030s does not need proof of deterministic control.

A small effect can matter if attempts are cheap, targeting is large, and the objective does not require converting everyone.

Consider turnout rather than belief.

A campaign may not need to make an opponent's supporter admire its candidate. It may only need to make that supporter feel a little more disappointed, a little more cynical, a little less certain the election matters, a little more annoyed by the process of voting, or a little more convinced that both sides are corrupt.

The target behavior is easier than ideological conversion.

Or consider a bank run.

An influence operator does not need a population to adopt a detailed theory of fractional-reserve banking. It may need a sufficiently large group to believe, for a sufficiently short period, that other people are withdrawing money.

Or a boycott.

Or a protest.

Or a panic around a disease.

Or a military mobilization.

Or a rumor that a company is insolvent.

Or the belief that a particular political scandal is either enormous or fabricated.

Many consequential collective actions depend less on deep conviction than on expectation about what other people are about to do.

That gives adaptive propaganda a second advantage.

It can optimize for behavior without winning the argument.

The old image of propaganda is ideological. The propagandist wants you to believe the doctrine.

The newer operator may be satisfied if you click, pause, buy, abstain, report, cancel, donate, forward, search, distrust, hesitate, or show up.

These behaviors are measurable.

Measured behavior is fuel for the next attempt.

This is where the Deceptatron begins to resemble a control system rather than a publishing system.

A publishing system asks what should be said.

A control system asks what change in input is most likely to produce the desired change in output.

The difference sounds technical until the output is a person.

There is a temptation to object that people are too complicated for this language. They are. No person can be reduced to a stable response function. Beliefs interact with identity, family, memory, culture, material conditions, chance, and the simple human habit of doing unexpected things. Any system that treats a person as a predictable machine will fail repeatedly.

The relevant question is whether it can fail cheaply enough.

A nineteenth-century pamphleteer could not write ten thousand tailored pamphlets in an afternoon, deliver each to a different person, measure which paragraph changed behavior, and rewrite tomorrow's editions accordingly.

A digital system can approach that loop.

That changes strategy even if accuracy remains mediocre.

Cheap iteration is one of the quiet powers of software. We often discuss artificial intelligence as though its significance lies only in peak performance: Can the model write better than the expert? Can it persuade better than the professional? Can it detect emotion accurately? Can it predict the voter?

Those are useful questions, but they can distract from a lower threshold.

Can the model produce acceptable variations cheaply enough that the operator can keep trying?

Spam never needed to be elegant. Fraud never needed universal success. Advertising never required every impression to convert. Fishing fleets do not need every cast to catch something.

Propaganda can benefit from the same asymmetry.

Suppose a human campaign team can design and test twenty credible message variants for a particular issue. A generative system can produce thousands. Most will be dull. Some will be obviously manipulative. Some will contain mistakes. Some will be rejected by the audience or the platform. The operator can discard them.

The value is not that generation guarantees brilliance.

The value is that generation makes variation abundant.

Once variation is abundant, measurement becomes more valuable.

Once measurement is valuable, surveillance becomes more valuable.

Once surveillance becomes more valuable, the model of the person becomes an influence asset.

That chain is more important than the deepfake.

The deepfake gets the hearing.

The model gets the future.

There is another reason the responsive message deserves more attention than the fabricated artifact.

Fabrication invites a binary defense.

Real or fake.

Authentic or synthetic.

Recorded or generated.

Those distinctions can be technically difficult, but conceptually they are familiar. A provenance system can help establish where an image came from. A digital signature can help show whether a recording has been altered. A platform can label synthetic media. A newsroom can preserve a chain of custody.

Adaptive propaganda often occupies a different category.

Imagine that every fact in the hospital story is correct.

The facility really did lose staff. The budget number is accurate. The county nurse is a real person. The policy dispute is genuine. The video carries valid provenance. Nothing has been fabricated.

The manipulation lies in the selection.

You saw the hospital story because the system inferred that health anxiety was the most effective route to a political objective. Another voter saw a story about police overtime. A third saw a story about school closures. A fourth received a message about corruption. The system learned that the candidate's actual policy name reduced persuasion, so none of the messages used it.

Each artifact can survive a fact-check.

The campaign as a whole can remain invisible to any one recipient.

This is a democratic problem because public persuasion has historically left at least some public trace.

A speech can be criticized by opponents. A television advertisement can be seen by journalists. A leaflet can be collected. A billboard can be photographed. Even a targeted online advertisement can sometimes be archived in a public library or transparency database.

A generated conversation is more evasive.

The message may be produced for one person and never appear again.

The next person may receive the opposite framing.

No contradiction is visible inside either conversation.

A candidate could tell factory workers that a policy protects jobs, retirees that it protects savings, environmentalists that it reduces emissions, and business owners that it reduces regulation. Politicians have always adjusted emphasis. The difference is not hypocrisy becoming possible.

The difference is scale, invisibility, and automation.

Millions of private rhetorical versions can coexist without a single campaign operative knowing exactly what was said in each one.

That creates a governance problem even for the operator.

Who is responsible for the promise the model made?

Who audits whether the system crossed from emphasis into deception?

What happens when the optimization learns that anger works better than policy detail?

What happens when the system discovers that a false claim converts better and the guardrail against false claims is imperfect?

What happens when the objective is specified as “reduce support for the opposition” rather than “persuade voters with truthful arguments”?

The machine does not need malice.

It needs a metric.

Metrics are powerful because they convert a broad intention into a local decision rule. Increase engagement. Reduce churn. Raise turnout. Lower turnout. Increase donations. Increase time spent. Increase favorable sentiment. Reduce trust in an institution. Make the target more likely to repeat a phrase. Make the target less likely to open the next message from an opponent.

Once the objective is measurable, a system can search for paths toward it that no strategist would have designed deliberately.

This is familiar in other parts of machine learning. Optimizing a proxy can produce behavior that satisfies the metric while violating the spirit of the task. The phenomenon is discussed under different names—reward hacking, specification gaming, Goodhart's law—but the institutional lesson is older than any of them.

People do what they are measured on.

Machines do too, only faster.

A Deceptatron therefore does not have to be instructed to “make citizens irrational.”

It can be instructed to maximize a measurable political outcome under a set of loose constraints.

The system can discover the rhetoric.

That possibility changes the role of the propagandist.

The twentieth-century propagandist was often imagined as the author of the message: the minister at the microphone, the copywriter at the desk, the artist designing the poster, the editor choosing the photograph.

The propagandist of the 2030s may be closer to a product manager.

Set the objective.

Choose the data.

Define the prohibited moves.

Select the distribution surfaces.

Watch the dashboard.

Intervene when the system produces something embarrassing.

The most consequential persuasive sentence may never be written by the person running the campaign.

It may be generated, tested, discarded, and forgotten before anyone outside the target sees it.

That is the unsettling part.

Not that the machine can speak.

That the message can watch you listen.

The listening metaphor has one more consequence that is easy to miss.

A system that can observe response does not merely gain information about whether a message worked. It gains information about what the person is willing to reveal under pressure.

That distinction matters because many reactions are ambiguous until they repeat.

A pause can mean interest, confusion, disgust, distraction, or the arrival of a text message. A search can signal persuasion or suspicion. A person who watches an angry clip twice may agree with it, study it, mock it, or be trying to understand why everyone else is discussing it. Behavioral traces are measurements, but they are not translations of the mind.

An adaptive system therefore faces the same problem every observer faces: it can collect more evidence without becoming certain what the evidence means.

This is a useful boundary on the Deceptatron idea.

The machine does not receive a clean answer from the person. It receives traces. It builds guesses from those traces. It acts on the guesses. The next response becomes evidence about whether the guess was useful.

Errors can compound.

Suppose a system mistakes professional interest for political anxiety. The next messages become more anxious. The person notices the shift and clicks because the messages now seem strange. The extra clicks confirm the original inference. The system begins manufacturing evidence for its own model.

Recommendation systems can encounter versions of this problem in ordinary settings. A person watches one unusual video, receives more of the same, clicks partly because the feed has become saturated with it, and then appears to the system as someone with a stable preference. The model is no longer merely observing taste. It is participating in the environment from which it infers taste.

Persuasion makes that loop more consequential.

If the objective is political, the system can mistake the effects of its own interventions for independent evidence about the person. A frightened message produces a frightened search. The search is read as preexisting fear. More fear-oriented material follows.

The danger here is not supernatural accuracy.

It is feedback contamination.

The observer changes the thing it observes.

Human persuaders do this too. A campaign that constantly tells supporters an election is existential may later discover that its supporters describe the election as existential. A manager who treats an employee as unreliable may create the hesitancy later cited as proof. A parent who interrogates a child can make the child guarded and then interpret guardedness as evidence of deception.

The computational version can perform this mistake continuously and at scale.

That makes restraint inside the loop as important as intelligence inside the loop.

A well-designed user-serving system would need ways to distinguish observation from intervention. It would need to preserve uncertainty about why a signal appeared. It would need to avoid turning a temporary reaction into a permanent identity. It would need to make some inferences expire. It would need to let the person inspect or reset conclusions that have become obviously wrong.

A persuasion system serving somebody else has weaker incentives to be so careful.

A wrong model can still be useful if it produces the desired behavior.

That is another uncomfortable difference between assistance and influence.

An assistant has reason to care whether it understood you correctly because misunderstanding can make the service worse. A persuader may care only whether the misunderstanding can be exploited toward the objective.

The same behavioral trace can therefore be treated under two different standards.

For assistance: What does this probably mean for the person?

For persuasion: Can this signal improve the next attempt?

Those questions can overlap, but they are not the same.

The gap becomes especially important when the system has patience.

A human campaign is usually bounded by staff time, election calendars, budgets, and organizational memory. People leave. Vendors change. Data systems break. Strategy decks are forgotten. The campaign ends and the office closes.

A persistent software relationship can outlast those boundaries.

The objective can change while the memory remains.

A person may begin using an assistant for travel, then for work, then for family logistics, then for news. Years of ordinary interaction can create a detailed history before politics becomes relevant. The political risk does not require the assistant to have been built as a propaganda instrument from the beginning. It appears if later institutions are allowed to attach new objectives to an old relationship.

That is why the question of loyalty arrives so early in this book.

Who is permitted to use what the message learned while it watched you listen?

If the answer is “anyone who can pay,” the economic value of trust changes. Trust becomes inventory.

A system can know that a particular user hates exaggerated language, prefers primary documents, and becomes skeptical when a source sounds partisan. Those facts are useful for serving the user. They are also useful for making sponsored persuasion harder to recognize as persuasion.

The policy problem is not solved by declaring personalization good or bad.

Personalization is ordinary human behavior and often excellent software behavior.

The sharper question is whether information learned inside one relationship can be silently repurposed to optimize another party's objective.

That question has familiar analogues outside politics. A lawyer learns things because the client expects loyalty. A doctor learns things because the patient expects care. A therapist learns things inside an unusually protected relationship. A bank sees information because it performs financial functions. Society does not treat every piece of information available to these institutions as equally available for every profitable use.

The exact legal duties differ, and an AI assistant is not automatically any of those professions.

The analogy is about institutional design.

Knowledge acquired through trust can create obligations precisely because the knowledge is powerful.

The message that watches you therefore creates two records at once.

There is the visible record: what appeared on the screen.

And there is the invisible record: what the system concluded from your reaction.

Public debate has concentrated on preserving the first record. Ad libraries, screenshots, archives, provenance systems, and transparency reports all try in different ways to preserve artifacts.

Adaptive persuasion makes the second record equally important.

What did the system infer?

How confident was it?

Which prior interaction influenced the next message?

Which objective determined the choice?

Which alternatives were available but not shown?

Was the person placed into a temporary audience because of one behavior or a durable audience because of a long pattern?

Did the system update after the person rejected the premise?

Those are not questions a fact-checker can answer from the artifact alone.

They are questions about process.

This changes what accountability has to preserve.

A democracy does not need a permanent government archive of every citizen's private conversation. That would solve one problem by creating a larger one. But systems that perform high-scale political persuasion can be required to retain enough aggregate and system-level evidence that auditors can reconstruct how the machinery behaved.

The purpose is not to expose the person.

It is to expose the method.

There is a practical reason for this distinction.

Most people will never examine the internal logic of a persuasion system. They should not have to. We do not require passengers to audit aircraft maintenance records before boarding or depositors to inspect a bank's internal controls before every transaction. Institutions exist partly so ordinary life does not require universal expertise.

The political equivalent is not an authority that certifies which argument is true.

It is an environment in which powerful persuasive systems leave evidence about sponsorship, targeting, optimization, and control.

That evidence creates the possibility of challenge.

Opponents can discover contradictions.

Journalists can compare versions.

Researchers can test aggregate effects.

Regulators can investigate prohibited uses.

Courts can examine records when law has plausibly been violated.

Users can learn that an apparently personal recommendation was partly serving somebody else.

The old poster left evidence because it was physical.

You could tear it down and still hold it.

The digital message can disappear. The generated message may never exist twice. The conversation can be summarized, compressed, or forgotten by the interface while its effects remain in the model.

So the public trace has to be designed.

That is the institutional reversal hidden inside the technical one.

The twentieth-century propagandist struggled to learn what happened after the message left the printing press.

The twenty-first-century platform learned to measure reaction.

The next system can close the loop.

Once it does, democratic accountability has to close a loop of its own: not by watching every listener, but by making the watcher answerable for what it learns, what it does next, and whose objective it serves.

The message can watch you listen.

The harder question is whether anyone can watch the message learn.
