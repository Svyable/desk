# The Invisible Coalition

Conspiracy is an attractive explanation because conspiracy has edges.

Somebody planned it.

Somebody met in a room.

Somebody gave the order.

Somebody can be exposed.

Modern information systems are harder to understand because consequential outcomes can emerge from actors who do not share a room, a plan, or even an objective.

A foreign intelligence service wants to weaken trust in an election.

A domestic political campaign wants to win the election.

A platform wants to maximize engagement.

An influencer wants followers.

A consulting firm wants a contract renewed.

A data broker wants to sell targeting capability.

A model provider wants usage.

A partisan publisher wants subscriptions.

A scammer wants money.

An ordinary citizen wants to express anger.

The same inflammatory story can serve all of them for different reasons.

No mastermind is required.

That is the invisible coalition.

The phrase does not mean the actors are secretly allied. Some may hate one another. Some may be unaware of the others. Some may be legitimate participants in democratic debate. Some may be criminals or foreign adversaries. The coalition exists at the level of incentive and effect.

A piece of content moves because enough systems find it useful.

This is one reason attribution is so difficult in networked propaganda.

A foreign operation may seed a narrative, but domestic actors make it popular.

A domestic rumor may arise organically, but a foreign service amplifies it.

A platform's ranking system may distribute the material because it holds attention, not because the platform agrees with it.

A journalist may cover the rumor in order to debunk it and inadvertently increase awareness.

A political opponent may quote the rumor to condemn it and give it another round of distribution.

A model may summarize the controversy because the web now contains many references to it.

The narrative acquires agency without possessing any.

It becomes useful to too many systems.

The propaganda of the 2030s will often work this way.

The Deceptatron is easier to imagine as a single machine because single machines make good villains. In practice, the adaptive loop may be assembled across institutions.

One company supplies personal data.

Another hosts the model.

Another controls distribution.

A contractor sets the objective.

Creators produce authentic material.

Synthetic personas fill gaps.

A measurement vendor estimates response.

A campaign uses the aggregate results.

No actor possesses the full stack.

That modularity lowers barriers because organizations can buy capability rather than build it.

The history of digital advertising is instructive. A small business does not need to operate a global surveillance network, build an auction, maintain a user graph, train ranking models, and own a browser. It buys an advertising product from companies that already built the infrastructure.

Influence can become a service in the same way.

Oxford researchers documented the professionalization of social media manipulation by 2020, including private strategic-communication firms working for political and government clients. The relevant point for the 2030s is not that every communications firm becomes a covert propaganda contractor.

It is that specialized capabilities can be purchased.

Account management.

Targeting.

Translation.

Synthetic media.

Audience research.

Influencer recruitment.

Sentiment measurement.

Bot detection avoidance, where illegal or abusive actors pursue it.

Narrative monitoring.

Model hosting.

Data enrichment.

The more modular the stack becomes, the harder it is to locate responsibility.

A model provider can say it did not know the strategic objective.

A data broker can say it never saw the message.

A platform can say it did not create the content.

A campaign can say a contractor acted outside instructions.

A contractor can say the model generated the wording.

A creator can say they were given talking points without knowing the sponsor.

Each statement may be partly true.

The system can still function.

This resembles other distributed harms. Supply chains allow companies to benefit from practices several contractual layers away. Financial crises emerge from decisions that are individually rational inside institutions and collectively disastrous. Pollution can come from many sources, none decisive alone. Cyberattacks combine vulnerabilities written by one company, infrastructure rented from another, credentials stolen elsewhere, and monetization through a fourth system.

Governance has learned, slowly, to follow the chain.

Propaganda governance will need to do the same.

The question cannot stop at “who wrote the post?”

Who paid?

Who targeted?

Who supplied the personal model?

Who controlled the distribution?

Who measured the outcome?

Who knew the objective?

Who had the ability to stop the system?

These are accountability questions, not censorship questions.

A democracy can preserve the right to speak while imposing duties on the industrial infrastructure that secretly scales and targets speech.

This distinction is essential because the invisible coalition contains legitimate actors.

A political consulting firm is not inherently a propaganda operation.

An influencer can advocate openly.

A data analytics company can perform lawful polling.

A platform can recommend content without endorsing it.

A model can translate political speech.

The harm comes from combinations, concealment, and objective.

That makes bright-line regulation difficult.

It also creates opportunities for narrow rules around conduct rather than ideology.

Undisclosed sponsorship.

Fraudulent identity.

Unauthorized use of sensitive personal data.

Synthetic impersonation.

Coordinated inauthentic behavior.

Paid political persuasion through private assistant memory.

Failure to maintain required records of large-scale automated political campaigns.

These are behaviors a legal system can define without deciding which political opinions are correct.

The invisible coalition also changes how democracies think about foreign influence.

The classic image is an adversary inserting propaganda into the domestic public sphere.

That still happens.

But the more efficient strategy is often to find existing fractures.

A foreign operation does not need to invent racial tension, economic anxiety, corruption, regional resentment, distrust of institutions, anger about immigration, or fear of war. Democratic societies produce real conflicts because they contain real interests.

The operator can amplify.

This is strategically attractive because authentic grievances provide camouflage and fuel.

A manufactured story can be disproved.

A real grievance cannot be fact-checked away.

The manipulation lies in scale, framing, coordination, and selective attention.

This should make democratic counter-propaganda humble.

A government that labels every amplified grievance “foreign disinformation” risks becoming propaganda itself. Citizens may have legitimate reasons for anger. Foreign actors can exploit a problem without creating it. Exposing the exploitation does not resolve the problem.

The strongest defense is often institutional competence.

A society with trusted elections is harder to destabilize through election rumors.

A public-health agency that communicates uncertainty honestly is harder to discredit when guidance changes.

A government that releases records quickly leaves less room for fabricated leaks.

A company that treats workers well gives hostile organizers fewer authentic grievances to amplify.

Propaganda attaches to weakness.

That is uncomfortable because it means information security extends into governance quality.

A state can improve bot detection and remain vulnerable if citizens believe the courts are corrupt for good reasons.

A platform can remove synthetic accounts and remain vulnerable if its recommendation incentives reward outrage.

A model provider can block malicious prompts and remain vulnerable if the broader web is flooded with coordinated source material.

The Deceptatron is an ecosystem problem.

Ecosystem problems rarely have one switch.

The invisible coalition can also operate commercially before it becomes political.

Consider a product launch.

The company pays creators.

Creators generate buzz.

Affiliates earn commissions.

Customers post genuine reviews.

The platform notices engagement and recommends more content.

News outlets cover the trend because the trend exists.

Search ranking improves because more pages mention the product.

AI assistants summarize the apparent consensus.

The product becomes popular partly because it appears popular.

No fraud is necessary.

Now introduce undisclosed synthetic reviews, paid influencers who present themselves as independent, generated comparison sites, or affiliates using conversational agents to steer customers. The boundary between marketing and manufactured reality becomes difficult to see from one artifact.

Political systems will inherit the same architecture.

A movement can appear culturally dominant because content, creators, recommendation, and press attention reinforce one another.

An opponent may accuse the whole phenomenon of being manufactured even when most participants are genuine.

The resulting uncertainty is itself useful to manipulators.

If every movement might be astroturf, citizens become cynical about spontaneous collective action.

That is the liar's dividend applied to organizing.

Real crowds become deniable.

Real enthusiasm becomes suspect.

Real testimony becomes “probably AI.”

A society saturated with synthetic possibility can lose confidence in authentic coordination.

This is why attribution cannot be only a forensic exercise performed after damage occurs.

Institutions need durable records of sponsorship and control.

Political organizations already keep financial records. Platforms can keep archives of paid political campaigns. High-scale synthetic persona networks can face stronger identity requirements without forcing every ordinary citizen to publish a legal name. Model providers can retain abuse-relevant logs under privacy-preserving policies. Contractors can carry duties to know their clients in high-risk political work.

The goal is to preserve the chain of responsibility.

Responsibility is a kind of provenance for action.

Who caused what to happen?

This is harder than media provenance because influence is distributed. There may be no single causal author.

Still, institutions can record relationships.

Sponsor.

Vendor.

Platform.

Agent.

Targeting basis.

Automation level.

Human controller.

These records would not necessarily be public in full. Privacy and security matter. But regulators and independent auditors need ways to reconstruct large campaigns without relying on leaked documents or whistleblowers.

The invisible coalition becomes dangerous when every participant can plausibly claim to be merely infrastructure.

Infrastructure is not innocent by definition.

A bank is infrastructure for finance and still has anti-money-laundering duties. A telecom carrier is infrastructure for communication and still has legal obligations. A marketplace is infrastructure for commerce and still confronts fraud rules. A cloud provider can be neutral about a customer's politics while enforcing abuse policies.

The same institutional maturity will come to persuasion systems.

The hard part will be preserving political neutrality while imposing process duties.

A model provider should not decide which party deserves to win.

It can decide that covert impersonation violates policy.

A platform should not enforce official truth.

It can require disclosure of paid political sponsorship.

A data broker should not judge ideology.

It can be prohibited from selling certain sensitive targeting capabilities for political persuasion.

An assistant should not censor political arguments.

It can refuse to let third parties secretly use private memory to optimize those arguments.

These rules target the architecture rather than the belief.

That is the path through the invisible coalition.

The 2030s will tempt us to search for the person behind the curtain.

Sometimes there will be one.

Often there will be a supply chain.

The supply-chain view creates a practical rule for responsibility: follow control, benefit, and knowledge separately.

They are not the same thing.

One actor may control a component without knowing the campaign's political objective.

Another may know the objective without controlling distribution.

A third may profit without seeing either the target or the message.

A fourth may possess the power to stop the activity after receiving credible notice.

Legal systems already separate these dimensions in other domains.

Negligence asks what someone should have known.

Agency asks who acted for whom.

Product liability asks where responsibility sits across manufacturers and sellers.

Financial regulation asks who benefits and who controls accounts.

Cybersecurity regimes sometimes impose duties according to access, risk, and incident knowledge.

Adaptive persuasion will need similarly differentiated duties.

The alternative is two bad extremes.

At one extreme, every infrastructure provider is treated as fully responsible for every political use. That would encourage over-removal, surveillance, and incumbent control.

At the other, every provider declares itself neutral plumbing and responsibility evaporates at each contractual boundary.

A workable system has to assign duties to the capabilities each actor actually controls.

A data broker can be responsible for prohibited sensitive targeting categories.

A platform can be responsible for sponsorship records and coordinated identity abuse it can observe.

A campaign can be responsible for the objective it authorizes.

A contractor can be responsible for misrepresenting sponsorship or exceeding the mandate.

A model provider can be responsible for enforcing product policies it has actually adopted and for responding to abuse signals within its visibility.

No actor has to become the universal editor of politics.

Each has to remain answerable for its part of the machinery.

This sounds bureaucratic because accountability often is.

That is a feature.

The alternative is a dramatic scandal followed by a hunt for one villain who may not exist.

Distributed systems require distributed records.

Contracts.

Sponsor identities.

Access logs.

Model and campaign identifiers.

Dates.

Targeting categories.

Authorization chains.

Incident reports.

These artifacts are boring until somebody needs to reconstruct what happened.

Then they become the difference between evidence and accusation.

The invisible coalition also complicates sanctions.

If a hostile foreign service uses domestic commercial infrastructure, shutting down one account may have little effect. Requiring every provider to identify geopolitical intent before serving a customer is unrealistic and dangerous.

Chokepoints should be used narrowly.

Payment flows can expose sanctioned actors.

Large political spend can trigger disclosure.

Mass account creation can trigger stronger identity assurance.

Repeated confirmed abuse can justify service restrictions.

The important property is proportionality.

The more industrial the operation, the more process obligations can attach without burdening an ordinary citizen posting political speech.

Scale is not perfect evidence of wrongdoing.

It is a reasonable trigger for recordkeeping.

A person sending fifty messages should not need an influence-compliance department.

An organization operating millions of adaptive political interactions occupies a different position in the information ecosystem.

Thresholds are familiar elsewhere.

Small businesses and banks do not face identical requirements.

A neighborhood campaign and a national political-ad network need not either.

This is one way to preserve low-cost democratic participation while making industrial opacity more expensive.

The invisible coalition also reveals a mistake in how manipulation success is sometimes measured.

Investigators want to know whether one operation persuaded people.

That question is important and often unanswerable.

A supply chain can create harm without any one component producing a measurable attitude shift.

A contractor lowers content cost.

A platform amplifies the material.

A synthetic network creates apparent consensus.

A newsroom covers the apparent trend.

A real politician reacts to the coverage.

Citizens then react to the politician.

Where did persuasion occur?

The causal chain has become social.

This is why infrastructure governance should not depend on proving that a particular operation changed a particular vote.

Fraud rules do not require proving that every attempted victim believed the scam.

Impersonation can be harmful even when the audience resists.

Undisclosed sponsorship can violate norms even when the campaign fails.

Some conduct is governable because it corrupts the process, not because its final behavioral effect can be isolated.

That distinction prevents operators from using uncertainty about persuasion effects as a universal defense.

At the same time, defenders should not exaggerate failed operations into strategic triumphs for the attacker.

A synthetic network with no audience is not equivalent to a national campaign.

An operation removed before distribution is different from one that reached millions.

Capability, deployment, reach, engagement, persuasion, and behavior remain separate.

The invisible-coalition framework should make those distinctions easier, not blur them.

Each stage can be measured independently.

Who assembled capability?

Who deployed it?

Where did it reach?

Did people interact?

Did attitudes change?

Did behavior change?

Often the evidence will stop before the last questions.

That is acceptable.

Institutional response can still focus on the conduct that is known.

This evidentiary discipline matters because influence operations produce incentives for exaggeration on every side.

Attackers want prestige.

Security companies want relevance.

Platforms want credit for disruption.

Governments want to demonstrate vigilance.

Political actors want proof that opponents were illegitimate.

A careful public record should resist turning every detected network into an election-changing superweapon.

The Deceptatron is dangerous precisely because it need not be magical.

Inflating mediocre operations teaches the public the wrong lesson.

It creates helplessness.

It can even serve the adversary's goal by making citizens believe manipulation is omnipresent and irresistible.

Democratic counterintelligence should therefore report confidence and effect separately.

We are confident these accounts shared control.

We are confident this sponsor paid.

We observed this reach.

We do not know how many people changed their minds.

That is stronger than theatrical certainty.

It also preserves credibility for the cases where evidence is substantial.

The supply chain offers one final defensive advantage.

Modularity creates many intervention points.

A single integrated propaganda ministry might be hard to penetrate.

A commercial stack depends on vendors with different incentives.

Payment providers dislike sanctions risk.

Cloud providers dislike abuse.

Platforms dislike fake accounts.

Model companies dislike reputational damage.

Advertisers dislike fraud.

Contractors dislike losing legitimate clients.

Researchers and journalists can investigate pieces independently.

The same fragmentation that diffuses responsibility can create friction.

Governance can use that fact without demanding centralized control of speech.

Make each layer carry a small, appropriate duty.

Make records compatible enough to reconstruct a campaign.

Protect whistleblowers.

Support independent research.

Punish fraudulent concealment more clearly than political viewpoint.

Let legitimate advocacy remain cheap.

The invisible coalition is not invincible.

It is difficult because it has no single head.

The defense should stop looking for one and learn to govern the joints.

The supply-chain view also changes what evidence of coordination should look like. Similar content is weak evidence by itself. People copy one another. News cycles synchronize attention. Advocacy groups legitimately share material. A useful investigation needs to distinguish common cause from common control.

That distinction suggests a record built around relationships rather than ideological resemblance. Which accounts or vendors shared payment infrastructure? Which contractor had authority over several nominally independent personas? Which campaign identifier connected otherwise different creative? Which model or distribution service received the same objective? Which party could change the behavior of the system when something went wrong?

The answer will often be incomplete. That is preferable to filling the gaps with a theory of conspiracy. A network can be described at different confidence levels: shared infrastructure is observed; common funding is documented; control is inferred; downstream adoption is organic. Keeping those layers separate makes enforcement more credible and protects legitimate participation from being absorbed into the operation merely because citizens repeated a message after encountering it.

It also gives the participants in the supply chain a clearer defensive duty. A provider does not need to know the politics of every customer. It needs enough records to answer for the capabilities it supplied and enough process to respond when credible evidence shows those capabilities are being abused. The narrower the duty, the easier it is to enforce without turning infrastructure firms into political referees.

The invisible coalition is difficult precisely because real coordination and emergent alignment can coexist. Governance gets stronger when it can say which one it actually found.
