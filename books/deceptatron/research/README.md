# Research Brief

## Thesis under test

*Deceptatron: Propaganda in the 2030s* asks what changes when political and commercial influence stop being organized mainly around messages and become organized around adaptive relationships.

The strong, dramatic version of the thesis would be that generative AI creates perfect individualized mind control. That claim is neither supported nor necessary.

The version worth testing is narrower:

> As the cost of generating, personalizing, delivering, and measuring persuasive communication falls, influence operators can shift from campaigns built around a finite set of messages to closed-loop systems that maintain a model of a target, choose the next intervention, observe response, and adapt. The political risk comes from the economics and persistence of repeated attempts, not from assuming any one attempt is irresistible.

The manuscript calls that closed-loop system a **Deceptatron**. The term is intentionally architectural rather than mystical. It refers to a stack that can combine target modeling, generative production, synthetic or real messengers, distribution, measurement, memory, and optimization toward an influence objective.

The central empirical question is whether the relevant capabilities are converging quickly enough that this is a useful way to understand propaganda in the 2030s rather than an overfitted metaphor for technologies that remain separate.

## Core empirical tests

1. **Persuasion capacity:** How persuasive are current large language models in controlled experiments, and how much does personalization change measured effects?
2. **Dialogue versus artifact:** Does sustained conversation materially differ from one-shot persuasive text, and what evidence exists that interactive systems can adapt arguments effectively?
3. **Target modeling:** Which kinds of personal data measurably improve persuasion, recommendation, prediction, or targeting, and how noisy are those inferences?
4. **Influence operations in practice:** How are state-linked, political, commercial, and covert operators actually using generative AI today? Is use concentrated in cheap content production or moving toward interactive targeting and automation?
5. **Synthetic social proof:** How do bots, fake accounts, coordinated personas, influencers, and generated media change perceptions of consensus, popularity, legitimacy, or social risk?
6. **Recommendation effects:** What can and cannot be causally attributed to ranking and recommendation systems in political attitudes, polarization, exposure, and engagement?
7. **Memory and sequencing:** What evidence supports the importance of timing, repetition, prior belief, identity, correction order, and continued exposure in persuasion or misinformation persistence?
8. **Trusted interfaces:** How quickly are AI assistants becoming mediators of search, news, purchasing, scheduling, and everyday decisions, and what governance exists around persuasive conflicts of interest?
9. **Provenance:** What can content provenance systems such as C2PA establish, and what can they not establish about truth, context, intent, or selection?
10. **Institutional response:** Which interventions preserve free expression while reducing covert adaptive persuasion, synthetic impersonation, undisclosed political targeting, and manipulation through high-trust interfaces?

## Falsification and counter-evidence

Every chapter should preserve evidence that weakens the central claim.

- Persuasion effects in laboratory settings may not generalize to high-salience real elections or durable behavior change.
- Political attitudes can be sticky, especially when attached to identity, party, religion, class, community, or long-standing moral commitments.
- Personalization can fail when personal data are shallow, wrong, stale, or interpreted through the wrong model.
- Generative content may increase volume without increasing effectiveness. Cheap spam can create cheap distrust.
- Covert influence operations often struggle to achieve meaningful engagement. Capability to generate content is not evidence of strategic impact.
- Recommender-system effects are heterogeneous and can be smaller or less directional than public debate assumes.
- Synthetic media can backfire by increasing generalized skepticism, including skepticism toward the operator's own preferred claims.
- Provenance can establish origin and editing history without establishing truth, but that narrower function may still be highly valuable.
- Regulation aimed at covert persuasion can become censorship, incumbent protection, or surveillance if designed badly.
- Human intermediaries, local institutions, social ties, and offline experience can remain more persuasive than algorithmic communication.
- The same adaptive communication systems can be used for legitimate education, public health, customer support, accessibility, and democratic participation. The risk is not personalization by itself but hidden optimization against a person's interests or political autonomy.

The manuscript should be willing to reduce the importance of the Deceptatron concept if evidence shows that personalization gains are weak, interactive systems do not scale, trusted interfaces remain plural and contestable, or institutions successfully separate personal modeling from political persuasion.

## Evidence lanes

### I. Propaganda before personalization

Use propaganda history to establish what is genuinely new. Study mass persuasion, wartime information control, advertising, public relations, psychological operations, audience segmentation, direct mail, and broadcast media. The book should not erase a century of sophisticated targeting merely to make AI appear revolutionary.

### II. Computational propaganda

Use Oxford Internet Institute work and other empirical research on bots, fake accounts, coordinated amplification, microtargeting, platform manipulation, and professionalized influence operations. Computational propaganda is the immediate prior art and the concept this manuscript extends rather than replaces.

### III. Persuasive models

Use preregistered and peer-reviewed experiments on LLM-generated political persuasion, personalized debate, dialogue, and attitude change. Separate statistically measurable opinion shifts from durable behavior. Record sample sizes, treatment conditions, effect measures, and limits.

### IV. Real-world adversarial use

Use first-party threat reports from model providers, major platforms, security companies, and government agencies where available. Track what operators actually use models for: drafting, translation, persona creation, research, comments, code, images, engagement, automation, or conversational interaction. Avoid assuming the most advanced technically possible tactic is already common operationally.

### V. Recommendation and ranking

Use causal studies, platform experiments, and independent research on feed ranking and recommendation. Distinguish exposure effects from attitude effects, engagement from persuasion, and correlation from causal evidence.

### VI. Synthetic media and provenance

Use work on deepfakes, synthetic audio, image generation, detection limits, watermarking, cryptographic provenance, and C2PA. Keep the crucial distinction between **authenticity** and **truth**. An authentic artifact can mislead; a synthetic artifact can convey something true.

### VII. Personal data and inference

Use privacy, advertising, pricing, data-broker, and profiling research to establish the scale of personal modeling. Include counter-evidence showing how inaccurate inferred attributes can be. The book's argument requires economically useful models, not omniscient ones.

### VIII. Misinformation correction and prebunking

Use meta-analyses and experiments on fact-checking, continued influence, inoculation, prebunking, repetition, and belief correction. The defensive chapters need evidence about what actually improves resilience rather than attractive institutional slogans.

### IX. Democratic and legal design

Study the EU Digital Services Act, political advertising rules, U.S. campaign law where relevant, consumer-protection authority, platform transparency regimes, privacy law, biometric law, provenance standards, and emerging AI governance. The goal is not to recommend one jurisdiction's legal model but to identify governable control points.

## Initial source spine

The source ledger should be expanded as the manuscript evolves. The first draft relies most heavily on these source families:

- Harold D. Lasswell's foundational work on propaganda analysis and the Institute for Propaganda Analysis as historical context.
- Jacques Ellul's *Propaganda* for the relationship between modern mass society and organized persuasion, treated as theory rather than empirical authority.
- Edward Bernays as a primary historical source on public relations and engineered consent, used critically rather than admiringly.
- Oxford Internet Institute research by Philip N. Howard, Samuel Woolley, Samantha Bradshaw, and collaborators on computational propaganda and organized social media manipulation.
- Meta, Google, Microsoft, OpenAI, and government threat reporting on influence operations, with the limits of provider visibility made explicit.
- Francesco Salvi, Manoel Horta Ribeiro, Riccardo Gallotti, Robert West, and collaborators on personalized conversational persuasion by GPT-4.
- Peer-reviewed 2025 work showing that LLM-generated political messages can shift policy attitudes in preregistered experiments.
- 2025–2026 experimental work on AI dialogues and voter persuasion across U.S., Canadian, and Polish election contexts.
- Research on correction, repetition, inoculation, and prebunking from political psychology and misinformation studies.
- C2PA technical specifications and security guidance for the capabilities and limits of cryptographic content provenance.
- FTC work on surveillance pricing and behavioral data as evidence of how detailed commercial observation can be operationalized.

## Source discipline

- Prefer original academic papers, primary platform threat reports, government reports, statutes and regulations, technical standards, and contemporaneous historical records.
- Do not use a platform's statement that it disrupted an influence operation as evidence that the operation would otherwise have succeeded.
- Separate **capability**, **deployment**, **reach**, **engagement**, **persuasion**, and **behavior change**. They are not synonyms.
- Record when a persuasion result concerns a laboratory debate, survey attitude, candidate preference, or real-world behavior.
- Do not infer a population-scale political effect from a statistically significant experimental treatment without discussing external validity.
- When citing personalized persuasion, record exactly which personal variables were provided to the model.
- Treat inferred traits and psychographic profiles as probabilistic and often error-prone.
- Never describe a recommendation algorithm as radicalizing, polarizing, or persuading solely because users who consume certain content are more extreme.
- Distinguish synthetic-media detection from provenance. Detection estimates whether content is synthetic; provenance records claims about origin and transformation.
- Distinguish provenance from truth. A valid signature can authenticate the source of a lie.
- Avoid tactical operational detail that would materially improve covert influence, evasion, harassment, impersonation, or political manipulation.
- For every major claim, keep a note titled **What would make this false?**

## What would make the book's central claim false?

The thesis should be materially weakened if, by the early 2030s, most of the following are true:

1. Personalized model-based persuasion continues to show little or no advantage over non-personalized communication outside narrow laboratory tasks.
2. Conversational AI remains too expensive, too unreliable, or too distrusted to support persistent influence at meaningful scale.
3. Personal models remain too inaccurate or legally inaccessible for political operators to use adaptively.
4. Major assistants adopt enforceable separation between user-serving recommendations and third-party persuasion objectives.
5. Provenance and identity systems make mass synthetic impersonation expensive enough that it ceases to be a major influence vector.
6. Platforms sharply reduce the behavioral observability needed to optimize persuasion loops.
7. Democratic institutions develop rapid, trusted, cross-partisan mechanisms for exposing coordinated influence without collapsing into censorship or partisan counter-propaganda.
8. The public adapts culturally, treating generated persuasion as ambient salesmanship rather than as a trusted interpersonal signal.

If those conditions emerge, the Deceptatron may remain a useful warning but not a defining system of the 2030s.

The book should say so.
