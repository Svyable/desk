# Interlude — Cross Out This Problem

Take a sheet of paper.

Write:

A  
B  
C

These are open problems.

Do not ask what they are. That would ruin the experiment.

Suppose A is famous. B is respectable. C is obscure enough that its Wikipedia page, if it had one, would be maintained by the person trying to solve it.

Now suppose someone proves A.

Cross it out.

~~A~~  
B  
C

Good.

We have reduced mathematical ignorance by one problem.

This is the model people carry around without noticing it.

Knowledge is a checklist. Proof is subtraction. Progress is what happens when the list gets shorter.

Now put A back.

We forgot what the proof contained.

The proof of A required a new method T.

Write T beside the list.

A — solved by T  
B  
C  
T

T turns out to prove a special case of B.

Add B₁.

A — solved by T  
B  
B₁ — solved by T  
C  
T

While proving B₁, somebody notices that T fails for a precise reason on a nearby case.

Call the obstruction Ω.

Add it.

A — solved  
B  
B₁ — solved  
C  
T  
Ω

Ω looks suspiciously like a phenomenon in another field.

A mathematician there reformulates it as a new conjecture D.

Add D.

A — solved  
B  
B₁ — solved  
C  
D  
T  
Ω

A graduate student asks whether T works in a more general setting.

It does not.

The failure is interesting.

Call the generalization E.

Add E.

A — solved  
B  
B₁ — solved  
C  
D  
E  
T  
Ω

Now cross out A.

~~A~~  
B  
B₁ — solved  
C  
D  
E  
T  
Ω

We solved one problem and the page got longer.

This is not a paradox.

It is what high-resolution ignorance looks like.

Before A was solved, some of these questions were invisible because nobody possessed T. Others were invisible because nobody had encountered Ω. Still others were too vague to become problems at all.

The proof did not merely fill a hole.

It sharpened the boundary of the hole.

Try the experiment again, but this time let an AI system participate.

Give it T.

Give it a formal library.

Give it the statement of B and one million nearby variations.

It returns 8,412 conjectures.

Do not add them to the page.

That would be silly.

Most conjectures are not frontiers.

Some are false. Some are true for boring reasons. Some are equivalent to things already known. Some are technically open and intellectually dead. Some are difficult because the representation is bad. Some are theorems in disguise. One of them may be excellent.

The problem has changed.

At the beginning of this interlude, your job was to cross out A.

Now your job is to decide which line deserves to be written next.

You have just experienced the Frontier Effect without solving anything.

The scarce resource moved.

It was proof.

Now it is attention.

Cross out the instruction at the top of the page.

Do not throw the page away.
