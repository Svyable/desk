# The Frontier Effect

*Problems, Proofs, and the Motion of Mathematical Attention*

**Sven Hardy Benson**

Draw a dot for every mathematician currently trying to prove something.

Now draw an arrow from each dot to the problem occupying that person's attention.

The picture would be useless almost immediately.

Some mathematicians would need ten arrows. Some problems would attract thousands. Some arrows would point to lemmas whose names mean nothing outside one seminar. Some would point to century-old conjectures. Many would move before the drawing was finished.

Good.

That moving mess is the subject of this book.

Mathematics is usually presented after the arrows have disappeared. A textbook contains the surviving path. Definitions arrive before the theorem that needs them. Lemmas appear in useful order. Dead ends are removed. Nobody spends six months on the idea that fails in line four.

Finished mathematics is clean because history has been edited out of it.

Research is the unedited version.

A problem sits open for fifty years. People enter. People leave. Techniques accumulate around it. A failed proof exposes an obstruction. A special case becomes a field. A young mathematician decides the problem is hopeless and works three doors down, where the tool eventually arrives that makes the original problem tractable.

Then someone proves it.

What happens to the arrows?

That question is more interesting than it sounds.

The naive answer is that they move to the next problem. Sometimes they do.

Sometimes a solved problem attracts more attention than it had while open.

Sometimes the proof creates a method that escapes into another field.

Sometimes verification takes years.

Sometimes the theorem is accepted but not understood.

Sometimes the problem disappears and leaves behind machinery so useful that the original question starts to look like an excuse.

Sometimes a machine generates ten thousand plausible next moves and the scarce resource stops being proof.

The frontier moves.

Not as a line.

As a distribution of attention.

This book begins with famous problems because they make the motion visible. Hilbert's list. Erdős's traveling market of questions. Fermat's impossible margin. Poincaré. Bounded prime gaps. They are unusually bright objects in a much larger sky.

But the thesis is not that famous problems drive mathematics.

The stronger claim is stranger.

Open problems are pieces of infrastructure.

They coordinate effort without managing it.

They tell people where resistance lives.

They create a vocabulary for proximity: close to Fermat, related to Riemann, conditional on P versus NP, an analogue of Poincaré. A mathematician can spend a career in the neighborhood of a problem without expecting to solve the problem itself.

Then the neighborhood changes.

The proof lands.

Or the bound falls.

Or an impossibility theorem shows that the requested algorithm cannot exist.

Or an independence result says the question was asking more of the axioms than the axioms can answer.

The status change is local.

The consequences are not.

That is the Frontier Effect.

I do not know yet how large it is.

That matters.

The book is not going to define a grand law and spend three hundred pages admiring it. The claim has to survive contact with data. If famous solutions do not redirect authors, citations, methods, seminar attention, proof infrastructure, or problem choice in measurable ways, then the thesis needs to shrink.

Good.

A theory that cannot lose is decoration.

The interesting possibility is that the effect exists but changes sign.

A breakthrough may release attention.

It may also recruit attention.

Yitang Zhang's 2013 result on bounded gaps between primes is a near-perfect example. Before Zhang, nobody knew whether infinitely many consecutive primes stayed within any fixed finite distance. Zhang proved that some fixed bound existed and gave seventy million.

Seventy million sounds terrible.

It was magnificent.

The important distance was not from 70,000,000 to 2.

It was from infinity to finite.

Then the number collapsed.

Thousands.

Hundreds.

Different proof architecture.

Different people.

Different tempo.

The frontier did not advance like an army.

It changed phase.

That phrase—changed phase—will return.

So will the number seventy million.

So will a margin in a seventeenth-century book.

So will a tiny cash prize from Paul Erdős.

So will a proof with a hole in it.

These are not decorations. They are instruments.

A good mathematical object can be looked at more than once and refuse to mean the same thing.

AI enters late in the book on purpose.

The temptation is to begin there because the machines are new and therefore loud.

But the phenomenon is older than computers.

Mathematics has always had more possible work than mathematicians.

It has always needed ways to allocate attention.

Problem lists do it.

Prizes do it.

Seminars do it.

Reputations do it.

Journals do it.

Taste does it.

A theorem does it.

AI changes the rate and the scale.

If machines can generate candidate proofs, conjectures, constructions, counterexamples and formal derivations much faster than human researchers can absorb them, then mathematical scarcity does not disappear.

It migrates.

From generation to verification.

From verification to interpretation.

From interpretation to selection.

From finding an answer to deciding which answer changes the map.

That migration may be the most important part of AI mathematics.

Not because machines will make mathematicians obsolete.

Because they may make mathematical attention visible as a resource.

This is a book about what happens when impossibility becomes less durable.

It is also a book about why some impossibilities are worth keeping around for a while.

Cross out a problem.

Watch where the arrows go.
