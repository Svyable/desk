# The Frontier Effect

*Problems, Proofs, and the Motion of Mathematical Attention*

**Sven Hardy Benson**

A frontier is not a line on a map. It is a concentration of unfinished business.

For a century, a mathematical problem can absorb careers without being solved. It can organize seminars, create technical dialects, reward particular forms of cleverness, repel others, and sit in the minds of people who are not working on it at all. Then somebody proves the thing.

The theorem survives. The frontier does not.

What happens next is the subject of this book.

The usual history of mathematics follows the winner into the room: the proof, the mathematician, the argument, the prize. I want to follow everybody else leaving the room. Where does the attention go? Which neighboring questions suddenly become tractable? Which techniques escape the problem that forced them into existence? Which researchers lose the mountain they had spent a career climbing? Which younger researchers decide that a different mountain now looks possible?

AI makes this movement harder to ignore because the interval between frontiers may be shrinking. Machines can generate candidates, formalize statements, search proof spaces, test constructions and, increasingly, contribute genuinely new mathematical objects. The scarce thing does not disappear. It moves.

That movement is the frontier effect.
