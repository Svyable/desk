# The Erdős Market

Paul Erdős did not need a foundation to make a problem famous.

He needed a table, a mathematician, and a question that refused to leave the room.

There is a difference between a problem list and a problem market. Hilbert's list was a monument. Erdős's problems moved. They crossed borders in conversations, letters, conferences, hotel lobbies, living rooms and blackboards. They changed hands. Some came with cash attached, not because the money could compensate for the work but because the number itself carried information.

Ten dollars.

Twenty-five.

One hundred.

Five hundred.

Three thousand.

The prizes were small enough to be absurd as wages and large enough to be memorable. By the late 1980s the outstanding obligations ran into the thousands of dollars. Erdős's valuations were not a clean metric, and he was not consistent enough for anyone to mistake them for one. But that was part of their charm. A prize told you that Erdős had thought about the problem long enough to put a number on his frustration.

The money was metadata.

This is a useful model for the Frontier Effect because the hard part of mathematics is not only solving problems. It is deciding which problems deserve to occupy a mind.

That selection is everywhere and almost invisible.

A researcher has more open questions available than years to pursue them. Even inside one narrow specialty there are conjectures inherited from older papers, technical gaps that might matter, generalizations that look plausible, examples that behave suspiciously, methods that ought to work but do not, and fashionable questions that arrive already carrying prestige. Most will never be attacked by that researcher. The first mathematical act is often not proof. It is refusal.

Erdős became unusually influential at this earlier stage.

He posed problems that were portable.

Portability is a technical virtue in a problem. The statement has to survive travel from one mind to another. It has to be precise enough to be attacked, open enough to admit different methods, and interesting enough that a mathematician will keep thinking about it after Erdős has left for the next city.

This sounds obvious until one looks at how much research fails the portability test. Many open questions are trapped inside the paper that created them. They require pages of local notation before anyone can tell what is being asked. Others are important only because a particular program needs them. Some are difficult but sterile: solve them and nothing else moves. Some are broad enough that no attack can find purchase.

Erdős had a taste for the opposite kind.

A good Erdős problem could be stated cleanly and remain nasty.

That clean statement did not make it shallow. In fact, one reason his problems persisted is that elementary surfaces often concealed deep structure. Questions about integers, graphs, sets, sequences and combinatorial configurations could be understood quickly by specialists and then resist them for decades.

This creates a particular kind of attention field.

If a problem requires a decade merely to learn what it says, it can attract only people already deep inside the area. If a problem can be stated over coffee but requires new mathematics to solve, the potential pool is much wider. It can jump specialties. It can catch a young mathematician before professional path dependence fully hardens. It can be remembered.

Memory matters.

The mathematics literature is enormous. A problem that cannot be remembered has to be rediscovered each time attention returns to it. A memorable problem carries its own retrieval system.

Erdős's cash prizes made the retrieval even easier. They created little handles.

The amount could signal his view of difficulty or interest. A problem with an Erdős prize acquired a story. The story did not replace the mathematics, but it increased the chance that the mathematics would be retold.

That mechanism still operates after his death. The modern Erdős Problems database tracks hundreds of problems, source references, status changes, formalizations, prizes, comments and links among related questions. Some entries show who is working on them. Some indicate whether the statement has been formalized. Some record that a problem has been solved, partially solved, generalized or superseded.

A mathematician who spent his life moving from room to room has become a searchable graph.

There is something almost too neat about that transition. Erdős himself was famous for resisting ordinary institutional life, yet his problem culture survives as infrastructure.

The deeper point is that a problem can outlive the person who allocated attention to it.

This is one reason frontier movement is cumulative. A solved problem does not erase the attention that gathered around it. It leaves citations, methods, people, notation, computational code, folklore, failed attempts and neighboring conjectures. The original bounty may be paid. The network remains.

Consider the discrepancy problem associated with Erdős.

In one formulation, assign each positive integer either +1 or -1. Look along homogeneous arithmetic progressions d, 2d, 3d, and so on, and form partial sums. Erdős asked whether, no matter how the signs are assigned, those sums must become arbitrarily large in absolute value.

It is the kind of question that looks like it ought to yield to a clever construction or a clever obstruction. It did not. Erdős offered a prize for its solution. The problem survived for decades.

Then it was solved in 2015 by Terence Tao.

That sentence is true but incomplete.

The route passed through a surrounding ecology of results, reformulations and collaborative work, including the Polymath projects. Tao's proof did not arrive because a community stared harder at the original statement. The decisive route connected discrepancy to a different structure involving multiplicative functions. The frontier moved sideways before it moved forward.

This is a recurring mathematical trick that deserves more respect than the word trick gives it.

When a problem is too hard in its native language, change the language.

Fermat's Last Theorem eventually became entangled with elliptic curves and modular forms. Poincaré became a Ricci-flow problem. Bounded prime gaps moved through sieve theory and distribution estimates. The original statement remains the destination, but the productive work often occurs somewhere that would have looked adjacent, or even unrelated, at the beginning.

A good problem therefore organizes two kinds of attention.

The first is direct: attempts to solve it.

The second is indirect: attempts to understand what a solution would have to know.

That second category can be much larger.

It is also where the most durable mathematics often comes from.

This explains an apparent paradox. A famous open problem can be mathematically productive even when almost everyone fails to solve it.

Failure is not one thing.

There is useless failure: an argument contains an error and leaves nothing behind.

There is diagnostic failure: an approach fails for a precise reason, revealing an obstruction.

There is transferable failure: a method does not reach the famous problem but proves something else.

There is generative failure: an attack creates new definitions, theorems or subfields that become independent of the original target.

Problem culture thrives on the last three.

Erdős was unusually good at keeping this culture supplied with targets.

He also understood, implicitly, that difficulty has to be calibrated. A problem can be too easy to coordinate serious attention. It can also be too hard.

If no one sees a plausible first move, a problem becomes decorative. It sits in survey articles as a monument to ignorance. Researchers may admire it without spending real effort.

The best research problems often sit near a moving boundary: hard enough that existing methods do not immediately finish them, close enough that some partial progress seems possible.

This boundary is not fixed.

A new theorem elsewhere can make an old problem newly attackable overnight.

That is another form of the Frontier Effect.

The problem has not changed. The neighboring mathematics has.

Erdős's habit of returning to problems over decades exploited this. A question that looked hopeless in 1960 might become plausible in 1985 because a new method had arrived. A problem that resisted one generation could be passed to another with a different toolkit.

The cash prize was patient capital.

Unlike a grant, it did not expire at the end of a funding cycle. Unlike a hiring fashion, it did not require a department to believe in the area. The problem simply remained available.

There is an important counterexample hiding here.

Not all attention should move.

The thesis of this book would be foolish if it treated researchers like particles that instantly flow toward the newest frontier. Mathematicians have taste, expertise, obligations and stubbornness. A person may keep working in a field after its headline problem is solved because the field has become more interesting, not less. A prize problem can be ignored for decades because the available techniques make it unproductive. A solution can increase attention by making an area legible to outsiders.

Attention is sticky.

That stickiness is not a flaw in the model. It is one of the forces the model has to explain.

A frontier moves through a population with different switching costs.

For a graduate student choosing a thesis, the cost of changing direction may be relatively low. For a mathematician who has spent thirty years developing a technique, it can be enormous. For a formal proof system, switching between domains may require libraries that simply do not exist. For an AI search system, the cost may depend less on career investment and more on representation, evaluator quality and compute.

The same solved problem therefore releases different amounts of usable attention from different agents.

Erdős's world made one of those switching costs unusually low: conversational entry.

You could be brought into a problem by another mathematician.

That sounds trivial. It is not.

Much of expert knowledge is not contained in the theorem statement. Someone has to tell you which special cases are dead, which papers matter, which tempting attack has already failed, which numerical examples are misleading, and why a small strengthening would change everything. A problem transmitted by a person arrives with compression.

Erdős was a compression layer.

His endless movement through the mathematical community allowed local knowledge to travel. He could bring a problem from one cluster of collaborators to another, notice that a technique from one subject might bear on a question in another, and carry back partial information. The social network was part of the mathematical apparatus.

That is why Erdős numbers are amusing but slightly backward.

The famous graph records coauthorship distance from Erdős. The more consequential graph would record problem flow.

Who gave a problem to whom?

Which problem crossed from combinatorics into number theory?

Which conjecture became tractable when someone recognized a hidden equivalence?

Which attempted solution created the lemma that another researcher needed five years later?

The data are much harder to recover, but that is the graph on which frontier motion actually runs.

Polymath made a piece of that graph public.

Instead of a problem moving through private conversations and letters, the conversation itself became readable. Ideas, failed ideas, corrections, numerical improvements and tactical shifts accumulated in public threads. The problem did not simply have a solver. It had a visible metabolism.

Machines change both sides of the problem at once.

The obvious reason is that machines can participate in pieces of problem solving.

The less obvious reason is that the problem ecology itself can become machine-readable.

A system can ingest formal statements, prior attempts, lemmas, numerical data, proof libraries and discussion archives. It can search not only for proofs but for promising subproblems. It can rank conjectures. It can suggest analogies. It can notice when two local conversations are structurally similar.

If that works, the first scarce resource to change may not be proof.

It may be problem selection.

Imagine an Erdős who can carry ten million problems.

That image is too flattering to current systems. Taste is not a database lookup, and generating conjectures is cheap compared with generating important conjectures. The hard part is deciding which question, if solved, changes the map.

Erdős had taste.

That is why the cash prizes matter more than they first appear. They were not an economic system. They were a record of judgment.

The amount said: I have seen many problems. This one is worth remembering.

AI can produce an ocean of mathematically valid questions. If it cannot distinguish the sterile from the fertile, abundance becomes noise.

And that upstream problem has a name mathematicians rarely formalize: taste.

Taste is not mystical. It is a compressed forecast. A mathematician looks at a statement and estimates, often without being able to articulate the whole calculation, whether the problem is deep, fertile, premature, ugly, trivial in disguise, technically blocked, connected to good machinery, or likely to expose something larger than itself. Experts disagree. They are often wrong. But research communities cannot function without these forecasts because proof effort is finite.

A dollar sign was a tiny public bet on mathematical fertility.

Erdős made his forecasts unusually visible.

The Frontier Effect therefore has an upstream problem.

Before attention can move efficiently from one frontier to the next, someone or something has to identify the next frontier.

Hilbert did it with twenty-three problems.

Erdős did it with hundreds.

Modern mathematics does it through papers, seminars, conjectures, prizes, online collaborations, benchmark problems, formal libraries and the reputations of people whose taste others trust.

The prize system gives a crude historical baseline for that coming abundance.

Erdős could remember and circulate hundreds of questions because a human mind was doing the curation. The scale was large by individual standards and tiny by machine standards. Even then, value came from exclusion. A problem made the traveling collection because it had survived his taste.

Modern databases can preserve far more, but storage is not curation. A million open questions with no reliable ordering may be less useful to a working mathematician than ten questions chosen by somebody whose judgment they trust.

This suggests a quantity the AI discussion usually skips: the ratio of generated problems to adopted problems.

If models make conjecture generation nearly free, that ratio could explode. The meaningful signal would not be how many conjectures appear, but how many attract sustained expert effort, connect previously separate techniques, survive first contact with counterexamples, and still look important after the novelty wears off.

Erdős's little dollar amounts were imperfect estimates of exactly that kind of durability.

AI may increase the number of proposed frontiers by orders of magnitude.

That does not automatically make the frontier move faster.

It may make the bottleneck visible.

The next chapter follows a case where the bottleneck moved in public. One mathematician proved that infinitely many prime gaps were bounded by seventy million. Within weeks, that number began collapsing.

The striking part was not simply that the bound improved.

It was the speed with which a newly breached frontier became common property.
