# Chapter 1 — Los Angeles

Before Ralph Bunche became a mediator, he learned to live inside a contradiction.

The country told him he was a citizen.

The country also organized daily life around the fact that he was Black.

Bunche was born in Detroit in 1904. His childhood did not begin with the institutions that would later claim him—Harvard, the State Department, the United Nations—but with family instability, illness, work, and a grandmother whose influence survived into the way Bunche described his own character.

When he was ten, the family moved to Albuquerque, New Mexico, hoping the climate would improve his parents' health. Both parents died within two years. His grandmother, Lucy Taylor Johnson, took Ralph and his sisters to Los Angeles.

She had been born into slavery.

That fact places only two generations between Bunche and the legal ownership of human beings in the United States. It also complicates every later photograph of him standing beneath the emblem of the United Nations. The international civil servant who would speak for universal principles had been raised by a woman whose own life began under a system that denied those principles at their root.

Bunche did not inherit abstraction from her.

He inherited expectation.

The family had little money. Bunche worked while he went to school, contributing to the household through jobs that included selling newspapers and other forms of paid work available to a boy who needed them. The later public story would emphasize his extraordinary academic performance, and rightly so. But achievement without the material conditions around it can become myth.

He did not simply rise.

He worked.

At Jefferson High School in Los Angeles, Bunche excelled academically and athletically. He debated. He played sports. He finished as valedictorian. At UCLA, he supported himself with an athletic scholarship and work, including janitorial labor, while studying international relations. He again graduated as valedictorian, summa cum laude, in 1927.

The list is almost too polished.

Valedictorian. Athlete. Debater. Scholar.

Biography loves such sequences because they make later success appear latent in the child. The danger is that they erase contingency. Plenty of gifted children do not reach Harvard. Plenty of hardworking families cannot turn excellence into opportunity. Bunche's path required ability, discipline, help, and institutions willing—sometimes reluctantly, sometimes partially—to open.

When Harvard offered Bunche support for graduate study, the Black community in Los Angeles also raised money to help send him east.

That detail belongs near the center of the story.

American achievement is often narrated as an individual climbing away from circumstance. Bunche's climb was communal. People who did not possess his future credentials invested in the possibility that he might obtain them.

He carried that investment into a discipline concerned with power.

Political science in the interwar years was not an innocent field for a Black scholar. The world Bunche studied was formally organized by states and empires that classified human populations unequally. European colonial powers governed enormous territories in Africa and Asia. In the United States, segregation and racial violence coexisted with constitutional language about equality and representative government.

For Bunche, race and international politics were never fully separate subjects.

The domestic color line and the imperial map were different systems, but both raised a question that would follow him through his career: how do institutions justify unequal power while claiming legitimacy?

That is a scholar's question.

It would later become a diplomat's problem.

Bunche's early experience also helps explain why his optimism should not be mistaken for naivete. A man raised by a woman born into slavery did not need instruction in the distance between declared principle and lived reality. A Black student working his way through American institutions in the 1920s did not have the luxury of believing that fairness automatically followed merit.

Optimism meant something harder.

It meant refusing to let hypocrisy exhaust the usefulness of principle.

If democracy violated its own claims, the claims could still be used against the violation.

If an international institution fell short of universality, universality could still be a standard by which to force change.

This was not faith in institutions as they were.

It was faith that institutions could be made answerable to what they said they were.

That distinction would define much of Bunche's public life.

He would later work for the United States government while criticizing segregation. He would help build the United Nations while understanding the colonial structures embedded in the international order. He would mediate among governments without assuming governments were morally equivalent. He would work for peace without believing conflict could be wished away.

The intellectual architecture was not complete in Los Angeles.

The habits were forming.

Work before recognition.

Argument without bitterness.

Ambition disciplined by evidence.

And a willingness to enter institutions whose contradictions were obvious because remaining outside them would not make those contradictions disappear.

In 1927, Bunche left Los Angeles for Harvard.

He was not yet a diplomat.

He was becoming a student of power.
