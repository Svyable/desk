## Reader promise

This book does not ask the reader to believe in telepathy. It asks a narrower and more dangerous question: after fifty years of increasingly formal attempts to test anomalous information transfer under sensory reduction, what exactly has survived, what has failed, and what experiment would finally deserve to change our minds?

The collision is between two literatures that are usually allowed to ignore each other. Ganzfeld parapsychology treats reduced patterned sensory input as a possible condition for anomalous information transfer. Modern perception research treats the same sensory regime as a laboratory for internally generated percepts, hallucination-like experience, adaptation and the brain's behavior when ordinary structure disappears. The same room therefore contains both the claimed signal and a powerful endogenous noise source.

The book's governing distinction is **experience versus information**. A vivid experience can be psychologically real without containing target information. A statistically unusual target match can exist without telling us its mechanism. Neither proposition is allowed to borrow proof from the other.

## Evidence posture

Four classes are maintained throughout:

1. **Verified / documented:** published experimental protocols and results; declassified government interest in remote viewing; established perceptual effects of homogeneous sensory stimulation; documented methodological disputes.
2. **Credible but incomplete:** aggregate statistical effects whose interpretation depends on inclusion rules, study quality, subject selection, independence, or analytic choices; retrospective accounts that are not themselves experimental records.
3. **Disputed / unconfirmed:** reliable telepathy as a mechanism; operationally dependable anomalous communication; claims that particular neurological states open a paranormal channel.
4. **Speculative synthesis:** the `information budget`, `experience-information split`, and `noise-source inversion` developed here as falsifiable tools for deciding what future experiments must measure.
