# The Room With No Signal

The room is designed to remove the world and then ask whether something gets in anyway.

There is a chair. There are translucent hemispheres over the eyes, traditionally made from halved ping-pong balls because science occasionally advances through objects bought for other purposes. A red light fills the visual field until edges become difficult to find. White or pink noise fills the ears. The participant is not asleep and not quite deprived of sensation. The better description is that ordinary sensation has been made boring.

Then the mind starts manufacturing weather.

People see shapes. Faces. Movement. Color where there is no object whose color they are seeing. Thoughts arrive with the peculiar authority thoughts sometimes acquire when there is nothing outside competing for attention. This part is not paranormal. It is one reason the experiment is interesting even if telepathy does not exist.

Somewhere else, a target exists.

That sentence is where the trouble begins.

The target might be a picture or a short video selected from a larger pool. In the classic telepathy version another person, the sender, attends to it while the participant in the featureless room reports images, impressions and associations. Later the participant or a judge attempts to identify the target among decoys. Chance has a number. The experiment therefore appears to have accomplished something folklore almost never does: it has given the impossible a denominator.

You can count hits.

For a subject that spent most of human history in séance rooms, temples, kitchens, trenches, dreams and stories about knowing who was calling before the telephone rang, this is a profound improvement. It is also where an argument can become deceptively clean. A denominator does not automatically produce a mechanism. A significant deviation does not tell us which part of the experimental system generated it. A failure to reach significance does not establish that every proposed effect is absent. And a person having an extraordinary experience in a room designed to encourage internally generated experience is not yet evidence that information crossed the room.

That last distinction sounds obvious. It has not behaved as though it were obvious.

The ganzfeld argument has survived for decades partly because it sits in an uncomfortable scientific middle. It is not merely a pile of ghost stories. There are protocols, target pools, randomization procedures, laboratories, critics, attempted replications, meta-analyses and counter-meta-analyses. The literature contains positive results. It also contains conspicuous failures. In 1999 Julie Milton and Richard Wiseman published a meta-analysis of thirty studies from seven independent laboratories conducted after the famous Bem-Honorton debate and found no significant overall evidence of above-chance performance. Two years later Lance Storm and Suitbert Ertel argued that a broader database changed the picture. In 2010 another positive meta-analysis produced another direct methodological fight, with Ray Hyman arguing that the apparent consistency depended on how studies and outliers were combined, and the authors replying that their procedures were legitimate and the anomaly remained.

This is not what a settled phenomenon looks like.

It is also not what an empty subject looks like.

An empty subject does not usually force intelligent opponents to keep arguing about inclusion rules, independence, statistical power, subject selection and what replication ought to mean. The question is what kind of residue is left after the argument is stripped of romance.

The easiest mistake is to start with the word telepathy. Telepathy is already an explanation. It smuggles a channel into the room before the experiment has demonstrated that there is a signal.

Start instead with information.

A target is selected. The receiver produces a report. A judgment procedure converts the report into a rank or choice. Across trials, the resulting choices either behave as chance predicts or they do not. If they do not, the first scientific object is not telepathy. It is a departure from the null model. Before that departure can become a new faculty of mind, it has to survive every ordinary place information can leak, every ordinary place analysis can bend, and every ordinary way a literature can preferentially retain attractive results.

This is less glamorous than asking whether minds can speak without bodies. It is also much more radical, because it gives the claim somewhere to die.

I want a psi experiment that can die.

Not rhetorically. Experimentally.

A claim that changes form whenever a control improves is not necessarily false; nature is allowed to be inconvenient. But inconvenience has to cash out in predictions. If the effect requires selected participants, specify the selection rule before the data. If it depends on experimenter-participant rapport, measure it before the outcome. If it disappears under automation, decide whether automation removed the phenomenon or removed a source of bias, then design the comparison that distinguishes those possibilities. If sensory homogenization matters, vary it. If a sender matters, remove the sender in matched trials. If the target pool matters, preregister the pool and its similarity structure. If the effect is information, quantify information.

The room itself makes this last demand unusually important.

Modern perception science has become interested in the ganzfeld for reasons that do not require extrasensory perception at all. Uniform visual fields can produce perceptual fading and internally generated visual phenomena. A 2025 review in *Neuroscience of Consciousness* examined ganzfeld-induced hallucinations and the conditions that modulate them. A 2025 *Open Mind* review treated the ganzfeld as a useful bridge among visual perception, consciousness, neural activity and qualitative experience. The apparatus escaped parapsychology. That should change how the old experiments are read.

The participant is not sitting in neutral silence waiting for either a psychic signal or nothing.

The participant is sitting inside a perception machine with the input statistics deliberately altered.

That does not explain away target matches. It does something more useful: it identifies a noise generator that belongs inside the causal model.

Suppose I put you in the red field and you report water, a bright flash, something metallic, a face, movement to the left, a feeling of cold. Later you are shown four possible targets. One is a film of a speedboat throwing spray. Another is a close photograph of a rose. Another shows a city street. Another shows a man cutting bread. You choose the boat.

What happened?

There are several questions hiding inside that one.

Did the procedure prevent ordinary sensory access to the target? Was the target selected in a way that neither participant nor experimenter could predict? Were the decoys comparable enough that one did not simply fit generic imagery better? Was the judging procedure fixed? How many descriptors would have seemed like a match after the fact? Was the trial one of many? Were all trials reported? Was the analysis specified before anyone knew the outcomes? Did the sender add anything compared with no-sender trials? Does the same participant do it again?

And there is a question that has received less attention than it deserves: how much information crossed the boundary, if any?

A binary success label throws away structure. If a participant chooses one target from four, the experiment has a familiar chance baseline. But an alleged communication channel should eventually permit stronger tests. Does performance scale with target-set size? Can the participant recover predefined features rather than holistic resemblance? Does mutual information remain above chance under preregistered scoring? Does the channel have anything resembling capacity? Can information be transmitted on demand rather than discovered statistically after hundreds of trials?

This is the information budget.

It is not a finding. It is a way to stop letting a mechanism borrow more capability than the experiment demonstrated.

The distinction matters because governments once cared about adjacent claims for exactly the reason governments care about strange things: even a weak effect can be interesting if an adversary might possess it. The United States funded remote-viewing research for years. CIA's own retrospective account says the work eventually passed through DIA programs associated with names including STARGATE and that a 1995 independent evaluation preceded the decision not to restore the program. The agency summarizes the problem in operational terms: some results appeared difficult to dismiss as randomness, but the phenomenon was judged too unreliable, inconsistent and sporadic for intelligence use.

That history is important here, but it is also dangerous.

Remote viewing is not the ganzfeld. A declassified government program does not validate every experiment filed under psi. Government interest proves government interest. Funding proves funding. An intelligence service investigating an adversary's claimed capability may be acting rationally even when the capability does not exist. This is especially true under conditions of technological surprise, where the cost of briefly investigating nonsense can be smaller than the cost of ignoring the one strange claim that turns out to work.

The Cold War produced many machines from this asymmetry. It also produced paperwork around things that did not become machines.

So this book will not use STARGATE as a ceremonial stamp reading THE CIA THOUGHT PSI WAS REAL. That sentence confuses institutional attention with scientific confirmation. The government record is useful for a different reason: it exposes the gap between statistical anomaly and operational capability. Intelligence is a brutal customer. A phenomenon that appears across a literature but cannot tell an analyst something specific, timely and reliable is not an intelligence technology. It is, at best, a research problem.

The ganzfeld gives us a cleaner version of that problem because its central output can be counted.

Counted things acquire an aura of hardness. That aura needs to be resisted too.

In the 1999 Milton-Wiseman analysis, the aggregate result from thirty studies did not reproduce the earlier positive effect. That is an important negative result. It is not the end of the story because later authors disputed the database boundaries and argued that broader aggregations produced positive effects. The dispute then moves one level upward: not whether one participant guessed one target, but which experiments belong in the population from which a scientific conclusion is drawn.

This is where anomaly research becomes a study of research itself.

If adding or removing a plausible class of studies reverses the conclusion, the phenomenon may be weak, heterogeneous, method-sensitive, publication-sensitive, or some combination of those. If selected participants perform differently, that could indicate a real individual-difference effect. It could also increase researcher degrees of freedom unless the selection criterion is stable and prospective. If improved protocols increase the effect, that is evidence worth taking seriously. If independent replications under those protocols fail, that matters too. The honest object is not a scoreboard marked believers and skeptics. It is the dependency structure of the result.

What does the result depend on?

This question is better than asking whether psi exists because it can make progress even before ontology is settled.

The same discipline applies to the perceptual side. The fact that homogeneous stimulation can produce hallucination-like phenomena does not establish that every ganzfeld report is endogenous. A brain capable of generating imagery could still, in principle, receive anomalous information. Noise does not prove the absence of signal. But if the experiment is explicitly designed to increase internally generated imagery, then any claim about anomalous information has to demonstrate target-specific structure against that imagery, not merely point to vivid subjective experience.

Experience and information have been allowed to hold hands for too long.

Separate them.

A participant says, with complete sincerity, that an image arrived unlike ordinary imagination. That is evidence about phenomenology. It may be valuable evidence. It is not yet evidence about the target. A participant selects targets above chance across a preregistered series. That is evidence about statistical association. It is not yet evidence about the subjective feeling that accompanied correct trials, and it is not yet evidence of telepathy. A sender is present. That is a feature of the protocol. It is not yet evidence the sender caused the result.

The chain has to be earned link by link.

The most interesting future experiment may therefore look less mystical than the classic room. It would be adversarially boring. Large enough to detect the effect size actually claimed. Multi-laboratory. Preregistered. Raw trial-level data public. Target generation and judging isolated. Decoy similarity measured rather than assumed. Sender and no-sender conditions randomized. Ganzfeld and non-ganzfeld conditions crossed. Participant-selection rules frozen in advance. Independent teams permitted to specify rival analyses before the blind is broken. The experiment should contain positive controls showing that the apparatus can detect ordinary information leakage when deliberately introduced and negative controls showing that the analysis does not manufacture target information where none exists.

Most important, it should define what result would count against the favored hypothesis.

Parapsychology is often accused of having survived by explaining failure after the fact. Skeptical research can commit the mirror error: designing experiments too weak to see the claimed effect and then treating noise as decisive absence. Both sides can become skilled at making their worldview unfalsifiable.

A good experiment is a small treaty against that impulse.

There is another reason to return to this peculiar room now. Neuroscience has become increasingly comfortable studying hallucination, predictive processing, perceptual priors, internally generated imagery and the constructive nature of experience. None of this proves psi. But it makes the old apparatus scientifically richer. The ganzfeld is no longer interesting only if telepathy works. It is a machine for making the border between world and model visible.

Put a person in a world with fewer edges and the brain does not politely stop perceiving. It goes looking for structure.

That fact creates the deepest tension in the book.

If anomalous information transfer exists, the brain receiving it would still have to turn whatever arrived into experience. The alleged signal would enter a system already guessing, completing, associating and inventing. The receiver would not be a radio. The receiver would be a nervous system trying to explain itself to itself.

This suggests a possibility that believers and skeptics should both dislike: even a real anomalous channel might look statistically weak because the biological decoder is terrible, while a nonexistent channel might look intermittently suggestive because the decoder is so good at generating meaningful material from ambiguity.

Those two worlds can produce disturbingly similar datasets.

That is why the argument cannot be settled by atmosphere, authority or one famous meta-analysis. It has to be settled by experiments designed to separate those worlds.

The room with no signal is therefore badly named.

There is always signal in it. Blood pressure. Breathing. Memory. expectation. The hiss in the headphones. Retinal noise. The geometry of the target pool. The experimenter's decisions. The participant's habits of association. The statistical machinery waiting outside. If telepathy is real, its signal must survive among all of these without being quietly constructed from them.

That is a much harder claim than saying people sometimes know things they should not know.

It is also a better one.

Because if something crosses that room after we have learned to count everything else crossing it, then we will finally have earned the right to be surprised.
