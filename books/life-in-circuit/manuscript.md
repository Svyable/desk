# Life in Circuit

## How bacteria discovered electricity before animals had nerves

### Chapter 1 — Cut the Wire

The experiment I keep coming back to involves a laser and a bacterium long enough to deserve the word *cable*.

Not long by our standards. Long by bacterial standards. A filament can stretch through sediment across distances that make an individual microbial cell look less like an organism than a component. One end occupies chemistry the other end cannot reach. Deep in mud there is sulfide and no oxygen. Near the surface there is oxygen and no sulfide. Textbook metabolism likes its reactants locally available. Cable bacteria refuse the arrangement.

They bridge it electrically.

Electrons released by sulfide oxidation in deeper anoxic sediment travel along the filament toward cells with access to an electron acceptor nearer the surface. The surrounding pore water completes the circuit ionically. Researchers can measure the resulting electric potential gradient. They can watch the geochemistry reorganize around the current. This is not a journalist saying bacteria are "basically wires." There is a wire-shaped organism and there is an electrical current.

Then, in work published in 2023, researchers found something stranger gathered around it.

Diverse bacteria clustered tightly around the anoxic portion of cable bacteria that retained an electrical connection to oxygen. The investigators cut the cable filament with a laser. The flock dispersed immediately. Raman measurements indicated that bacteria nearer the intact cable were more oxidized, while physical contact appeared uncommon. The authors proposed that some surrounding organisms might be transferring electrons through unidentified soluble intermediates to the cable and thereby gaining indirect respiratory access to oxygen they could not themselves reach.

That last mechanism remains a proposal. Keep the verbs straight. The flock was observed. Its response to severing was observed. Redox state was measured. The exact electron-transfer mechanism was not.

But stop there for a moment.

A centimeter-scale living conductor had been physically severed and a community organized around its electrical state changed behavior.

If this happened in a brain, nobody would struggle to find language dramatic enough.

Because it happens in mud, the temptation runs the other way. Reduce it to metabolism. Call it a clever redox trick. Put the bacterium back into the chapter where microbes eat sulfur and continue toward animals, neurons and the supposedly interesting part of life.

I think that is a mistake.

Not because cable bacteria are tiny brains. They are not known to be. Not because electricity implies consciousness. It does not. The mistake is believing that "metabolism" makes the phenomenon conceptually small.

Metabolism is the original engineering problem of life. An organism has to obtain electrons from somewhere, dispose of them somewhere else, capture usable free energy in between, keep chemistry from equilibrating itself into death, and do all of this inside an environment that refuses to arrange donor and acceptor conveniently beside one another.

Cable bacteria solve part of that problem by making distance electrical.

That sentence deserves more attention than it usually gets.

A redox reaction can be split across space. The organism does not need the chemistry of its electron source and sink to occupy the same microscopic neighborhood. It can connect them. The sediment becomes something closer to a circuit board than the familiar soup of metabolites in a petri dish—not because the mud stops being chemistry, but because electrical current changes which pieces of chemistry can interact across distance.

In freshwater sediment, investigators measured the expected pH and electric-field signatures and found cable-like Desulfobulbaceae filaments where separated anodic and cathodic reactions were electrically coupled. In marine systems, electric-potential microelectrodes measured millivolt-scale gradients across millimeters to centimeters of active sediment. Experiments under photosynthetic biofilms showed that changes in oxygen production at the surface could produce rapid, repeatable changes in sulfide centimeters below.

Sunlight changed. Oxygen changed. Deep mud answered.

Not through a root. Not through a blood vessel. Through a microbial electrical connection.

This book begins there because the phenomenon punctures several habits at once.

We think electricity in biology means nerves. It does not.

We think long-range coordination in an organism requires an animal-style signaling system. It does not necessarily.

We think respiration is something a cell does with chemicals in its immediate environment. Not always.

And we often imagine an extraterrestrial biosphere by asking what molecules its organisms might eat. We may need to ask what redox zones they can wire together.

That last move is speculation. The cable bacterium in the mud is not.

The distinction will matter on nearly every page that follows.

### A circuit is not a nervous system

Electricity is dangerous prose.

The moment biological current appears, neurons sneak into the paragraph. Networks become communication. Communication becomes information processing. Information processing becomes intelligence. Soon a sulfide-oxidizing bacterium is contemplating existence because somebody measured microvolts in mud.

No.

An electron current can do metabolic work without carrying a symbolic message. A voltage gradient can be physiologically important without encoding a representation. Coordinated behavior can emerge from shared electrochemical constraints without requiring a central processor, subjective experience, or anything resembling thought.

The interesting version survives after all of those claims are removed.

Life has repeatedly evolved ways to export electron transfer beyond the conventional boundary of a cell. *Geobacter sulfurreducens* became famous when conductive-probe measurements supported the idea that pili could participate in extracellular electron transfer to iron oxides. The field subsequently became more complicated—which is what healthy fields do. Cytochrome filaments, pili, extracellular matrices and structural effects now compete or cooperate in different models and preparations. "Bacterial nanowire" turned out not to name one simple universal device.

Good.

A mystery becoming molecularly untidy is often a sign that we have reached biology rather than branding.

Cable bacteria present the distance problem in its most theatrical form because centimeters are enormous on the microbial scale. Their filaments can contain thousands of cells. Longitudinal conductive structures run along the filament envelope. The organism can position itself across opposing redox zones and move to maintain the connection as those zones shift.

It is alive, so the wire crawls.

### The animal bias

A human neuron may extend an axon far beyond the dimensions of its cell body. We recognize immediately that geometry matters. A meter of axon changes what an organism can coordinate.

Yet when bacteria create conductive architecture across centimeters, we tend to file the result under an exotic metabolic pathway.

Part of this is justified. Neural signaling and cable-bacterium conduction are physically and functionally different. Neurons propagate electrochemical signals through excitable membranes. Cable bacteria conduct respiratory electrons connecting redox half-reactions. Collapsing the two would destroy precisely what is interesting about both.

But part of our reaction is historical vanity. We inherited a ladder of life in which bacteria sit at the bottom as bags of chemistry and animals eventually discover communication, coordination, electricity, social organization and cleverness. Modern microbiology keeps sawing pieces off that ladder.

Quorum sensing did it to communication.

Biofilms did it to collective architecture.

Horizontal gene transfer did it to the tidy tree.

Extracellular electron transfer does it to the electrical boundary of metabolism.

The question is not whether bacteria secretly possess our accomplishments. The question is how many things we named after ourselves were actually much older design spaces.

Electricity is one of them.

### What the mud can change

Cable bacteria do not merely move electrons without consequence. Their metabolism can restructure sediments. By coupling sulfide oxidation to remote oxygen reduction, they alter pH and iron-sulfur cycling. Field work in seasonally hypoxic basins has shown how their activity can build pools of oxidized iron minerals capable of binding sulfide, delaying the onset of euxinic conditions.

A filament measured in micrometers across can participate in chemistry that changes whether toxic sulfide reaches the overlying water.

Scale is slippery in biology.

The organism is microscopic. The circuit is macroscopic relative to the organism. The geochemical consequence can reach an ecosystem.

This matters for astrobiology for a simple reason. Planetary habitability is often discussed as a map of chemical ingredients: water, carbon, electron donors, electron acceptors, nutrients. But availability is partly a problem of geometry. A donor ten millimeters from an acceptor is effectively inaccessible to a microbe if no transport process can connect them. Evolve a conductor and the energetic map changes without changing the inventory of chemicals at all.

On another world, the question "Is there enough oxidant?" may be less useful than "Can life reach it electrically?"

We are nowhere near claiming cable bacteria on Europa, Enceladus or Mars. We have no such evidence. But terrestrial biology has demonstrated the principle that living systems can convert spatially separated redox reservoirs into one metabolic circuit. That principle belongs in the set of things we remember before declaring an environment energetically disconnected.

### The book's wager

The wager is not that electricity explains life.

It is that our ordinary picture of metabolism is too local.

The history of electroactive microbes suggests that an organism's effective biochemical reach can extend beyond its membrane, beyond a single cell and, in some cases, across distances enormous relative to microbial dimensions. Minerals can become electron acceptors. Protein structures can become conductors. Multicellular filaments can become cables. Neighboring organisms may organize around access to those cables. Sediments can behave as coupled electrical-geochemical systems.

That is enough.

We do not need to add consciousness to make it strange.

We need to notice what is already there.
