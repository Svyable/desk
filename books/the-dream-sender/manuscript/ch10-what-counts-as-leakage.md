# What Counts as Leakage

Sensory leakage is often discussed as if it were a single hole.

It is better understood as a plumbing diagram.

The dreamer is in one room. The target is selected somewhere else. A sender may see it. Staff move between spaces. Equipment creates timing signals. Transcripts are recorded, typed, copied, and judged. Targets are stored, shuffled, opened, returned, perhaps reused. Every transition creates a potential path by which information correlated with target identity could reach the endpoint without telepathy.

Most possible paths will be implausible. The job is not to imagine infinitely many. It is to identify those consistent with the actual procedure and determine whether they could generate the observed effect.

This standard is important because “leakage” can become a rhetorical weapon. If a skeptic merely says leakage was possible, the claim can be made unfalsifiable: there is always some conceivable cue. Good criticism names the cue.

Likewise, “soundproof room” can become a rhetorical shield for defenders. Acoustic isolation addresses sound. It does not address staff knowledge, target handling, transcription, judging, scheduling, or data selection.

The right unit is the information path.

A modern threat model for a dream experiment would start with the target identity and trace every person and system that can access it before the primary outcome is locked. The fewer nodes, the better. Ideally, no human who interacts with the dreamer or prepares the transcript knows the target. The sender, if used, should be the only human with access, and that access should be physically or digitally separated from the collection pipeline.

This is much easier now than it was in 1966.

A server can generate a random target. The target can be encrypted for the sender. The sleep technician can have no access. Audio can upload automatically to a write-once repository. A transcription service can process anonymous files. Judges can receive randomized candidate pools. The analysis script can be preregistered and hashed. Target identity can be revealed only after all scores are committed.

The entire experimental night becomes an auditable sequence.

One could go further and eliminate the sender for some conditions. If the target is randomly selected only after the dream report has been irreversibly committed, ordinary leakage from target to sleeper becomes temporally impossible under conventional causality. Any above-chance association would then be classified as precognitive rather than telepathic, assuming the randomization and timestamping are trustworthy.

This is conceptually cleaner than many older designs because it separates hypotheses through timing.

Of course, parapsychology has long entertained precognition. But a modern protocol can make the distinction operational rather than terminological.

Leakage can also occur through target structure without anyone transmitting the identity. Suppose target pools are reused and the participant learns the kinds of images likely to appear. The dreamer cannot know which target is active but can produce imagery tuned to the library. This may increase general matching performance depending on how scores are calculated.

Suppose certain targets are physically different—larger envelopes, worn edges, handling marks, file positions. The sender or staff may behave differently. Suppose a technician who knows the target asks follow-up questions in subtly different ways during awakenings. The dreamer may elaborate target-relevant themes without anyone consciously cueing.

These are ordinary experimenter effects.

The history of psychology is full of them. Clever Hans did not need telepathy to answer arithmetic questions; the horse responded to involuntary cues from humans. Facilitated communication did not need hidden linguistic competence to produce messages; facilitators could unknowingly guide movement. Ouija boards do not need spirits; ideomotor action is enough to create meaningful motion.

These precedents should make anomalous researchers unusually enthusiastic about role separation.

But leakage is not the only ordinary explanation. Statistical and publication processes can mimic information transfer even when the laboratory is perfectly sealed. This matters because improving physical isolation alone may not make the result trustworthy.

A common debate structure goes wrong here. Defenders say the rooms were isolated, therefore ordinary explanation is exhausted. Critics say some staff member could have cheated, therefore nothing is learned. Both skip the larger ecology.

The anomaly can be manufactured after the night.

If many outcomes are scored and only some reported, if target pools are changed, if pilot studies are selectively included, if judges are added or dropped, if effect sizes are computed in multiple ways, or if positive series are more likely to be published, a clean room can still produce dirty inference.

This is why the phrase “fraud or telepathy” is such a waste of time.

Most false scientific results are not fraud. They are the emergent product of reasonable choices made under uncertainty, filtered by incentives. A researcher tries several analyses because no standard exists. One looks persuasive. The journal likes it. Another team copies the method imperfectly. A meta-analysis later treats the studies as comparable. Nobody ever lies.

The resulting literature can still be wrong.

A good leakage audit therefore has at least four layers: physical sensory leakage, interpersonal cueing, procedural information leakage, and analytical leakage. The last is not leakage in the literal sense, but the analogy helps: information about the desired result can enter decisions that were supposed to be independent of it.

Preregistration is a barrier against analytical leakage.

Blinding is a barrier against interpersonal leakage.

Automated target handling is a barrier against procedural leakage.

Physical separation is a barrier against sensory leakage.

None is sufficient alone.

There is also adversarial leakage testing. Instead of merely asserting that the protocol is secure, invite independent skeptics to inspect it before data collection. Ask them to construct ordinary-information attacks. Can they predict targets above chance without the dreamer? Can they infer target identity from metadata, timing, filenames, image dimensions, randomization logs, or staff behavior? Pay a bounty for successful exploits.

This is standard thinking in computer security and almost absent from historical parapsychology.

The analogy should not be romanticized. Human experiments are not software systems. But extraordinary information-transfer claims are unusually well suited to red-team logic because the core assertion is about information appearing where conventional pathways say it should not.

Treat the conventional pathways as an attack surface.

If a red team finds one, fix it and rerun. If repeated attacks fail and the anomalous signal persists, skepticism becomes more expensive in exactly the right way.

There is a final trap. Some believers interpret increasingly elaborate controls as hostile to the phenomenon, as though suspicion itself might suppress psi. Perhaps psychological atmosphere matters. If so, design around it rather than using it as a veto. Keep participants comfortable and experimenters supportive while technical blinding happens invisibly in software. The human environment can be warm while the information architecture is merciless.

That is an experiment I would trust more.

A real effect does not need careless plumbing.
