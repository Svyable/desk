# The Dream Sender

Maimonides, Dream ESP, and the Fifty-Year Argument About Sleeping Minds

Sven Hardy Benson

This is a book about a peculiar experimental object: a dream that has been turned into a score.

At Maimonides Medical Center in Brooklyn in the 1960s and early 1970s, Montague Ullman, Stanley Krippner, Charles Honorton, and collaborators built a laboratory around the new ability to identify rapid-eye-movement sleep. A sleeping participant could be awakened from REM and asked for a report while a person elsewhere in the building attended to a randomly selected image. Later, blind judges compared the dream material with the possible targets. Some formal series produced statistically significant correspondence. An early independent replication did not. Decades later, meta-analysts would still be arguing about whether the collected literature contains a small anomaly, a small-study artifact, or both.

That sentence is less satisfying than either conclusion usually offered about parapsychology. It is also closer to the record.

The four evidence classes used throughout this book are simple. Documented facts include published protocols, reported results, replications, archival records, and the existence of the laboratories and papers themselves. Credible but incomplete evidence includes retrospective descriptions and procedural details that are plausible yet cannot always be reconstructed from surviving records. Disputed claims include the inference that statistically unusual dream-target correspondence demonstrates telepathy or another extrasensory channel. Speculative synthesis includes every proposed mechanism, from unknown information transfer to ordinary experimenter effects not yet identified. The point of the classification is not to hide behind agnosticism. It is to keep different kinds of uncertainty from borrowing each other's clothes.

No claim in this manuscript should be read as establishing telepathy. No failed replication is treated as establishing fraud or impossibility. The argument is methodological and historical before it is metaphysical: a scientific culture reveals itself in the way it handles observations it dislikes, but anomalous researchers reveal themselves too, especially when a small effect survives by changing statistical clothing each time the room gets brighter.
