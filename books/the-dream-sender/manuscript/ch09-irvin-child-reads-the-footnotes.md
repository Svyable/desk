# Irvin Child Reads the Footnotes

Irvin L. Child did not need telepathy to be real for his complaint to matter.

In 1985 he published “Psychology and Anomalous Observations: The Question of ESP in Dreams” in American Psychologist. The article examined how psychological reviews had treated the Maimonides dream experiments. Child argued that some accounts neglected relevant work or distorted procedures badly enough to give readers a false impression of what had been done.

The paper occupies an unusual place in this history because it changes the target.

The experimenters are no longer the only people under methodological inspection. The reviewers are.

This is healthy and uncomfortable. Skepticism is usually described as a quality of individual judgment: do not believe extraordinary claims too easily. But organized skepticism is an information system. Most scientists cannot read every primary paper. They depend on reviews, textbooks, handbooks, trusted colleagues, editorial decisions, and reputational cues. If those filters become sloppy around stigmatized topics, the scientific community can believe it is exercising rigor while consuming inaccurate summaries.

Child’s critique does not establish that the dream results reflect ESP. It establishes a simpler norm: criticism must be traceable to the experiment criticized.

That norm sounds obvious until one studies controversy.

A field that attracts bad claims also attracts bad debunking. Once enough nonsense accumulates around a subject, critics are rewarded for speed. A plausible ordinary explanation may be offered without checking whether the relevant control existed. A hypothetical leakage path becomes a remembered leakage event. A weak replication record becomes “nobody ever replicated it.” A statistical criticism that applies to one series is generalized to all.

Believers collect these errors and make their own mistake: if critics are careless, perhaps the anomaly is stronger than advertised.

No.

A bad argument against a claim is not evidence for the claim. It is evidence that the argument was bad.

This sentence should be printed above every archive of anomalous research.

Child’s article is particularly valuable because he was not asking readers to abandon scientific standards for an unpopular topic. He was asking psychologists to apply their own standards of discourse. That is a more radical demand than it appears. It refuses both taboo and exemption.

The Maimonides work should not receive a pass because researchers were marginalized. It should not receive an invented flaw because telepathy seems implausible.

The article also raises a prior-probability problem that science never entirely escapes. Extraordinary claims should face stronger evidentiary demands because they conflict with well-supported models and because the base rate of error is high. That is sensible Bayesian reasoning even when nobody writes the equation.

But a low prior is not permission to misreport the likelihood.

If an experiment has stronger controls than a critic claims, the evidence should be updated accordingly even if the posterior belief remains low. This is one place where debates about psi often become morally confused. Believers hear “low prior” as closed-mindedness. Skeptics hear “take the data seriously” as credulity. Both can be avoided by separating stages of inference.

First ask what happened. Then ask how likely it is under competing models.

Child was fighting over the first step.

There is a contemporary analogue in UAP research, intelligence assessment, nutrition science, replication controversies, and any field where the label arrives before the evidence. Once a claim enters a contaminated category, readers stop adjudicating individual cases. “UFO” can mean hoax, balloon, classified aircraft, sensor artifact, astronomical object, or unresolved observation. “Psi” can mean telepathy, precognition, clairvoyance, psychokinesis, fraud, statistical artifact, or a century of incompatible experiments. Category judgment substitutes for case judgment.

The substitution is efficient. It is also where anomalies can be lost—or manufactured.

Anomalies are lost when a real but inconvenient observation is dismissed because nearby claims are absurd. They are manufactured when weak observations gain credibility because a community correctly identifies unfair criticism and then overcorrects.

The dream-telepathy literature contains both pressures.

Child’s paper became a favored citation among parapsychologists because it documented misrepresentation by mainstream critics. That is understandable. But its strongest use is not as a credential for telepathy. It is as a control on the debate.

Read the primary methods.

When a critic alleges sensory leakage, identify the path. When a defender claims blind judging, identify exactly who was blind to what and when. When a review says the studies were replicated, list the replications and their outcomes. When somebody says “the effect has been demonstrated for fifty years,” ask for a prospective effect size and the next experiment’s prediction interval.

This is tedious work.

It is also the only way out.

One reason parapsychology remains intellectually sticky is that both believers and skeptics can find examples of the other side behaving badly. The debate becomes a courtroom where methodological sins are used to impeach entire communities. A fraud in one laboratory stains unrelated studies. A careless skeptic is offered as proof of institutional bias. The evidence itself recedes.

Child’s intervention can be read as a plea to reverse that sequence.

There is another lesson in his choice of venue. American Psychologist is not a parapsychology house journal. The paper therefore entered the mainstream disciplinary record as a criticism of mainstream disciplinary behavior. This complicates simple stories of total suppression. Psychology did publish the challenge.

Institutions are rarely one thing.

A field can marginalize a topic and still publish dissent. A journal can host an anomalous paper without endorsing its conclusion. A discipline can be simultaneously too skeptical in its summaries and correct in its ultimate refusal to accept an effect as established.

The world is allowed to be that untidy.

If I were designing a contemporary version of Child’s project, I would automate part of it. Build a database of every methodological claim made about the Maimonides studies in reviews and textbooks. Link each claim to the primary paper or archive needed to evaluate it. Label the claim supported, unsupported, ambiguous, or applicable only to a subset of studies. Publish the table.

Then do the same to pro-psi histories.

The result would be more useful than another essay about open-mindedness. It would show where the record bends under reputation.

That is the right legacy for Child here. He did not make the dream sender more mysterious.

He made the footnote accountable.
