# The Small-N Problem

Small studies do not merely have less evidence. They inhabit a different psychological world.

When an experiment runs eight nights, each night matters too much. One extraordinary session can swing the result. One participant can become the literature. One judge can change the apparent effect. A protocol adjustment that would disappear inside a thousand-trial study can become the hinge of the conclusion.

This is not an accusation against the Maimonides researchers. It is why their work needs to be read with the mathematics of small samples in the foreground.

Small-n research is common when experiments are expensive. Sleep laboratories are expensive. Participants must arrive, sleep under monitoring, tolerate awakenings, produce reports, return across nights, and remain motivated. Senders and technicians have to be present. Targets and transcripts have to be handled. A dream experiment is not a web survey that can add ten thousand observations by leaving a server running over the weekend.

That practical reality explains small studies. It does not neutralize their statistical behavior.

The problem begins with variance. When sample sizes are small, estimates move around. If the true effect is zero, some studies will still look large. If the true effect is small, some studies will look spectacular and others negative. Researchers, journals, participants, journalists, and later historians are not equally interested in every point on that distribution. The large-looking result gets oxygen.

This creates what later methodological literature calls a small-study effect: smaller studies can show larger estimated effects for reasons that include publication bias, selective reporting, methodological differences, or chance.

Matthew Howard’s 2018 reanalysis of the dream-ESP meta-analytic database is important because he found a relationship between effect size and sample size and reported a much smaller pooled estimate when studies were weighted by inverse variance. His overall r of about .07 is not zero, but it is a different scientific creature than the unweighted r around .20 emphasized in the earlier meta-analysis.

The disagreement does not reduce to one side knowing statistics and the other not knowing them. Weighting heterogeneous studies is a real methodological choice. If larger studies are better estimates of a common underlying effect, precision weighting is attractive. If protocols differ so much that large studies are not measuring exactly the same thing as small ones, weighting by precision can give a few designs disproportionate authority. Random-effects models, fixed-effects models, unweighted averages, robust variance approaches, and Bayesian hierarchical models embody different assumptions.

The correct response is not to pick the method that produces the metaphysics one prefers.

The correct response is to ask what data-generating process each model assumes.

This is where the dream literature becomes a useful teaching case. The field wants to estimate something called a dream-ESP effect. But what is the population of studies? REM-monitored telepathy nights at Maimonides? Home dream studies? Precognitive target selection? Static pictures? Dynamic videos? Selected participants? General volunteers? Human judges? Different scoring rules? Combining them produces statistical power at the price of conceptual blur.

A mean effect across incompatible paradigms can be numerically precise and scientifically vague.

Small samples worsen this because moderator analyses become unstable. If an effect seems stronger with dynamic targets, is that a property of dynamic targets or an accident of a handful of studies? If selected subjects do better, is selection identifying talent or regression to the mean? If believer experimenters get larger effects, is that interpersonal context, analytic flexibility, publication selection, or a genuine experimenter-psi interaction?

Every subgroup creates another small sample.

Parapsychology often responds to inconsistent replication by searching for moderators. This is reasonable. It is also the exact statistical environment in which false moderators flourish. The more dimensions one searches, the easier it becomes to tell a compelling story about why the effect appears here and disappears there.

A mature field eventually decides which moderators matter enough to bet on.

That means prospective multi-laboratory tests. Choose one or two variables from the historical literature. Specify the interaction effect in advance. Power the study for that interaction, not merely the main effect. Use enough participants that no one memorable dream can dominate. Publish the null if it comes.

This is where large-n design changes the emotional texture of the research. In a sufficiently large study, the individual hit loses narrative sovereignty. The effect becomes a small difference in distributions.

If dream ESP is real at r = .07, that may be exactly what it looks like.

An effect that small would still be scientifically remarkable if reliably demonstrated under secure conditions, because the implication is not proportional to the effect size. A tiny reproducible violation of ordinary information pathways would matter enormously. The mistake is assuming that enormous theoretical consequence excuses weak estimation.

It demands the opposite.

A small effect with enormous consequences is the situation in which one should want the largest samples, cleanest preregistration, and most adversarial replication.

The historical literature also teaches another small-n lesson: p-values can become talismans. A p-value tells us how surprising data at least as extreme would be under a specified null and analysis plan. It does not tell us the probability that telepathy is real. It does not incorporate publication bias, protocol flexibility, or prior plausibility unless those are modeled separately. It does not tell us the probability the result will replicate.

In a field with a low prior and a high false-positive ecology, p = .05 is cheap.

Even p = .001 can be cheaper than it looks if many analyses, studies, or target pools were tried and only some became visible. Conversely, a preregistered p = .001 from a large multisite study with a cryptographically sealed information chain would be expensive evidence even for a low-prior hypothesis.

The same number can mean different things because the experiment around it changes the evidentiary price.

This is why debates about “statistical significance” in old psi studies often feel sterile. The central question is not whether the published calculation crossed a conventional threshold. It is how many unseen researcher choices and unpublished outcomes stand behind the calculation.

A historical reconstruction can partially recover this. Laboratory notebooks, correspondence, grant reports, conference presentations, and dissertations may reveal planned studies that never reached journals. Dates matter. If a promising exploratory analysis was later presented as confirmatory without clear separation, the evidence should be discounted. If null studies were actively published, the field deserves credit.

The aim is not to conduct a moral audit of dead researchers using 2026 norms.

It is to estimate how much surprise the surviving record is entitled to.

Small-n also interacts with participant selection in ways worth making concrete. Suppose a dreamer ranks the correct target in the top half on all eight nights. Under a simple chance model with independent 50/50 outcomes, that sequence is unusual. It can feel like a discovered talent. Select that person for a replication and the next eight nights become a test.

If performance returns to ordinary, the field can say the talent vanished.

Statistics says: first ask how many people or series could have been selected.

Extreme performance is more likely to be followed by less extreme performance because selection captured both stable ability, if any, and favorable noise. This is regression to the mean, one of the least mystical forces in science and one of the most frequently rebranded.

The solution is not philosophical. It is architectural. Qualification samples and confirmation samples must be independent. If the same data that nominate a psychic also help prove the psychic, the system is grading its own audition.

The Maimonides era did not have the replication-crisis vocabulary we have now. Psychology as a whole had not yet learned how many apparently respectable effects would shrink under larger preregistered tests. That historical humility matters. Parapsychology was not uniquely foolish for running small, flexible studies. Much of behavioral science did.

The difference is consequence.

If a conventional priming effect vanishes, a narrow psychological claim dies. If dream telepathy survives, our account of information and cognition changes. High consequence should have generated unusually decisive replication architecture.

Instead, the field often remained small because it was marginal, and remained marginal because it was small.

That loop may be sociologically understandable. It is scientifically expensive.

A future study has no excuse to repeat it.
