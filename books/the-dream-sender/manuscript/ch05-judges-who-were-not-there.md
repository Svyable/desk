# Judges Who Were Not There

The most important people in a dream-telepathy experiment may be the ones who never saw the sleeper.

Independent judges were meant to sever the experiment’s social story from its numerical result. A participant can wake excited. An experimenter can notice a striking phrase. A sender can feel certain that the image got through. None of that should decide whether the night counts. The transcript goes elsewhere. A person who was not there compares it with the candidate targets.

This is a beautiful idea.

It is also where the experiment admits what it really is. The dependent variable is not the dream. The dependent variable is a judgment about the relationship between a record of the dream and a target set.

Once that is clear, judge behavior deserves the same seriousness as EEG scoring.

A judge brings language, culture, visual association, tolerance for metaphor, and personal weighting of details. One judge may value literal objects: horse, water, bridge, child. Another may privilege mood: menace, celebration, confinement. A third may connect narrative structure: pursuit, transformation, public spectacle. If the experiment’s scoring instructions leave these weights implicit, each judge implements a private model.

Private models can still average into stable performance. Human observers routinely make reliable classifications without being able to articulate every feature they use. Radiologists, wine tasters, chess players, pathologists, and birders all develop expertise partly through tacit pattern recognition. The question is not whether judgment is subjective. The question is whether it is reproducible and blind.

Dream ESP adds a nasty complication: there is no ground-truth semantic distance between a dream and a painting. The target identity is ground truth. The degree of resemblance is not.

This means the experiment should distinguish two claims. First: judges can reliably agree on which candidate target best matches a given transcript. Second: the actual target is selected more often than chance. If the first claim fails, the second becomes difficult to interpret because the measurement is unstable. If the first succeeds and the second succeeds prospectively, the anomaly becomes stronger.

The historical Maimonides work used external judges precisely because the investigators understood this problem. That deserves credit. One of Irvin Child’s later complaints was that some skeptical summaries described the work in ways that ignored or distorted such controls. His 1985 American Psychologist paper is worth reading because it attacks a scientific failure different from telepathy: the failure to criticize the experiment that was actually done.

Child did not prove ESP by catching reviewers in mistakes. He showed why accurate criticism matters.

This is a surprisingly difficult norm to maintain in controversial science. Once a field becomes associated with credulity, critics can feel licensed to approximate. A weakness that would be embarrassing if attributed incorrectly in cancer research becomes acceptable shorthand in parapsychology because “there were probably other problems.” The mirror image occurs among believers, who may summarize a failed replication as methodologically flawed without applying the same severity to a successful study.

Both habits are corrosive because they turn method into team property.

The judge who was not there gives us a neutral place to stand. What information did that judge receive? In what order? Was the judge blind to target identity? Were the candidate images presented identically? Did the judge know how many targets had already been used? Were transcripts edited? Were associations supplied? Were judges allowed to communicate? Was the score defined before judging began? How many judges were used, and how was their output combined?

These questions are not allegations. They are the anatomy of the endpoint.

A strong modern design would make judging nearly impossible to improvise. The transcript presented to judges would be generated automatically from timestamped recordings under a predeclared transcription policy. Candidate targets would appear in randomized order. Judges would complete the task independently through software that records every rating and prevents backtracking if that is part of the protocol. The primary aggregation rule would be registered. Inter-rater reliability would be reported whether impressive or embarrassing.

Then comes the more interesting possibility: replace or supplement human judges with algorithms.

This sounds like a clean escape from subjectivity. It is not. A semantic embedding model has been trained on human-generated data and implements its own map of similarity. A vision-language model may find correspondences humans miss or hallucinate relationships humans reject. Different models will disagree. Model choice can become the new researcher degree of freedom.

Still, computational scoring has one enormous advantage: it can be frozen.

A model version, preprocessing pipeline, prompt if any, and distance metric can be locked before the experiment. The scoring function can then be applied identically to every transcript-target pair. Human judges can remain as a secondary endpoint. If both independent human and frozen machine scorers favor actual targets above chance, the space of ordinary explanations narrows.

The goal is not to automate belief. It is to reduce retrospective elasticity.

There is a larger lesson here about anomalous evidence. Many extraordinary claims depend on expert interpretation: a radar operator sees an unusual track, a pathologist sees strange tissue, a linguist sees structure in an inscription, a materials scientist sees an isotopic anomaly, a witness sees impossible motion. The interpreter is often treated as a transparent window onto the phenomenon. Usually the interpreter is part of the instrument.

Good science does not remove the human. It calibrates the human.

The Maimonides judges were an early version of that instinct. Their distance from the experimental night was designed to prevent social contagion. But distance alone is not enough. A blind judge can still be biased by the structure of the target pool, the wording of the transcript, or the scoring rule. Independence is a condition, not a cure.

This also complicates the idea of a spectacular hit. A dream might contain imagery that seems uncannily close to the target. Such nights are unforgettable. They are also statistically dangerous because they invite post hoc feature selection. The proper question is not how amazing the best hit looks after target reveal. It is how the full distribution of judged matches compares with what the protocol predicted before the series began.

A field built around anecdotes naturally resists this. The reader wants the night where the dreamer saw something astonishing. The statistician wants every night, including the ones with dental appointments, vague corridors, and no recall.

The statistician is right.

But the anecdote can still be useful if handled carefully. A striking match can help us understand what the scoring system considers signal. It can suggest hypotheses about target features. It can reveal how human raters construct resemblance. What it cannot do is carry the inference by itself.

One of the reasons the Maimonides program remains culturally alive is that dream reports are narratively sticky. People can imagine themselves in the experiment. Few readers emotionally identify with a p-value. This makes the judge’s role even more important because the judge stands between a story and its conversion into evidence.

The same discipline should apply to negative results. A replication can fail because the phenomenon is absent, because the original was a false positive, because the replication changed something important, or because the new study was underpowered. Those possibilities should be evaluated with the same procedural granularity. “Failed to replicate” is not a spell either.

This is why the 1971 Belvedere and Foulkes paper matters so much. It is not merely a zero placed next to a positive. It is a different laboratory attempting a related procedure with a participant who had previously produced suggestive results. To understand what the failure means, one has to compare target construction, sensory isolation, awakening procedures, judging, subject behavior, statistical tests, and sample size.

The judge who was not there forces that work.

If we cannot specify exactly how a match was produced, we do not yet know what failed to reproduce.

That sentence applies far beyond dreams.
