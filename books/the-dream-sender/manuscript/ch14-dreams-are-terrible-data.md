# Dreams Are Terrible Data

Dreams are nearly perfect raw material for a human being who wants to discover a pattern.

They are long enough to contain many possible matches, short enough to retell selectively, emotional enough to feel meaningful, fragmentary enough to tolerate reinterpretation, and private enough that the report becomes the only public record.

This is not an argument against studying them.

It is a description of the measurement problem.

Dream researchers have spent decades developing methods for collecting and coding dream reports precisely because ordinary morning recollection is unreliable. REM awakenings improve temporal proximity. Standardized prompts can reduce interviewer influence. Content-analysis systems can code recurring categories. Multiple raters can estimate reliability. None of these tools makes the dream itself directly observable.

The dream remains an experience reconstructed through language.

That distinction matters enormously in a target-matching experiment because language is generative. A participant may initially report “I was somewhere crowded.” A follow-up question elicits “maybe a festival.” Another association brings up “bright flags.” The target later turns out to be a street carnival. Which layer is the data?

If the scoring protocol allows associations elicited after the initial report, those associations can increase semantic richness. That may reveal genuine latent dream content. It also increases the number of possible target matches.

More words mean more hooks.

A fair experiment therefore needs to decide before collection whether the primary endpoint uses the immediate dream report, later associations, or both as separate outcomes. The participant should not be allowed to generate new target-relevant material after target exposure if that material contributes to the primary score.

Again, this sounds obvious in retrospect. It is exactly the kind of procedural choice that older research programs developed while doing the work.

Dream reports also vary in length. One participant produces three sentences. Another produces two pages. Longer reports have more semantic surface area and may match more targets. If judges simply rank which image “fits best,” verbosity may interact with target discriminability in complex ways.

A modern scoring system should test this directly using null data. Take dream reports from unrelated nights, vary transcript length through predeclared truncation, and measure how often scorers produce high-confidence matches. If longer reports inflate apparent discriminability, length must be modeled or controlled.

The same is true of image complexity.

A visually rich target contains more features to match. A complex dream report paired with a complex target may generate high resemblance by combinatorics. Target pools should therefore be normed for feature density, not merely selected because the images look different to an experimenter.

These are ordinary measurement issues that can account for extraordinary-looking individual cases without accounting for an aggregate above-chance preference for the actual target. That last distinction is important. Flexible semantics explains why anecdotes are cheap. It does not automatically explain a correctly blinded forced-choice effect across many trials.

Skeptics should not stop at “dreams are vague.”

They should show how vagueness generates the reported statistic.

One way is simulation. Build a large corpus of dream reports collected with no target experiment at all. Pair them randomly with target pools from the historical studies or closely matched modern libraries. Recruit blind judges. Measure the distribution of first-place rankings, confidence, semantic scores, and striking anecdotal correspondences. This creates an empirical null model for meaningful coincidence.

The result would teach us how weird chance looks when given dreams.

This kind of null is more persuasive than theoretical handwaving because humans routinely underestimate the extremity of patterns produced by high-dimensional data. Give enough text to enough images and some pairings will look supernatural.

Large language models now offer another dangerous opportunity. Ask a model to explain why a dream resembles each target and it will produce fluent correspondences for all of them. This makes visible what human cognition was already doing: semantic search is cheap.

But models can also become useful controls. Freeze a model and ask it to score millions of shuffled dream-target pairs. Map the null distribution. Then compare locked experimental pairings. Use several unrelated model families to avoid trusting one representational geometry.

The point is not to let AI decide whether telepathy exists.

It is to measure how much “meaning” a powerful pattern matcher can extract from noise.

Dream data have another property: they invite symbolism. A literal match is easy to understand. A dream of a lion and a target image of a lion is striking. A dream of being trapped and a target showing a caged animal can also seem striking. A dream of authority matched to a lion as a symbol of power stretches further.

Where does scoring stop?

The more symbolic latitude allowed, the more the experiment depends on judge culture and theory. Psychoanalytic interpretation, archetypal symbolism, personal association, and ordinary semantic resemblance are not the same metric. Combining them produces an endpoint with no stable scale.

A decisive experiment should privilege literal and pre-normed semantic features in the primary outcome. Symbolic interpretations can be exploratory. If psi depends primarily on metaphor, that is itself a testable claim: pre-register the metaphor coding system and show that independent raters apply it reliably.

The dream is allowed to be strange. The score is not.

This is the paradox at the heart of the Maimonides enterprise. Researchers chose dreams partly because dreams are rich, and then had to discipline the richness enough to count them. Every control that makes the score cleaner risks removing the very associative texture that proponents think carries the effect.

This tension appears elsewhere in consciousness research. Mystical experiences lose texture when reduced to questionnaire items. Pain becomes a number from zero to ten. Emotion becomes valence and arousal. Scientific measurement compresses lived experience.

The compression can miss something real.

It can also be the only reason the claim becomes testable.

The solution is not to choose between rich phenomenology and rigid measurement. Keep both layers and do not let them substitute for each other. Preserve full audio and narrative reports for qualitative analysis. Define a narrow quantitative endpoint for the confirmatory hypothesis.

If the primary endpoint fails, the rich narratives may still be interesting as dream research. They do not rescue telepathy.

If the endpoint succeeds, the narratives can help investigate mechanism after replication.

That sequencing protects the dream from being flattened and the statistic from being enchanted.

Dreams are terrible data.

That is why they are such a good test of whether we know how to build evidence.
