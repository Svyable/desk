# The Result That Would Change My Mind

People discussing anomalous evidence are often asked what would change their mind. The question is useful only if the answer is operational.

A believer who says “nothing, because psi is inherently elusive” has left science.

A skeptic who says “nothing, because telepathy violates physics” has also left the experiment.

Physics is allowed to make extraordinary claims extraordinarily unlikely. It is not allowed to immunize itself against reproducible data.

My own threshold for dream ESP is not one spectacular dream. It is not a p-value from a small study. It is not a meta-analysis of heterogeneous historical work. It is not a declassified intelligence memo saying somebody took the subject seriously. Institutional interest is evidence of interest.

The result that would change my mind is a pattern of locked, independent prediction.

I want multiple laboratories, precommitted protocols, complete reporting, secure target handling, independent judges, and an effect large enough to remain after ordinary error budgets are quantified. I want the primary analysis written before the first sleeper enters the room. I want skeptics to inspect the plumbing. I want raw audio committed before target reveal. I want the effect to appear in new participants and not depend entirely on a handful of famous high scorers.

Most of all, I want the effect to survive a mechanism-discriminating sequel.

If a sender is required, remove the sender and watch performance fall. If the target can be selected after transcript lock and performance remains, stop calling the process telepathy and confront the timing problem. If increasing the target pool predictably reduces accuracy according to an information-capacity model, we have learned more than another significant rank sum could teach us.

A real anomaly should become narrower as we study it.

That sentence is central.

Weak fields often become broader. When a result fails, the theory absorbs another moderator. When a mechanism fails, the category widens. Telepathy becomes ESP. ESP becomes anomalous cognition. Anomalous cognition becomes consciousness effects. The noun grows while predictive specificity shrinks.

The result that would change my mind would force the opposite movement.

It would identify conditions.

Perhaps the effect only occurs in REM awakenings within a narrow time window. Perhaps emotional target salience matters. Perhaps interpersonal familiarity matters. Perhaps none of these matter and the only robust variable is target timing relative to transcript lock. Whatever the pattern, independent teams should be able to exploit it to make the next experiment easier to predict.

This is what distinguishes discovery from recurring surprise.

What result would move me toward the null?

A large adversarial multicenter study with confidence intervals tight enough to exclude the effect sizes that sustain the historical literature. If the study rules out r = .07 or the corresponding forced-choice advantage under a high-integrity protocol, much of the existing case should be reinterpreted as small-study ecology, measurement flexibility, publication selection, or some combination.

That would not prove no anomalous effect can ever occur in dreams.

Science rarely proves a universal negative.

It would make the specific experimental tradition much less interesting as evidence for one.

This asymmetry is important. Extraordinary claims often retreat from falsification by becoming existential: perhaps psi occurs once in a billion dreams, perhaps only spontaneously, perhaps only under conditions science disrupts. Those possibilities cannot be eliminated. They also cannot support a laboratory science until operationalized.

The claim worth testing is the one the literature actually implies: under specified experimental conditions, dream reports contain target-relevant information at rates exceeding chance.

That claim can lose.

Another result would change my mind about a different issue: if a careful archival reconstruction showed that the famous positive Maimonides series depended on a concrete ordinary information leak or undisclosed selection rule capable of generating the effects, the historical case would weaken sharply even without a new experiment.

Conversely, if original records closed procedural uncertainties more strongly than critics assumed, the historical evidence would improve.

Archives can move priors too.

This is one reason the book resists the lazy division between “hard science” and “history.” When a claim depends on who knew a target, when a transcript was typed, or whether a session was included, documentary reconstruction is part of the experiment’s evidence chain.

The same standard applies to testimony. A participant can sincerely remember extraordinary correspondences. A researcher can sincerely report that controls were strict. Those accounts are useful clues. They are not substitutes for contemporaneous records when records exist.

The four evidence classes protect this distinction. Published protocol is documented. Retrospective recollection is credible or not depending on context. Claims of successful information transfer are disputed until independent replication. Mechanisms remain speculative.

Nothing is gained by promoting an item to a stronger class because the weaker class feels unsatisfying.

There is a more personal reason to state thresholds in advance. Anomalous subjects exploit identity. People become “skeptics” or “believers,” and evidence begins to threaten social membership. A surprising result feels like losing to the other team.

Precommitted thresholds weaken that incentive.

If a large study hits, the skeptic does not have to become a mystic. The skeptic updates toward a real unexplained effect.

If it fails, the proponent does not have to confess foolishness. The proponent updates away from the effect sizes implied by the old literature.

Both positions remain intellectually honorable.

The result that would change my mind is therefore less dramatic than the phenomenon it tests.

That is usually a good sign.
