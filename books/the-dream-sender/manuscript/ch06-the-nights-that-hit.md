# The Nights That Hit

Every anomaly has favorite nights.

They are the sessions retold because the target and the report seem to touch at too many points. A dramatic image is selected. The sleeper produces imagery with similar objects, mood, or action. The judges rank the actual target highly. The researchers feel the rare sensation that the apparatus has stopped being ridiculous and begun to point somewhere.

The danger begins when the favorite night becomes the phenomenon.

Ullman and Krippner’s published reports did not rest only on anecdotes. Their 1970 American Journal of Psychiatry paper described three studies and reported that ratings for actual target-transcript combinations exceeded ratings for incorrect combinations. This is the empirical core that makes the historical work harder to wave away. There were procedures, target pools, reports, judges, and statistics.

Yet the emotional force of the research still came from particulars. Human beings are built to notice a strange correspondence more readily than a small shift in a rank distribution. A single dream that appears to contain a target’s central image can feel more persuasive than thirty nights that together yield p = .03.

This is not unique to parapsychology. Medicine remembers miracle recoveries. Finance remembers the trade that called the crash. Intelligence remembers the source who was right when everyone else was wrong. Science builds statistical machinery partly because memory is a terrible sample manager.

The favorite-night problem has several layers.

First is selection. Was the night specified as special before the result was known? If not, it is illustration, not independent evidence.

Second is feature selection. How many elements of the dream could have been matched to how many elements of the target after reveal? A report with fifty semantic features and a picture with fifty semantic features creates thousands of possible pairings. Three impressive overlaps can coexist with a sea of non-overlap.

Third is narrative compression. When a case is retold, irrelevant dream details tend to disappear. The target-relevant features remain because those are why the story is being told. The result becomes cleaner in memory than it was in the transcript.

Fourth is denominator loss. The reader hears about one extraordinary night without hearing about the series of ordinary nights that made it possible to encounter one by chance.

The cure is not to ban examples. It is to keep the denominator attached.

This is where archival work on the Maimonides series could still add value. A modern reconstruction should collect every available target, transcript, judge score, protocol note, and session disposition. The research question would then become richer than whether the original p-values were computed correctly. We could ask how memorable hits arise within the full distribution.

Do spectacular-looking cases receive correspondingly high blind scores? Do later retellings emphasize the same features judges used? Are certain target types overrepresented among famous hits? Do target pools differ in discriminability? Are there nights that look spectacular narratively but score poorly because decoys fit equally well?

Those questions can turn folklore back into data.

There is also the problem of optional stopping. Small experimental series are especially vulnerable when decisions about how long to continue are influenced by interim success, even informally. The historical papers need to be read for planned sample sizes, terminated series, pilot sessions, exploratory analyses, and transitions between protocols. A significant result means something different if twenty sessions were planned and all twenty reported than if investigators tried multiple variants and foregrounded the one that worked.

I am not asserting that the Maimonides team engaged in undisclosed optional stopping. The point is that old small-sample literatures often do not preserve the information needed to rule out every modern concern. The correct label is uncertainty, not accusation.

This distinction matters because controversial fields are magnets for moralized methodology. Critics suspect believers of fooling themselves. Believers suspect critics of moving goalposts. Both sides begin interpreting procedural ambiguity as evidence of character.

Better to inventory the ambiguity.

The nights that hit also raise an issue sometimes called the decline effect in parapsychology: striking early performance followed by weaker later results. There are many ordinary reasons such a pattern can appear. Regression to the mean is the first. Early success may be why a line of research continues. Publication selection favors promising beginnings. Small samples fluctuate. Procedures change. Participants tire. Investigators learn which analyses look interesting.

A real psychological or anomalous process could also change over time. Habituation, boredom, expectancy, sleep disruption, relationship changes, and learning are plausible moderators. But again, a moderator becomes science only when it predicts future data.

“Psi is shy” is not a mechanism.

If boredom matters, measure boredom. If novelty matters, manipulate novelty. If belief matters, preregister belief scales and interactions. If experimenter attitude matters, assign experimenters randomly across sites. If selected high-performing participants matter, qualify them on one dataset and test them on another without redefining the threshold.

The dream paradigm is ideal for exposing this discipline problem because almost every rescue hypothesis sounds psychologically plausible. Dreams are sensitive to stress. Sleep is sensitive to context. Participants differ in recall. Images differ in salience. Relationships differ in emotional charge. A failed result can always be explained by something.

That explanatory richness is a liability unless constrained.

The best scientific fields slowly convert plausible stories into measured variables and discard most of them. The worst accumulate stories faster than they accumulate predictive power.

What, then, should we do with the nights that hit?

Keep them.

Keep the raw transcript. Keep the entire target pool. Keep the exact ranking. Keep the other judges. Keep the failed nights around it. Keep the protocol deviations. Keep the time stamps. Keep the selection procedure. Keep enough information that a stranger can reconstruct why the case felt remarkable and determine whether it remains remarkable under blind alternatives.

A modern repository could make this almost trivial. Every session could be deposited as a sealed record before target reveal. Once the series ends, all materials could become public. Outside teams could reproduce the scoring and build null distributions through massive permutation. Famous hits would no longer float free of their denominators.

That would change the culture of anomalous research more than another theory of consciousness.

It would also make positive results more persuasive. Skeptics often underestimate how much confidence transparent raw data can buy. If a preregistered multicenter study posted every dream recording, every target seed, every judge rating, and every failed night, the usual suspicion that interesting sessions were curated would weaken dramatically.

The cost is that some beloved cases might become less impressive.

Science is allowed to disappoint us.

The 2017 meta-analysis attempted to look past famous individual nights by pooling studies. Its reported positive effect is therefore important, but aggregation introduces a new denominator problem: which studies entered the pool, how were effect sizes calculated, how were small studies weighted, and what publication process generated the available literature? The favorite night becomes the favorite study.

Howard’s 2018 reanalysis is powerful precisely because it changes the unit of skepticism. He did not need to identify a fraudulent dream. He asked what happens when larger, more precise studies receive more weight. His reported overall effect shrank substantially to r = .07, and he found a relationship between sample size and effect size consistent with concern about small-study inflation.

The original meta-analytic authors disputed his choices. Good. That is what should happen. The argument moves from whether a dream “looks psychic” to what statistical model best represents heterogeneous experiments.

It is less cinematic and more useful.

There is a final reason to preserve favorite nights. If a genuine effect exists, extreme cases may contain mechanistic clues. Perhaps they cluster around certain target features, sleep stages, participants, or relationship structures. But this can only be learned if the extremes are analyzed against the complete dataset rather than canonized.

An outlier can be a clue.

It can also be what an outlier usually is.
