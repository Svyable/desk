# Meta-Analysis Enters the Bedroom

Meta-analysis is what happens when a field becomes old enough to have an attic.

The boxes contain positive studies, null studies, different protocols, different target types, different scoring rules, different participants, different decades. A meta-analyst opens them and asks whether the mess contains a common signal.

In 2017, Lance Storm, Simon Sherwood, Chris Roe, Patrizio Tressoldi, Adam Rock, and Lorenzo Di Risio published a meta-analysis of laboratory dream-ESP studies covering 1966 through 2016. They divided the material into fourteen Maimonides Dream Lab studies and thirty-six independent studies. Their homogeneous dataset of fifty studies yielded a mean effect size they reported as .20 and a highly significant combined result. They concluded that the paradigm merited continued investigation and recommended design improvements.

This is one of the strongest facts available to proponents because it changes the shape of the claim. The story is no longer “one famous laboratory got lucky.” On the authors’ analysis, the broader literature contains a positive aggregate.

That statement is accurate and incomplete.

A meta-analysis is not a giant experiment. It is a model of experiments selected by history.

If the historical sample is biased, the meta-analysis can estimate the bias more precisely. If small positive studies are more likely to appear than small nulls, pooling the visible literature does not restore the missing drawers. If protocols are heterogeneous, a common effect size may combine phenomena that should not be combined. If effect-size calculations are not commensurate, numerical synthesis can give false unity.

These are generic meta-analytic problems, not special pleading for psi.

The 2017 paper tried to address heterogeneity and moderators. That matters. The correct way to criticize it is not to say “meta-analysis is garbage” or “psi cannot be meta-analyzed.” It is to inspect inclusion criteria, effect-size extraction, weighting, dependence among studies, publication-bias tests, and sensitivity analyses.

The debate that followed is therefore more scientifically valuable than the headline p-value.

Matthew Howard’s 2018 comment took the same database and applied inverse-variance weighting. His pooled estimate was much smaller, around r = .07. He also reported evidence of a relationship between effect size and sample size. In plain language, smaller studies tended to show larger effects.

That pattern is exactly what one worries about in a literature built from modest experiments.

Storm and several colleagues replied in 2019, challenging Howard’s approach and defending aspects of their original treatment. Their response argued that his reanalysis did not simply reveal an obvious statistical mistake; weighting choices in this literature are contested and interact with heterogeneity and outliers.

This is not a nuisance around the real evidence.

It is the real evidence.

If the existence of the anomaly depends strongly on whether one averages studies equally or gives large studies more weight, then the field has not yet reached the stage where the phenomenon towers over analytic choice. That does not mean there is no effect. It means the effect is near the scale of the modeling decisions used to estimate it.

That is a dangerous place for a metaphysical conclusion.

It is an excellent place for a new experiment.

Meta-analysis should not be asked to do what a prospective consortium can do better. Historical synthesis is valuable for estimating likely effect sizes, identifying moderators worth testing, locating procedural vulnerabilities, and deciding how large the next study must be. It should generate the design of a decisive test.

The dream-ESP literature sometimes treats meta-analysis as a destination: after enough studies, the aggregate significance is offered as cumulative proof. But cumulative significance can grow even while the estimated effect shrinks. With enough studies, a tiny systematic bias becomes overwhelmingly significant.

Effect size matters.

So does predictive success.

Imagine the 2017 meta-analysis is exactly right and the true standardized effect is around .20. A properly powered preregistered study should be able to observe something in that neighborhood within reasonable uncertainty. Imagine Howard is closer and the true effect is .07. The required sample becomes much larger. Imagine the entire residual is publication or design bias. A large transparent study should collapse toward chance.

These are testable differences.

The literature therefore provides enough information to stop arguing abstractly about whether replication is “hard.” One can calculate sample sizes for a range of plausible effects, choose a design capable of distinguishing them, and proceed.

A complication remains: what counts as an independent study?

Research programs reuse participants, target libraries, judges, laboratories, investigators, and procedures. If multiple reports share underlying data or personnel, treating them as statistically independent can understate uncertainty. Specialist fields are especially vulnerable because the same small network produces much of the literature.

This does not invalidate the studies. It changes the hierarchical structure of the data.

A modern meta-analysis should model laboratory and investigator clusters explicitly. It should distinguish independent replications from extensions by overlapping teams. It should reconstruct whether published “studies” contain separate samples or multiple analyses of related sessions. Raw session-level data would make this much easier.

Another complication is quality scoring. Meta-analysts sometimes rate studies on methodological quality and test whether quality predicts effect size. This sounds straightforward and is not. Quality scales can be subjective. A study can be excellent on blinding and weak on power. Another can be large but use a noisy endpoint. Compressing all of that into one score hides tradeoffs.

The dream literature needs a threat-model approach instead.

For each study, score specific vulnerabilities: target randomization, concealment, role separation, transcript locking, judge blinding, predefinition of outcomes, complete reporting, sample size, target-pool construction, independence of replication. Then ask which vulnerabilities predict effect.

If studies with weaker concealment produce larger effects, that is evidence for an ordinary pathway. If effects remain stable as controls strengthen, the anomaly becomes more interesting.

The 2017 authors reported that study quality improved over time and did not find a simple relationship between quality and effect size, while noting effect-size decline over the 51-year period. These findings deserve inspection rather than slogan. Time is a bundle of changing methods, sample sizes, publication norms, participant populations, and technology.

A decline over decades could reflect better controls.

It could reflect regression after early small-study inflation.

It could reflect a real phenomenon sensitive to cultural expectation.

It could be statistical noise.

The meta-analysis cannot choose among those stories without stronger design-level information.

There is something almost comic about using fifty years of dreams to estimate a number with two decimal places. The comedy should not obscure the achievement. Researchers preserved enough formal structure across decades that aggregation is possible at all.

The question is what we ask the number to carry.

It can carry the statement that the literature, under certain inclusion and analytic choices, shows a positive pooled association.

It cannot carry a sender across a room.
