# Morning Comes Anyway

Every dream experiment ends with an ordinary event: the sleeper wakes up.

The electrodes come off. The target is eventually revealed. The strange night becomes paper.

That transformation is what has kept the Maimonides work alive for more than half a century. The investigators did not leave only stories about uncanny dreams. They left methods, published results, target comparisons, and enough procedural structure for other researchers to disagree with them in technical language.

Some of the disagreement favors the anomaly. Some does not.

Ullman’s 1966 paper established a laboratory method and reported preliminary findings. Ullman and Krippner’s 1970 report described three studies with significant target-transcript correspondence. Belvedere and Foulkes published a failure to replicate in 1971. Irvin Child later argued that some mainstream psychological reviews had distorted the Maimonides procedures and evidence. Decades after that, a 2017 meta-analysis reported a positive pooled effect across fifty dream-ESP studies. Howard’s 2018 reanalysis made the estimated effect much smaller under inverse-variance weighting and identified a small-study concern. The original meta-analytic team answered.

That sequence does not spell TELEPATHY.

It does not spell NOTHING either.

It spells unresolved measurement under low prior probability.

There are people who find that conclusion cowardly. They want the book to choose a universe. Either minds can reach across rooms or gullible researchers mistook coincidence for contact.

The record has not earned either simplicity.

The most important thing the history gives us is a design specification for the next experiment. We know where ambiguity lives: target pools, semantic scoring, small samples, participant selection, laboratory effects, information handling, unpublished sessions, and meta-analytic weighting. None of these problems is technically mysterious anymore.

A modern study can close them.

If nobody chooses to run that study, the cultural status of dream telepathy should change. It should move from “unsolved scientific controversy” toward “historically interesting claim that has not purchased a decisive modern test.” That is not the same as false. It is a judgment about evidentiary maintenance.

Claims have maintenance costs.

A result from 1970 does not become stronger merely because people continue citing it. Every decade adds new methods and new expectations. A field that wants an old anomaly to remain scientifically live has to re-expose it to current controls.

This is especially true now because psychology has changed around parapsychology. Preregistration, registered reports, open data, multicenter replication, automated randomization, versioned analysis code, and computational null models make it possible to design against many of the ambiguities that once seemed unavoidable.

The tools arrived.

The question remains.

That asymmetry is revealing.

Perhaps the institutional stigma is still too strong. Perhaps funding is too scarce. Perhaps researchers who care about psi prefer paradigms they believe are more productive. Perhaps skeptics see no reason to spend money on a low-prior claim. Perhaps everyone benefits a little from the dispute remaining unresolved.

I do not know.

I know what would count as progress.

Make the target system auditable. Make the transcript immutable before reveal. Make the judges independent. Make the sample large. Make the analysis precommitted. Make the raw materials public. Make the laboratories plural. Make the result able to fail.

Then sleep.

There is a temptation at the end of a book like this to widen into philosophy of consciousness. Dreams already feel close to the border between private reality and public measurement. Telepathy adds another border. One could end with Jung, Freud, quantum nonlocality, panpsychism, or the ancient idea that dreams open doors ordinary waking cognition keeps shut.

I am going to resist.

The odd fact that deserves our attention is smaller and harder.

Researchers put sleeping people in a laboratory, selected images elsewhere, collected dream reports, and sometimes obtained target correspondences that their statistical procedures treated as unlikely under chance. Another laboratory failed to reproduce one important result. Later analysts can still make the pooled effect grow or shrink by defensible choices about how studies should be weighted.

That is enough mystery for one room.

The disciplined response to a weak anomaly is not to starve it of imagination. It is to starve it of escape routes.

If something remains after that, we can afford to become strange.
