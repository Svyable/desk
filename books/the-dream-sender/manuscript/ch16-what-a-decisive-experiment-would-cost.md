# What a Decisive Experiment Would Cost

A decisive dream-ESP experiment would be expensive by the standards of parapsychology and cheap by the standards of science.

That mismatch is part of the story.

The old literature accumulated through small laboratories, specialist investigators, short series, selected participants, and decades of procedural variation. The result is enough evidence to sustain argument and not enough to end it. A modern study should be designed around ending specific arguments.

Start with the information boundary.

No person who interacts with the sleeper can know the target. Target selection is automated. The randomization service is independently audited. The target is encrypted and shown only to the designated sender, if the sender condition is used. Every awakening is recorded with synchronized time stamps. The raw audio is committed to a public hash ledger before anyone outside the collection team can access target identity. Transcripts are generated under a predeclared policy and frozen.

Then the scoring boundary.

The primary outcome is a forced ranking of one actual target against a fixed number of decoys drawn according to a pre-registered algorithm. Human judges are recruited before the series begins and work independently. A frozen computational semantic scorer provides a secondary endpoint. Target libraries are normed in advance for complexity, emotional salience, semantic overlap, color, object count, and narrative content.

Then the publication boundary.

The entire protocol is a registered report accepted in principle before results are known. Every site commits to publish regardless of outcome. Every session appears in the final dataset, with protocol deviations labeled rather than quietly removed. Exclusion criteria are executable code.

Then the replication boundary.

At least five independent sleep laboratories run the same protocol. Investigators include proponents, skeptics, and researchers with no prior stake in psi. Staff rotate through standardized training. Site identity is modeled. No single laboratory can control target generation, judging, analysis, and publication.

Then the sample-size boundary.

Power the study against the smaller effect, not the heroic one.

If Howard’s inverse-variance estimate around r = .07 is close to the truth, the required sample will be large. That is inconvenient and exactly why it matters. A study powered only to detect the larger historical estimates can fail and leave everyone where they started: proponents say the effect is small, skeptics say it is absent.

Design the study so the confidence interval itself is informative.

The budget would include sleep-lab nights, participant compensation, technicians, target-system engineering, independent security review, judge compensation, preregistration and statistical support, centralized data infrastructure, and replication coordination. Depending on the exact design, this could run into the low millions of dollars.

That sounds extravagant until one compares it with the cultural cost of a century of unresolved claims.

Science spends more to answer questions with much smaller implications.

A funder would still be rational to ask why this question deserves priority. The answer cannot be “because telepathy would change everything.” Many low-probability ideas would change everything if true. Expected scientific value depends on both consequence and tractability.

Dream ESP has one advantage: it is tractable.

No new particle accelerator is required. No classified sensor network is needed. No inaccessible artifact must be obtained. The core claim can be operationalized with existing sleep technology and modern data systems. The major barriers are coordination, rigor, and willingness to accept a decisive null.

This makes the problem unusually suitable for adversarial collaboration.

Before the study, proponents should list the conditions they believe are necessary for a fair test. Skeptics should list the ordinary leakage and analysis pathways they want closed. Both sides sign off on the protocol. The document should specify what result each side would consider evidentially meaningful.

The purpose is not to make everyone agree afterward. That is impossible.

The purpose is to prevent everyone from redesigning the standard after seeing the data.

A strong protocol would include internal positive controls. Sleep and dream-recall measures should reproduce known relationships so that a null psi result cannot be blamed on a nonfunctioning laboratory. Target scoring should be validated on intentionally cued dreams or waking descriptions to show that the judge system can detect genuine target information. Randomization and blinding should be penetration-tested.

This matters because a failed extraordinary experiment can fail for boring reasons. A decisive null requires a functioning apparatus.

A decisive positive requires even more.

If the primary outcome exceeds the preregistered threshold, the result should not be announced as telepathy. The next step is automatic replication under a variant designed to discriminate mechanisms. Remove the sender. Delay target selection until after transcript lock. Increase distance. Change target modality. Expand the target pool. Introduce shielding relevant to any proposed physical mechanism.

The first positive establishes an anomaly in the protocol.

The next experiments ask what the anomaly is.

This sequencing protects against the historical tendency to name the phenomenon too early.

Cost also has an epistemic meaning. A field reveals what it believes by what it is willing to risk. If proponents believe decades of small studies collectively indicate a real effect, they should prefer one large experiment even if it could erase much of the historical case. If skeptics believe the effect is entirely artifactual, they should welcome a protocol designed by both camps rather than dismissing the subject as beneath testing.

There is a symmetrical cowardice available to everyone.

The believer can say the phenomenon is too delicate for hostile standardization.

The skeptic can say the prior is too low to justify a definitive test.

Both positions preserve the argument.

A good experiment spends money to make the argument poorer.

It removes explanations.

That is what decisiveness costs.
