# Pictures That Can Dream

A target image in a dream experiment is not just a stimulus. It is a trap for interpretation.

The early investigators wanted pictures rich enough to leave a footprint if information about them somehow reached the sleeper. A flat gray square would be easy to score but psychologically thin. A dramatic painting, magazine illustration, or emotionally loaded scene offers color, action, objects, relationships, and mood. It gives the hypothetical signal many ways to appear.

It gives coincidence the same privilege.

Imagine a target pool containing a bullfight, a harbor, a portrait, a religious scene, a crowded street, an animal, a landscape, and an abstract image. A dream report includes running, fear, red fabric, people watching, and a sense of being cornered. The bullfight may look like an obvious match. But obviousness is not a property of the dream alone. It is a relationship among the report, the target, the decoys, the scorer, and the scoring instructions.

If the decoys are weak, the target wins too easily. If several decoys share features, the target may lose even when a literal element appears. If the target pool is reused, participants or staff may learn its texture. If the scorer knows which images have been targets more often, expectation can leak without any paranormal process.

The target pool is therefore part of the instrument.

This is one reason dream ESP is more difficult than it first appears. Researchers are not merely randomizing which picture is selected. They are constructing the universe against which resemblance is measured. A target can only be “best” relative to its alternatives.

Modern experimental psychology would approach this with stimulus norming. Images could be rated in advance for semantic content, emotional valence, visual complexity, color distribution, number of people, motion, novelty, and memorability. Pools could be generated so that targets and decoys are balanced along these dimensions. Machine vision and language models could now quantify some similarities before human judging begins, though using those tools introduces its own modeling assumptions. The critical move is to define the target space before the dream arrives.

The older researchers did not have embeddings or computerized image databases. They had curatorial judgment.

Curatorial judgment is not automatically bad. It can be excellent. But when the endpoint is semantic resemblance, the curator becomes part of the measurement system. That part should be visible.

The dream-target experiments also expose a philosophical mistake common in debates about anomalous evidence: treating “meaning” as if it were either objective or arbitrary. It is neither. Humans agree surprisingly well about many semantic relationships. A dream of a ship, waves, sailors, and a dock really is more similar to a harbor painting than to a geometric grid. At the same time, human meaning is flexible enough to support connections that were not apparent before a target is revealed.

That is why blinded judging matters.

A blind judge cannot be impressed by the story of the night. The judge sees a transcript and a set of candidate images. This removes some expectancy effects while preserving the underlying semantic task. If multiple judges independently favor the actual target, the result becomes more interesting.

But the judges themselves need calibration. Agreement matters. If one judge sees the bullfight and another sees the abstract image, the scoring system is unstable. A robust paradigm should report inter-rater reliability and should specify how disagreement is handled. Better yet, it should make the primary outcome a simple forced choice with a predeclared aggregation rule.

The historical literature did various versions of this, but the field never converged on a single standard strong enough to make every later experiment directly comparable. That matters when meta-analysis arrives decades later. Pooling different scoring methods can turn methodological diversity into apparent evidence for a common effect.

The picture has another problem: salience.

Some images are dreamlike before anyone dreams them. Surreal art, violent scenes, mythic figures, erotic imagery, religious iconography, and dramatic landscapes overlap with motifs that appear naturally in dreams. If such images are disproportionately selected because researchers think they are “good targets,” the experiment may be testing whether judges can match archetypal dream material to archetypal art.

That can still be done rigorously. It just requires controls drawn from the same distribution.

A strong target pool should be difficult for an uninformed dream report to game. One way to test this is with permutation. Take dream reports from nights when a given target was not used and score them against the pool. Take reports from unrelated participants. Ask whether the actual experimental pairing has more structure than shuffled pairings. If the scoring system produces high apparent correspondence under random reassignment, the instrument is manufacturing signal.

This is exactly the kind of analysis that modern data infrastructure makes cheap. It is also the kind that should be done before one invokes unknown channels between minds.

The target-image question becomes even stranger when the literature moves from static images to dynamic targets such as film clips. Dynamic material contains more features and more narrative. That may make a true signal easier to recognize. It may also make accidental matching easier because there are more semantic handles.

The 2017 meta-analysis reported that the difference between dynamic and static targets approached but did not reach conventional significance. The temptation is to tell a mechanism story anyway: dreams are dynamic, therefore films should transmit better. But “approached significance” is not a permission slip for mythology. It is an invitation to design a prospective test with enough power to decide the question.

This is where anomalous research often loses discipline. A suggestive moderator becomes part of the folklore before replication. Years later it is cited as a property of the phenomenon. The literature grows a physiology and a psychology out of correlations that were never forced to survive.

The safer move is almost boring: freeze the target architecture and vary one thing at a time.

Pictures also force a question about information quantity. If a dream contains enough target-related material for a judge to identify the correct image among eight alternatives, how many bits of information have been transferred, assuming transfer is the right model? A correct one-of-eight choice corresponds to three bits in the crude forced-choice sense. But the dream report is not a three-bit message. It is a rich narrative later compressed into rank. The apparent information capacity of the phenomenon depends entirely on how consistently the ranking exceeds chance across trials.

This is one place where engineering language helps without pretending the system is engineered. A genuine channel should allow us to ask about capacity, error rates, and coding. Can targets be chosen from larger pools? Does performance decline predictably as alternatives increase? Can repeated nights transmit different attributes independently? Can the receiver distinguish color from shape, or scene from object? Does redundancy help?

These questions are devastating to vague phenomena because they make success expensive. A field can survive for decades on a small excess of first-place rankings. It is much harder to survive a demand for scalable information transfer.

The Maimonides program never became that. It remained psychologically richer and technically weaker: images, dreams, judges, significant correspondences, failures, arguments.

That does not make the experiments worthless. It tells us what kind of evidence they are.

The best historical reading is not “they proved dream telepathy” or “they merely saw faces in clouds.” They built an apparatus that tried to make meaning measurable, and the apparatus produced some surprising results. The measurement problem was never fully separated from the anomaly problem.

A modern study could do that separation more aggressively than the original investigators could. Build large pre-normed target libraries. Lock pools algorithmically. Use independent human ratings and frozen computational similarity measures. Include adversarial decoy construction designed to maximize semantic confusion. Publish every raw transcript before target reveal through timestamped commitments. Allow outside teams to run alternative scoring pipelines on the same locked data after the primary endpoint has been decided.

If the signal remains, it becomes harder to call it a judge artifact.

If it disappears, we learn what the old apparatus was doing.

Either result is better than a picture that keeps dreaming on our behalf.
