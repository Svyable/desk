# The Envelope in Brooklyn

The envelope matters because it is where the romance has to stop.

There is a version of dream telepathy that requires almost no machinery. Two people sleep miles apart. One dreams a burning tower; the other wakes from a nightmare of fire. A phone call at breakfast turns coincidence into a story, and the story improves with use. The dream becomes more specific in memory. The timing tightens. Details migrate. Nobody is lying. Nobody has to be. Human recollection is an editor with poor disclosure practices.

The Maimonides dream experiments were interesting because Montague Ullman and his collaborators tried to make the story harder to improve after the fact. The target was chosen from a pool. A designated sender, often called the agent in the literature, attended to the chosen image. The sleeper was monitored physiologically and awakened to report dream content. The eventual comparison was not supposed to depend on the experimenters saying, after coffee, that a dream “felt like” the picture. Typed material could be compared with the target pool by judges who had not been present for the night.

This is the first reason the work deserves something better than folklore. The investigators understood, at least in broad form, that an extraordinary claim becomes less interesting when the experiment leaves too many doors open for ordinary information. They built doors and locks. One can argue about whether the locks were good enough. That argument is the book.

Ullman’s 1966 paper in Archives of General Psychiatry described the methodology and preliminary findings. By 1970, Ullman and Stanley Krippner reported three studies in the American Journal of Psychiatry in which the ratings assigned to actual target-transcript combinations were significantly higher than ratings assigned to incorrect combinations. These are published facts. They are not synonyms for telepathy. The distinction sounds pedantic until one notices how often both sides erase it. Enthusiasts convert a significant result into a channel of communication. Dismissers convert a controversial interpretation into a reason not to learn what the result actually was.

The physical arrangement was almost theatrical in its simplicity. A person slept under observation. Another person, elsewhere, spent time with an image. The dreamer was awakened around REM periods and spoke into the experimental record. The target did not have to be guessed as a code word. The experiment depended on resemblance. That single word—resemblance—contains most of the trouble.

If I show you eight images after you tell me a dream, you can usually find connections to several of them. A city can become any city. Water can become emotion, weather, glass, a harbor, a swimming pool, or the blue background of a painting. A face can become a crowd. A red field can become blood even if the dream never mentioned red. The human mind is magnificent at discovering correspondence, especially when it knows correspondence is expected. The Maimonides researchers tried to constrain that flexibility by using target pools and independent judging rather than relying only on the dreamer’s own morning intuitions.

That design choice is more important than the paranormal label. It is an early attempt to convert free-form human material into a discrimination task. Somewhere in the procedure there must be a forced comparison: this dream report matches this target better than those alternatives. If the actual target consistently wins under blind conditions, something requires explanation. If it does not, the compelling anecdotes can return to the breakfast table where they belong.

The difficult word is consistently.

Parapsychology has always had spectacular single runs. So does every field that lets small samples meet flexible analysis. The value of an experiment is not that it produces a number with several zeros after the decimal point. The value is that the number can be made to survive new people, new rooms, new randomization, new judges, and hostile hands. Dream research adds a complication: the raw material is unstable before statistics ever touch it. Dreams are recalled fragments shaped by awakening, language, attention, and later association. The target is usually a complex image. The comparison is partly semantic. The instrument is a person judging another person’s narrative about an experience that vanished on waking.

This does not make experimentation impossible. It makes the chain of custody longer than it first appears.

A useful way to read the Maimonides program is therefore as a sequence of transformations. A sleeping brain produces experience. An awakening produces recollection. Speech produces a report. Transcription produces text. A target pool produces alternatives. Judges produce ratings or ranks. Statistics produce a result. A paper produces a claim. A review article produces a reputation. A textbook produces memory of the field. At every stage, information is lost, compressed, selected, or reframed.

The paranormal hypothesis enters only after all of those ordinary transformations have been made explicit.

That ordering matters because one of the laziest habits in anomalous research is to start at the strangest interpretation and work backward. If the result is surprising, perhaps consciousness is nonlocal. If consciousness is nonlocal, perhaps dreams are a receiver. If dreams are a receiver, the hit makes sense. It is a lovely staircase. It is also backwards. The experiment has not yet earned the first step.

The equally lazy skeptical move is to declare the subject impossible on theoretical grounds and treat every procedural criticism as interchangeable. If telepathy cannot exist, any flaw will do. But flaws are not decorative. A real leakage path is different from a hypothetical one. A biased randomization scheme is different from an accusation that the investigators must have cheated. A failed replication is different from a successful replication with smaller effect. Scientific criticism becomes stronger, not weaker, when it is specific enough to be wrong.

This is where the envelope returns.

A sealed target envelope is not proof against all leakage. It is an attempt to define who knows what and when. That is the right instinct. The serious question is whether the experiment’s information boundaries were complete. Who selected the target? Who handled the pool? Who entered which rooms? When were transcripts typed? Could any cue travel through scheduling, paper handling, conversation, or judging? Were all planned sessions reported? How were ambiguous nights treated? How many analyses were tried before one became canonical?

The surviving papers answer some of these questions and leave others to reconstruction. Later critics and defenders disagree sharply about what weaknesses matter. That disagreement should not be smoothed into a retrospective verdict. It is part of the object being studied.

The most useful thing about the Maimonides story is that it is old enough to have consequences. We are not trapped inside a press release. Other laboratories had time to try versions of the experiment. Critics had time to identify vulnerabilities. Defenders had time to answer. Meta-analysts had time to aggregate. Another methodologist had time to re-weight the same database and shrink the estimated effect. The anomaly, if there is one, has been given decades to become less mysterious.

It did not become simple.

Edward Belvedere and David Foulkes published a failure to replicate in 1971. Their experiment is especially uncomfortable for anyone who wants the historical narrative to be a clean march toward paranormal confirmation because they restudied a participant associated with an earlier successful series under a related procedure in a different laboratory. The result did not reproduce the earlier pattern. This is not an obscure philosophical objection. It is exactly the kind of event science asks for when a claim depends on a delicate arrangement: move the arrangement.

A single failure does not erase positive studies, just as a single positive study does not establish an effect. What it does is attack the easiest story. If the effect were robust, portable, and strong, replication should have become boring. It did not.

Decades later, a 2017 meta-analysis by Lance Storm and colleagues would report a positive overall effect across dream-ESP studies from 1966 through 2016. Matthew Howard reanalyzed that database in 2018 using inverse-variance weighting and reported a much smaller overall effect, arguing that large effects in small studies were driving much of the impression. Storm and several coauthors replied in 2019, disputing parts of Howard’s statistical treatment and defending their conclusions. Nobody had to invent a conspiracy. The disagreement could be produced by choices about how studies of very different sizes and designs should contribute to a pooled estimate.

This is a much more interesting place to begin than a campfire story about telepathic dreams.

Suppose the Maimonides results were entirely ordinary artifacts. Then the case still matters because intelligent researchers built controls, published in mainstream psychiatric journals, attracted serious methodological defense, and produced a literature that continued to yield small positive aggregate effects under some analyses. Explaining how an artifact survives that much friction is scientifically useful. It tells us about flexible outcomes, judging, publication, small samples, experimenter effects, and the sociology of controversial evidence.

Suppose, instead, that a small anomalous correspondence really survives. Then the history is even more demanding. The effect is not a cinematic mind-to-mind transmission. It is weak, inconsistent, sensitive to procedure, and difficult to distinguish from the statistical conditions under which weak false positives flourish. Any theory would have to explain why the phenomenon prefers ambiguity.

That last sentence is not a joke. It is a constraint.

A new channel of information should eventually become an engineering problem. Once discovered, it should be possible to amplify, calibrate, bound, and exploit. Telegraphy did not remain a suggestive excess of meaningful dots. Radio did not survive for fifty years as a meta-analysis of people occasionally guessing the broadcast. If dream telepathy is a physical or biological capacity, then somewhere between a sleeping brain and a target image there should be regularities that let an experimenter predict when it strengthens and when it fails.

The old literature offers proposed regularities—REM timing, target vividness, selected participants, sender-receiver relationships, dynamic imagery, emotional salience. Some may be real. Some may be after-the-fact ecology growing around a small effect. The way to tell is not to admire the pattern. It is to make the pattern pay rent in a preregistered experiment.

That language did not belong to the 1960s. The underlying demand did.

Ullman’s great contribution, whether or not any telepathy occurred in Brooklyn, was to make the dream report answer to an external target. Once that move had been made, the phenomenon could no longer live entirely in anecdote. It had entered a room with clocks, electrodes, sealed materials, and judges.

The envelope did not settle the matter.

It made settlement imaginable.
