# Nineteen Kilowatts

A mystery becomes dangerous to itself when someone assigns it watts.

In 2002, the EMBLA team tried. Their optical report put one Hessdalen light cluster near 100 kilowatts. Two years later, Massimo Teodorani's long survey gave the same photographed cluster a much lower figure: 18.8 kilowatts, with an estimated uncertainty of about thirty percent.

That disagreement is more useful than either headline number.

The later calculation is not magic performed on a bright photograph. Teodorani's appendix lays out the chain. A Kodak Ektachrome slide was scanned. The cluster image was resized and converted to black and white. Pixel counts were related to the film's density-exposure curve. The calculation used the camera's 270 mm focal length, f/2.8 aperture, a lens-transmission factor of 0.9, an assumed five-second duration, an optical-visibility distance of fifteen kilometres and a source distance of nine kilometres. The last number mattered enormously because inferred radiant power grows with distance squared.

The nine-kilometre distance was not picked from empty air. The cluster appeared close to the silhouette of the Skarvan hill, and the researchers used maps, radar tracks and later three-dimensional terrain reconstruction to support the geometry. They also ran the same photometric procedure on a known streetlight in the photograph. It came out at roughly 317 watts, about sixty times fainter than the cluster.

That is the strongest case for taking the nineteen-kilowatt result seriously. The estimate had geometry, a calibration curve, an uncertainty budget and an object of known distance in the same scene.

It also had weaknesses worth keeping in the room. No scanning densitometer was available, so the film density was not measured directly from the negative. Several quantities still had to be estimated. Most importantly, the number assumes that light radiated in the way the model expects. A source with strong directional emission can make an isotropic-power calculation tell the wrong story while every arithmetic step remains correct.

And then there is the earlier 100-kilowatt result. The 2002 report used a different reduction and treated nine kilometres as a lower-limit distance for several frames; its table ran from roughly 36 to 100 kilowatts. The 2004 analysis did not merely repeat that number with better prose. It produced a substantially different answer.

For a book about anomalies, this is exactly the sort of embarrassment worth preserving.

A power estimate is not an intrinsic property handed to us by a photograph. It is the end of a chain: film response, scanning, exposure, aperture, transmission, atmospheric visibility, range, duration and emission geometry. Change enough of that chain and a light can move from nineteen kilowatts toward one hundred without the photons themselves changing.

The tempting response is to throw the estimate away. That goes too far. Nineteen kilowatts for five seconds is about ninety-four kilojoules of radiated energy. If an event at that power lasted ten minutes, it would radiate more than eleven megajoules. Even an order-of-magnitude downward revision would leave an energy source to explain.

The opposite mistake is more seductive: to quote nineteen kilowatts as though a meter had been connected to the object.

Hessdalen gives us a better lesson. The number is useful because the assumptions are inspectable. A chemical explanation has to say how much reactive material is available and at what rate it releases energy. An electrical explanation has to identify a field, current path or reservoir large enough to pay the bill. A plasma model has to account not only for ionization and confinement but for the observed optical output. A conventional-light explanation has to reproduce the photometry and geometry with an ordinary source.

That last possibility deserves the same arithmetic as the exotic ones. If a distant headlight, aircraft lamp or other directional source can generate the recorded film density under a plausible geometry, the spectacular power estimate may collapse without anything mysterious happening in the valley. If it cannot, the ordinary explanation has acquired a measurable problem rather than merely a rhetorical one.

This is what the watts are for.

Not to make the light sound impressive. To make every explanation submit an invoice.
