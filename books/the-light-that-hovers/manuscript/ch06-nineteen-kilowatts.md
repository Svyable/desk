# Nineteen Kilowatts

A mystery becomes dangerous to itself when someone assigns it watts.

Massimo Teodorani's 2004 long-term survey of Hessdalen reported radiant-power estimates reaching roughly nineteen kilowatts for some luminous phenomena. Earlier EMBLA material discussed even higher values under particular assumptions. These numbers circulate because they make the lights feel physical. A source radiating kilowatts cannot be dismissed as imagination.

But power estimates are where measurement assumptions become architecture.

To infer radiant power from a camera, one needs detector calibration, atmospheric transmission, source spectrum, exposure, aperture, range and an assumption about emission geometry. If distance is uncertain by a factor of two, an isotropic luminosity estimate changes by a factor of four. If the source radiates directionally, the conversion changes again. If the image is saturated, brightness information can be lost exactly when the event is most dramatic.

None of this means the estimate is wrong. It means the estimate is a model.

The useful question is what a kilowatt-scale atmospheric light would require. A normal incandescent lamp turns much of its input into heat. A discharge can convert electrical energy into visible emission with different efficiency. A chemically burning aerosol can carry stored energy. A plasma maintained by an external electromagnetic field could radiate while drawing energy from a larger region. A luminous structure that persists for minutes cannot be understood from peak power alone; integrate power over time and the energy budget grows quickly.

Ten kilowatts for ten minutes is six megajoules of radiated energy before inefficiency is counted.

That is enough to make hand-waving uncomfortable.

Where would the energy come from? Local atmospheric electric fields store energy, but ordinary fair-weather fields are weak. Thunderstorm fields can be enormous, yet Hessdalen lights are not restricted to obvious thunderstorms. Chemical energy in aerosols or dust is possible but must explain replenishment and luminosity. Geological electrochemistry has been proposed, but a plausible voltage source is not automatically a power supply capable of sustaining the observed light.

A serious theory should therefore publish a budget with units.

Energy in. Energy stored. Visible energy out. infrared losses. conductive losses. lifetime. volume. temperature. ion density. If the proposed mechanism draws from an electric field, specify field strength and region. If it burns material, specify mass and reaction enthalpy. If it relies on microwave trapping, specify the source and confinement.

This is where ball-lightning physics becomes relevant. Many models were invented because witness reports imply lifetimes and apparent energies that simple hot-plasma expansion cannot support. Some models move the energy reservoir outside the visible ball. Others use chemical combustion, charged aerosols, microwave cavities or electrical structures that are only partly ionized.

Hessdalen may contain no classical ball lightning at all. But it asks the same accounting question.

The power number is valuable even if later recalibration cuts it by ten. Physics does not require a spectacular answer. It requires that every proposed mechanism meet the measured bill.

A bright point in the valley is easy to romanticize.

A six-megajoule invoice is harder.
