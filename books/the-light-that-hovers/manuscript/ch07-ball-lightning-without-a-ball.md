# Ball Lightning Without a Ball

Ball lightning occupies an unusual scientific category: respectable enough to publish about, embarrassing enough that many physicists would rather discuss the paper than the phenomenon.

Reports are old. The canonical object is a luminous roughly spherical structure appearing near thunderstorms, sometimes moving slowly, sometimes entering buildings, sometimes persisting for seconds longer than an ordinary discharge should. The variation in reports is so wide that one immediate possibility is the same one that haunts Hessdalen: several phenomena have inherited one name.

E. L. Hill's 1960 review treated ball lightning as a physical phenomenon but doubted the simplest hot-plasma picture. Stanley Singer's 1964 Physical Review paper used conservation arguments to show why a self-confined plasmoid is difficult to keep alive at the reported energies and times, then examined a glow-discharge model sustained by atmospheric electric fields. Decades later, microwave, aerosol, combustion and plasma-domain theories continued to compete. Laboratory groups have produced luminous balls that reproduce some reported properties, though no experiment has established that one laboratory mechanism explains the natural class.

That unresolved history matters for UAP analysis because `ball lightning` is often used as though it were a species with a field guide entry.

It is not.

It is a family of observations plus models.

This distinction changes how Hessdalen should be discussed. Saying `the Hessdalen lights may be related to ball lightning` can mean something useful: both involve persistent compact atmospheric luminosity whose lifetime and energy demand mechanism. It can also mean nothing: two round lights are being grouped because the vocabulary ran out.

The only productive comparison is property by property.

Duration. size. spectrum. relation to thunderstorms. electric field. motion relative to wind. heat signature. radar reflectivity. sound. material residue. occurrence near conductors. response to terrain. recurrence rate. A model that fits ordinary thunderstorm ball lightning may fail in a cold Norwegian valley under clear weather.

The comparison becomes stronger if both phenomena turn out not to be fully ionized hot plasma. Weakly ionized air, charged aerosols and chemical emission can sustain light with lower thermal burden. Dusty plasma physics adds another layer: charged particles can organize collectively while the neutral gas remains comparatively cool.

The word `cold` here is relative, not magical. A non-equilibrium plasma can have energetic electrons while the bulk gas remains much cooler. Fluorescent lamps live in that territory. So do many industrial discharges.

That matters because a bright atmospheric object does not need to be a miniature Sun.

It still needs a power supply.
