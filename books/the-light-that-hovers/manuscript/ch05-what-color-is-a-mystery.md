# What Color Is a Mystery?

Yellow is not a chemical analysis.

Witnesses in Hessdalen often describe white, yellow, red or blue lights. Those words are useful for finding an event in a log. They are almost useless for identifying the source.

The human visual system compresses a spectrum into three broad channels and then edits the result according to adaptation, contrast and brightness. A sodium lamp can look yellow. A hot incandescent source can look yellow-white. A plasma dominated by particular atomic lines can look yellow. A distant white light filtered through haze can become warm. A saturated digital sensor can bleach color toward white.

Spectroscopy is the escape hatch.

Light from an excited gas can carry narrow emission lines associated with atoms or molecules. A thermal source produces a different continuum. Reflected sunlight carries the spectrum of the illuminating source plus absorption and surface effects. Artificial lamps leave fingerprints. If a Hessdalen event is truly a luminous atmospheric process, its spectrum should constrain temperature, composition and excitation mechanism.

The EMBLA campaigns attempted exactly this. Some spectra were reported and interpreted as compatible with thermal or plasma-like radiation. The difficulty is that field spectra are brutally easy to overread. The source may be faint, moving and unresolved. Atmospheric absorption modifies the signal. Instrument response varies with wavelength. Scattered light from villages or vehicles can contaminate the slit. Saturation can flatten peaks. Low spectral resolution can merge lines that would otherwise identify a species.

A modern campaign should therefore treat every spectrum as a paired measurement. The anomalous source gets a spectrum. So do known headlights, aircraft lights, stars, power-line corona and local lamps at similar angular elevations. Calibration lamps establish wavelength. Standard stars establish throughput. Raw frames remain public.

If a repeated unexplained line appears, then things get interesting.

Suppose the spectrum showed strong nitrogen and oxygen emission typical of electrical discharge. That would support an atmospheric ionization mechanism but not identify the energy source. Suppose it showed a smooth thermal continuum corresponding to several thousand kelvin while the object remained stationary for minutes. That would create an energy-budget problem. Suppose it matched a high-pressure sodium lamp. The mystery would probably drive away on a road.

There is dignity in all three outcomes.

Spectroscopy also disciplines claims about color change. A light that appears to shift from white to red may be cooling, changing excitation state, moving through haze, saturating the camera differently, or simply changing brightness relative to the observer's adaptation. A time-resolved spectrum can distinguish those possibilities.

The larger UAP lesson is that appearance should be translated into quantities as early as possible. `Orange orb` is a witness phrase. `Emission dominated by 589-nanometer sodium lines` is a physical clue. `Broadband continuum with temperature estimate and known range` is an energy constraint.

The instrument does not make the event less strange. It makes strangeness expensive.

That is what we want.
