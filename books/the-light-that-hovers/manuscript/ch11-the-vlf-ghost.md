# The VLF Ghost

Radio anomalies are seductive because they are invisible twice.

You cannot see the signal, and you usually cannot see the transmitter.

During the EMBLA work, unusual very-low-frequency signals were reported in roughly the kilohertz range and at first discussed as potentially associated with the Hessdalen phenomenon. Later analysis by colleagues in radio astronomy argued that the observed features could be explained by anthropogenic sources, including power infrastructure.

This is one of the healthiest episodes in the whole story.

An anomaly was measured. An exotic association was proposed. A conventional source model improved. The signal became less mysterious.

That should be treated as success.

VLF and ELF environments are crowded in ways a casual field observer may not appreciate. Power grids generate harmonics. switching equipment radiates. navigation and military transmitters can propagate enormous distances. lightning produces broadband radio atmospherics. ionospheric conditions change propagation. local cables couple into receivers. A signal that appears only at one site may be local contamination; a signal heard across stations may still be a distant transmitter.

Modern spectrum monitoring makes this tractable. Deploy synchronized receivers at multiple baselines. direction-find the source. record raw waveforms. maintain a database of known transmitters and power-line frequencies. compare with lightning networks and geomagnetic conditions.

If a luminous event emits radio energy, the radio signature should be spatially and temporally associated with the light. Direction finding should point toward the optical position. signal onset should match. propagation delay should be negligible on valley scales. A radio feature that starts ten minutes later is not evidence merely because it occurred the same night.

This chapter is a warning against coincidence hunger.

When many sensors operate, something unusual is always happening somewhere in the data. The more channels one inspects after the event, the easier it becomes to find a wiggle worth narrating. Prospective thresholds matter.

The VLF ghost teaches a pleasant lesson: good instrumentation can destroy a favorite clue and improve the investigation at the same time.
