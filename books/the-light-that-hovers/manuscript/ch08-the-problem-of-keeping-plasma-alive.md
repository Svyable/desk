# The Problem of Keeping Plasma Alive

Ionize air and then stop paying attention. The electrons disappear.

They recombine with ions, attach to molecules, diffuse away, transfer energy through collisions and radiate. A compact luminous plasma in ordinary atmosphere is a machine with a leak in every direction.

This is why `plasma` is not an explanation for a minute-long light. It is a list of new requirements.

A hot fully ionized plasma at atmospheric pressure wants to expand and cool. Magnetic self-confinement is difficult at the modest currents available in natural atmospheric events. Singer's old plasmoid analysis made this problem explicit. Later models often solve it by changing what the object is: a weakly ionized glow discharge fed by an external field, a charged aerosol whose chemistry supplies energy, a microwave-supported cavity, or a structure where only a small fraction of particles are ionized.

Each solution predicts different behavior.

A microwave-supported plasma should depend on an external electromagnetic field or trapped radiation and may show characteristic emission and interaction with conductors. A chemical aerosol should leave products and depend on available material. A field-fed glow discharge should correlate with electric-field conditions. A dusty plasma should have particle dynamics, charge distributions and scattering properties distinct from a clear hot gas.

This is the moment Hessdalen research could become much more discriminating. Do not ask whether an event `looks like plasma`. Ask which plasma regime could survive the measured lifetime.

Measure the spectrum. thermal infrared. polarization. electric field. radio emissions. radar cross section. wind. aerosol concentration. atmospheric conductivity. If a theory requires a field of a certain magnitude, put field mills in the valley. If it requires dust, sample aerosols before, during and after events. If it requires thunderstorms, score the event rate against convective weather.

The theory should be allowed to die.

That sentence sounds obvious, but anomaly fields often protect mechanisms because the underlying phenomenon is rare. When an expected variable is missing, the model can always retreat into `unusual conditions`. A recurrent hotspot removes that excuse. If Hessdalen produces enough events, preregister what a mechanism predicts before the next season.

A theory of persistent plasma should be especially vulnerable because plasma is measurable in many independent ways.

Free electrons affect radio propagation. excited species emit lines. hot gas radiates infrared. charged structures interact with electric fields. particulate plasma scatters light. A model cannot indefinitely hide behind visible brightness alone.

If the light survives while every plasma diagnostic remains absent, the plasma hypothesis should weaken.

That would make the mystery better, not worse.
