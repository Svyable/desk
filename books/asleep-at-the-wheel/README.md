*How Civilization Learned to Schedule Around a Biology It Never Bothered to Ask.*

## Premise

The working thesis is that sleep behaves like a hard, measurable biological constraint — closer to gravity than to a personal preference — and that a long, well-documented list of institutions built their schedules around convenience, tradition, and status display instead of around it. Medicine trained generations of doctors on a stamina cult founded by a surgeon who was himself dependent on cocaine. Nuclear plants and oil tankers put some of their highest-consequence decisions in the hands of people working the worst hours of the circadian cycle. Regulators tightened rules for truckers, train engineers, and residents only after specific, named, investigated failures. Governments still argue every year over which hour of the clock to take from everyone at once. The book follows that pattern from the 1953 discovery of REM sleep through control-room disasters, transportation accidents, interrogation rooms, school bells, night shifts, the pharmaceutical and wearable industries built to manage the damage, a bestselling sleep-science book that got some of its own facts wrong, astronauts orbiting without a sunset, elite athletes who treat sleep as a competitive edge, new parents who signed no such contract, and the always-on knowledge economy AI is now quietly rewriting again. It closes by asking what an institution actually looks like once it takes its own workers' biology as seriously as it takes its balance sheet.
