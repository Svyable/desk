# Chapter 5 research audit: duty hours, sleep, and the policy-level evidence boundary

Checked 2026-09-14 for *Asleep at the Wheel*, Chapter 5, “Halsted’s Ghost.”

## Scope

This is a bounded evidence audit for the chapter’s residency-duty-hours argument. It does not change the manuscript. The current chapter already avoids the crude claim that shorter shifts automatically make hospitals safer; the main remaining risk is subtler: readers can too easily take later noninferiority trials as evidence that extended shifts themselves were shown to be harmless.

That is not what those trials established.

## The clean early intervention

Landrigan et al. randomized interns in two intensive-care units to a traditional schedule containing frequent shifts of 24 hours or more or to an intervention schedule that eliminated those extended shifts and reduced weekly hours. Interns made 35.9% more serious medical errors on the traditional schedule: 136.0 versus 100.1 per 1,000 patient-days. Nonintercepted serious errors were 56.6% more frequent, and serious diagnostic errors were 18.6 versus 3.3 per 1,000 patient-days.

The intervention did more than shorten one shift. It reduced work by nearly 20 hours per week, increased average daily sleep by nearly an hour, used an additional intern, and included structured overlap for handoffs. The experiment therefore supports the claim that this redesigned ICU schedule reduced intern errors; it is not a pure estimate of shift length isolated from staffing, weekly hours, sleep opportunity, or handoff design.

Source: Christopher P. Landrigan et al., “Effect of Reducing Interns’ Work Hours on Serious Medical Errors in Intensive Care Units,” *New England Journal of Medicine* 351 (2004): 1838–1848. https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa041406

## What the later flexibility trials actually tested

The 2016 FIRST trial randomized 117 general-surgery residency programs to standard duty-hour policies or more flexible policies that waived some rules governing shift length and time off while retaining the 80-hour weekly ceiling. Flexible policies were noninferior for the trial’s primary postoperative patient outcome. Residents in flexible programs were also less likely to report leaving during an operation or handing off an active patient problem.

That result is genuine counterevidence to the simple policy story that stricter shift-length rules must always improve patient outcomes. It shows that continuity, handoffs, workload distribution, supervision, and local schedule design can offset some expected benefits of a tighter rule.

It does not show that fatigue is harmless or that a 24-hour-plus period of wakefulness has no performance cost.

Source: Karl Y. Bilimoria et al., “National Cluster-Randomized Trial of Duty-Hour Flexibility in Surgical Training,” *New England Journal of Medicine* 374 (2016): 713–727. https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1515724

The 2019 iCOMPARE patient-safety trial makes the boundary unusually explicit. Sixty-three internal-medicine programs were randomized to standard rules or flexible rules. The patient-safety analysis found no apparent harm when programs were *permitted* greater scheduling flexibility. But the authors caution that the trial “does not evaluate what happens when the trainees work extended shifts”: flexible programs did not use their latitude uniformly, and both standard-length and extended shifts existed within the flexible arm.

That sentence should govern any future manuscript interpretation of iCOMPARE. The treatment was a program-level scheduling policy, not an individual-level randomization to one prolonged bout of wakefulness.

Source: David A. Silber et al., “Patient Safety Outcomes under Flexible and Standard Resident Duty-Hour Rules,” *New England Journal of Medicine* 380 (2019): 905–914. https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1810642

## The sleep result complicates the easy story further

The companion iCOMPARE sleep study used actigraphy and sleepiness/alertness measures in a subset of programs. Average sleep time was 6.85 hours per 24 hours in flexible programs and 7.03 hours in standard programs. Flexible programs met the prespecified noninferiority criterion for sleep duration and subjective sleepiness. They did **not** establish noninferiority for objectively measured psychomotor alertness.

This matters because it blocks two opposite overclaims.

The trial does not support saying that flexible duty-hour rules caused large chronic sleep losses across the measured two-week periods. But it also does not support saying that objective alertness was equivalent under the two policy regimes. More importantly, a similar *average* amount of daily sleep can conceal different timing, fragmentation, circadian placement, recovery opportunities, and acute extended-shift exposure.

Source: Mathias Basner et al., “Sleep and Alertness in a Duty-Hour Flexibility Trial in Internal Medicine,” *New England Journal of Medicine* 380 (2019): 915–923. https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1810641

## Strongest counterargument to the chapter’s instinct

The strongest objection is not that sleep deprivation has no effect. It is that hospital outcomes are produced by a system rather than by the fatigue state of one resident in isolation.

A stricter cap can reduce acute sleep loss while increasing handoffs, cross-coverage, work compression, or discontinuity. A flexible policy can permit longer shifts without requiring every program to use them, and programs may compensate with supervision, staffing, schedule choice, or different assignment of work. Patient mortality or complication rates can therefore remain similar even when the physiology of an individual prolonged shift remains unfavorable.

The chapter is strongest when it treats that as real counterevidence, not as an annoyance to be explained away.

## Safe manuscript boundary

A defensible formulation for a later prose pass is:

> Extended wakefulness can impair clinicians and, in at least one randomized ICU redesign, removing frequent 24-hour-plus shifts while reducing weekly hours substantially reduced serious intern errors. But residency scheduling is a systems problem. Large later trials found that giving programs more flexibility within an 80-hour week did not worsen major patient outcomes, and iCOMPARE did not directly randomize trainees to extended shifts. Shift length, total hours, sleep timing, handoffs, staffing, supervision, workload, and continuity all contribute to the outcome.

The useful distinction is between **physiological impairment evidence** and **policy-package outcome evidence**. Neither should be used to erase the other.

## What would force the argument to narrow again

The book should narrow further if well-powered randomized or strong quasi-experimental evidence shows that, under comparable staffing and workload, recurrent extended shifts do not measurably worsen objective alertness, error rates, injuries, or other validated performance outcomes. It should also narrow if tighter shift rules consistently worsen patient outcomes through handoffs or work compression even when those transition risks are actively mitigated.

Conversely, future claims should strengthen only when a study separates shift duration from weekly workload, staffing, handoff design, supervision, circadian timing, and self-selection rather than treating a duty-hour policy label as the exposure itself.

## Editorial use

The current Chapter 5 already contains the right conceptual destination: “Sleep matters. So does system design.” This evidence audit makes that sentence harder to turn into false equivalence. The physiology of prolonged wakefulness and the performance of an entire duty-hour policy are different empirical questions, and the most interesting residency evidence is precisely that both matter at once.
