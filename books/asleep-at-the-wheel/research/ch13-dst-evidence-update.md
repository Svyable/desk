# Chapter 13 evidence update — daylight saving time and cardiovascular claims

Rechecked September 6, 2026 for `ch13-losing-an-hour-gaining-a-heart-attack.md`.

## New disconfirming evidence

Jennifer A. Rymer and colleagues analyzed the American College of Cardiology Chest Pain–MI Registry from 2013 through 2022, covering 168,870 patients presenting with acute myocardial infarction at 1,124 U.S. hospitals. In *JAMA Network Open* (2025), they compared the weeks before, during, and after both spring and fall daylight-saving transitions. They found no statistically significant difference in acute myocardial-infarction incidence in a transition week versus the adjacent comparison weeks, and no significant difference in the studied in-hospital outcomes.

This is stronger counterevidence than the chapter currently records because it directly tests myocardial infarction in a large contemporary clinical registry. It does not prove that the spring transition has zero physiological or safety effect. The study is cross-sectional, its exposure is calendar-week membership rather than individual sleep loss or circadian phase, and a null average result can coexist with effects in narrower subgroups or with non-cardiac harms. But it materially weakens any sentence implying that a population-level spring heart-attack increase is now the settled reading of the literature.

Source: Rymer JA, Li S, Chiswell K, et al., “Daylight Savings Time and Acute Myocardial Infarction,” *JAMA Network Open* 8, no. 9 (2025): e2530442. DOI 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2025.30442. PubMed PMID 40924425.

## A separate long-run model should not be treated as observed outcomes

Lara Weed and Jamie Zeitzer’s 2025 *PNAS* paper modeled circadian burden under permanent standard time, permanent daylight time, and biannual switching, then linked the modeled burden to county-level prevalence data. The result is useful for the mechanism and for showing that location and chronotype matter, but its projected changes in obesity and stroke are model outputs rather than counts observed after a policy change.

That distinction became more important after the authors published a 2026 correction. They reported a coding error that had allowed nonzero irradiance during solar night in the original implementation and updated the deposited code and dataset. The correction does not turn the paper into evidence against circadian effects; it is a reason to be especially explicit about what is modeled, what is measured, and how much causal weight the book gives the numerical projections.

Sources: Weed L, Zeitzer JM, “Circadian-informed modeling predicts regional variation in obesity and stroke outcomes under different permanent US time policies,” *PNAS* 122 (2025): e2508293122, DOI 10.1073/pnas.2508293122; correction, *PNAS* 123 (2026): e2612351123, DOI 10.1073/pnas.2612351123.

## Chapter boundary for the next prose pass

Chapter 13 is already right to reject the headline version of “spring forward causes heart attacks.” The evidence trail can now be sharper:

- keep Janszky and Ljung (2008) as the historically important registry signal;
- keep the 2024 meta-analysis as evidence that a modest pooled spring association has appeared across studies, with heterogeneity;
- retain the 2024 nationwide cardiovascular claims analysis as evidence that any average cardiovascular effect is probably small;
- add Rymer et al. (2025) as direct contemporary MI counterevidence finding no significant transition-week increase;
- do not use the Weed/Zeitzer policy-model projections as observed disease prevention, especially without noting the 2026 correction.

The strongest defensible argument is therefore institutional rather than sensational: moving civil time can perturb sleep and circadian alignment, and the burden is unlikely to be evenly distributed, but the size and even detectability of specific population-level cardiac outcomes depends heavily on study design and population. That is enough for the book’s infrastructure argument. It does not need “heart attack Monday” to be literally reliable.
