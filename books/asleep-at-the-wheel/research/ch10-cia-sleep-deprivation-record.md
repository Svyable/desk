# Chapter 10 evidence update — the CIA's administrated sleep deprivation

## Why this note exists

Chapter 10 is strongest when it does not ask the reader to accept a generic claim that coercive sleep deprivation was severe. The public record is more specific, and stranger: the CIA converted prolonged wakefulness into a governed technique with thresholds, approvals, medical participation, and eventually a 180-hour ceiling. That bureaucratic precision is useful evidence for the chapter's argument that an institution can make an extreme biological intervention look ordinary by making it administrable.

## Primary record

### The technique was formally categorized and approval-bound

A declassified CIA interrogation guideline from January 2003 distinguished "Standard" from "Enhanced" techniques. It classified sleep deprivation beyond 72 hours as an Enhanced Technique, required Headquarters approval in advance for Enhanced Techniques, limited their use to approved interrogators and specified detainees, and called for medical and psychological participation in the process.

That matters for the chapter's institutional argument. The technique was not merely improvised sleeplessness. It sat inside an authorization architecture.

Source: CIA, *Guidelines on Interrogations Conducted Pursuant to the Presidential Memorandum of Notification of 17 September 2001* (January 2003), declassified and released through the CIA Reading Room: https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/GUIDELINES%20ON%20INTERROGATI%5B15789367%5D.pdf

### The Senate record documents both the ceiling and the consequences

The Senate Select Committee on Intelligence's 2014 findings say sleep deprivation involved keeping detainees awake for up to 180 hours, usually standing or in stress positions and sometimes with their hands shackled above their heads. The Committee also reported disturbing hallucinations in at least five detainees subjected to prolonged sleep deprivation and said the CIA continued the deprivation in at least two of those cases.

The full Committee Study also shows why a single number can obscure the policy's evolution. In January 2004, CIA Headquarters moved the line between "standard" and "enhanced" sleep deprivation from 72 hours to 48 hours. The Study describes one detainee, Adnan al-Libi, undergoing sessions of 46.5 hours, 24 hours, and 48 hours with only three hours of sleep between them. A nominal threshold therefore did not necessarily describe the total burden of repeated sessions.

Sources:

- Senate Select Committee on Intelligence, *Findings and Conclusions*, finding 3 (2014): https://www.intelligence.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/sites-default-files-press-findings-and-conclusions.pdf
- Senate Select Committee on Intelligence, *Committee Study of the Central Intelligence Agency's Detention and Interrogation Program*, S. Rpt. 113-288, p. 134 and surrounding record (2014): https://www.intelligence.senate.gov/sites/default/files/publications/CRPT-113srpt288.pdf

### The 180-hour proposal itself generated internal-policy concern

The Senate executive summary records that a proposal allowing sleep deprivation up to 180 hours raised concerns among National Security Council principals. That detail is useful counterevidence against portraying the government as institutionally unanimous about the extremity of the practice. The policy had defenders, but the upper bound was consequential enough to produce concern inside the executive decision process.

Source: Senate Select Committee on Intelligence, *Executive Summary* (2014): https://www.intelligence.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/sites-default-files-press-executive-summary-0.pdf

## Strongest counterargument

The chapter should preserve two distinct objections to a simple "coercion destroys truth" story.

First, the Senate Committee's minority views disputed the majority's assessment of effectiveness and argued that intelligence obtained from detainees subjected to enhanced techniques contributed to counterterrorism reporting. A historical account should distinguish that dispute from the much narrower physiological claim that prolonged sleep loss degrades cognition.

Second, an interrogation method need not make every resulting statement reliable by itself to have intelligence value. Investigators can corroborate leads against independent reporting. The real epistemic question is comparative: whether coercive sleep deprivation produces sufficiently valuable incremental information to justify the false leads, confabulation risk, verification burden, medical harm, legal cost, and moral cost it also creates. The public record does not permit the book to collapse that contested program-level question into the slogan "sleep deprivation makes people lie."

The Senate's 2007 hearing record nevertheless gives the chapter a contemporaneous medical challenge to the technique's epistemic premise. Testimony there described prolonged sleep deprivation as capable of producing confusion, delusions, paranoia, and psychosis — states that are poor foundations for accurate recall. That is better framed as a reliability mechanism than as proof that every statement obtained under deprivation was false.

## Editorial implication for Chapter 10

A later manuscript pass can make the chapter more concrete by replacing some of its repeated general statements about "rules," "limits," and "monitoring" with the actual sequence:

- beyond 72 hours was formally categorized as enhanced in the January 2003 guideline;
- the standard/enhanced threshold was later moved to 48 hours;
- repeated sub-threshold sessions could still accumulate extreme wakefulness;
- the program ultimately permitted sleep deprivation up to 180 hours;
- hallucinations occurred in documented cases, and deprivation sometimes continued afterward;
- the 180-hour ceiling itself drew concern among National Security Council principals.

The point is not that bureaucracy failed to set a limit. It did set limits. The more interesting fact is that **a limit can discipline a practice without making the practice moderate**. That distinction fits the chapter's existing voice and is harder to dismiss than a generalized indictment of bureaucratic euphemism.

No manuscript claim should say the 180-hour figure was Khalid Sheikh Mohammed's specific duration unless the cited passage establishes that attribution directly. The Committee's findings securely establish "up to 180 hours" at the program level; precision about an individual case should be tied to the exact case record.
