# Chapter Briefs

Research and argument briefs, not prose outlines. Each chapter should still find its own narrative shape from what the evidence supports. Target 3,800–4,400 words per chapter. Verify every fact below by finding the primary source yourself (WebSearch/WebFetch) before writing it as settled; if a detail below turns out to be wrong or unverifiable, drop or correct it rather than repeating it. Add every source you actually use to your batch's source ledger (see the note at the end of this file).

## Chapter 1 — The Third of Your Life You're Not Supposed to Notice
**Slug:** `ch01-the-third-of-your-life`
**Job:** Open the book on Randy Gardner's 1964 wakefulness experiment (reportedly about 264 hours, monitored informally with Stanford researcher William C. Dement and Bay Area sleep researchers involved, following media attention to a science-fair project) and use it to introduce sleep as a massive, understudied, biologically non-negotiable fraction of a human life.
**Verify:** Exact duration commonly cited, who monitored him and how, what happened to him afterward (recovery sleep pattern), what is and is not actually known/confirmed about long-term effects, and the ethical status of the experiment by later research standards (it would very likely not clear a modern institutional review board).
**Counterevidence:** Popular retellings of Gardner's case have accumulated some exaggeration and mythology over the decades; flag where the record is less certain than legend.
**Handoff:** End by naming the book's actual claim — sleep behaves like a hard constraint most institutions schedule around rather than for — and preview the range of institutions the book will visit.

## Chapter 2 — The Night of the Moving Eyes
**Slug:** `ch02-the-night-of-the-moving-eyes`
**Job:** Tell the history of how sleep became a measurable scientific object: Nathaniel Kleitman's University of Chicago sleep laboratory, graduate student Eugene Aserinsky's 1953 discovery (with Kleitman) of rapid eye movement sleep, the subsequent identification of sleep stages via EEG, and William Dement's role in founding sleep medicine as a field (Stanford's sleep clinic).
**Verify:** The 1953 Science paper's actual authorship and findings, the often-told detail that Aserinsky first tested the EEG equipment on his own child, Kleitman's earlier "Operation Tiger" isolation studies (Mammoth Cave and free-running circadian work), and Dement's founding-clinic dates.
**Counterevidence:** Note what the 1950s discovery did *not* settle — REM sleep's function was and remains debated; avoid overclaiming a tidy "REM equals dreaming" resolution.
**Handoff:** From measurement to mechanism — what a clock inside the body actually looks like.

## Chapter 3 — The Clock Inside You
**Slug:** `ch03-the-clock-inside-you`
**Job:** Explain circadian biology for a general reader: the suprachiasmatic nucleus, light entrainment, melatonin's actual signaling role (not a sedative), the molecular clock genes (period, timeless, clock, cycle) identified in Drosophila work that won Jeffrey C. Hall, Michael Rosbash, and Michael W. Young the 2017 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, and chronotype research (owls vs. larks) including Till Roenneberg's concept of "social jetlag."
**Verify:** The 2017 Nobel citation and the specific gene mechanism (transcription-translation feedback loop) in plain language; Roenneberg's social-jetlag definition and a real published estimate of its prevalence.
**Counterevidence:** Chronotype is influenced by age, light exposure, and genetics together — avoid presenting it as pure fixed genetic destiny.
**Handoff:** A body with its own clock meets a civilization that built artificial light and around-the-clock schedules — introduce the cultural myth chapter.

## Chapter 4 — Edison's Lie
**Slug:** `ch04-edisons-lie`
**Job:** Trace the cultural construction of sleeplessness as virtue: pre-electric "segmented sleep" (first sleep/second sleep) documented by historian A. Roger Ekirch's research into early-modern sleep patterns; Thomas Edison's public anti-sleep rhetoric versus documented evidence he napped frequently; the durability of "successful people sleep less" claims (e.g., stories about Margaret Thatcher) and what more careful reporting or research says about them.
**Verify:** Ekirch's actual sourcing (court records, diaries, literature) and publication (his book *At Day's Close* and earlier journal article); at least one primary or well-documented secondary source on Edison's real sleep/napping habits; treat any Thatcher sleep-duration claim cautiously and mark it as a contested/undersourced legend if it is one — do not repeat a number you cannot verify.
**Counterevidence:** Genuine short sleepers exist (see Chapter 3 tie-in and the DEC2 gene research referenced elsewhere in the book) — distinguish rare biological short-sleepers from a cultural performance of sleeplessness.
**Handoff:** From cultural myth to the institution that built one of the most extreme versions of it into professional training — medicine.

## Chapter 5 — Halsted's Ghost
**Slug:** `ch05-halsteds-ghost`
**Job:** Tell the history of American medical residency's extreme-hours culture: William Stewart Halsted's founding role at Johns Hopkins and his own well-documented cocaine and later morphine dependence, which historians connect to his stamina-focused, always-present training model; the case of Libby Zion (died 1984 at New York Hospital), the resulting Bell Commission and New York State's 1989 resident-work-hour regulations, and the ACGME's 2003 national 80-hour weekly cap (later revisions).
**Verify:** The documented history of Halsted's addiction (widely covered in medical-history literature) and the historiographical link scholars draw to residency culture; the Libby Zion case facts (her death, the grand jury/Bell Commission findings about resident supervision and fatigue) and the actual regulatory dates and hour limits.
**Counterevidence:** Note the real, still-debated research (multiple RCTs and observational studies, e.g. the FIRST and iCOMPARE trials) on whether shorter shifts measurably improved patient safety versus possibly worsening handoff-related errors — represent this as a genuine open tradeoff, not a solved problem.
**Handoff:** From an institution that eventually regulated fatigue to industries that only did so after watching people die.

## Chapter 6 — The Reactor at 1:23 A.M.
**Slug:** `ch06-the-reactor-at-123-am`
**Job:** Chernobyl's April 26, 1986 disaster (reactor 4 explosion at 1:23 a.m. local time) and Three Mile Island's March 28, 1979 accident (beginning around 4 a.m.), both during a graveyard-shift circadian low point, using each event's own investigation record (IAEA/INSAG reports for Chernobyl; the Kemeny Commission for Three Mile Island) to describe what role shift timing, training, and operator fatigue/vigilance played alongside the other documented causes (reactor design flaws, procedural violations, instrumentation failures).
**Verify:** Exact times and shift facts from the primary investigation reports; be precise that both accidents had multiple, well-documented causes beyond fatigue and that investigators did not necessarily rank fatigue as the primary cause — represent the actual causal weighting honestly.
**Counterevidence:** Circadian-low-point clustering of industrial accidents is a real pattern in the safety literature, but avoid implying fatigue alone caused either disaster; both had serious independent design and procedural failures.
**Handoff:** From power plants to the sea and the sky — transportation accidents with clearer single-operator fatigue findings.

## Chapter 7 — Asleep at the Helm
**Slug:** `ch07-asleep-at-the-helm`
**Job:** The Exxon Valdez grounding (Prince William Sound, March 24, 1989) — the National Transportation Safety Board's finding on the third mate's workload and fatigue at the time he was left in charge of the wheelhouse; and the Space Shuttle Challenger disaster (January 28, 1986) — the fatigue of NASA and Morton Thiokol engineers and managers during the overnight teleconference the night before launch, as documented in the Rogers Commission report and Diane Vaughan's research on "normalization of deviance."
**Verify:** The NTSB's actual stated findings on Valdez fatigue and crew scheduling; the Rogers Commission's documented timeline of the overnight January 27 teleconference, including Roger Boisjoly's warnings about O-ring performance in cold temperatures.
**Counterevidence:** Challenger's proximate cause was O-ring failure in cold weather, not fatigue per se; fatigue is one documented contributor to the decision process, not the mechanical cause — keep this distinction explicit.
**Handoff:** From institutional command decisions to a single tired person operating a single machine.

## Chapter 8 — The Man Who Fell Asleep at the Throttle
**Slug:** `ch08-the-man-who-fell-asleep-at-the-throttle`
**Job:** The December 1, 2013 Metro-North derailment at Spuyten Duyvil, New York, where engineer William Rockefeller Jr. was later found to have severe undiagnosed obstructive sleep apnea; the NTSB investigation and probable-cause finding; the subsequent push for railroad and transit sleep-apnea screening (FRA/FTA policy activity) and any documented resistance or implementation gaps.
**Verify:** NTSB docket findings and probable cause language for this specific accident; the actual post-accident regulatory or policy response and its real scope/limits (screening mandates are more limited than popular accounts sometimes suggest).
**Counterevidence:** Note that sleep apnea screening in transportation remains inconsistently applied industry-wide; do not claim a fully solved problem.
**Handoff:** From rail to road — commercial trucking's much larger, much less centrally controlled fatigue problem.

## Chapter 9 — Miles to Go Before You Sleep
**Slug:** `ch09-miles-to-go-before-you-sleep`
**Job:** Trucking fatigue and regulation: the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration's Hours-of-Service rules, their history and repeated revisions; the June 2014 New Jersey Turnpike crash involving a Walmart truck driver (Kevin Roper) who investigators found had been awake roughly 24+ hours, which injured several people including comedian Tracy Morgan and killed comedian James "Jimmy Mack" McNair; the 2017 nationwide Electronic Logging Device (ELD) mandate and industry pushback/compliance debates; gig-economy delivery driver scheduling pressure as a contemporary extension of the same problem.
**Verify:** NTSB's findings on the 2014 crash; the actual FMCSA hours-of-service limits (driving/on-duty caps, mandated breaks) at time of writing and how they've changed; the ELD mandate's real effective date and its documented safety-data effect (mixed evidence — represent honestly, some studies show improvement, others show limited or contested effects).
**Counterevidence:** Hours-of-service compliance does not guarantee alertness (a legally rested driver can still be fatigued due to circadian timing or sleep disorders); note this limit of a purely rules-based fix.
**Handoff:** From accidental fatigue to fatigue used deliberately, as a weapon.

## Chapter 10 — The Interrogation Room
**Slug:** `ch10-the-interrogation-room`
**Job:** Sleep deprivation as coercive interrogation: historical use (documented Soviet-era and other 20th-century interrogation practice), and the post-9/11 CIA "enhanced interrogation" program's documented use of sustained sleep deprivation (the Senate Intelligence Committee's 2014 torture report executive summary is the primary public source, including specific documented cases and duration figures), contrasted with medical and psychological consensus (e.g., statements from medical/psychological professional bodies) on the physiological and cognitive harm of extended sleep deprivation and on the unreliability of information extracted under it.
**Verify:** Specific figures and cases from the declassified Senate report executive summary; positions of relevant professional medical/psychological associations on interrogational sleep deprivation.
**Counterevidence:** Present the program's stated intelligence-community rationale accurately even while reporting the harm and reliability findings — this is a chapter that needs to be factually careful and non-sensationalized, sourced to primary documents rather than secondary dramatization.
**Handoff:** From coercion to a form of scheduling violence done to children by well-meaning institutions: school start times.

## Chapter 11 — The Bell That Rings Too Early
**Slug:** `ch11-the-bell-that-rings-too-early`
**Job:** Adolescent circadian delay (the biological shift toward later sleep and wake times during puberty, documented by researchers including Mary Carskadon) versus early high-school start times; the Seattle Public Schools 2016–2017 start-time-delay natural experiment studied by University of Washington researchers (published results on sleep duration and, in a widely cited finding, classroom performance/attendance); California's 2019 SB 328 statewide later-start-time law and the practical pushback it drew (bus routing, after-school sports, working-parent schedules, teacher union concerns).
**Verify:** Carskadon's core circadian-delay findings and publications; the actual University of Washington/Seattle study's design, sample, and headline results (do not overstate a natural-experiment's causal strength); SB 328's real provisions and effective dates.
**Counterevidence:** Represent the real logistical costs school districts face (transportation contracts, staffing, extracurricular scheduling, family child-care needs) as genuine constraints, not merely irrational resistance.
**Handoff:** From students to the adults working nights so the daytime economy can keep running.

## Chapter 12 — The Shift That Never Sleeps
**Slug:** `ch12-the-shift-that-never-sleeps`
**Job:** Night-shift and rotating-shift work: the International Agency for Research on Cancer's 2007 classification of "shift work that involves circadian disruption" as a Group 2A probable human carcinogen (and any later IARC monograph updates), the underlying epidemiological evidence and its real limitations/uncertainty, metabolic and cardiovascular research on shift workers, and the demographic/economic reality of who works nights (healthcare workers, manufacturing, logistics, security, hospitality, often lower-wage and disproportionately immigrant labor).
**Verify:** The exact IARC classification language, year, and monograph number; at least one large occupational cohort study (e.g., nurse cohort research) on shift-work health associations, stated with appropriate epidemiological caution (association, not proven individual causation).
**Counterevidence:** Note that some individuals tolerate night work well, that some of the epidemiological evidence is observational and contested, and that not all "shift work" is biologically equivalent (permanent night shift differs from rapidly rotating shifts).
**Handoff:** From a chronic, invisible institutional cost to a single, oddly universal one — the day the whole country loses an hour.

## Chapter 13 — Losing an Hour, Gaining a Heart Attack
**Slug:** `ch13-losing-an-hour-gaining-a-heart-attack`
**Job:** Daylight Saving Time transitions as a case study in institutions overriding circadian biology by law: the Swedish myocardial-infarction correspondence by Imre Janszky and Rickard Ljung (New England Journal of Medicine, 2008) reporting a short-term increase in heart attacks after the spring transition; other studies on traffic accidents and workplace injuries around DST changes; the U.S. legislative history (Uniform Time Act, the 1974–75 year-round DST experiment and its unpopularity, and the more recent "Sunshine Protection Act" push) as an example of a policy fight that is really an argument about whose circadian preference should be law.
**Verify:** The Janszky/Ljung NEJM correspondence's actual findings and stated limitations; the 1974 year-round DST trial's real history and why it was reversed; current status of Sunshine Protection Act legislation as of the most recent verifiable date.
**Counterevidence:** Some later, larger studies have produced more mixed or smaller effect estimates for the DST/heart-attack link than the original 2008 finding — represent the literature's actual state rather than treating one letter as definitive.
**Handoff:** From a law argument to a marketplace response — the industries that sell you sleep.

## Chapter 14 — The Insomnia Economy
**Slug:** `ch14-the-insomnia-economy`
**Job:** The sleep-aid pharmaceutical industry's history from barbiturates through benzodiazepines to non-benzodiazepine "Z-drugs" (zolpidem/Ambien and relatives), including documented adverse-event patterns (complex sleep-related behaviors) that led the FDA to require boxed warnings (the FDA's 2019 safety communication is a real, citable primary source); the U.S. regulatory quirk that lets melatonin be sold as an unregulated dietary supplement rather than a drug, and peer-reviewed testing (e.g., a 2023 JAMA Pediatrics or similar study on gummy melatonin content accuracy) showing large discrepancies between labeled and actual dose; and Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Insomnia (CBT-I) as the clinical first-line treatment recommended by major medical bodies, and why it remains underused relative to pills.
**Verify:** The FDA's actual 2019 boxed-warning action and the drugs it covered; a real peer-reviewed melatonin-content study with its actual findings; the professional-guideline status of CBT-I as first-line treatment (e.g., American College of Physicians guidance).
**Counterevidence:** Prescription sleep medication and melatonin both have legitimate, evidence-supported uses for some patients and situations; avoid blanket condemnation, focus on the documented gaps between marketing/regulation and evidence.
**Handoff:** From pills to devices — the wearable industry's promise to finally make sleep visible.

## Chapter 16 — The Book About Sleep That Got Sleep Wrong
**Slug:** `ch16-the-book-about-sleep-that-got-sleep-wrong`
**Job:** Use the public scientific controversy around Matthew Walker's bestselling *Why We Sleep* (2017) — specifically Alexey Guzey's widely covered 2019 blog critique alleging multiple factual and statistical errors, subsequent corrections made to later editions/audiobook, and commentary from other sleep researchers on the episode — as a case study in how sleep science gets simplified, dramatized, and occasionally distorted on its way from the laboratory to a mass audience, and why the book you are reading tries to flag its own uncertainty instead of borrowing false authority.
**Verify:** The actual substance of at least two or three of Guzey's most substantiated specific critiques (not just the existence of controversy); what Walker or his publisher changed, if anything, in response; how credentialed sleep researchers who commented on the episode assessed its seriousness (views varied — represent that range).
**Counterevidence:** *Why We Sleep*'s broad thesis (chronic insufficient sleep has real health costs) remains supported by the wider peer-reviewed literature independent of the specific disputed claims; be careful not to let a fair critique of specific errors curdle into "sleep science itself is unreliable."
**Handoff:** From a controversy about sleep on Earth to sleep with no Earth at all — astronauts.

## Chapter 17 — Nine Days Without a Sunset
**Slug:** `ch17-nine-days-without-a-sunset`
**Job:** Astronaut sleep aboard the International Space Station, where the station orbits Earth roughly every 90 minutes producing about 16 sunrises and sunsets per crew day; published research (e.g., work involving Charles Czeisler's group and colleagues, such as Barger et al.'s research on astronaut sleep) documenting that measured average in-flight sleep duration often runs meaningfully below NASA's roughly 8.5-hour scheduled sleep period, along with documented use of sleep medication in orbit; implications discussed by NASA and researchers for longer-duration Mars-transit mission planning (lighting design, schedule countermeasures).
**Verify:** The actual orbital period and sunrise/sunset count; the specific published sleep-duration findings and their source study; NASA's own scheduled sleep allotment figure; real, cited discussion of Mars-mission circadian planning rather than speculation.
**Counterevidence:** Note operational counter-measures (engineered lighting, strict schedules) that have improved crew sleep over the decades of the space program; this is not a uniformly worsening problem.
**Handoff:** Back to Earth, to a place where sleep is treated as a competitive weapon rather than an inconvenience: professional sports.

## Source ledger instructions for writing agents

Each writing batch keeps its own CSV at `research/source-ledger-batchN.csv` (N = your batch number) using exactly this header and no others:

```
id,year,author_or_institution,title,source_type,book_use,url
```

- `id`: lowercase, stable, unique across the whole book — prefix with your chapter number, e.g. `aatw-ch01-001`, `aatw-ch01-002`, `aatw-ch02-001`.
- `year`: the publication year of that specific source (not your access date).
- Every field must be non-empty. No duplicate `id` or `url` value anywhere in the finished book.
- Use one row per distinct source you actually cite or rely on in your assigned chapters. Real URLs only — verify each one resolves to the source you're citing.
