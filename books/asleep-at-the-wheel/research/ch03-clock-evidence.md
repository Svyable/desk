# Chapter 3 evidence notes — The Clock Inside You

## Chapter job

Explain circadian timing without turning a living regulatory system into a cartoon “sleep clock.” The chapter should begin with the fruit-fly mutants that made the internal clock experimentally visible, then move from molecular feedback loops to the human suprachiasmatic nucleus, light entrainment, melatonin, chronotype, and social jetlag.

The central institutional point is that clock time and biological time are different systems. Electric light, school schedules, shifts, commuting, and social obligations can move behavior without instantly moving the underlying circadian phase. That mismatch is measurable, but it is not a fixed genetic destiny and it is not identical across people.

## Opening case — the flies whose days were wrong

In 1971, Seymour Benzer and Ronald Konopka reported Drosophila mutants with altered biological rhythms: some became arrhythmic, while others showed abnormally short or long circadian periods. The mutations mapped to the same genetic locus, later named `period`.

The 2017 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was awarded to Jeffrey C. Hall, Michael Rosbash, and Michael W. Young “for their discoveries of molecular mechanisms controlling the circadian rhythm.” The Nobel committee’s scientific background traces the line from the 1971 mutants to the 1984 isolation of `period`, the discovery that PER protein accumulates at night and degrades during the day, and later work on `timeless`, TIM/PER complexes, and the CLOCK/CYCLE transcriptional machinery.

Sources:

- Nobel Prize, “The 2017 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine — Advanced Information: Discoveries of Molecular Mechanisms Controlling the Circadian Rhythm.” https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/2017/advanced-information/
- Nobel Prize, press release, October 2, 2017. https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/2017/press-release/
- Konopka RJ, Benzer S. “Clock mutants of Drosophila melanogaster.” *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*. 1971;68(9):2112–2116. https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.68.9.2112

Use carefully:

- The Nobel-recognized mechanism was established largely in fruit flies. Humans use homologous clock machinery and the same broad feedback-loop principle, but do not imply that every fly gene maps one-for-one onto the human system.
- `timeless` is historically central in Drosophila; mammalian clock networks differ in important details. Keep the prose at the level of a recurring transcription/translation feedback loop rather than an exhaustive gene catalog.
- The useful idea is not “a clock gene.” It is that cells can generate approximately 24-hour oscillations through delayed negative feedback.

## Plain-language molecular mechanism

The Nobel committee’s account provides a clean conceptual description. CLOCK and CYCLE activate transcription of `period` and `timeless`. PER and TIM proteins accumulate, form a complex, enter the nucleus, and inhibit the activity that generated them. As PER/TIM are degraded, inhibition fades and the cycle begins again. Additional regulatory loops stabilize and tune the oscillation.

Use analogy sparingly. A thermostat is too simple because the system is oscillatory and distributed. The most useful phrasing is: the products of clock-gene activity eventually suppress the machinery that made them, creating a delayed feedback loop that rises and falls across roughly a day.

## The human coordinator — the SCN

In mammals, the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), a small paired structure in the anterior hypothalamus above the optic chiasm, acts as a central circadian pacemaker. It receives photic information from the retina and coordinates rhythms across physiology and behavior. Peripheral tissues also contain molecular clocks; the SCN is better described as a central coordinator than as the only clock in the body.

Source:

- Golombek DA, Rosenstein RE. “Physiology of circadian entrainment.” *Physiological Reviews*. 2010;90(3):1063–1102. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20664079/

Boundary:

- Do not say the SCN “causes sleep.” Sleep timing reflects interaction between circadian timing and homeostatic sleep pressure, among other factors.
- Do not imply removing social clocks reveals one perfectly stable natural bedtime. Light exposure, activity, meals, age, and other cues affect timing.

## Light enters through a non-image pathway

Retinal light information reaches the SCN through intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells that express melanopsin, alongside input from rods and cones. These pathways help regulate circadian entrainment, melatonin suppression, pupil responses, and other non-image-forming responses to light.

Source:

- Gooley JJ, Lu J, Chou TC, Scammell TE, Saper CB. “Melanopsin in cells of origin of the retinohypothalamic tract.” *Nature Neuroscience*. 2001;4:1165. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11713469/

Use: enough to explain why light is biological information even when a person is not consciously “looking at” anything important.

## Ordinary evening light can move the human clock

A controlled human study by Zeitzer and colleagues exposed subjects to different intensities of late-evening light. The human circadian pacemaker proved more sensitive than simple bright-versus-dark intuition suggested. Approximately 100 lux — in the range of ordinary indoor illumination — produced more than half the phase-delay response produced by very bright light in the experiment and substantially suppressed melatonin.

Source:

- Zeitzer JM, Dijk DJ, Kronauer R, Brown E, Czeisler C. “Sensitivity of the human circadian pacemaker to nocturnal light: melatonin phase resetting and suppression.” *Journal of Physiology*. 2000;526 Pt 3:695–702. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10922269/

Use carefully:

- Do not convert this into “100 lux always shifts your clock by X hours.” Response depends on timing, intensity, duration, prior light exposure, spectrum, and individual sensitivity.
- The narrative value is that indoor light is not biologically neutral simply because it is dim compared with daylight.

## Melatonin is a timing signal, not a simple sedative switch

Melatonin secretion rises at night under circadian control and provides a hormonal signal of biological night. Reviews describe it as a reliable marker of circadian phase and an output of the central clock. Exogenous melatonin can have sleep-promoting and phase-shifting effects depending on dose and timing, but endogenous melatonin is not a simple “sleep chemical” whose level directly equals sleepiness.

Source:

- Arendt J. “Melatonin: characteristics, concerns, and prospects.” *Journal of Biological Rhythms*. 2005;20(4):291–303. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16077149/
- Zisapel N. “New perspectives on the role of melatonin in human sleep, circadian rhythms and their regulation.” *British Journal of Pharmacology*. 2018;175(16):3190–3199. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29318587/

Use:

- Say melatonin helps signal biological darkness/night and can shift timing when taken at appropriate times.
- Avoid “melatonin makes you sleep” as the chapter’s mechanism.
- Chapter 14 owns supplement-market dosing and regulation; keep this chapter physiological.

## Chronotype is a phenotype, not a moral category

Roenneberg and colleagues use the Munich ChronoType Questionnaire (MCTQ) to characterize variation in sleep timing. Chronotype ranges from earlier to later timing and reflects interaction among the circadian system, age, sex, genetics, and environment, especially light exposure.

Sources:

- Roenneberg T, Kuehnle T, Juda M, et al. “Epidemiology of the human circadian clock.” *Sleep Medicine Reviews*. 2007;11(6):429–438. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17936039/
- Roenneberg T, Kumar CJ, Merrow M. “The human circadian clock entrains to sun time.” *Current Biology*. 2007;17(2):R44–R45. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17240323/
- Roenneberg T, Wirz-Justice A, Merrow M. “Life between clocks: daily temporal patterns of human chronotypes.” *Journal of Biological Rhythms*. 2003;18(1):80–90. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12568247/

Important counterevidence:

- Chronotype changes across the lifespan. Population data generally shift later through adolescence and early adulthood, then earlier with age.
- More outdoor/natural light exposure is associated with earlier timing and less extreme lateness. Artificial light environments can alter the distribution.
- Genetics matters, but “night owl” is not an immutable genotype or an excuse to ignore schedule and light.

## Social jetlag — two clocks disagree

Roenneberg and colleagues define social jetlag as the discrepancy between sleep timing on workdays and free days, usually operationalized through the difference in midsleep timing. The concept captures how externally imposed schedules can force people to sleep at one phase during the workweek and another when social constraints relax.

Sources:

- Wittmann M, Dinich J, Merrow M, Roenneberg T. “Social jetlag: misalignment of biological and social time.” *Chronobiology International*. 2006;23(1-2):497–509. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16687322/
- Roenneberg T, Allebrandt KV, Merrow M, Vetter C. “Social jetlag and obesity.” *Current Biology*. 2012;22(10):939–943. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22578422/

The 2012 paper states explicitly that chronotype is shaped by genetic background, age, sex, and environment, including light exposure. Its obesity result is observational association, not proof that changing sleep timing alone causes or cures obesity.

### How common?

Threshold prevalence varies by population and method, which is itself useful. A prospective actigraphy study of healthy young adults reported baseline social jetlag greater than one hour in 50% of participants and at least two hours in 17%; the authors noted prior adult studies reporting roughly 63–69% at one hour or more and 26–33% at two hours or more. A Japanese nationwide MCTQ survey found more than one hour in 40.1% of respondents. These are not universal human constants.

Sources:

- Wong PM, Hasler BP, Kamarck TW, et al. “Social jetlag, chronotype, and cardiometabolic risk.” / related prospective young-adult actigraphy work, prevalence discussion accessible at PMC: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6800574/
- Komada Y, Okajima I, Kitamura S, et al. “A survey on social jetlag in Japan: a nationwide, cross-sectional internet survey.” *Sleep and Biological Rhythms*. 2019;17:417–422. https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41105-019-00229-w

Use in prose:

- A safe sentence is that in several adult samples, one-hour-plus social jetlag is common and has ranged from roughly one-third to around two-thirds depending on population and measurement.
- Do not quote one prevalence as the prevalence of “society.”

## Countercase — genuine short sleepers exist

A 2009 report identified a rare human `DEC2` mutation associated with a familial short-sleep phenotype and showed related effects in model organisms. It is a useful reminder that individual sleep duration and timing have real genetic components.

Source:

- He Y, Jones CR, Fujiki N, et al. “The transcriptional repressor DEC2 regulates sleep length in mammals.” *Science*. 2009;325(5942):866–870. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19679812/

Use only as a short boundary. Chapter 4 owns the cultural story of claiming exceptional short sleep.

## Mechanism to carry into prose

The body does not contain one wall clock. It contains oscillators coordinated by light and other cues. The SCN is a central coordinator; molecular feedback loops run in many tissues. Sleep pressure rises with time awake while circadian timing pushes alertness and sleep propensity up and down across the day. A person can therefore be tired yet temporarily alert at a circadian-favored time, or have spent enough hours in bed but be asked to perform at a biologically adverse phase.

This is the reason “eight hours somewhere in the 24” is not a complete scheduling theory.

## Falsification / what would weaken the chapter

The chapter would overreach if it treated chronotype as fixed genetics, melatonin as a simple sedative meter, the SCN as the sole clock in the body, or social jetlag as a diagnosis with one universal threshold and one proven disease pathway.

Keep visible:

- strong individual variation in phase and light sensitivity;
- age and environmental light shift chronotype;
- social jetlag studies are often observational and definition-dependent;
- some people are genuine short sleepers;
- institutions face real constraints that prevent every schedule from matching every individual clock.

## Handoff to Chapter 4

Once a body can generate its own timing and light can move that timing, electric light becomes more than a convenience. It is an environmental signal that civilization learned to extend on demand. Chapter 4 should ask how artificial light and the people who championed it helped turn wakefulness into a moral and economic virtue.
