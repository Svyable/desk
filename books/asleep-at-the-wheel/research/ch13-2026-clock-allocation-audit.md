# Chapter 13 evidence audit — clock transition versus clock allocation

Checked September 9, 2026.

## Editorial question

Chapter 13 risks collapsing two different policy questions:

1. What is the measurable cost of changing civil time twice a year?
2. If switching stops, which fixed clock should govern winter mornings and evenings?

The revision separates them. The transition evidence is useful for estimating acute disruption; it does not by itself choose permanent daylight saving time over permanent standard time.

## Evidence rechecked

### Acute myocardial-infarction signal

Janszky and Ljung's 2008 *New England Journal of Medicine* correspondence remains the historical anchor for the spring-transition myocardial-infarction signal. It is observational registry evidence and should not be represented as individual-level causal proof.

Source already in ledger: `ch13-janszky-ljung`.

### 2024 meta-analysis

Stutz et al. pooled twelve studies from ten countries. The spring transition estimate was RR 1.04 (95% CI 1.02–1.07) with moderate heterogeneity; the autumn estimate was 1.02 (95% CI 0.99–1.05). After excluding one study with inconsistencies, the spring estimate remained 1.04 while the autumn estimate moved to 1.00. The authors explicitly call for stronger quasi-experimental designs and appropriate control periods.

This supports a narrow manuscript claim: a small spring increase may exist, but the size is uncertain and the literature is heterogeneous.

Source already in ledger: `ch13-ami-meta` — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38888468/

### Large U.S. cardiovascular counterevidence

The existing source ledger includes Khera et al.'s nationwide U.S. analysis as counterevidence that aggregate cardiovascular effects around daylight-saving transitions are small and unlikely to be clinically important. The manuscript uses this to prevent the chapter from turning the original Swedish result into a morality tale.

Source already in ledger: `ch13-cv-counter`.

### Current federal-law structure

Current U.S. Department of Transportation guidance remains the book's authoritative source for the Uniform Time Act structure: jurisdictions can opt out of daylight saving time and remain on standard time, but current law does not give ordinary DST-observing states a unilateral route to permanent advanced time.

Source already in ledger: `ch13-dot-time`.

### 2026 Sunshine Protection Act status

The House passed H.R. 139 on July 14, 2026 by 308–117. The Senate received the measure July 15 and referred it to the Committee on Commerce, Science, and Transportation. Fresh reporting and current status checks on September 9 showed no Senate passage or enactment.

The revised chapter therefore says explicitly that the bill has not become law and treats House passage as evidence of a live allocation choice, not as a settled policy outcome.

## Strongest counterargument retained

The book's general thesis could tempt the chapter to imply that circadian alignment should dominate every clock-policy decision. That would be too strong.

Even if morning light has a privileged circadian role, evening daylight can create real household, recreation, transportation, retail, and social benefits. Latitude and longitude change the practical distribution. Institutions can also shift school and work start times independently of the legal clock, at least in principle.

The defensible chapter claim is narrower: civil time is a policy technology for allocating social activity relative to solar time. Biology should enter that allocation explicitly, but it is not the only legitimate input.

## Narrative refinement

The previous version contained several good distinctions but repeatedly announced them through short standalone morals and paired corrections. The revision consolidates those into one argument built around the difference between **transition cost** and **steady-state light allocation**. It preserves the chapter's proportionality: the DST transition is a modest perturbation for most people, not an analogue for chronic night work or severe sleep deprivation.
