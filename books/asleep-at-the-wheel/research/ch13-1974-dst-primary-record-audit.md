# Chapter 13 research note: the 1974 daylight-saving reversal

Checked September 14, 2026.

## Why this note exists

Chapter 13 uses the 1974–75 U.S. experiment with extended daylight saving time as evidence that ending seasonal clock changes does not settle the separate question of where winter daylight should fall. The manuscript's broad account is sound, but the episode is easy to turn into a cleaner morality tale than the primary record supports. This note fixes the chronology and separates documented political reaction from stronger causal claims.

## Primary chronology

The Emergency Daylight Saving Time Energy Conservation Act of 1973, Public Law 93-182, advanced civil time continuously beginning **January 6, 1974**, with the experiment originally scheduled to run through **April 27, 1975**. The policy was enacted during the energy crisis; it was an energy-conservation experiment, not a sleep or circadian-health trial.

Congress did not simply wait for the experiment to expire. In **October 1974**, Public Law 93-434 amended the trial so standard time would return for the winter period beginning **October 27, 1974**, with daylight saving time resuming **February 23, 1975**. A later Congressional Research Service history describes the sequence the same way.

Authoritative sources:

- Congressional Research Service, *Daylight Saving Time (DST)*, R45208: https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R45208/R45208.7.pdf
- Congressional Record, March 4, 1974, S. 3102 discussion: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-CRECB-1974-pt4/pdf/GPO-CRECB-1974-pt4-5-2.pdf

## What the dark-morning evidence actually establishes

The political backlash was real and began quickly. On **January 28, 1974**, Senator Lawton Chiles introduced legislation to terminate the emergency act and invoked reports of eight Florida schoolchildren killed in predawn traffic crashes. That is useful contemporaneous evidence of the political salience of dark school mornings.

It is **not**, by itself, evidence that daylight saving time caused those deaths. The Congressional Record establishes what lawmakers were reacting to and saying; it does not supply a controlled causal estimate. The manuscript should therefore use the school-safety episode as evidence of perceived and practical morning-light costs, not as a quantified mortality effect of permanent daylight saving time.

Primary source:

- Congressional Record, January 28, 1974, introduction of S. 2905: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-CRECB-1974-pt1/pdf/GPO-CRECB-1974-pt1-5-2.pdf

By March, repeal or curtailment proposals were already on the Senate record. Senator William L. Scott's March 4 remarks explicitly described the recently enacted year-round policy and proposed ending the emergency experiment in October 1974. This helps the chapter avoid implying that opposition appeared only after a full winter of observation.

## Strongest counterargument

The 1974 episode is not a clean experiment proving that permanent standard time is socially optimal. It occurred during an oil crisis, under the school, commuting, energy, and transportation arrangements of the early 1970s. Political reaction bundled genuine dark-morning inconvenience with highly salient accident reports, expectations about energy savings, and a rapidly changing national emergency.

The later legislative reversal therefore supports a narrower proposition: **winter clock allocation has visible distributional costs, and those costs can become politically decisive even when the original policy goal is unrelated to circadian health.** It does not establish the present-day net welfare ranking of permanent standard time, permanent daylight saving time, or seasonal switching.

## Editorial implication

The current chapter is right to resist the slogan that America "tried permanent daylight saving time and hated it." A future prose pass should make the chronology slightly more concrete—January 6 start, opposition already visible by late January and March, October statutory revision—while refusing to convert contemporaneous schoolchild deaths into a causal DST statistic.

That treatment fits the chapter's larger distinction between **transition cost** and **steady-state light allocation**. The 1974 record is strongest as political evidence that morning light is not an abstract variable: changing its clock location changes who travels, waits, works, and attends school in darkness.
