# Chapter 13 — 2026 daylight-saving evidence boundary

This note is for a later prose pass on `manuscript/ch13-losing-an-hour-gaining-a-heart-attack.md`. It updates two fast-moving parts of the chapter without changing the manuscript in this run: the cardiovascular evidence around the spring transition and the federal legislative status of permanent daylight saving time.

## What the cardiovascular evidence can support

The chapter is right to resist the popular shorthand that one lost hour produces a large, cleanly attributable wave of heart attacks.

Hurst et al. (2024), a systematic review and meta-analysis of twelve studies from ten countries, estimated a pooled relative risk of acute myocardial infarction of 1.04 after the spring transition (95% CI 1.02–1.07), with moderate heterogeneity. The autumn estimate was close to null. The authors explicitly call for stronger quasi-experimental designs and comparisons with places or years without daylight-saving transitions.

Primary/authoritative record:
- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38888468/
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.3238/arztebl.m2024.0078

A newer 2025 U.S. analysis further complicates any simple causal story. The study examined 168,870 acute-myocardial-infarction patients treated at 1,124 hospitals around spring and fall daylight-saving transitions. Its large contemporary clinical sample is useful counterevidence against presenting the spring clock change as a major cardiovascular event. It should be read alongside, not as a replacement for, the meta-analysis because the studies use different populations, periods, outcomes, and designs.

Primary record:
- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40924425/

### Editorial boundary

A defensible chapter-level formulation is narrower than “DST causes heart attacks”: observational evidence suggests a small spring-associated increase in myocardial infarction in some datasets, but effect sizes are small, estimates vary across studies, and newer large clinical analyses give less support to a clinically important aggregate cardiovascular effect.

The strongest counterargument should remain inside the argument: even if the acute cardiac effect is small, that does not establish that clock changes are biologically neutral. Circadian timing, sleep duration, morning light, school/work timing, geography, and the distribution of inconvenience remain separate questions. Conversely, evidence of circadian disruption does not by itself decide whether permanent standard time or permanent daylight saving time is the better civil-time policy.

## 2026 federal-policy status

H.R. 139, the Sunshine Protection Act of 2025, passed the U.S. House on July 14, 2026 by 308–117. It was received in the Senate on July 15 and referred to the Committee on Commerce, Science, and Transportation. As checked September 9, 2026, the measure had not become law.

Authoritative/official records:
- House Committee on Rules page for H.R. 139: https://rules.house.gov/bill/119/hr-139
- U.S. Department of Transportation daylight-saving-time guidance: https://www.transportation.gov/regulations/daylight-saving-time
- Authenticated introduced bill text (GPO/Congress): https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/hr139/BILLS-119hr139ih.pdf

The current federal framework still permits states to exempt themselves from observing daylight saving time; jurisdictions that do observe it must follow the federally prescribed start and end dates. That asymmetry matters for the chapter because “stop changing clocks” is not one policy choice. Permanent standard time and permanent daylight saving time distribute winter morning and evening light differently.

### Strongest counterargument

The best case for permanent daylight saving time is not that biology is irrelevant. It is that civil time coordinates many legitimate interests at once: evening recreation and commerce, commuting, school schedules, household routines, public preferences, geography, and seasonal light. Morning-light arguments therefore deserve weight without being treated as a trump card.

The book’s stronger contribution is to expose the hidden physical choice. A legislature can change clock labels instantly; it cannot move sunrise. Institutions then decide whether to keep schedules fixed to the new label or adjust them to the changed relationship between clock time and solar time.

## Recommended use in a later prose pass

Do not add another stack of studies. Use at most one sentence of newer cardiovascular counterevidence and one precise sentence on the 2026 bill status. The chapter already has the right narrative shape. The value of this update is evidentiary restraint: keep the heart-attack example small enough that the larger argument about civil time and biological time does not depend on a fragile headline effect.
