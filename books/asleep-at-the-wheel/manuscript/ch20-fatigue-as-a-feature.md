# Fatigue as a Feature

The office used to have a door.

That is not nostalgia. Offices had plenty wrong with them. They required commutes, concentrated interruptions, rewarded presence, and made it possible to confuse sitting under fluorescent lights with useful work. But a physical workplace contained one accidental piece of labor regulation: eventually, most people left it.

The laptop came home.

Then the phone learned email. Messaging became continuous. Documents became collaborative. Meetings became links. A worker could answer from a kitchen table, an airport gate, a child's soccer practice, or bed.

The liberation was real.

So was the leak.

In the Bureau of Labor Statistics' 2025 American Time Use Survey, thirty-five percent of employed people did some or all of their work at home on days they worked. For people who worked at home, the category includes both scheduled and unscheduled work. The same survey shows that on days worked, employed people averaged more than eight hours in work and work-related activities.

Those averages do not prove that remote work steals sleep. Many people gain sleep by removing a commute. Some gain control over when they work. A parent may be able to do a school pickup without losing an afternoon. A night owl may shift concentrated work later. Flexibility can be a genuine circadian benefit.

The problem is not the home.

It is the missing edge.

Knowledge work has unusually weak natural stopping rules. A factory line stops. A store closes. A plane lands. A patient is handed to another clinician. A document can always be improved. A queue can always be reduced. There is always another message because somebody somewhere is awake.

Global companies turn time zones into productive continuity. Work passes from California to Singapore to London and back again. That can be excellent system design when the work passes cleanly. It becomes terrible human design when the people are expected to pass with it.

The phrase always on is revealing. Machines are supposed to be always on.

People are increasingly asked to approximate the condition.

For much of the industrial era, fatigue was visible because the work was visibly physical. A miner slowed. A machinist's hands became less steady. A driver drifted. Knowledge work disguises impairment better. The tired analyst can still type. The exhausted manager can still join the call. The engineer can still move a ticket to done.

Output survives long enough to hide judgment loss.

This book has returned to that problem because it is the most dangerous property of fatigue. Sleep loss does not reliably announce itself by making a person collapse. It can degrade performance while preserving the appearance of participation.

Digital work intensifies the ambiguity. Many tasks have no immediate physical consequence. A bad paragraph can be fixed tomorrow. A mediocre slide deck rarely kills anyone. That makes it tempting to conclude that fatigue matters less.

Sometimes it does.

Risk should be proportional to consequence. Nobody needs an aviation-style fatigue-management system for choosing the font in an internal memo.

But knowledge workers also write production code, approve financial transfers, interpret medical images, moderate dangerous content, run security operations, manage electrical grids, make legal decisions, and coordinate emergency response. The category knowledge work contains both low-stakes and very high-stakes cognition.

The common vulnerability is that the product is thought.

Fatigue can slow reaction, narrow attention, weaken short-term memory, and impair judgment. NIOSH lists those effects in its workplace guidance. None requires a person to feel dramatically sleepy.

The modern workday also has a peculiar relationship with efficiency. Every tool that removes friction creates a plausible argument for doing more.

Email eliminated the time required to type, print, route, and deliver a memo. It did not eliminate memos. Messaging eliminated much of email's formality and produced more messages. Video conferencing removed travel between meetings and made it possible to schedule meetings back to back across continents. Cloud documents removed version delays and made revision continuous.

Artificial intelligence accelerates the pattern again.

A worker can now draft faster, summarize faster, generate alternatives faster, search faster, code faster, and respond faster. These are useful capabilities. They may remove drudgery and shorten tasks that once consumed evenings.

Or they may raise the expected volume of work until the evening is full again.

The outcome is not a property of the model. It is a property of the institution using it.

Productivity technology has always contained this ambiguity. A faster process can buy leisure or buy throughput. Markets and managers often prefer throughput because throughput appears on a dashboard. Recovered sleep rarely does.

That creates a new version of an old bargain. The organization provides tools that let a worker accomplish more in an hour, then quietly revises what counts as an acceptable hour.

The danger is not that AI never sleeps.

Servers have never slept. Spreadsheets have never slept. The danger is using a machine's availability as a benchmark for a human's responsiveness.

A system that can answer at 2:14 a.m. changes the social meaning of waiting until morning. Customers become accustomed to instant replies. Managers can generate requests faster. Employees can clear tasks from bed. The marginal cost of asking falls toward zero while the human cost of receiving the request does not.

This is how fatigue can become a feature without appearing in any product specification.

It emerges from incentives.

The employee who responds at night looks committed. The team that accepts an early call accommodates headquarters. The founder who works through the weekend tells a story investors understand. The junior worker learns that availability is a form of status competition.

Nobody needs to order anyone to sleep less.

A culture can do it with praise.

The mechanism resembles the medical residency culture we encountered earlier. Halsted's system did not survive for a century because every hospital administrator conducted a careful experiment and concluded that exhausted residents delivered better care. It survived because endurance became entangled with professional identity, staffing economics, training tradition, and prestige.

Knowledge work can make the same mistake with responsiveness.

The counterargument deserves more than a sentence. Flexible work has allowed millions of people to arrange jobs around disability, caregiving, geography, and chronotype in ways a rigid office day could not. Remote work can return commuting time to sleep. Asynchronous work can reduce the tyranny of the meeting. AI can remove tedious overnight labor and make shorter workdays economically possible.

All of that is real.

The relevant question is who captures the saved time.

If a tool reduces a four-hour task to one hour and the worker goes home three hours earlier, technology has purchased time. If the organization assigns four tasks, technology has purchased output. Neither choice is automatically wrong. The second becomes a sleep problem when every efficiency gain is converted into demand while biological recovery remains fixed.

There is a limit to how many times the day can be optimized before optimization reaches the night.

The American Time Use Survey offers a useful corrective to apocalyptic claims that nobody sleeps anymore. In 2025, respondents reported an average of about nine hours a day in sleeping. Survey measures have limitations: time in the sleep category is not identical to physiologically measured sleep, averages hide short sleepers and long sleepers, and weekdays differ from weekends. Still, the number is a warning against telling an easy decline story unsupported by the data.

Civilization has not simply stopped sleeping.

It has distributed sleep opportunity unevenly and built systems in which some people are repeatedly asked to trade it for flexibility, income, care, status, or responsiveness.

That is a harder problem because there is no universal bedtime to legislate.

The better target is work design.

Does the organization create unnecessary overnight alerts? Are response expectations explicit, or are employees forced to infer them from who gets promoted? Can work move across time zones without requiring the same person to follow it? Are hazardous decisions scheduled with fatigue in mind? Does an efficiency tool reduce workload, or merely increase the rate at which work arrives? Can an employee say I am too fatigued to do this safely without converting a biological fact into a moral confession?

These questions sound less glamorous than a new wearable or supplement. They are also closer to the source of the problem.

For seventy years, sleep science has become steadily better at measuring the individual.

The next step is measuring the schedule.
