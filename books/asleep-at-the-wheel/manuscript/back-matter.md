## Epilogue

None of the people in this book set out to build a civilization that runs on borrowed time. The surgeon who trained residents on thirty-six-hour shifts believed he was building discipline. The plant operators on the graveyard shift believed they were doing the same job their colleagues did at nine in the morning, only in the dark. The parents up every ninety minutes with a newborn believed, correctly, that there was no schedule available to them that would have let them sleep through it. Nobody in a control room, a cockpit, a locker room, or a nursery sat down and decided that fatigue was an acceptable operating cost. It became one anyway, one reasonable-sounding decision at a time, made by people who were rarely asked to weigh the specific, measurable thing they were trading away.

That is the pattern this book has tried to hold still long enough to look at. Not a single villain, not a single fixable rule, but a long list of institutions that each discovered, on their own timeline and usually after a documented failure, that sleep behaves like the load-bearing wall it is. Some of them changed. Hospitals capped hours after a family insisted on knowing why their daughter died. Regulators mandated apnea screening after a train left the rails a few hundred feet from a curve everyone already knew was dangerous. A handful of employers, school districts, and national health services ran real pilots and published real numbers instead of a testimonial. None of that adds up to a solved problem. It adds up to proof that the problem is solvable, which is a different and more useful thing to know.

The honest version of this book's argument is narrower than a slogan. It is not that eight hours will fix everything, or that every accident in these pages had one cause, or that every industry that runs at night is negligent for doing so. Some of the institutions in this book face constraints — a hospital that cannot simply close overnight, a ship that cannot stop crossing an ocean, a newborn who does not know what a schedule is — that no amount of circadian literacy will dissolve. What changes when an institution takes sleep seriously is not that the hard tradeoffs disappear. It is that they get made on purpose, by people who were told the actual cost, instead of being made by default and discovered later in an investigation report.

## Acknowledgments

This manuscript drew on the published work of sleep researchers, accident investigators, historians, and public-health economists whose names appear throughout its chapters and its source ledger — Nathaniel Kleitman and Eugene Aserinsky's original discovery of REM sleep chief among them, a debt that runs through nearly every later chapter in some form. It also drew on the plainer, more painful record kept by the National Transportation Safety Board, the Rogers Commission, the Kemeny Commission, and the many families and colleagues who insisted that an institution explain itself after something went wrong. Any errors of fact or emphasis that remain are the manuscript's own, not theirs.

## About the Author

Sven Hardy Benson writes narrative nonfiction about the systems that quietly decide how modern life actually runs — markets, institutions, infrastructure, and, in this case, the biological clock every one of those systems is built on top of whether it admits it or not.
