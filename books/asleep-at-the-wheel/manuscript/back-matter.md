## Epilogue

None of the people in this book set out to build a civilization that runs on borrowed time. Hospitals wanted continuity, freight networks wanted movement, schools wanted workable buses, families wanted to care for children, and twenty-four-hour systems wanted somebody awake when the rest of the city was not. The recurring problem was not a single villain or a single bad rule. It was that each reasonable-sounding decision could price its visible benefit while leaving recovery to be absorbed somewhere else.

That is why the most useful changes in these pages were rarely declarations about sleep itself. They were changes to hours, staffing, start times, screening, control rooms, handoffs, automatic safeguards, and the evidence allowed into a scheduling decision. None abolished the underlying tradeoff. They made more of it visible before a tired person had to carry it alone. That is enough of a standard for an institution to begin with.

This manuscript drew on the published work of sleep researchers, accident investigators, historians, and public-health economists whose names appear throughout its chapters and its source ledger, Nathaniel Kleitman and Eugene Aserinsky's original discovery of REM sleep chief among them, a debt that runs through nearly every later chapter in some form. It also drew on the plainer, more painful record kept by the National Transportation Safety Board, the Rogers Commission, the Kemeny Commission, and the many families and colleagues who insisted that an institution explain itself after something went wrong.
