# The Reactor at 1:23 A.M.

At 1:23 in the morning on April 26, 1986, the operators of reactor number four at the Chernobyl nuclear power plant were already deep inside a test that had not gone according to plan.

The test was meant to answer a narrow engineering question. If outside electric power were lost, could the slowing turbine-generator continue producing enough electricity for a short interval while emergency diesel generators came up to speed? The plant was preparing to shut the reactor down for maintenance, which created an opportunity to run the experiment.

The shutdown began on April 25. Then the electrical grid controller asked the plant to delay the power reduction. The reactor continued operating for hours at partial power. Near 11:10 that night, the reduction resumed.

After midnight, the sequence became more difficult.

According to the International Atomic Energy Agency's later INSAG-7 review, an operational mistake during the transition caused reactor power to fall to about 30 megawatts thermal, far below the intended level. The operators raised power again, but the reactor was now in an awkward condition. Control rods had been withdrawn. The operating reactivity margin was lower than desirable, although the operators did not have a direct instrument telling them its current value. Several safety systems had been disabled or placed in configurations associated with the planned test.

At 1:23:04, the turbine stop valves began to close and the rundown test began.

Thirty-six seconds later, at 1:23:40, the emergency protection button was pressed.

The control rods started moving into the core.

The reactor surged.

Within seconds, the power excursion destroyed the reactor.

Because the hour is so memorable, Chernobyl has often appeared on lists of disasters that happened at night. It is tempting in a book about sleep to treat the clock as an explanation. The body is at a circadian low in the early morning. Vigilance is harder to sustain. Night work is biologically expensive. The accident happened at 1:23 a.m. The story seems to assemble itself.

The official record does not let us do that.

INSAG-7, published in 1992 as a substantial reconsideration of earlier explanations, does not identify fatigue as the cause of the Chernobyl accident. It does not present the catastrophe as a case in which sleepy operators simply made a fatal mistake.

Its conclusions are more unsettling.

The review shifted much of the causal weight toward features of the RBMK reactor design, weaknesses in safety analysis, deficient operating procedures, and a safety culture in which operators had not been given complete information about some of the system's dangerous characteristics. The reactor had a large positive void coefficient under relevant conditions. More notoriously, the design of the control rods meant that the initial movement of rods during an emergency shutdown could temporarily add positive reactivity in parts of the core rather than immediately suppressing it. The emergency protection system could therefore behave in a way that contradicted the ordinary intuition behind an emergency stop button.

The operators also made decisions and violated requirements that mattered. INSAG-7 did not absolve them. But it rejected the convenient idea that the accident could be explained principally by a few reckless people improvising against a basically safe machine.

The machine and the institution had been built with too much dependence on correct human action.

That distinction belongs at the center of any serious discussion of fatigue.

If a safety system works only when a person interprets ambiguous information correctly, remembers a procedural constraint, resists production pressure, recognizes a rare configuration, and acts quickly at 1:23 in the morning, then the biological condition of the operator matters even if no investigation can prove that fatigue caused the event.

Human fallibility is part of the design environment.

It would be bad history to rewrite Chernobyl as a sleep-deprivation accident. It would be equally strange to design a twenty-four-hour control system as though the people operating it had no circadian rhythm.

The difference is between causal attribution and safety engineering.

Causal attribution asks what evidence shows happened in this accident.

Safety engineering asks what conditions a system should be able to survive next time.

Those are not the same question.

The Chernobyl chronology shows why.

The test itself had been postponed for hours because the power grid needed the reactor. That delay moved work planned around a shutdown into the night. A new shift inherited the experiment. The reactor passed through unstable operating conditions. Operators lacked a simple real-time display of an important safety parameter. Procedures and design characteristics interacted in ways that made the system difficult to reason about from the control room.

None of those facts proves a tired operator missed something an alert operator would have caught.

Together they describe a system with very little room for imperfect cognition.

Nuclear power is an unusually revealing industry because it cannot choose to operate only when people are naturally alert. A reactor runs continuously. Electricity demand does not stop at midnight. Emergencies, maintenance, alarms, weather events, equipment failures, and grid disturbances can occur at any hour.

The engineering problem is therefore not how to abolish night work.

It is how to make night work survivable.

Seven years before Chernobyl, another nuclear accident had already exposed a related weakness in a very different reactor design.

At about four in the morning on March 28, 1979, the Three Mile Island Unit 2 reactor near Middletown, Pennsylvania, experienced a failure in its secondary cooling system. The main feedwater pumps stopped delivering water to the steam generators. The turbine and reactor shut down automatically, as designed.

Then a pressure-relief valve opened.

It should have closed again.

It did not.

The control room indication led operators to believe the valve had received the command to close. It did not show them the physical position of the valve itself. Coolant continued escaping from the primary system while the panel suggested that the valve was shut.

The operators faced a second misleading signal.

As coolant was lost, water rose in the pressurizer. They had been trained to avoid letting the pressurizer become completely full, and the high level made them fear that the system contained too much water. In response, they reduced the flow of emergency cooling water.

The reactor core was actually losing coolant.

The instrumentation made the local evidence look like a different problem.

The U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission's account of the accident describes the cause as a combination of equipment malfunctions, design-related problems, and worker errors. It does not say the accident happened because the control-room crew was tired. The famous four-a.m. starting time belongs in the chronology, not in a verdict the evidence cannot support.

Again, the more useful lesson is about the system's demand on cognition.

The control room delivered hundreds of alarms and indications. Some information was misleading. Some important information was not displayed in the form operators needed. Training did not adequately prepare the crew for the accident as it unfolded. The people in the room constructed a model of the reactor from the signals available to them, and the model was wrong.

That can happen at four in the afternoon.

At four in the morning, a safety system should assume it can happen more easily.

Circadian science gives us good reason for that assumption without needing to retrofit fatigue into an accident report. Alertness, reaction time, sustained attention, and cognitive performance vary across the twenty-four-hour day. The nuclear industry now treats fatigue and circadian factors explicitly as human-performance concerns. NRC guidance defines circadian factors in terms of naturally recurring variation in alertness and performance, and the agency's fitness-for-duty framework includes fatigue-management requirements.

The change in language matters.

Early safety systems often treated the operator as the final layer of reliability. If automation failed, the operator would notice. If an alarm sounded, the operator would interpret it. If several instruments disagreed, the operator would infer the hidden state. If the procedure did not fit the situation, the operator would exercise judgment.

The human being was the flexible component.

That flexibility is real. People can integrate weak signals, recognize patterns no designer anticipated, improvise around damaged equipment, and coordinate with other people when a procedure stops making sense. Entire accidents have been prevented by operators doing things the original designers did not script.

But flexibility has a cost.

The same human nervous system that can improvise has finite attention, working memory, and wakefulness. It can be overloaded by alarms. It can become anchored on a first explanation. It can misread a control whose indication does not match the physical state of the machine. It performs differently after a normal night's sleep than after a long shift crossing the circadian low.

A robust system uses human adaptability without assuming human perfection.

Three Mile Island forced the American nuclear industry to confront this problem directly. The aftermath changed operator training, emergency procedures, control-room design, human-factors engineering, inspection, and regulatory oversight. The lesson was not merely to tell operators to pay more attention. It was to make the plant easier to understand when something went wrong.

That is a profound shift in where responsibility sits.

If a person misinterprets a display, one response is to train the person harder.

Another is to ask why the display made the dangerous interpretation plausible.

If a crew misses a signal in a field of alarms, one response is to demand greater vigilance.

Another is to ask why the system cannot distinguish a consequential alarm from a hundred less important ones.

If a procedure requires a person to remember an unusual reactor characteristic during an unstable transition, one response is discipline.

Another is to ask why the hazardous state is available without stronger interlocks, clearer information, or an automatic barrier.

Sleep science pushes us toward the second category of questions because fatigue is not unusual misconduct.

It is predictable variance.

A nuclear plant that runs twenty-four hours a day knows in advance that some consequential decisions will be made at biologically difficult hours. That fact belongs in design just as temperature, vibration, radiation, and equipment wear belong in design.

The NRC eventually made working hours part of that design problem too.

Long before today's detailed fatigue-management rules, the agency articulated limits intended to prevent excessive overtime from reducing mental alertness and decision-making capability. Nuclear operators and other covered workers are now subject to work-hour controls and fatigue-management programs rather than a vague expectation that professionals will know when they are too tired.

The exact limits are not a biological proof that a person is safe beneath one number and unsafe above it. Like residency duty hours, they are enforceable boundaries placed around a continuous risk.

The important change is institutional ownership.

Fatigue is no longer merely the operator's private responsibility.

The facility must manage schedules, minimum breaks, overtime, fitness for duty, and circumstances in which fatigue can become a safety issue. It must treat a sleepy operator less like a person with weak character and more like a degraded component whose condition the system has a duty to anticipate.

That language can sound dehumanizing until one notices its moral advantage.

Machines are not blamed for needing cooling.

Steel is not accused of lacking commitment when it expands with heat.

A battery is not told to develop resilience after its charge is exhausted.

When a human limitation becomes part of engineering, the person gains permission to be biological.

The hard part is that people are not batteries.

A worker can sometimes perform brilliantly while tired. A veteran operator may recognize a pattern instantly that a rested novice misses. A crisis can temporarily increase arousal. Teams can compensate for one another. Caffeine, light, movement, conversation, strategic naps, stable schedules, and experience can all improve performance.

This variability is useful, but it makes bad systems tempting.

Because tired people often succeed, an institution can keep borrowing against them until one does not.

That is why accident narratives matter so much. They reveal the hidden assumptions a successful system normally conceals.

Before Three Mile Island, the control room could look adequate because operators usually had enough information for normal operations. During the accident, the mismatch between display and plant state became consequential. Before Chernobyl, the emergency shutdown system could look like protection because an emergency shutdown generally ought to reduce reactivity. Under the accident conditions, a design feature made the first movement of the rods dangerous.

Normal success had hidden an edge case.

Fatigue behaves similarly.

A night shift can run smoothly for months. The work is familiar. The plant is stable. The crew drinks coffee. Nothing happens. The organization learns that the schedule is fine.

Then a rare event arrives during the circadian low and demands exactly the kind of flexible, sustained, high-stakes reasoning the schedule has made harder.

The absence of prior failure was never evidence that biology had adapted away.

This does not mean every twenty-four-hour institution should double its staffing at night or forbid difficult work before dawn. Risk differs by task. A routine monitoring period is not the same as a complex maintenance evolution. A stable reactor is not the same as a reactor being restarted or shut down. A well-practiced response is not the same as novel diagnosis under ambiguous alarms.

A sleep-literate system allocates protection where the cognitive demand is highest.

That can mean predictable rotations rather than chaotic ones. It can mean enough recovery time between shifts. It can mean scheduling discretionary high-risk maintenance away from known low points when possible. It can mean allowing a worker to report fatigue without turning the declaration into a disciplinary confession. It can mean alarm design that helps rather than overwhelms. It can mean independent verification for an unusual configuration, or an automatic interlock where human memory is too weak a barrier.

It can also mean better automation.

Automation is often proposed as the obvious solution to tired operators. Let the machine watch itself. Let software filter alarms. Let automatic protection act faster than a person can.

Nuclear power already depends heavily on that logic.

But automation changes the human job rather than eliminating it. The more reliable normal operation becomes, the more the operator's remaining work concentrates around anomalies, maintenance, transitions, and failures. Those are precisely the moments when context matters most. A person can spend hours supervising a stable system and then suddenly be asked to understand something the automation could not resolve.

This is the vigilance paradox of high reliability.

The better the machine handles the routine, the rarer and stranger the human intervention becomes.

A badly designed automated system can therefore leave the operator both bored and essential.

The person receives less practice in direct control while retaining responsibility for the hardest cases.

Sleep makes that arrangement more fragile because passive monitoring is itself difficult to sustain during circadian lows. A control room does not have to be physically exhausting to be biologically demanding. Quiet can be as dangerous as chaos when the task is to remain ready for an event that almost never happens.

The engineering answer is not to romanticize the alert operator of the past.

It is to design for a human who will sometimes be tired, sometimes overloaded, sometimes bored, sometimes wrong, and still capable of rescuing the system when given the right information and authority.

Chernobyl and Three Mile Island point toward that answer from opposite directions.

Chernobyl showed the danger of a reactor design and safety culture that could put operators in conditions they did not fully understand and then rely on them to behave correctly inside those conditions.

Three Mile Island showed the danger of a control room that could represent the plant in a way that made an incorrect diagnosis reasonable.

Neither accident needs a fatigue verdict to belong in a history of institutional sleep.

They belong because their industries eventually stopped treating human performance as an unlimited reserve.

The clock on the wall did not cause either accident.

It told the designers something they should have known about the person looking at the instruments.

The next cases make the connection more direct.

Ships, trains, trucks, and aircraft put a human being inside a moving system and ask that person to maintain attention for hours. When attention fails, investigators can sometimes reconstruct the sleep history, work schedule, and final minutes with unusual precision.

At sea, one of the most famous examples would leave eleven million gallons of crude oil in Prince William Sound.

This time, fatigue would not be merely a background condition inferred from the hour.
