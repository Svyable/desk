# The Insomnia Economy

Insomnia is an unusually marketable problem because the customer is awake while suffering from it.

At two in the morning, the remedies are all available. Prescription drugs have advertisements and patient guides. Melatonin sits on pharmacy shelves in bottles and gummies. Mattresses promise cooling. Headbands promise brain sensing. Apps promise breathing exercises. Podcasts promise stories boring enough to induce unconsciousness. A person who cannot sleep has time to compare them.

The market does not need to invent the problem. Insomnia is real, common, and sometimes brutal. The interesting question is what happens when a biological function becomes a consumer category before its causes have been separated.

A night of wakefulness can come from grief, pain, hot weather, a baby, a medication, depression, anxiety, menopause, sleep apnea, restless legs syndrome, jet lag, a late espresso, a barking dog, rotating shifts, a bad mattress, a deadline, or no obvious reason at all. Chronic insomnia disorder is narrower than "I slept badly." It involves persistent difficulty sleeping together with meaningful daytime consequences despite adequate opportunity for sleep.

Those distinctions are clinically useful and commercially inconvenient.

The easiest product to sell is one that treats sleeplessness as a single missing ingredient.

For much of the twentieth century, medicine tried versions of that approach with increasingly sophisticated sedatives. Barbiturates were widely used and carried serious risks of tolerance, dependence, overdose, and dangerous interactions. Benzodiazepines offered another route into sedation and anxiety relief, while bringing their own dependence and impairment concerns. Later came the non-benzodiazepine hypnotics commonly called Z-drugs: zolpidem, eszopiclone, and zaleplon.

The name made them sound like a new category. Their central promise was familiar: take something tonight and sleep.

That promise can be useful. A person in acute distress after bereavement or illness may benefit from short-term pharmacologic help. Someone with carefully diagnosed insomnia may decide with a clinician that a medication's benefits outweigh its harms. A traveler crossing time zones, a hospitalized patient, or a person whose sleep has collapsed during another treatment may have a legitimate reason to use a hypnotic.

The mistake is not that sleeping pills exist.

It is believing that sedation and restored sleep are identical products.

In April 2019, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration required its strongest boxed warning for eszopiclone, zaleplon, and zolpidem because of rare but serious injuries associated with complex sleep behaviors. People had reported sleepwalking, sleep driving, cooking, taking medications, wandering into dangerous environments, and other actions performed while not fully awake. Some episodes caused severe injury or death.

The warning did not say these medicines were useless. It said their benefits came attached to a failure mode serious enough to deserve the most conspicuous kind of labeling the agency uses.

That is a good model for thinking about the entire insomnia economy. A remedy can work and still have a boundary.

Prescription medicines at least enter a regulatory system built around the idea that boundaries matter. The evidence can be imperfect, but dosage, indication, manufacturing, labeling, contraindications, and adverse-event reporting are formal questions.

Then there is melatonin.

Melatonin occupies an odd legal and cultural space in the United States. The body makes it. The molecule participates in circadian timing. In many countries it is treated more like a medicine. In American stores it can be sold as a dietary supplement.

That classification changes what "on the shelf" means.

The FDA does not approve dietary supplements for safety and effectiveness before they are marketed in the way it approves drugs. Manufacturers have responsibilities for safety, labeling, and manufacturing practice, and the agency can act against unlawful products. But a bottle of melatonin is not evidence that the FDA reviewed the product and concluded that the labeled dose produces the promised sleep outcome.

This distinction is easy to lose because the packaging does not feel provisional. A child-friendly gummy can look more controlled than a prescription bottle. The dose is printed in milligrams. The typography is calm. The word "sleep" appears near a moon.

The precision can be cosmetic.

In 2023, Pieter Cohen and colleagues bought twenty-five melatonin gummy products sold in the United States and chemically analyzed them. One contained no detectable melatonin. Among the products that did contain melatonin, the measured quantity ranged from 74 percent to 347 percent of what the label claimed. Twenty-two of the twenty-five products were inaccurately labeled by the study's criterion.

That result deserves both attention and restraint.

It was a small study. It tested one sample from each brand. It focused on gummies rather than every form of melatonin. It could not establish that every batch from a company would be similarly variable. The researchers themselves stated those limitations.

But the study also punctured a very specific consumer assumption: that a label saying five milligrams necessarily means the product delivers something close to five milligrams.

The problem is more consequential with children because flavored gummies are easy to mistake for candy and because pediatric dosing is especially vulnerable to uncertainty. Federal health agencies have also documented a substantial rise in pediatric melatonin exposures and emergency visits over the past decade.

Again, the clean moral is not "melatonin bad."

Melatonin has legitimate uses. It can shift circadian timing when used appropriately. Evidence supports benefit for some jet-lag and circadian-rhythm situations. Short-term use appears relatively safe for many adults. People with certain sleep problems may find it useful under medical guidance.

Its cultural role, however, has drifted beyond circadian medicine.

A hormone that signals biological night has been marketed as if it were a universal off switch.

That difference takes us back to the clock inside the body. Melatonin secretion normally rises in darkness as part of the circadian system. Taking exogenous melatonin can influence timing. The effect depends on dose and, crucially, when it is taken relative to the person's internal clock.

A drug-like instruction—"take this when you want to sleep"—can therefore miss the mechanism.

The insomnia market flourishes partly because mechanisms are hard to sell. They require diagnosis. They require asking what kind of sleeplessness a person has. They may require changing light exposure, wake time, time in bed, caffeine, work schedules, bedroom behavior, treatment of another illness, or expectations about sleep.

A pill asks less of the night.

The treatment with the strongest professional claim to first-line status for chronic insomnia is not a pill at all.

Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia, usually called CBT-I, combines several techniques. It may restrict the amount of time a person spends in bed to consolidate sleep. It may use stimulus control so the bed stops functioning as a place for wakeful worry. It addresses catastrophic beliefs about sleeplessness. It stabilizes timing. Sleep hygiene can be part of the package, but CBT-I is not simply a list telling people to avoid caffeine and buy curtains.

The American College of Physicians recommended CBT-I as initial treatment for chronic insomnia in its 2016 guideline. Sleep-medicine organizations have issued similar guidance in clinical practice.

This creates an economic puzzle.

Why does the treatment that asks people to relearn sleep behavior often feel less visible than the products sold beside the pharmacy register?

Part of the answer is access. A competent CBT-I clinician takes training and time. Insurance coverage can be uneven. Rural patients may have few specialists. Waiting lists turn a theoretically first-line treatment into a practical second or third line. Digital CBT-I programs can expand access, but they are not equally suitable for every patient and not every commercial app is an evidence-based treatment.

Part of the answer is psychology. Chronic insomnia often makes people desperate for immediate relief. CBT-I can initially feel counterintuitive or even unpleasant. Someone exhausted from poor sleep may be told to spend less time in bed. The treatment asks for consistency before it guarantees comfort.

A sedative makes a simpler proposition.

Take this tonight.

Markets favor interventions whose timing matches the pain.

The benefit of a sleeping pill can begin within hours. The benefit of reorganizing behavior may take weeks. The product that solves tonight will often feel more valuable than the process that changes next month, even if the longer process has a better evidence profile for chronic insomnia.

This is not unique to sleep. Health markets repeatedly reward immediacy. Painkillers compete with physical therapy. Weight-loss products compete with slower changes in diet, activity, environment, and medication. Attention aids compete with workload design. The fast intervention is not necessarily wrong. It simply receives an economic advantage because its benefit arrives near the point of purchase.

Insomnia adds one more complication: trying hard to sleep can make sleep harder.

Sleep is partly voluntary in preparation and largely involuntary in execution. A person can dim lights, get into bed, turn off a phone, and create an opportunity for sleep. They cannot command the final transition with the same muscle they use to stand up from a chair.

That makes insomnia especially vulnerable to performance anxiety.

A bad night produces concern. Concern produces monitoring. Monitoring makes wakefulness more salient. The person goes to bed earlier to compensate, spends more time awake in bed, watches the clock, calculates remaining sleep, and becomes more distressed by each passing minute.

Some products interrupt that loop. Others monetize it.

The distinction can be subtle because the same device or supplement may help one person and intensify another person's preoccupation. White noise may mask a real environmental problem. A cooling mattress may help someone whose bedroom is genuinely too warm. A carefully prescribed hypnotic may break an acute cycle. Melatonin may shift a delayed circadian phase. CBT-I may address learned wakefulness.

The useful question is not whether the product is natural, technological, pharmaceutical, or behavioral.

It is what problem the product is actually solving.

A market organized around "sleep" tends to flatten those differences because the customer arrives with a symptom, not a mechanism.

That flattening is profitable enough to shape language. People now talk about sleep optimization when many of them are simply trying to sleep normally. Products promise deeper sleep, restorative sleep, clean sleep, quality sleep, recovery sleep, and readiness for tomorrow. The nouns multiply because "unconscious for long enough" is difficult to trademark.

Clinical sleep medicine is less poetic. It separates insomnia from sleep apnea, circadian disorders, parasomnias, hypersomnolence, restless legs syndrome, and other conditions because the treatment depends on the distinction.

The consumer market has an incentive to blur it back together.

There is also a political convenience in private remedies. A company can sell a worker melatonin without changing the shift schedule that pushed the worker's sleep late. An employer can offer meditation software without changing midnight email expectations. A school can teach sleep hygiene while ringing the first bell at a biologically hostile hour. A hospital can provide fatigue education without staffing enough people to reduce chronic overtime.

The product individualizes a scheduling problem.

Sometimes that is appropriate. Institutions cannot arrange every person's bedroom, anxiety level, parenting duties, or genetic chronotype. Personal treatment matters.

But the direction of responsibility is worth watching.

If a civilization repeatedly designs days that produce sleepless nights, the emergence of a large insomnia market does not prove the market is fraudulent. It proves there is demand.

The question is whether the demand is being treated at the level where it originates.

Prescription sedatives, supplements, and behavioral therapy all reveal different answers. Drugs can suppress wakefulness. Melatonin can alter timing. CBT-I can retrain the learned relationship between bed and sleep. None of them can by itself rewrite a night shift, move a school bell, shorten a commute, settle a newborn, or eliminate economic insecurity.

The sleepless person still has to wake up into the same society.

And increasingly, that person wakes up with a score.

The newest promise in the insomnia economy is not merely to help you sleep.

It is to tell you exactly how well you did.
