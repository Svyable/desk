# Quantified but Not Understood

A wristwatch can now tell you that you slept badly before you have decided how you feel.

That is a remarkable reversal.

For most of human history, sleep was inferred from memory, witness, and consequence. You woke rested or you did not. A spouse reported snoring. A parent noticed a child wandering at night. A physician asked when you went to bed and how often you woke. The laboratory changed that by adding electrodes, eye movements, muscle tone, airflow, oxygen, heart rhythm, and trained interpretation.

Consumer wearables changed it again by moving measurement out of the laboratory and onto millions of bodies.

The appeal is obvious. Sleep is invisible while it happens. A device promises to return the missing hours as data.

Total sleep time. Deep sleep. REM sleep. Restfulness. Efficiency. Readiness. Recovery. A score out of one hundred.

The vocabulary differs by manufacturer, but the psychological product is similar: the night becomes legible.

That legibility can be useful. A person who routinely sleeps five and a half hours may discover that the problem is not mysterious fatigue but insufficient time in bed. Someone whose bedtime varies wildly can see the pattern. A shift worker can compare schedules. A patient can bring weeks of approximate sleep timing to a clinician instead of reconstructing it from memory.

The device can make an invisible habit visible.

Then the screen assigns stages.

This is where confidence should slow down.

Clinical polysomnography measures sleep using multiple physiological signals, including brain electrical activity, eye movements, and muscle tone. Trained scoring rules use those signals to classify wakefulness and sleep stages. A ring or watch generally does not have those channels. It infers sleep from proxies such as movement, heart rate, heart-rate variability, skin temperature, and proprietary algorithms.

The distinction is not a technical footnote. It explains why a wearable can be good at estimating one thing and weaker at another.

Movement is useful for distinguishing broad patterns of sleep and wake, especially across many nights. It is less direct evidence of whether the brain is in N2, N3, or rapid-eye-movement sleep at a particular minute.

Manufacturers have improved their algorithms substantially. Modern devices are not the crude step counters that early sleep apps were. Some perform reasonably well for total sleep time and broad sleep-wake estimates under particular conditions. That progress deserves recognition.

It does not turn an inference into an electrode.

A 2024 laboratory study compared five commercial sleep trackers, research-grade actigraphy, and polysomnography in fifty-three young adults during a simultaneous overnight recording. Most of the consumer devices estimated total sleep time comparably to actigraphy. But the errors were not random. Devices tended to overestimate nights with shorter wake time and underestimate nights with longer wake time, and stage estimates showed device-specific proportional biases.

That is a more interesting result than saying wearables are accurate or inaccurate.

They are instruments with error structures.

The problem is that consumers rarely see the error structure. They see the score.

A clinical laboratory report arrives inside a medical context. A technician can note signal quality. A physician can interpret the result alongside symptoms and history. A consumer app usually converts uncertainty into interface design. A ring collected imperfect signals, an algorithm made inferences, a company selected thresholds, and the user receives a moon icon colored green or red.

The uncertainty has not vanished. It has been designed out of sight.

In 2018, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine issued a position statement on consumer sleep technology. It acknowledged the promise of these devices but warned that, without adequate validation and appropriate regulatory clearance, consumer technologies should not be used to diagnose or treat sleep disorders. The statement also suggested a more constructive role: patient-generated data can support conversations with clinicians when used in the context of proper evaluation.

That middle position is less satisfying than either extreme.

Wearable companies do not need to be dismissed as toys. Sleep specialists do not need to pretend that millions of nights of longitudinal data are meaningless. But a device that helps describe a pattern is not automatically qualified to diagnose the cause of the pattern.

This distinction matters most when the user is worried.

A low deep-sleep number can feel like evidence of brain failure. A low REM number can feel like lost memory. A readiness score can tell a healthy person to expect a bad day. The body has not changed between the moment before the screen lights up and the moment after it. The interpretation has.

That creates a new pathway into insomnia.

In 2017, Kelly Glazer Baron, Sabra Abbott, Nancy Jao, Natalie Manalo, and Rebecca Mullen published three clinical cases under a deliberately awkward new word: orthosomnia.

The term combined orthos, meaning correct, with somnia, sleep. The patients had become preoccupied with achieving the sleep numbers reported by their trackers. They spent extra time in bed trying to improve the data. They worried when the device said sleep quality was poor. The attempt to perfect sleep appeared to be reinforcing behaviors that can maintain insomnia.

Three cases do not establish an epidemic.

Orthosomnia is not a formal diagnosis simply because the word is memorable. The original paper was a case series, useful for identifying a phenomenon rather than measuring its prevalence. Most people who wear a sleep tracker will not become clinically anxious about it.

But the mechanism is believable because insomnia already contains a monitoring problem.

The sleepless person watches the clock. The quantified sleeper watches the dashboard.

Both are trying to answer a question sleep resists: Am I doing it yet?

A tracker can intensify that question by converting a subjective morning into a test result. Someone may wake feeling fine and then discover a score of sixty-two. The score can overwrite experience. A person who felt tired can receive a ninety and wonder whether the fatigue is illegitimate.

This is sometimes called a nocebo effect in casual discussion, but that label can be too tidy. The interesting issue is simpler: information changes attention.

Once sleep becomes a metric, users can optimize for the metric.

That is not always the same as optimizing for sleep.

A person may go to bed earlier to increase total sleep opportunity, which can help if the person was chronically sleep restricted. Another may spend ten hours in bed to chase an eight-hour sleep target, increasing wakefulness in bed and weakening sleep efficiency. Someone may avoid an evening with friends because alcohol or a late bedtime could lower a recovery score. Another may use the same warning to notice a pattern that genuinely harms performance.

The number does not tell us which person is behaving wisely.

Context does.

The more sophisticated the device becomes, the easier it is to forget that every metric embeds a choice about what matters.

A wearable may reward regularity. That is generally sensible. It may penalize late timing even when a late chronotype has arranged a stable schedule. It may estimate recovery partly from heart-rate variability, which can be influenced by exercise, alcohol, illness, stress, medications, menstrual cycle, temperature, and measurement conditions. It may classify still wakefulness as sleep. It may interpret restless sleep as wake.

None of this makes the metrics useless.

It makes them measurements.

Measurements always have a domain.

The laboratory world is full of this humility because researchers have learned to ask what an instrument actually detects. A thermometer does not measure infection. A pulse oximeter does not diagnose pneumonia. A blood-pressure cuff does not explain why pressure is elevated. The value comes from the measurement plus interpretation.

Consumer technology often collapses those two steps because interpretation is where the product experience lives.

A raw acceleration trace is not compelling. "You need more recovery" is.

This creates a business incentive to move from description toward judgment.

The same progression has happened across health technology. Step counters became activity scores. Heart-rate sensors became stress indicators. Glucose monitors became food rankings. Sleep trackers became readiness systems.

A consumer rarely buys a sensor because sensing is beautiful. The sensor must say what to do.

That pressure can exceed the evidence.

Sleep staging provides a good example. Even trained human scorers using full polysomnography do not agree on every epoch. Sleep is divided into categories because categories are useful, not because the brain flips clean switches visible from outside. A wearable adds another layer of inference and then presents the result with decimal precision.

Precision of display is not precision of knowledge.

The paradox is that consumer trackers may be most valuable when used less literally.

Look at trends rather than single nights. Use bedtime and wake-time estimates to identify routines. Notice whether alcohol, travel, illness, or late work consistently changes the pattern. Treat a stage estimate as an estimate. If symptoms are serious—loud snoring, witnessed breathing pauses, severe daytime sleepiness, persistent insomnia, unusual movements—seek medical evaluation rather than waiting for a wearable to declare a diagnosis.

That is less exciting than a score.

It is also closer to how measurement works.

There is an institutional lesson hiding inside the personal gadget.

Organizations love dashboards for the same reason individuals do. Dashboards turn messy systems into numbers that can be compared. What gets measured gets managed, the slogan says.

The slogan leaves out the second half: what gets measured can also get gamed, overinterpreted, and mistaken for the thing itself.

If an employer offered workers a sleep score and then kept the same destructive shifts, measurement would become surveillance rather than care. If a sports team rewarded recovery metrics without understanding individual variation, athletes would learn to manage the metric. If an insurer someday priced risk using consumer sleep data, uncertainty that once produced a colored ring on a phone could become a financial consequence.

The technology is therefore not merely about self-knowledge. It raises a governance question about who gets to interpret biological data.

For now, most sleep wearables remain voluntary consumer products. Users choose them because they want information. That is a meaningful protection.

But the direction of travel is worth noticing. Watches detect arrhythmias. Rings are used in research. Smartphones estimate behavior. Employers offer wellness platforms. The boundary between consumer curiosity and health infrastructure is porous.

Sleep may be especially attractive for quantification because it occupies so much time and because the sleeper cannot directly observe it.

That makes humility more important, not less.

The most useful sleep tracker may be the one that helps a person notice a pattern and then gets out of the way.

The least useful may be the one that creates a nightly performance review for a process that cannot be forced by effort.

We began this book with researchers struggling to make sleep visible at all. Aserinsky and Kleitman needed electrodes, paper traces, patient observation, and years of argument to establish that sleeping brains moved through recurring states.

Seventy years later, a ring can produce a stage graph before breakfast.

That is genuine progress.

It is also a reminder that visibility and understanding are different achievements.
