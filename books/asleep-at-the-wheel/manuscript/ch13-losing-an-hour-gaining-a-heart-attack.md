# Losing an Hour, Gaining a Heart Attack

At 2:00 on a Sunday morning, a government can make an hour disappear.

No hospital closes. No truck stops moving. No newborn agrees to postpone a feeding. The clocks simply jump from 1:59 to 3:00, and everybody who lives by those clocks is expected to absorb the difference by Monday.

It is one of the stranger experiments modern states conduct on their populations because almost nobody experiences it as an experiment. Daylight saving time arrives disguised as housekeeping. Phones update themselves. Microwave clocks blink until somebody remembers how to set them. The practical nuisance is small enough to joke about, which makes the biological question easy to miss.

An hour is not much until you take it from nearly everyone at once.

In October 2008, two Swedish researchers, Imre Janszky and Rickard Ljung, published a short correspondence in the New England Journal of Medicine examining myocardial infarctions around the spring and autumn clock changes. The paper was not a grand clinical trial. It was an analysis of a national registry. Its claim was correspondingly modest: the incidence of acute myocardial infarction appeared to rise after the spring transition and shift in a different pattern after the autumn transition.

The result fit an intuitive story. Spring daylight saving time removes an hour from the social night. For many people it also advances required wake time relative to the body's internal clock. Sleep gets shorter. Morning light arrives at a different clock hour. Commuting and work continue as scheduled. The cardiovascular system, like everything else, receives no memo explaining that the lost hour was legislative.

A finding like that is irresistible to headlines. "Spring forward" becomes "heart attack Monday." The temptation is to promote a small epidemiological signal into proof that the clock change kills people on schedule.

The evidence is not that clean.

A 2019 meta-analysis pooling seven studies and more than 115,000 subjects found a modest increase in acute myocardial infarction risk after the spring transition, about five percent in the pooled estimate, while the autumn effect was not comparably clear. A later meta-analysis covering twelve studies again found a small spring increase and substantial heterogeneity among the studies. Then a very large U.S. claims-data analysis involving more than thirty-six million people concluded that any cardiovascular increase around the spring transition was probably small and unlikely to be clinically important at the population level.

This is what mature evidence often looks like. The first striking result survives, but smaller than its retelling.

The useful conclusion is not that one missing hour is a cardiac weapon. It is that shifting social time produces measurable physiological and behavioral perturbations in at least some studies, with the strongest signals appearing after the spring advance. The magnitude is debated. The direction is more plausible than the dramatic version.

There is another reason to take the clock change seriously even if its average medical effect is small: averages are not schedules.

A person who already sleeps seven or eight hours, works predictable daytime hours, and can adjust bedtime may experience the transition as an inconvenience. A nurse finishing nights, a teenager already waking before biological dawn, a parent of a baby, a long-haul driver, or somebody with chronic short sleep begins closer to the edge. The same one-hour perturbation lands on different reserves.

That problem runs through this book. Institutions like uniform rules because uniform rules are easy to administer. Biology distributes the cost unevenly.

Daylight saving time has always been an administrative solution before it was a biological question. The clock does not create daylight. It reallocates the labels attached to daylight, moving an hour of usable light from the morning side of the social day to the evening side.

That can be useful.

Retailers like later light. People may exercise, shop, dine, or play outside after work. Some crime patterns may change. Some traffic risks shift with illumination. Parents may prefer children having more daylight after school. Workers who spend most of the day indoors may reasonably value an extra bright hour when they are free.

The debate gets confused when those benefits are dismissed as frivolous and the health arguments are treated as dispositive. They are neither. Clock policy is an allocation problem. Morning light and evening light are not interchangeable to human circadian biology, but they are not interchangeable to commerce, recreation, transportation, or family life either.

The United States learned this the dramatic way in 1974.

The Arab oil embargo had turned energy conservation into an emergency. Congress passed the Emergency Daylight Saving Time Energy Conservation Act, and the country moved onto year-round daylight saving time beginning January 6. The theory was straightforward: more daylight in the late afternoon might reduce demand for lighting and energy.

Then winter mornings arrived.

In northern cities, sunrise moved very late by the clock. Children waited for school buses in darkness. Morning commuters traveled before dawn. The abstract hour of evening light had become a concrete hour of morning dark.

Public support collapsed. Contemporary accounts and later government reviews found that the promised energy and safety gains were difficult to isolate from other changes. Congress amended the experiment before it ran its intended course and restored standard time for part of the winter.

The 1974 episode is often told as proof that permanent daylight saving time was tried and failed. That is directionally fair, but the details matter. The country was living through an oil crisis. School schedules were designed for a different clock. The intervention was national and abrupt. Energy use, vehicle technology, commuting patterns, school transportation, and residential lighting have all changed since then.

History is evidence, not a rerun.

It does establish one point that modern arguments sometimes forget: abolishing clock changes and choosing which clock to keep are separate decisions.

Sleep scientists and circadian researchers often prefer permanent standard time because morning light is the strongest natural cue for keeping the human clock aligned with the day. Permanent daylight saving time pushes clock time later relative to solar time. In winter, that can mean darker mornings and more waking before dawn.

Political advocates often prefer permanent daylight saving time because people experience evening light directly. An hour after work feels like usable life. An hour before work is, for many people, an hour they were going to spend indoors anyway.

Both positions can be rational because they optimize different things.

The federal legal structure adds another layer. Under the Uniform Time Act, states can exempt themselves from daylight saving time and remain on standard time, which is why Hawaii and most of Arizona do not participate in the seasonal switch. States cannot simply choose permanent daylight saving time under current law. That asymmetry has produced years of state resolutions and federal bills designed to "lock the clock."

The politics were still moving while this chapter was being drafted.

On July 14, 2026, the U.S. House of Representatives passed H.R. 139, the Sunshine Protection Act, by 308 votes to 117. The bill would make daylight saving time permanent while preserving existing state exemptions. It went to the Senate the next day. As of early September, the Senate had not passed it and no floor vote was scheduled; the chamber was in its late-summer pro forma period and was not due to resume regular business until mid-September.

This is a useful snapshot because it captures the strange consensus and disagreement around the clock. Large majorities say they dislike changing it. The harder question is what should replace the change.

Permanent standard time aligns more closely with solar time and circadian arguments. Permanent daylight saving time gives people more evening light. The current system preserves later winter sunrises at the price of two annual transitions. There is no option that changes the tilt of the Earth.

The phrase "daylight saving" encourages magical thinking. We do not save daylight. We rename it.

That renaming matters because schedules attach to names.

Suppose a school opens at 8:00. Under permanent daylight saving time, an 8:00 January start may occur at the same solar moment that 7:00 represented under standard time. The law has not altered the sun. It has altered the relationship between the sun and the institutional number printed on the schedule.

Institutions could respond by moving the schedule. Schools could start later in winter. Employers could allow seasonal flexibility. Transit could adapt. In practice, however, schedules are sticky. Once the clock says eight, eight begins to feel natural.

That is the hidden power of civil time. It converts a policy choice into common sense.

The heart-attack debate matters most as a warning against confusing the commonness of a rule with biological neutrality. A one-hour transition is small. It is also one of the rare moments when a whole society perturbs sleep timing in the same direction on the same night. That makes it scientifically interesting even when the measured effect is modest.

It also gives us a cleaner contrast with the previous chapter. Night workers live with circadian displacement repeatedly. Daylight saving time gives daytime workers a tiny, synchronized sample of what it feels like when social time moves and the body does not immediately follow.

Most people recover quickly. Some barely notice. That counterevidence matters because it keeps the claim proportional. The spring transition is not equivalent to shift work, chronic sleep restriction, jet lag across multiple time zones, or a night on call.

But the mechanism belongs to the same family.

The external clock moves first.

The body catches up later.

The political argument over daylight saving time is therefore less trivial than its recurring jokes suggest. It is a negotiation over which inconvenience society prefers to distribute: darker winter mornings, earlier winter evenings, or twice-yearly temporal whiplash.

The answer cannot come from cardiology alone. Nor can it come from retail sales, school-bus routes, or the preferences of people who like to barbecue at eight-thirty in June. A serious decision has to hold several ledgers at once.

That is the difficult part of treating sleep as infrastructure. Once biology gets a seat at the table, it does not automatically get every vote.

The market has found an easier response to this kind of conflict.

When schedules, stress, light, travel, work, parenting, and habit keep people awake, a company does not need Congress to move the clock.

It can sell them sleep.
