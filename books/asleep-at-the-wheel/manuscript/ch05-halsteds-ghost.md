# Halsted's Ghost

William Stewart Halsted did not ask his trainees to use cocaine.

That is worth saying first because the history of medical residency is often told too neatly. Halsted, one of the founding physicians of Johns Hopkins Hospital, became addicted to cocaine while experimenting with it as a local anesthetic in the 1880s. Treatment with morphine helped him get off cocaine and left him dependent on morphine for the rest of his life. He then built a surgical training system famous for intensity and prolonged presence in the hospital. The temptation is to draw a straight line: stimulant-addicted surgeon invents sleepless residency.

The evidence will not carry that sentence.

Halsted's drug dependence is well documented. His role in shaping modern surgical training is well documented. The proposition that his addiction directly produced the residency model is a later historical interpretation, intriguing but not provable in the way a laboratory result is provable. A 2020 historical analysis in the Canadian Journal of Surgery argues that the structure of Halsted's resident pyramid may have helped him conceal his continuing addiction while preserving excellent patient care and academic work. The authors themselves describe their method as a reinterpretation of known facts.

That is a more interesting story anyway.

Institutions rarely inherit one person's secret in a simple form. They inherit arrangements that worked well enough to survive.

Halsted's arrangement worked.

He was a brilliant and exacting surgeon. At Hopkins he advanced antiseptic practice, tissue handling, anesthesia, vascular and endocrine surgery, hernia repair, breast surgery, and surgical education. His method emphasized careful technique over speed. His training system gave young surgeons increasing responsibility under a hierarchy of senior residents and faculty. It immersed them in the hospital long enough to watch disease, operations, complications, and recovery unfold.

The word resident described a way of life before it became a job title.

A trainee was not simply an employee who arrived, completed a shift, and left. The hospital was the educational environment, the clinical environment, and often effectively the resident's home. Presence was part of the pedagogy.

There was logic in this.

Medicine is full of events that do not occur on schedule. A patient admitted in the afternoon may deteriorate at midnight. The wound a surgeon closes today can bleed tomorrow. A diagnosis becomes clearer as symptoms evolve. A trainee who repeatedly hands every patient to someone else may gain hours of rest and lose the continuity through which judgment is built.

Experience is not downloaded. It accumulates through contact.

For generations, American residency treated time in the hospital as one of the raw materials of competence. More cases meant more pattern recognition. More nights meant more emergencies. More responsibility meant faster maturation. The structure also supplied hospitals with a workforce capable of covering patients around the clock.

Education and service grew together.

That combination made hours difficult to challenge because every reduction could be described as a loss in two currencies at once. Fewer hours might mean less exhaustion. They might also mean fewer cases, more handoffs, more fragmentation, and more work shifted to somebody else.

The argument was not invented by sadists. It came from real properties of medicine.

The culture around the argument became something else.

Long hours turned into evidence of commitment. Surviving them became part of professional identity. Senior physicians who had trained under extreme schedules could reasonably believe that the experience had made them better. They had seen more, endured more, and learned to function under pressure. Their own careers were proof that the system could produce excellent doctors.

The missing counterfactual was harder to see.

Would they have become equally good physicians with more sleep and better-designed continuity? Would fewer people have left medicine? Would errors have fallen? Would additional handoffs have created different errors? Which hours were educational and which were merely service? The traditional system had no control group.

Then an eighteen-year-old woman died at New York Hospital in March 1984.

Her name was Libby Zion.

The case has been repeated so often that it can sound simpler than the record. Zion arrived with fever, agitation, and other symptoms. She was taking phenelzine, a monoamine oxidase inhibitor. In the hospital she received meperidine, among other treatment. The drug combination can contribute to serotonin toxicity. Her condition worsened rapidly, and she died within hours.

Her father, Sidney Zion, was a journalist and lawyer with the ability and determination to make the death impossible for the medical establishment to treat as a private tragedy. He believed inexperienced and exhausted house officers had been left with too much responsibility and too little supervision.

The resulting investigations pulled several problems into public view at once.

One was supervision. Young physicians were making consequential decisions without the degree of attending oversight that critics believed the situation required.

One was knowledge. Serotonin syndrome and the danger of combining particular drugs were not as familiar in 1984 as they would later become. The case involved diagnostic uncertainty and pharmacology, not merely a resident too tired to read a chart.

One was workload and time awake.

Residents in that era could work extraordinarily long shifts and weeks. The Zion case gave the public a specific death through which to examine a system physicians had largely treated as an internal educational matter.

The reform that followed in New York did not say fatigue explained everything.

The Bell Commission, formally the state's Ad Hoc Advisory Committee on Emergency Services, recommended changes in both supervision and working conditions. New York incorporated work-hour restrictions into hospital regulations effective in 1989. Postgraduate trainees with inpatient responsibilities were generally limited to an average of eighty scheduled hours per week over four weeks and no more than twenty-four consecutive hours, with rest requirements and provisions for supervision and workload.

The number eighty now sounds severe only if one forgets what came before.

It was also not a physiological constant handed down by sleep science. It was a policy boundary inside a complicated system. The rule had to balance training, staffing, continuity, enforcement, and fatigue with a number hospitals could operationalize.

New York stood largely alone for years.

National rules arrived in 2003, when the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education imposed common duty-hour standards across accredited residency programs. The core included an eighty-hour weekly maximum averaged over four weeks, one day free of duties in seven, restrictions on in-house call, and limits on continuous duty. The exact details have changed in later revisions, but the eighty-hour ceiling remains part of the national structure.

The remarkable fact is how much work an eighty-hour limit still permits.

A standard full-time American workweek is forty hours. Residency reform did not turn medical training into ordinary office work. It placed a ceiling on a culture in which the absence of a common ceiling had been treated as professionally normal.

The reforms also changed the research question.

Once schedules differed, medicine could begin comparing them.

In 2004, Christopher Landrigan and colleagues reported a randomized study of interns working in intensive care units. The intervention eliminated extended shifts of twenty-four hours or more and reduced weekly work hours. Interns made fewer serious medical errors under the intervention schedule than under the traditional schedule. A companion study found more sleep and fewer attentional failures with reduced hours.

Those findings were powerful because they moved the argument out of autobiography.

A senior physician could still say, I survived thirty-hour shifts and learned a great deal. The study asked a different question: what happened to measurable performance when the schedule changed?

The answer supported the obvious biological concern. Long wakefulness can degrade attention and increase error.

It did not settle residency design.

Shorter shifts create more transitions between clinicians. Every handoff is an opportunity for information loss. Continuity itself has safety value. A resident who leaves at a fixed hour may hand an unstable patient to someone who has not watched the case evolve. A trainee whose hours are tightly constrained may see fewer unusual cases or have less opportunity to follow an operation through its postoperative course.

Medicine therefore faced a genuine systems problem rather than a contest between humane people and cruel people.

How do you reduce fatigue without replacing fatigue errors with handoff errors?

Large later trials made the answer less tidy than activists on either side wanted.

The FIRST trial, published in 2016, randomized general surgery residency programs to standard duty-hour policies or more flexible policies that relaxed some limits on shift length and time off between shifts while retaining the eighty-hour weekly ceiling. The flexible policy was noninferior to the standard policy for the primary patient-outcome measure. Residents under flexible rules reported some educational and continuity advantages, while concerns about well-being remained.

The iCOMPARE trial asked a related question in internal medicine. Its patient-safety analysis found that flexible duty-hour policies were noninferior to standard policies for thirty-day mortality among Medicare patients. Other reports from the trial examined resident education, sleep, and experience rather than pretending one outcome could summarize the entire policy.

These trials do not prove that long shifts are harmless.

They show that a scheduling rule enters a system full of compensating mechanisms. Supervision, staffing, handoffs, workload, patient mix, institutional culture, and the total weekly schedule all change what a twenty-four-hour period means. A flexible program operating under an eighty-hour weekly cap is not the same system as a hospital where residents routinely work unbounded weeks.

This distinction is central to the book.

Sleep matters. So does system design.

The easiest version of fatigue advocacy says tired people make mistakes, therefore shorter shifts make systems safer. The first clause is well supported. The second can fail if the redesign introduces other hazards or if the freed hours are consumed by additional work elsewhere.

The easiest version of the traditionalist argument says continuity matters, therefore long shifts are educationally necessary. The first clause is also well supported. The second can fail if continuity is purchased with a level of impairment that defeats the purpose of being present.

Hospitals cannot solve the problem by choosing one slogan.

They have to decide what knowledge must remain with a person, what knowledge can survive a handoff, what supervision must be available, what work can be shifted to other professionals, and how much fatigue the system is willing to ask a clinician to carry at the point of consequence.

The phrase duty hours can hide the rest of the architecture.

Suppose two residents each work seventy-eight hours in a week. One works mostly predictable daytime shifts with protected sleep opportunities and manageable patient loads. The other rotates across days and nights, receives late schedule changes, covers a high-acuity service, and leaves each shift too activated to sleep immediately. The weekly number is identical. The biological experience is not.

The same is true of a twenty-four-hour shift.

A resident who has intermittent opportunities to sleep and can hand off when dangerously fatigued is not in the same condition as one moving continuously among unstable patients. Regulation needs numbers because numbers can be enforced. Biology is less cooperative.

This is why modern ACGME requirements extend beyond a single maximum. They address supervision, transitions of care, workloads, well-being, fatigue recognition, and rest. The language has evolved because the problem was never merely arithmetic.

The irony is that residency culture understood this in fragments long before it admitted it in policy.

Hospitals had call rooms because residents sleep. Senior physicians took over when juniors were overwhelmed because supervision matters. Teams existed because no individual could hold the whole hospital. Schedules rotated because continuous presence by one person is impossible.

The institution already contained admissions that its heroic mythology denied.

Halsted himself is the perfect symbol of the contradiction.

He is sometimes caricatured as the drug-fueled father of sleepless training, but the historical record is stranger. His morphine dependence appears to have made him more withdrawn and private, not a permanently stimulated superman racing around the wards. The residency pyramid may have allowed the service to function when he was unavailable. If that interpretation is right, the system that later celebrated total resident presence partly grew around a chief whose own availability was complicated.

Institutions can inherit form while forgetting function.

The Halsted model spread because it trained excellent surgeons and organized a teaching service. Over decades, living in the hospital stopped being literally necessary, but prolonged presence remained culturally meaningful. New technology made it possible to monitor more patients, order more tests, document more care, and treat sicker people. The work expanded to fill the available resident.

Then regulation pushed back.

The pushback did not abolish exhaustion. Residents still work nights. They still face circadian disruption, emergency extensions, sick patients, emotionally intense decisions, and weeks that would be considered extreme in most occupations. The difference is that fatigue is now officially a safety variable rather than a character test the institution can ignore.

That is substantial progress.

It also exposes a harder truth.

Rules can limit hours without changing the demand for care. When one person leaves, another person, team, or system must receive the work. A hospital that reduces resident hours without redesigning staffing can convert one exhausted doctor into several rushed handoffs. A hospital that adds hospitalists, advanced practice clinicians, better scheduling, better handoff tools, and stronger attending supervision may genuinely reduce the biological load.

Sleep-literate design is therefore expensive in the same way other safety design is expensive.

It requires capacity.

That makes the debate resemble the problem Edison concealed with his naps. Someone has to pay for recovery. If the individual physician absorbs it privately, the hospital sees more available labor. If the hospital protects it, staffing costs become visible.

The economics do not make the biology optional.

They explain why institutions resist acknowledging it.

The Zion case broke through that resistance because a death acquired a name, a family, press attention, investigations, and eventually regulation. But even that case did not prove that fatigue alone killed Libby Zion. The enduring policy lesson is more modest and more useful: a system cannot assign consequential work to exhausted trainees, under-supervise them, and then treat the resulting risk as a private test of professional toughness.

Medicine spent decades learning to put a ceiling on the hours.

The next chapter moves to systems where the consequences of a bad night can unfold in seconds and where the operator is not only tired but sitting inside a control architecture whose designers have already decided how much error one human being is allowed to make.

At 1:23 in the morning on April 26, 1986, that architecture failed catastrophically at Chernobyl.
