# The Interrogation Room

There is a difference between being kept awake because a system is badly designed and being kept awake because the system is working exactly as intended.

The distinction becomes clear in an interrogation room.

In the previous chapters, fatigue appeared as spillover. A resident stayed too long because the hospital needed coverage. A driver arrived at work already exhausted because the regulation measured duty time rather than sleep. An engineer crossed a curve too fast because an untreated disorder and a schedule change met in the same body. The institution wanted performance and accepted too much fatigue as collateral damage.

Interrogational sleep deprivation is different.

The impairment is the instrument.

The idea is old enough to predate modern sleep science. Preventing a prisoner from sleeping requires no specialized equipment, leaves less obvious evidence than many forms of physical assault, and can be defended in bureaucratic language as environmental control rather than violence. A person can be made to stand, sit, move, answer questions, endure light, noise, cold, or repeated interruption. The body supplies the rest.

By the time the United States built a post-September 11 detention and interrogation program around what officials called enhanced interrogation techniques, the physiological effects of extended wakefulness were hardly mysterious. Sleep research had spent decades documenting the deterioration of attention, cognition, mood, pain tolerance, judgment, and perception that follows prolonged deprivation.

That did not prevent sleep deprivation from becoming formal procedure.

The public record is unusually detailed because years later the Senate Select Committee on Intelligence reconstructed the program from millions of pages of CIA cables, memoranda, emails, operational records, and internal reviews. Its 2014 executive summary describes detainees kept awake for extraordinarily long periods, often while standing and restrained.

Khalid Sheikh Mohammed was subjected to one period of sleep deprivation lasting about seven and a half days, approximately 180 hours.

That number deserves to be read slowly.

Randy Gardner, the teenager from Chapter 1, stayed awake for 264 hours as a science-fair stunt surrounded by friends, researchers, and people whose objective was to keep him awake without breaking him. His experiment became famous partly because it pushed voluntary wakefulness far beyond ordinary experience.

A CIA detainee was kept awake for roughly two thirds of that duration in custody, under coercion, as part of an interrogation regime.

The same physiology appears in both cases.

The moral and institutional structures do not.

The Senate report described sleep deprivation as one of several approved techniques. In some cases detainees were kept standing with their hands shackled above or near shoulder level; in others they were allowed forms of seated restraint. The stated objective was to weaken resistance and increase compliance while staying within legal guidance that officials believed permitted the practice.

The program's defenders argued that these methods produced intelligence that helped disrupt plots, identify operatives, and locate senior al-Qaeda figures. Minority views attached to the Senate investigation disputed parts of the majority report's judgment that the techniques were ineffective and emphasized information obtained from detainees during or after coercive treatment.

That disagreement matters.

A serious account cannot simply erase the government's stated rationale because the techniques were harsh. The policy question was never whether sleep deprivation makes a person feel bad. That was obvious. The claimed bargain was that imposing severe stress could produce information whose value exceeded the cost.

The problem is that the bargain depends on several assumptions at once.

The first is that the detainee possesses useful information.

The second is that the interrogator can distinguish resistance from ignorance.

The third is that reducing the detainee's capacity to regulate emotion, attention, memory, and judgment improves rather than degrades the quality of the information produced.

The fourth is that whatever information emerges can be separated quickly enough from confabulation, guessing, compliance, misunderstanding, and deliberate deception to be operationally useful.

Sleep deprivation directly complicates the third and fourth assumptions.

A profoundly sleep-deprived person does not become an empty vessel containing pure truth.

The brain under sleep loss becomes less reliable.

Attention narrows and fluctuates. Working memory weakens. Executive control deteriorates. Emotional regulation worsens. Perceptual distortions become more likely as deprivation lengthens. A person may become more suggestible, more desperate to end the immediate situation, and less capable of tracking complex questions or remembering what has already been said.

None of that means every statement made under sleep deprivation is false.

That would be as careless as claiming every statement is true.

The epistemic problem is discrimination.

An interrogator needs information that can survive contact with reality. The more a method increases the rate of compliance without increasing the rate of accuracy, the more it risks producing a larger pile of statements that must be checked against scarce time, other intelligence, surveillance, captured records, and independent sources.

This is not an abstract concern in the Senate record.

The committee documented instances in which detainees gave fabricated or unreliable information during coercive interrogation. It also documented internal CIA doubts, disputes about representations of effectiveness, and cases where intelligence attributed to enhanced techniques had other possible origins or was acquired before those techniques were used.

The minority response contested some of those judgments and argued that the majority applied an unfairly narrow standard to a complex intelligence program.

That leaves a narrower conclusion that does not depend on settling every historical dispute.

Sleep deprivation is not a truth serum.

It changes behavior.

It does not reliably label the truth inside that behavior.

The legal argument surrounding the program often moved differently from the scientific argument. Lawyers asked what techniques could be authorized under statutes, treaties, executive authority, and Justice Department interpretations. Operators asked what could be done in the field. Policymakers asked what risks might be justified after an attack that had killed thousands of people and amid fear of another one.

The body did not care which memorandum had approved the schedule.

After two days, three days, five days awake, the same homeostatic pressure accumulated. The circadian system continued cycling. Microsleeps became harder to suppress. Attention became less stable. Stress hormones rose. Pain and discomfort became harder to regulate. The detainee's ability to think coherently did not become stronger because the technique had a legal opinion attached to it.

This is one of the recurring themes of institutional fatigue.

Organizations are good at administrating categories that bodies do not recognize.

On duty.

Off duty.

Approved technique.

Unauthorized technique.

Rest period.

Interrogation period.

The biological system does not reset itself when a form is signed.

That mismatch becomes especially dangerous when an institution is trying to measure not physical output but cognition itself.

A tired truck driver can still be judged against a lane line or a braking distance. A sleep-deprived detainee is being judged on memory, consistency, plausibility, resistance, and responsiveness while the interrogation method is actively degrading the same capacities the interrogator is trying to evaluate.

The method disturbs the instrument and then asks the instrument to report precisely.

That would be a bad laboratory design even before the ethical problem entered the room.

The ethical problem does enter the room.

Medical and psychological organizations have spent years debating professional participation in interrogation. The most important issue for this book is not professional politics but the boundary they expose: clinicians know that prolonged sleep deprivation is a physiological stressor with cognitive and psychiatric consequences. Once that knowledge is brought into a coercive setting, it can be used to protect a detainee or to calibrate how far the coercion can go.

Monitoring does not automatically make the practice humane.

Sometimes monitoring allows a system to approach a limit more confidently.

That pattern appears elsewhere in dangerous work. A truck's electronic log can make it easier to run legally to the edge of the fourteen-hour window. A wearable can turn a worker's sleep into another metric without giving the worker more control over the schedule. A physician standing beside an interrogation can be asked not whether the technique is good, but whether the subject can endure another hour.

Measurement can constrain power.

It can also sharpen it.

The 180-hour figure is revealing for that reason. A maximum makes the technique look bounded. There is a ceiling. There are rules. There is a required recovery period afterward. The bureaucracy has translated deprivation into an administrable number.

But a maximum of 180 hours does not become biologically moderate because it is precise.

Precision can hide the underlying extremity.

Seven and a half days without normal sleep is not an ordinary extension of wakefulness. It is the intentional production of profound impairment.

The program did not need sleep science to know that the impairment was severe. The severity was part of the intended effect.

This creates a strange inversion of the arguments seen in occupational fatigue.

In trucking, medicine, rail, and aviation, institutions often say they want alert people but struggle to redesign schedules around that goal.

In coercive interrogation, the goal was to push the person away from normal alertness because normal cognition was treated as part of the resistance to be overcome.

That makes the interrogation room useful for understanding the politics of sleep.

Sleep is not merely a health behavior.

Control over sleep is a form of power.

Parents discover a benign version of this almost immediately with infants. Employers exert a negotiated version through shifts, call schedules, travel expectations, and overtime. Schools exert it through start times. Prisons exert it through lights, checks, noise, and routine. Militaries use it in training and operations. Interrogators can use it deliberately as coercion.

The person who controls when another person may sleep controls something unusually basic.

Food can be rationed. Movement can be restricted. Speech can be compelled or punished. Sleep is different because the body eventually attempts to take it back.

That gives the controller leverage for a while and a limit forever.

There is another reason sleep deprivation has been attractive as a coercive method.

It is psychologically legible.

Anyone who has been awake through a sick child's fever, a red-eye flight, an overnight deadline, or a double shift knows the first edge of it. No special imagination is required to understand why repeated nights without restorative sleep can become intolerable.

The familiarity can make the technique seem less violent than it is.

A sleepless night is common.

A week of enforced wakefulness is not.

The difference between those things is not merely quantitative. Extended deprivation changes perception, judgment, impulse control, emotional stability, and the capacity to perform even simple mental work. A familiar discomfort becomes a fundamentally different state when duration and coercion multiply it.

This is where public discussion sometimes becomes trapped by vocabulary.

If torture is imagined only as visible physical injury, sleep deprivation can be described as not touching the body.

But the body is exactly what is being acted upon.

Wakefulness is being extended until normal neurological function degrades.

That is why arguments about whether a particular interrogation practice crossed a legal definition cannot substitute for describing what the practice actually did.

The historical record should be precise enough to carry both facts at once.

Officials faced genuine uncertainty about future attacks after September 11 and believed detainees might possess information capable of saving lives.

The program also deliberately used prolonged sleep deprivation as a coercive tool, including episodes measured in days, despite the well-established fact that extended wakefulness impairs the cognitive functions interrogation depends upon.

A reader does not have to choose between acknowledging the threat environment and acknowledging the physiology.

The more frightening the threat, the more important reliable information becomes.

That is what makes the epistemic weakness of coercive sleep deprivation more than an ethical footnote.

Bad information has costs.

It can send analysts toward the wrong person.

It can consume surveillance capacity.

It can reinforce an interrogator's prior beliefs because a desperate subject starts naming whatever seems likely to end the questioning.

It can create false confidence when consistency is mistaken for truth or inconsistency is mistaken for deception.

It can also contaminate later interviews because the detainee no longer remembers which story came from memory and which story was assembled under pressure.

The stronger the pressure, the more important verification becomes.

At that point, external corroboration does much of the epistemic work the coercive method was supposed to provide.

That is not a paradox unique to interrogation.

It is a recurring problem whenever institutions use fatigue to increase output.

The resident who stays another twelve hours may produce more coverage and worse judgment.

The truck driver who keeps moving may produce more miles and worse reaction time.

The detainee kept awake may produce more words and less reliable cognition.

Output and usefulness are not the same variable.

The CIA program eventually ended, and subsequent executive policy prohibited the agency from using the same set of enhanced interrogation techniques. The legal and political arguments continued. So did disputes about effectiveness, accountability, and what should be remembered from the period.

For this book, the interrogation room marks the outer edge of the argument.

Most institutions do not want to damage sleep.

They want something else badly enough that sleep becomes expendable.

Here, sleep itself became the thing to be taken.

That makes what comes next almost more unsettling.

The next chapter moves from detainees to teenagers.

No one designing a school schedule intends to impair children.

The people making the decision are usually trying to coordinate buses, teachers, athletics, parents, buildings, and budgets.

The biology is known.

The harm is unintended.

The bell still rings.
