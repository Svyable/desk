# Miles to Go Before You Sleep

At 12:55 in the morning on June 7, 2014, a Walmart tractor-trailer was traveling north on the New Jersey Turnpike near Cranbury.

Traffic ahead had slowed to less than ten miles per hour in a construction zone. The posted work-zone limit was forty-five.

The truck was doing sixty-five.

The driver did not begin responding to the slowed traffic until he was about two hundred feet from the limousine van ahead of him. He braked and steered left. The truck struck the van, beginning a chain collision involving six vehicles and twenty-one people. One passenger in the van, comedian James McNair, died. Four other passengers were seriously injured.

The National Transportation Safety Board reconstructed the driver's previous day from work records, electronic data, cell-phone activity, and other evidence because the driver and his family declined to be interviewed.

The reconstruction produced a schedule that looked almost impossible to reconcile with safe driving.

Before reporting to Walmart's distribution center in Smyrna, Delaware, the driver had traveled about eight hundred miles from his home in Georgia in his personal vehicle. Investigators concluded that he had little opportunity to sleep during that trip. He then worked and drove another 451 miles. At the time of the crash, he had been awake about twenty-eight and a half hours since a four-hour nap.

His commercial work clock had not yet expired.

He had been on duty thirteen hours and thirty-two minutes of the fourteen-hour duty day allowed under federal hours-of-service rules.

The truck's electronic logging system was doing its job.

It could tell him how close he was to the legal limit.

It could not tell him that the legal limit had become irrelevant hours earlier because he had arrived at work already sleep-deprived.

The NTSB determined that the probable cause of the crash was driver fatigue due to his failure to obtain sleep before reporting for duty. The fatigue delayed his response to slowing and stopped traffic and contributed to his excessive speed in the work zone. The Board also found that the failure of passengers in the van to use available seat belts and properly adjusted head restraints contributed to the severity of their injuries.

The case is almost designed to expose the boundary of hours-of-service regulation.

The rule governs work.

Sleep happens outside work.

Federal rules for property-carrying commercial drivers currently allow a maximum of eleven hours of driving after ten consecutive hours off duty. Driving must occur within a fourteen-consecutive-hour window after coming on duty. A thirty-minute interruption is required after eight cumulative hours of driving without such a break, and weekly on-duty limits apply across seven- or eight-day periods.

These are substantial constraints on an industry that once treated endurance as a business advantage.

They are not sleep prescriptions.

Ten hours off duty can contain eight hours of sleep. It can also contain a shower, meal, commute, childcare, paperwork, exercise, noise, insomnia, a second job, or a twelve-hour drive from Georgia to Delaware.

The regulator can reserve time.

It cannot occupy the time on the driver's behalf.

This is the same distinction Chapter 8 made in rail, but trucking adds economic pressure that makes the gap harder to ignore. A long-haul driver is not simply a worker with a start time and an office. The job includes waiting at terminals, finding parking, loading, unloading, traffic, weather, dispatch decisions, appointment windows, and stretches of road that do not care when the biological night arrives.

A legal driving window therefore becomes a scarce asset.

Once the clock starts, delay has value.

A driver who spends three unpaid hours waiting for a dock may have fewer legal hours left to cover the miles that produce income. A traffic jam consumes the same fourteen-hour window as movement. A shipper's delay can push the remaining drive deeper into the night.

This is why fatigue regulation is inseparable from the economics of freight.

The federal rule says when a driver must stop.

The compensation system helps determine how much pressure the driver feels before that point.

The NTSB's Cranbury investigation was careful about the individual driver. It noted that Walmart's pay structure did not obviously force him to accept the final assignment; investigators found that he had enough time to take a layover and still complete the later backhaul. The Board did not turn the crash into a simple story of a company ordering an exhausted employee to keep moving.

The driver made consequential choices.

He also worked inside a regulatory architecture that had no way to see the most important part of his day because it happened before he logged on.

That is the limit of a clock-based rule.

It measures time categories rather than physiological state.

This is not a reason to abandon the rule.

Without an hours-of-service ceiling, the incentive to convert wakefulness directly into freight miles becomes stronger. A carrier can schedule more driving. A driver paid by the mile can earn more by continuing. A shipper can demand a tighter appointment. The biological cost is displaced onto the person behind the wheel and the strangers sharing the road.

The rule draws a line through that market.

The harder question is whether the line is enough.

The NTSB had been asking versions of that question for decades. Its fatigue recommendations have included science-based hours limits, sleep-disorder screening, fatigue-management programs, education, and technologies that can detect or mitigate drowsy-driving risk.

Cranbury gave the Board a particularly clear example of why the pieces cannot substitute for one another.

The driver was operating under an electronic log. His hours were visible. The carrier had a strong regulatory compliance profile. He was still dangerously fatigued.

Compliance and fitness for duty diverged.

Electronic logging devices were supposed to solve a different problem.

Paper logbooks depend heavily on self-report. A driver records when driving begins, when it ends, when off-duty time occurs, and other duty states. The system can work, but the economic incentive to stretch available hours makes falsification tempting and enforcement difficult. A paper record can be made to describe a legal day even when the truck's movement tells another story.

Electronic logs connect the record more directly to vehicle operation.

The federal ELD rule, adopted in 2015 and implemented through a phased compliance period beginning in 2017, required most drivers who already had to maintain records of duty status to use compliant electronic logging devices, subject to specified exceptions. By December 2017, the mandate was operational for much of the industry, with later transition deadlines for some older electronic systems.

The immediate logic was strong.

If hours-of-service rules matter, the record used to enforce them should be difficult to rewrite after the fact.

Research before the full mandate supported that premise. A carrier-level observational study comparing ELD-equipped and non-equipped trucks found lower hours-of-service violation rates and lower crash rates among the equipped vehicles after adjustment for several factors. The study was not a randomized trial, and its carrier sample leaned toward larger fleets, but it suggested electronic records could improve both compliance and safety.

Then the mandate produced a more complicated natural experiment.

Researchers using inspection and crash data found that ELD adoption clearly increased compliance with hours-of-service rules, especially among smaller carriers and independent operators that had been more likely to use paper records.

That was the intended first-order effect.

Crash reductions were much harder to demonstrate.

A 2021 study in the Journal of Operations Management found no corresponding reduction in crash counts among the smaller carriers most affected by the mandate. It also found evidence that unsafe-driving violations such as speeding increased for those carriers, consistent with the possibility that when drivers could no longer stretch the work clock on paper, some responded by trying to cover the same work faster.

More recent research has continued to challenge the assumption that electronic monitoring automatically produces safer roads. Different data sets and methods yield different estimates, and the causal question is difficult because trucking changed in many ways during the mandate's rollout. But the broad lesson is already useful.

A rule can succeed on the metric it directly controls and disappoint on the outcome it was meant to improve.

The ELD makes the work clock more truthful.

It does not change the appointment time.

It does not shorten the route.

It does not eliminate detention at the warehouse.

It does not create a parking space when the driver reaches the legal limit.

It does not force a driver to sleep during off-duty time.

It does not make sixty-five miles per hour become forty-five when the remaining delivery window feels tight.

Monitoring closes one form of discretion and can move pressure elsewhere.

That does not make electronic logging a failure. Better compliance is valuable. More accurate records improve enforcement, accident reconstruction, carrier management, and research. A system that cannot measure work time has little chance of governing it.

The mistake is to confuse measurement with mechanism.

The same error appears throughout institutional sleep policy.

A hospital limits resident duty hours and assumes fatigue is solved, even if residents use the released time on commuting or administrative work.

A railroad gives an engineer enough nominal time off and misses untreated apnea.

A carrier enforces an electronic fourteen-hour window and assumes a driver reporting at hour zero is rested.

The schedule can be compliant while the body is not.

This is why fatigue management programs exist alongside hours limits.

A serious program has to reach beyond the arithmetic of on-duty time. It can teach workers and managers how circadian timing, sleep debt, long commutes, medical disorders, and recovery interact. It can establish processes for declaring fatigue. It can make dispatchers participants rather than treating the driver as the only person responsible for a schedule. It can examine whether pay and appointment systems reward choices that undermine rest.

The NTSB emphasized this after Cranbury. Walmart addressed fatigue in driver training but did not have the kind of structured fatigue-management program that might have focused more attention on long commutes and off-duty behavior. The Board also made the uncomfortable point that hours-of-service rules cannot dictate lifestyle choices outside work.

That is true and incomplete.

An employer should not control an employee's private life.

It still controls the consequences it is willing to accept when the employee reports for safety-sensitive duty.

A pilot who arrives intoxicated cannot defend the condition by saying the drinking occurred off the clock. A train engineer with an untreated condition that produces involuntary sleep presents a safety problem whether the diagnosis belongs to private medicine or occupational medicine. Fatigue sits in the harder middle because ordinary tiredness is common, variable, and difficult to measure objectively in real time.

The institution therefore needs several imperfect defenses.

Time limits.

Education.

Medical screening where justified.

A culture that lets a worker stop.

Scheduling that respects circadian timing when operations allow it.

Technology that can warn or intervene when attention fails.

Economic design that does not turn every saved minute into another obligation.

None of these guarantees alertness.

Together they make fatigue less dependent on one person's willingness to admit weakness while everyone else is waiting for the load.

The trucking case also reveals why a sleep-literate institution cannot simply optimize for maximum rest opportunity without considering autonomy.

Drivers often dislike rigid rules for good reasons. A mandatory break can arrive when a person is fully alert and minutes from a safe parking location. A fixed driving window can keep running while a driver waits at a shipper through no fault of their own. Rules written for a generic route can interact badly with weather, congestion, home time, and the geography of truck parking.

Flexibility can improve safety when it lets a driver align work with actual conditions.

Flexibility can reduce safety when it becomes a euphemism for longer work.

That tension explains decades of political fighting around hours of service. Carriers, drivers, safety advocates, unions, shippers, insurers, regulators, and researchers do not disagree only because some value safety and others value money. They often disagree about where risk appears after a rule moves it.

A stricter clock can reduce fatigue from excessive work and increase pressure from inflexible delivery windows.

A more flexible clock can let a driver stop during a traffic jam and preserve later driving time, or it can create another route to a longer biological day.

The design has to follow the whole system.

This is why the final detail of the Cranbury crash is so instructive.

The driver's electronic system was warning him that he was approaching the end of his fourteen-hour duty period.

The warning was accurate.

It was also late by almost a day in the only sense that mattered.

The driver had been awake about twenty-eight and a half hours.

A truthful log can still describe the wrong variable.

Transportation regulation has spent more than a century trying to turn fatigue into numbers that inspectors, employers, and courts can use. That work is necessary. It has saved lives. It has also demonstrated the stubborn gap between administrable time and biological time.

The next chapter leaves accidental fatigue behind.

In interrogation rooms, sleep loss is not an unwanted side effect of the schedule.

It is the schedule's purpose.

There is a broader regulatory lesson inside that gap between the legal clock and the biological one. Rules work best when the thing they measure is close to the harm they are trying to prevent. Speed limits measure speed. Blood-alcohol limits measure a chemical exposure. Hours-of-service rules measure something one step removed: time available for work and recovery.

That distance is unavoidable because alertness is not a single field an inspector can read from a dashboard.

It means the rule has to be judged as a risk-control system rather than as a definition of safety.

A driver at hour ten is not necessarily safe because the rule permits hour eleven. A driver at hour eleven is not necessarily unsafe because the clock says stop. The line is administrative. Its value comes from reducing the frequency with which economic pressure can push people into increasingly risky territory, not from discovering a physiological cliff that exists at exactly the same minute in every driver.

This is also why exceptions deserve scrutiny even when they sound reasonable. Every exception has a story. Bad weather delayed the route. The shipper held the truck. The driver is close to home. The load is urgent. Parking is unavailable. Some exceptions make the system safer by allowing a driver to respond to reality. Others make the ceiling porous enough that the old pressure simply returns through another door.

The difficult design work lies in distinguishing the two.

The driver sees this conflict from the cab. Dispatch sees it from a network. The shipper sees an appointment. The consignee sees inventory. The regulator sees aggregate crash risk. The public sees a tractor-trailer passing at highway speed. Each actor is optimizing a different piece of the same system.

Fatigue gets created in the gaps between them.

Detention is a good example. A driver can arrive rested and legal, then spend hours waiting to load. The delay does not look like driving, but it consumes the same day. When the truck finally moves, the driver may be closer to the circadian night, closer to the end of the legal window, and under more pressure to make up lost distance. The shipper did not order unsafe driving. The driver did not necessarily begin the day fatigued. The schedule became worse through accumulated delay.

A sleep-literate freight system would notice that the risk was produced collectively.

That does not require turning every warehouse into a sleep laboratory. It requires putting consequences next to the choices that create them. If detention repeatedly compresses safe driving time, the cost should not disappear into the driver's remaining hours. If parking shortages force people to continue after they would prefer to stop, parking is a fatigue issue. If pay rewards miles but not waiting, compensation is a fatigue issue. If appointment systems ignore realistic travel time, scheduling is a fatigue issue.

This is where the phrase personal responsibility can become both true and insufficient.

The Cranbury driver chose to make the long personal trip before reporting. That choice mattered. So did the fact that the system had no direct way to see the resulting wakefulness when he arrived. The two statements can coexist.

Good policy has to survive that coexistence. It cannot require every accident to have one morally satisfying owner.

The same is true of technology. Electronic logs solved part of an honesty problem. They made certain kinds of time harder to falsify. That is real progress. But once the record became more truthful, the remaining mismatch between measured compliance and actual safety became easier to see.

The lesson is not that measurement fails.

It is that measurement reveals the next problem.

The first problem was invisible work time. ELDs made it visible. The next problem was that visible work time did not capture sleep obtained before work, circadian timing, medical disorders, economic pressure, or delayed reaction once the truck was moving.

A mature system keeps going.

It asks which variables are still outside the frame.

That habit of following the missing variable is what connects the transportation chapters to the rest of this book. Hospitals counted hours but not always continuity. Schools counted instructional time but not biological timing. Wearables count signals but can overstate what the signals mean. Employers count availability while recovery happens elsewhere.

The clock is useful.

The clock is not the body.

In the next room, institutions would exploit that distinction deliberately. Sleep deprivation would stop being an accidental by-product of work and become a technique applied to another person.
