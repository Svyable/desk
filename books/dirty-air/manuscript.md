# Dirty Air

## How Pollution Could Be the First Thing We Learn About an Alien Civilization

Sven Hardy Benson

---

## 1 — Dirty Air

There is a wonderfully human assumption buried inside the search for extraterrestrial intelligence: that intelligence, once sufficiently advanced, will want to say hello.

We build radio telescopes and listen for the hello. We look for optical pulses that might be the hello. We discuss beacons, deliberate broadcasts, mathematical greetings, narrow-band carriers, laser flashes, messages assembled by minds that have somehow survived the difficult interval between discovering electromagnetism and acquiring the wisdom to introduce themselves politely to strangers across forty light-years.

Maybe.

There is another possibility.

Maybe they are not trying to tell us anything.

Maybe they are running an air conditioner.

This is less romantic, which is one reason I like it.

A civilization does not have to communicate in order to alter a planet. It only has to do things. Burn fuel. Manufacture refrigerants. Smelt ore. Fertilize fields. Move nitrogen around. Make plastics. Warm buildings. Cool buildings. Build enough machines that the atmosphere becomes, among its other jobs, a waste ledger.

We know this because we have already performed the experiment.

Look at Earth from the right instrument and human industry stops being sociology. It becomes spectroscopy.

Nitrogen dioxide absorbs light. Sulfur dioxide absorbs light. Formaldehyde has a spectrum. Carbon monoxide has a spectrum. Chlorofluorocarbons have spectra. From orbit, instruments built by humans can map chemical consequences of other humans whom they never see. A satellite does not need to resolve a refinery worker, a diesel engine, a traffic jam or a smokestack to know that chemistry below has become geographically strange.

NASA's TEMPO instrument was designed to measure pollution over North America from geostationary orbit. Its measurement suite includes ozone, nitrogen dioxide, sulfur dioxide, formaldehyde and aerosols. The important conceptual point is not that TEMPO is SETI. It isn't. The point is that civilization has already demonstrated the underlying inference on its own planet:

**technology can become remotely detectable chemistry.**

Now move the telescope farther away.

Much farther.

At interstellar distance an Earth-like planet collapses almost completely. Continents disappear. Cities disappear. Machines disappear. People disappear. The civilization becomes a fractional change in photons.

That sounds like losing information. It is also a useful kind of compression.

A planet's atmosphere mixes consequences together. Biology can write into it. Geology can write into it. Oceans write into it. Volcanoes write into it. Lightning writes into it. Technology, if there is enough technology, may write into it too.

This is where the search for life and the search for intelligence begin to touch in an odd way. Astrobiologists have spent decades asking which atmospheric gases might betray biology. Oxygen is interesting because on Earth it is maintained far from the abundance expected without life. Methane becomes interesting in combinations and contexts where replenishment matters. No single molecule is a magic word. The problem is always source, sink, lifetime, context, disequilibrium.

Technosignatures inherit exactly the same difficulty, except the suspect is industry.

In 2021, Ravi Kopparapu and colleagues examined nitrogen dioxide as a possible atmospheric technosignature. This was not a detection. It was a detectability calculation. That distinction is the load-bearing wall of this book.

NO2 has natural sources. Lightning makes nitrogen oxides. Biology participates in nitrogen chemistry. A molecule does not become artificial because humans happen to produce a lot of it here.

But on modern Earth, human activity is a major source of lower-atmospheric NO2. Kopparapu's question was therefore deliciously practical: if somebody else industrialized a planet in a chemically recognizable way, could a future telescope notice the excess?

Their models suggested that under favorable assumptions an Earth-like civilization around a Sun-like star could produce a potentially detectable NO2 signature for a future large visible-light telescope, although at substantial observing cost. Cooler stars alter the photochemistry and can make NO2 survive longer. Clouds, aerosols, natural production and instrumental limits complicate everything.

That is not evidence for aliens.

It is evidence that the question has escaped science fiction.

CFCs make the problem stranger.

Chlorofluorocarbons are interesting precisely because biology is not known to make them in meaningful natural abundance. Humans manufactured them because they were useful. Then we discovered that their chemical stability—the trait that made them so useful—helped them survive long enough to reach the stratosphere, where chlorine chemistry damaged ozone.

A manufactured molecule became a planetary consequence.

Then we noticed.

Then, remarkably, we tried to stop.

The Montreal Protocol is therefore part of the history of technosignatures whether anybody intended it to be or not. It demonstrates that a technological species can create an atmospheric signature, understand the signature, recognize it as harmful, coordinate internationally and deliberately reduce the process producing it.

That sequence creates a problem for SETI.

Suppose pollution is detectable only during a civilization's dirty adolescence.

Suppose technological maturity makes a planet quieter.

We would then be searching with an instrument biased toward civilizations during a particular developmental interval: old enough to industrialize, careless enough to pollute at planetary scale, and not yet competent or motivated enough to remove the signature.

Our telescope would not be a neutral census device.

It would be a machine for finding mistakes.

### The first-contact selection effect

Imagine two planets.

On Planet A, a technological civilization runs a large industrial economy. Its energy system produces nitrogen oxides. It uses synthetic atmospheric chemicals with long lifetimes. It has not yet closed its material loops. Its atmosphere contains spectral consequences that cannot easily be reconciled with the modeled natural background.

On Planet B, a civilization is older by five thousand years. Its manufacturing is clean, its energy sources produce no globally significant atmospheric byproducts, and its materials are recycled with absurd efficiency. It does not transmit high-power beacons because it has no reason to.

Which civilization is easier for us to find?

Possibly A.

If so, the universe visible to technosignature searches could be systematically younger, dirtier and more wasteful than the universe of technological civilizations actually is.

This is not a small philosophical detail. It changes what a detection would mean.

Find CFC-like chemistry on a planet and the first temptation will be to ask: *Who made it?*

The second question should be: *Why is it still there?*

Atmospheric lifetime matters. Production rate matters. Stellar ultraviolet matters. Vertical mixing matters. Surface sinks matter. A gas could outlive the industrial process that produced it. Depending on chemistry, a civilization could disappear while part of its atmospheric fingerprint remained.

We are accustomed to archaeology being underfoot.

On another world it may be overhead.

### Evidence ladder

This subject attracts overstatement because the steps are easy to compress. We will not compress them.

A spectral feature is not a molecule until competing identifications and instrumental effects have been addressed.

A molecule is not an anomalous abundance until atmospheric retrieval and model uncertainty permit the claim.

An anomalous abundance is not an industrial source until natural pathways have been bounded.

An industrial source is not an intelligent civilization unless nonbiological technology—or whatever broader category we ultimately require—is actually the best explanation.

And a technological signature is not a message.

This ladder matters most after a tantalizing observation, when everybody wants to climb it in one afternoon.

The book will use four evidence classes throughout.

**Documented:** measurements, instrument capabilities, laboratory spectra, established atmospheric chemistry, published exoplanet observations, and explicit technosignature modeling.

**Credible but incomplete:** modeled detectability under assumptions that future observations may or may not satisfy.

**Disputed or unconfirmed:** claimed technological interpretations lacking independent observational confirmation.

**Speculative synthesis:** what the distribution of pollution signatures might tell us about technological lifetimes, environmental governance, collapse, terraforming or the visibility of mature civilizations.

There is currently no verified detection of alien industrial pollution.

That sentence should survive every chapter.

### The delicious inversion

The old picture of SETI is a civilization deliberately spending energy so somebody else will know it exists.

Pollution inverts that.

A technosignature can be information nobody meant to send.

That makes it closer to intelligence work than conversation. You infer activity from residue. You infer capability from waste. You learn something about an unseen system because no system is perfectly sealed.

The industrial atmosphere is exhaust intelligence at planetary scale.

Which raises the nastiest possibility in the book.

Perhaps the first thing we learn about another civilization will not be its mathematics, its art, its anatomy, its politics or its philosophy.

Perhaps the first thing we learn is that it has an emissions problem.

And perhaps, somewhere else, that is the first thing anybody learns about us.
