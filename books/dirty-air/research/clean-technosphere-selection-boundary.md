# Dirty Air — clean-technosphere selection boundary

## Editorial question

Chapter 1's most interesting claim is not that pollution can be a technosignature. Published models already establish that as a search hypothesis. The larger claim is that pollution could bias a technological census toward a civilization's dirty interval: `Our telescope would not be a neutral census device. It would be a machine for finding mistakes.` That sentence is worth keeping only if the research distinguishes a vivid possibility from an established selection effect.

This note tests that boundary before later manuscript expansion.

## Primary papers recovered

### NO2: Kopparapu et al. 2021

Ravi Kopparapu, Giada Arney, Jacob Haqq-Misra, Jacob Lustig-Yaeger and Geronimo Villanueva, “Nitrogen Dioxide Pollution as a Signature of Extraterrestrial Technology,” *The Astrophysical Journal* 908 (2021). Primary preprint: https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.05027

The paper uses a one-dimensional photochemical model plus synthetic spectra for an Earth-like planet around Sun-, K- and M-type stars. Its useful quantitative boundary is narrower than the manuscript's general rhetoric: for the cloud-free Sun-like case, present-Earth anthropogenic NO2 at 10 pc reaches approximately S/N 5 after roughly 400 hours with a hypothetical 15 m LUVOIR-like telescope over 0.2–0.7 μm. Clouds and aerosols reduce detectability and can mimic the broad NO2 feature. Cooler stars change photolysis and abundance. The paper therefore supports *conditional modeled detectability*, not the proposition that industrial NO2 is generically an easy technosignature.

The paper also notes that historical terrestrial NO2 was higher than today's level. That is useful for Chapter 10 only if the manuscript reconstructs what period, inventory and emissions assumptions produce the comparison instead of turning “older Earth was dirtier” into a universal technological-development curve.

### CFCs: Lin, Gonzalez Abad & Loeb 2014

Henry W. Lin, Gonzalo Gonzalez Abad and Abraham Loeb, “Detecting Industrial Pollution in the Atmospheres of Earth-like Exoplanets,” *The Astrophysical Journal Letters* 792 (2014). Primary preprint: https://arxiv.org/abs/1406.3025

The paper examines CF4 and CCl3F and estimates detectability around a white dwarf, with concentrations around ten times present terrestrial levels. That host-star/geometry assumption matters. It should not be generalized into “JWST can detect Earth's CFCs on an Earth twin.” The manuscript should reconstruct the target star, transit geometry, abundance multiple and integration-time assumptions whenever this result is used.

This is especially important because the paper's pollutants are not interchangeable. CF4 and CCl3F differ in atmospheric chemistry and persistence. “CFC” should not become a narrative bucket in which one modeled molecule silently supplies another molecule's lifetime or spectrum.

## Strongest counterevidence to the dirty-window thesis

A 2024 scenario paper from the same broader technosignature research community is more useful to this book than another pollution example: Jacob Haqq-Misra, George Profitiliotis and Ravi Kopparapu, “Projections of Earth's technosphere. I. Scenario modeling, worldbuilding, and overview of remotely detectable technosignatures,” primary preprint https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.00067.

Its ten future-Earth scenarios include expansive technospheres that can be spectrally indistinguishable, in the modeled atmospheric sense, from pre-agricultural Earth. That directly supports one half of *Dirty Air*: technology does not guarantee a strong atmospheric industrial signature.

But it does **not** establish the other half: that dirty civilizations will dominate detections. A selection effect needs a denominator. At minimum the book would need assumptions or evidence about:

1. how long civilizations occupy high-pollution versus low-pollution states;
2. the abundance and detectability of non-pollution technosignatures in those states;
3. telescope wavelength, contrast, distance and observing-time selection functions;
4. host-star and atmospheric photochemistry distributions;
5. whether pollution concentration rises monotonically with industrial scale rather than changing species as technology changes;
6. whether regulation removes emissions faster than atmospheric reservoirs remove the molecules already emitted.

Without those quantities, “dirty-window hypothesis” is a good hypothesis and a bad census conclusion.

## The Montreal Protocol is a better empirical case than the manuscript currently uses

The opening treats the Montreal Protocol mainly as a moral/intellectual sequence: a species creates an atmospheric signature, recognizes harm, coordinates and reduces the process. The more interesting empirical point is that **production, emission and atmospheric abundance have different clocks**.

NOAA's long atmospheric histories document the rise and later decline of major CFCs: https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/ocean-carbon-acidification-data-system/oceans/CFC_ATM_Hist2015.html . NOAA's Pacific Marine Environmental Laboratory describes these compounds as anthropogenic tracers with well-characterized atmospheric input histories: https://www.pmel.noaa.gov/gobop/go-ship/cfc/atmospheric-inputs-chlorofluorocarbons .

Montzka et al. 2018 then found that CFC-11's atmospheric decline had slowed because emissions had unexpectedly increased: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-018-0106-2 . A later global analysis found emissions fell sharply again from 2018 to 2019: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-021-03260-5 .

That history gives Chapter 10 a much stronger narrative mechanism than “we polluted, then we stopped.” A remote observer sees an atmospheric stock shaped by old production, banks, leakage, destruction, chemistry, transport and new emissions. Governance can change the source term before the spectrum finishes changing. Conversely, an anomalous atmospheric abundance need not describe the civilization's present industrial practice.

For the book's archaeology claim, this is the useful terrestrial control: **the atmosphere can lag the behavior that wrote into it.** Do not upgrade that into a claim that an alien civilization is extinct, mature or environmentally incompetent from one persistent molecule.

## Required manuscript boundary

Keep the opening's imaginative inversion, but later chapters should distinguish three propositions:

- **Pollution is a modeled technosignature candidate.** Supported for specific molecules, atmospheres, stars and instrument assumptions.
- **Some technologically advanced states may be atmospherically quiet in the searched bands.** Supported as a plausible scenario and by future-Earth modeling; absence is not evidence of absence of technology.
- **A survey will preferentially detect dirty technological phases.** Not yet established. This requires a population/time-selection model and comparison with competing technosignatures.

The strongest counterargument should remain alive: a mature civilization could be *more* detectable, not less, if its energy use, engineered illumination, structures, propulsion, communication leakage or deliberate beacons scale upward while pollution falls. Clean atmosphere and technological invisibility are different propositions.

## Surgical later prose opportunity

When Chapter 10 is drafted, use the CFC-11 history as the concrete case. A treaty changes production; atmospheric concentration responds slowly; unexpected emissions alter the decline; later measurements show the emissions falling again. That sequence earns the book's “atmospheric archaeology” idea without asking a hypothetical Planet A/Planet B comparison to carry the whole argument.

In Chapter 1, retain the selection-effect passage as explicitly conditional. Do not add more rhetorical caveats there. The research work belongs downstream, where the reader can discover that the telescope is sampling a convolution of industrial behavior, chemistry and time rather than receiving a civilization's current environmental report card.

## Defeat condition added

Retire or substantially narrow the book-level dirty-window claim if a realistic multi-technosignature population model shows that detectability rises with technological scale even as atmospheric pollution falls, or that plausible dirty phases are too brief/weak to contribute materially to the detectable population.
