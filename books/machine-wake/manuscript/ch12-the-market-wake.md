# Chapter 12 — The Market Wake

A market changes when the cost of asking changes.

For most of commercial history, comparison required effort. A person visited stores, made phone calls, read catalogs, asked colleagues, waited for quotes or hired someone to search on their behalf. Even online shopping did not remove the human bottleneck. The buyer still opened tabs, read listings and decided when enough comparison was enough.

Agents can make asking almost free.

A purchasing agent can request ten quotes instead of three. A travel agent can recheck fares every hour. A software buyer can ask vendors to answer hundreds of security and contract questions before a human joins the conversation. A consumer agent can watch prices continuously and switch providers when a better offer appears.

Cheap search sounds like pure efficiency.

It also changes the behavior of the market being searched.

This is the market wake: the patterns left behind when automated actors can inquire, compare, bid, negotiate and transact at a scale that human attention once limited.

Financial markets encountered this transition earlier than most sectors. Electronic trading replaced slower human processes. Algorithms learned to submit, cancel and modify orders rapidly. Market design had to account for machine speed, message volume and strategies that did not resemble floor trading. Exchanges built rate limits, surveillance systems and new rules around a world in which an order could be created and withdrawn before a person had time to blink.

AI agents will bring milder versions of the same dynamic into ordinary commerce.

Consider a hotel.

Today, a hotel sees searches, bookings and cancellations through travel sites and direct channels. Revenue-management systems adjust prices based on demand, occupancy, season and competitors. Human travelers tolerate some search friction, so not everyone checks every possible rate every minute.

Now imagine millions of travel agents acting continuously. They can monitor every reservation, rebook when prices fall, cancel refundable rooms, test alternate dates, request upgrades and compare packages. A generous cancellation policy designed for human behavior can become a computational option exercised at scale.

The hotel's response may be to change the policy.

Free cancellation windows shrink. Rates become more differentiated. Rebooking is restricted. Agents receive dedicated interfaces with quotas. Loyalty status becomes more important. Prices may update more frequently.

The buyer's optimization becomes the seller's new environment.

This feedback is the essence of the market wake.

A policy that is attractive when a few people exploit it can become unsustainable when machines exploit it systematically. The same is true of price guarantees, coupons, return rules, promotional credits and limited inventory.

Arbitrage turns exceptions into products.

Markets depend on friction more than they admit. Some friction is waste: search costs that prevent a buyer from finding the better seller. Some friction supports cross-subsidies: inattentive customers pay more while attentive customers shop around. Some friction prevents gaming. Some gives people time to reconsider. Automation removes friction indiscriminately unless policy distinguishes among these roles.

This can be good for consumers.

An agent that renegotiates insurance, internet service, subscriptions and travel can attack the loyalty penalty—the tendency for passive customers to remain on worse terms because switching is tedious. Competition becomes more intense when buyers can compare continuously. Small sellers can be discovered by agents that search beyond the brands people already know.

The machine can turn market complexity against the firms that created it.

A contract full of fees and exceptions is difficult for a person to evaluate. An agent can parse it, compare expected cost under a user's behavior and rank alternatives. Fine print becomes machine-readable competition.

This could be one of the most valuable consumer effects of AI.

It also provokes a counter-move: sellers optimize for agents.

Websites were designed first for humans, then for search engines, then increasingly for recommendation systems. Commerce may develop an agent-facing layer where products expose structured attributes, prices, availability, policies and proofs. Sellers will learn which fields influence agent decisions and tune them.

Search-engine optimization becomes agent optimization.

This can improve information quality if agents reward clear structured data. It can also produce a new form of manipulation. A seller may discover that agents overvalue one certification, ignore a fee hidden in an unstructured field, or rely on a review metric vulnerable to synthetic influence. The seller does not need to persuade the human. It needs to persuade the buyer's software.

Advertising enters a stranger phase.

Much advertising is designed to influence human attention and emotion. An agent shopping under explicit constraints may be less vulnerable to a beautiful commercial and more vulnerable to whatever data source it treats as authoritative. Marketing budgets can shift toward product feeds, ranking systems, paid placement inside agent interfaces and attempts to shape the evidence agents retrieve.

The market wake becomes informational.

A consumer may think the agent “searched the market” when it searched only vendors with compatible APIs. A provider may pay to be the default tool. A platform may prefer merchants that pay a commission. The gatekeeper does not disappear when the buyer gets an agent. It moves into the agent's selection layer.

This is why economic agency requires transparency about incentives.

Does the shopping agent receive affiliate fees? Does it favor services owned by its provider? Can sellers pay for ranking? Does it search the open web or a curated marketplace? Which constraints came from the user and which came from platform policy?

A machine acting for you can also act on a business model you did not choose.

The market wake is therefore partly a conflict-of-interest problem.

The same issue appears in negotiation.

An agent can negotiate many small transactions that humans currently accept on posted terms. Imagine asking for a hotel late checkout, a software discount, a better phone plan, a delivery fee waiver or a different payment schedule. Humans do this selectively because negotiation costs time and social energy.

Machines do not feel awkward.

If agents negotiate every transaction, sellers may automate the other side. Two systems bargain within milliseconds, each estimating the other's reservation price. The visible price becomes less important than the rules governing the negotiation.

This can create efficiency by matching willingness to pay with willingness to sell. It can also create discrimination at unprecedented granularity.

A seller's agent might infer that one buyer is unlikely to switch and offer a worse deal. A buyer's agent might misrepresent urgency. Dynamic pricing can become dynamic haggling.

Markets will need norms about what information agents can use and what forms of discrimination are legal or acceptable.

Again, AI did not invent personalized pricing. Airlines, hotels, insurers and online platforms already use extensive segmentation. Agents increase speed, responsiveness and the number of contexts where personalization is economical.

The machine wake magnifies existing incentives.

Automated bidding creates another effect: noise.

If thousands of agents request quotes they have little intention of accepting, suppliers bear the processing cost. A construction company may spend time preparing detailed bids for machine-generated opportunities. A freelance marketplace may flood workers with requests. A cloud provider may receive speculative capacity inquiries. A restaurant reservation system may see tables temporarily held by agents optimizing schedules.

Cheap asking can impose expensive answering.

Markets respond by pricing the request.

A supplier may charge for detailed proposals, require deposits, demand proof of seriousness or limit automated inquiries. An API may meter calls. A reservation may require a card. A marketplace may score buyers based on conversion rates.

Friction returns as an anti-spam mechanism.

This is a general law of the machine wake. When automation removes the cost from one side of an interaction, the other side often adds a cost to restore signal.

Email became cheap to send, so filters and reputation systems appeared. Account creation became cheap, so CAPTCHAs and phone verification appeared. API calls became cheap, so rate limits appeared. Agent inquiries will become cheap, so proof of intent may appear.

A market needs to distinguish demand from computation pretending to be demand.

This distinction can be difficult when agents act speculatively on legitimate behalf. A travel agent searching twenty possibilities for one traveler creates nineteen losing queries that were still necessary to compare. The supplier sees traffic; only one transaction emerges.

The ratio of inquiry to purchase can rise dramatically even if total real demand is unchanged.

Infrastructure has to scale for the wake, not only the sale.

This affects data. Firms may interpret search volume as interest, adjust inventory or prices and discover that agents are generating automated curiosity rather than human intent. Behavioral analytics developed around people can break when a large share of behavior belongs to software.

The click stops meaning what it used to mean.

Digital advertising faced a version of this with bots and fraudulent traffic. Agentic commerce makes machine behavior legitimate rather than merely fraudulent. Analytics systems need to identify the actor type and the authority behind it.

A hotel should know whether one person searched once or one person's agent searched fifty times.

Otherwise the machine wake distorts the market's sensors.

This is why agent identity matters economically as well as securely. An authenticated agent can tell a service, in a privacy-preserving way, that it represents a real customer and perhaps that repeated queries belong to the same shopping task. The service can optimize without tracking more personal information than needed.

Poor identity design will encourage surveillance because businesses will still need to infer what traffic means.

The market wake also appears in price discovery.

If buyer agents become excellent at finding the lowest price for interchangeable goods, price dispersion may fall. Sellers with higher prices must justify them through service, location, reputation or bundling. That sounds like textbook competition.

But if a few agent platforms mediate most demand, sellers may become dependent on those platforms. The platform can charge for access, rank merchants or impose terms. The competitive pressure among sellers coexists with concentrated power at the interface.

This is not a contradiction. It is a familiar platform pattern.

The consumer gets a better search tool and the search tool becomes a gatekeeper.

Open protocols can reduce this risk. If merchants publish structured offers that many agents can read, buyers can switch assistants without losing market access. If transaction history and preferences are portable, agent providers compete on quality rather than lock-in. If one company owns both the agent and the marketplace, conflicts need disclosure and perhaps regulation.

The shape of the interface becomes market structure.

There is another possibility: agents create new markets for tiny decisions.

Human transaction costs make many exchanges not worth negotiating. You do not auction every minute of your home's electricity demand or sell access to every idle computer cycle. Software can manage microtransactions that humans would never tolerate manually.

Smart energy systems already do versions of this. Machines respond to prices and grid signals. AI agents could extend automatic optimization across bandwidth, cloud compute, delivery windows, insurance coverage, subscriptions and personal data permissions.

The economy becomes more continuously negotiable.

This can improve resource allocation. It can also make life exhausting if every ordinary product becomes a variable contract managed by opaque agents. Convenience depends on the machine absorbing complexity rather than passing a dashboard of micro-decisions back to the user.

Delegation returns.

The user needs policies: save money unless reliability falls below this threshold; never sell location data; prefer refundable travel; do not switch energy plans more than once a year; spend up to this amount without asking. The agent turns values into bids.

That is a more consequential role than recommending a product.

It can change markets by making dormant preferences actionable.

Millions of people say they prefer privacy, local production, low carbon, union labor, accessibility or repairability but do not have time to research every purchase. Agents can enforce those preferences consistently if reliable data exists.

Demand becomes more legible.

Sellers may respond by providing stronger evidence of attributes buyers care about. Certifications, supply-chain data, carbon reporting and repair scores can become machine-readable competitive inputs. This could reward firms whose advantages are hard to communicate through ordinary advertising.

It could also reward whoever controls the standards defining those attributes.

Every machine-readable preference needs a taxonomy. Who decides what counts as “local,” “sustainable,” “independent,” “secure” or “fair labor”? The agent may inherit categories from a platform, regulator or certification body.

Markets run on classifications as much as prices.

Agents can make those classifications more powerful by applying them automatically.

This creates an opportunity for users to own their policies. Instead of one platform defining “best,” a person can select or author preference rules. Organizations can publish procurement policies that agents execute. Civil-society groups can offer policy packs. Regulators can mandate disclosures that make certain constraints enforceable.

The agent becomes a policy engine at the point of purchase.

That is potentially transformative because policy often fails through attention. A company may have a twenty-page procurement standard that employees inconsistently remember. An agent can apply every rule to every transaction.

Consistency is valuable and unforgiving.

Bad rules also become more powerful when automated. An exclusion criterion written carelessly can block every small supplier. A risk score can become an invisible trade barrier. An outdated vendor list can prevent better offers from being considered.

The market wake can harden bureaucracy into code.

This is why automated markets need appeal and exception paths. The agent should be able to explain which constraint rejected an option. A seller should know why it was filtered. A user should be able to relax a rule deliberately rather than discovering that the machine silently narrowed the market.

Competition depends on visibility into exclusion.

There is a final market residue: strategic behavior between machines.

Once participants know they are dealing with agents, they will design to influence agents. Sellers may present decoy options that make another offer look better. Negotiation agents may probe for the buyer's maximum price. Buyer agents may conceal preferences. Platforms may randomize behavior to prevent exploitation.

Economics becomes partly protocol design.

The machine wake is not a passive trail left after transactions. It changes the rules that determine the next transaction.

This is why the rise of agentic commerce will be less like adding a helpful shopping feature and more like changing market microstructure.

Search becomes continuous. Offers become structured. Requests multiply. Verification becomes necessary. Identity distinguishes real delegated demand from spam. Pricing adapts. Friction disappears in one place and reappears in another.

The winners will not simply be the firms with the smartest agents.

They will be the markets that learn how to interpret machine behavior without confusing volume for demand, automation for authority or optimization for consent.

When asking becomes free, answering becomes the scarce side of the trade.
