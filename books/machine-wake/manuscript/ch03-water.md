# Chapter 3 — Water

A data center can be dry inside and thirsty outside.

Walk through a modern server hall and you may see no exposed water at all. There are racks, cables, metal cabinets, sealed cooling loops, sensors and polished floors. The room can feel more like a laboratory than a utility plant. Yet somewhere beyond the white space, heat has to leave. Depending on the design, water may be one of the most efficient ways to help it go.

This is where a simple statement becomes dangerous.

“AI uses water” is true in the broad sense and nearly useless without a boundary.

Water can be consumed at the data-center site through evaporative cooling. It can be circulated in a closed loop and largely reused. It can be withdrawn by an electricity generator far from the computing facility. It can be used to manufacture semiconductors. A data center can use more water in one season than another. The same amount of computing can have very different water consequences in different climates, with different cooling equipment, different electricity sources and different server efficiencies.

Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory researchers studying workload-level water use found variation exceeding ten thousandfold across combinations of these factors. That is not a rounding error. It is a warning against turning a complex system into a single viral number.

The important story is not that the number is unknowable. The important story is that design matters enormously.

To understand why, begin with an ordinary cooling tower.

Warm water arrives from a condenser loop. Air moves through the tower. A small portion of the water evaporates. Evaporation removes heat from the remaining water, which can then return cooler to the system. The process is effective because changing liquid water into vapor carries away a large amount of thermal energy. It is the same reason perspiration cools skin.

But evaporated water has left the liquid system. It must be replaced. Additional water may also be discharged as blowdown because dissolved minerals become more concentrated as pure water evaporates. The cooling tower can recirculate most of its water repeatedly and still consume a meaningful amount because the mechanism works by letting some water escape as vapor.

This creates an uncomfortable linguistic problem. People often use “water use,” “withdrawal” and “consumption” as if they were interchangeable. They are not. Water withdrawn from a river, aquifer or municipal system may be returned after use. Water consumed through evaporation is not returned to the same local source on the same schedule. A facility can withdraw a great deal and consume little, or withdraw less and consume a higher fraction.

For communities trying to understand local consequences, that distinction is not academic.

Water has location in a way electricity often appears not to. A megawatt-hour can be transmitted across a regional grid. Water infrastructure is more constrained by watersheds, aquifers, treatment plants, pipes and local rights. A gallon used in a wet region is not equivalent to a gallon used during drought in a basin under stress. An annual average can hide a summer peak that matters more than the yearly total.

This is one reason data-center debates become politically sharp. The infrastructure can arrive at extraordinary scale while local water systems were designed for a different pattern of demand. A project may be economically valuable, technically efficient and still force a community to ask whether its resource assumptions have changed.

The worst public discussion treats the answer as obvious in advance. One side imagines an alien industrial facility draining a town for trivial chatbots. The other treats water concern as ignorance because modern facilities can use closed-loop or water-free cooling. Both positions skip the site.

The site is the argument.

Some data centers use evaporative cooling because it can reduce electricity required for mechanical refrigeration. That can create a tradeoff: use more water to use less electricity. In a region where water is abundant and power is constrained or carbon-intensive, the trade may be sensible. In a drought-prone region, a design that consumes more electricity but little water may be preferable. If the electricity itself is generated by a water-intensive thermal plant, the comparison becomes more complicated again.

Water and energy can be substitutes in the cooling system and complements in the larger system.

This is what environmental accounting looks like when it grows up. There is no single dial labeled sustainability. Turning one input down can turn another up.

The Department of Energy's guidance for federal data centers has long described opportunities to improve cooling-tower water efficiency by managing cycles of concentration, blowdown, controls and maintenance. More recent federal research is pushing water-free and advanced cooling designs for very high-density computing. At the same time, the Department of Energy has emphasized that many modern facilities use closed-loop systems that greatly reduce water needs compared with older or evaporative designs.

All of those things can be true together because “a data center” is not one machine.

A useful water budget has at least three layers.

The first is direct facility water. This includes water used on site for cooling, humidification or other building functions. It is the water most likely to appear in a local utility bill and local political debate.

The second is indirect operational water. Electricity generation can consume water, depending on the generating technology and cooling design. A data center that uses little water at the property can therefore still have a water footprint through the grid. As electricity systems change, this indirect component changes too.

The third is embodied water in construction and manufacturing. Semiconductor fabrication is water-intensive and requires extremely pure water. Building materials and equipment carry water footprints from supply chains. These are real but belong to a different decision horizon. A chip's manufacturing water is part of its life-cycle burden; it should not be casually added to a per-query number as though each query independently caused the chip to be manufactured.

The boundary determines the meaning of the metric.

This matters because the machine wake is full of allocation problems. Shared infrastructure serves many users and many workloads. A cooling plant does not know which evaporated molecule belonged to which prompt. A server may run one model during the day and another at night. Hardware might have been purchased for peak capacity and spend part of its life lightly utilized. Electricity sources vary by hour. Assigning a precise environmental burden to one answer requires assumptions about all of this.

An estimate can still be useful if the assumptions are visible.

The problem comes when uncertainty is hidden behind decimal places.

Suppose an analyst measures total annual water consumption for a facility, total computing work, and the proportion associated with one service. A per-task estimate can then be constructed. But the estimate may change if workload mix changes, if the facility installs new servers, if weather changes, if the operator switches cooling modes, if the local grid changes generation, or if model efficiency improves. The number is not a property of language. It is a property of a system during a period.

This is good news because properties of systems can be engineered.

Server efficiency is one of the most powerful water variables because every unit of electricity avoided at the server reduces heat that must be rejected and may reduce water used by electricity generation. Higher utilization can also lower water per useful task by spreading fixed facility costs across more productive work. Better cooling controls can reduce unnecessary evaporation. Using reclaimed water can reduce demand for potable supplies. Locating facilities where climate supports dry cooling or economization can change the tradeoff. Matching workloads to regions or hours with favorable resource conditions can change indirect impacts.

None of these options is free.

Reclaimed water requires treatment, pipes, contracts and a reliable source. Dry cooling can perform less efficiently in high temperatures and can consume more electricity. Moving a workload to another region can add network latency or conflict with data-residency rules. Running equipment harder to improve utilization can reduce the spare capacity available for demand spikes. Raising coolant temperatures can improve efficiency but requires hardware designed to tolerate them. Water-saving choices can move cost into capital, energy, land or operational complexity.

The right question is therefore not “Does AI use water?” It is “What water problem are we solving, in which place, over which time, and what moves when we solve it?”

That question is especially important because water is emotionally legible.

Most people have a direct relationship with water scarcity. A dry reservoir, a lawn restriction, a well that drops, a river under stress and a municipal rate increase are easier to imagine than a terawatt-hour. When a large data-center proposal arrives, citizens may reasonably compare industrial water demand with homes, farms, parks and other local uses. Developers may respond with global economic benefits or facility-level efficiency metrics. Both sides can be discussing real things while talking past each other because the scales differ.

A community does not live inside a global average.

This is where transparency becomes part of the wake.

If a facility's water demand is material, the useful information is not a promotional claim that the building is efficient. It is the expected source, annual consumption, peak demand, seasonal variation, treatment requirements, drought response, percentage of reclaimed water, cooling modes, and how estimates change under expansion. If local officials are granting incentives or infrastructure capacity, they need to know what resource obligations travel with the project.

The same principle applies to AI companies buying cloud capacity. A procurement team that asks only for price and computing performance will optimize only those dimensions. If water exposure matters to the organization, it has to appear in site selection, vendor evaluation and workload design. Metrics become operational only when somebody is responsible for them.

This sounds obvious, but invisible infrastructure tends to escape ordinary product ownership.

A product team can add a model feature without knowing which facility will execute it. A cloud provider can move work among regions. The user can be thousands of miles from the cooling plant. Responsibility stretches along the supply chain until every participant can plausibly say that the water decision is made somewhere else.

The machine wake is often created by nobody in particular because each actor owns only a layer.

This is why life-cycle thinking needs governance, not only measurement. Measurements identify where burdens appear. Governance decides who is allowed to move them.

Consider a company that reduces direct water use by switching from evaporative cooling to a more electricity-intensive system during hot weather. Its site metric improves. If the additional electricity comes from generation with substantial water consumption or higher carbon emissions, another footprint may worsen. The facility has not cheated. It has optimized the metric it was given.

A mature system therefore uses multiple metrics and acknowledges tradeoffs. Water usage effectiveness can help operators understand site water relative to IT energy. Power usage effectiveness helps with facility energy overhead. Carbon accounting adds another dimension. None is a complete environmental score. The purpose of separate metrics is precisely to prevent one number from swallowing unlike effects.

People are sometimes disappointed by this conclusion because they want technology questions to produce simple moral rankings. The world is less cooperative.

A hyperscale facility using highly efficient servers and carefully managed cooling may consume a large absolute amount of water because it performs an enormous amount of computing. A small inefficient data center may consume little in total and far more per unit of useful work. Which is better depends on whether the question is local scarcity, global efficiency, total demand, marginal expansion or something else.

Scale and intensity answer different questions.

This distinction becomes more important as AI shifts from training toward continuous inference and agentic operation. A training run is a large but bounded event. Inference is a stream. If AI becomes embedded in search, productivity software, customer service, vehicles, science and personal agents, the cumulative operating footprint can exceed attention paid to occasional headline training runs. Water demand follows total heat rejected across the system, not the news cycle.

At the same time, hardware and model efficiency can reduce water per task. The LBNL finding of enormous variation means some combinations of technology and siting are vastly better than others. The future burden is not predetermined by today's facilities.

There is a useful analogy with agriculture.

It would be absurd to ask how much water “food” uses and expect one answer. Rice grown in one place, tomatoes in another, beef production, greenhouse vegetables and rain-fed grain have different water relationships. Yet water accounting across agriculture is still valuable. It helps identify scarce basins, inefficient practices and tradeoffs. The sophistication comes from refusing to confuse the category with the measurement.

AI deserves the same seriousness.

Another complication is that water systems themselves require energy. Municipal water has to be extracted, treated, pumped and sometimes heated. Wastewater has to be handled. Reclaimed-water systems need infrastructure. A data center using less water may reduce energy elsewhere in the local system. A project that funds new reclaimed-water capacity may create benefits beyond the facility. Conversely, a large industrial user can require costly upgrades whose economics are distributed across ratepayers unless contracts are designed carefully.

The wake can change infrastructure even when the resource is not exhausted.

This is an important point. Resource conflict is not only about running out. A town can have plenty of total annual water and still need a new pipe, pump, treatment train or storage facility to serve a concentrated load. The same is true for electricity. The physical network has capacities that matter independently of regional totals.

When a data center arrives, engineers think in flows. How many gallons per minute? At what pressure? At which temperature? With what water chemistry? What happens during a heat wave? What happens if one pump fails? What happens during maintenance? Can the system switch modes? How quickly can demand change?

These are better questions than any slogan because they reveal the actual machine.

They also reveal opportunities that disappear at the level of ideology.

A facility might use outside air during cool periods, dry coolers during moderate weather and evaporative assistance only during the hottest hours. It might capture rainwater or use treated wastewater. It might raise the temperature at which coolant leaves the server so heat can be rejected more easily. It might schedule flexible computing for cooler times, though many workloads cannot wait. It might select hardware for energy efficiency because saving electrical energy also reduces cooling demand.

The wake can be shaped across time as well as space.

This becomes particularly powerful when computation is deferrable. Some AI work is latency-sensitive: a person waiting for an answer will not accept a six-hour delay because the afternoon is hot. Other work, such as batch inference, model evaluation, data processing or some training, can move in time. A scheduler that understands electricity prices, grid carbon and cooling conditions can choose when to run flexible work. The physical system becomes part of the computing scheduler.

This is a recurring theme in mature infrastructure. Once the easy efficiency gains are captured inside components, coordination between components becomes valuable.

A machine does not have to know the weather to answer a question. The system operating millions of machines may benefit from knowing it.

There is also a social reason to design the water wake carefully. Technologies earn trust partly through how they behave when local conditions are difficult.

A facility that consumes heavily during normal years but can reduce demand during drought is a different neighbor from one whose cooling design makes water consumption inflexible. A company that publishes meaningful water data is easier to evaluate than one that offers only global replenishment commitments. Replenishing water somewhere can produce real ecological benefit, but it does not necessarily solve the hydraulic constraint in the place where a data center operates. Accounting should not erase geography.

The same caution applies to the word “net.” Net-zero, net-positive and water-positive claims can be useful portfolio concepts while obscuring local timing. A gallon restored to one watershed does not flow through the pipe serving another. A wet-season project does not automatically replace a dry-season peak. Aggregate accounting can support stewardship, but physical systems remain stubbornly local.

The machine wake is where the aggregate meets the pipe.

Water may be the clearest example in this book of why residue should be studied as an engineering variable rather than as an accusation. The heat exists. The facility must reject it. Water is one tool. Sometimes it is the best tool. Sometimes it is the wrong one. The outcome depends on equipment, climate, energy, scale, policy and the alternatives available.

The people designing the system therefore deserve better than a demand for purity. They need a hierarchy of constraints.

Protect drinking-water reliability. Respect ecological limits and legal rights. Do not hide peak demand behind annual averages. Prefer reclaimed or non-potable supplies where they make technical and economic sense. Improve server and facility efficiency before building infrastructure to dispose of unnecessary heat. Use water where its thermal advantage justifies the local cost. Publish enough information that communities can see whether promises survive actual operation. Revisit assumptions as hardware density and climate conditions change.

This is not a glamorous agenda. It is how infrastructure becomes ordinary enough to last.

The small box on the screen makes AI appear to live in language. The water system reminds us that it also lives in climate, pipes, cooling towers, treatment plants, pumps and local politics.

The answer does not contain a drop.

The wake might.
