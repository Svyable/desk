# Chapter 6 — The Denominator Problem

A success rate is a story about its denominator.

This is why extraordinary percentages should make us curious before they make us impressed.

If most seeded clouds rain afterward, we need to know what qualified a cloud for seeding. Operators do not select random patches of sky. They select clouds judged physically promising. That means the intervention group is preloaded with conditions favorable to the outcome.

This is not fraud. It is competent operation.

It is also a statistical problem.

Imagine a doctor who treats only patients already likely to recover and then reports the proportion who improved. The number may be accurate and still tell us little about treatment effect. Weather modification creates a similar selection problem, except the patient dissolves while you are observing it.

The proper denominator depends on the claim.

If the claim is **operational feasibility**, then the relevant question may be: when crews identify seedable clouds under mission conditions, how often can they execute the intervention?

If the claim is **microphysical efficacy**, the question is: compared with physically matched unseeded clouds, how much does seeding change ice formation or precipitation development?

If the claim is **hydrologic yield**, the denominator becomes storms, watersheds, seasons, or target-area precipitation.

If the claim is **military effect**, the denominator becomes route condition, traffic, or logistical throughput.

Popeye's legend slides among these denominators as if they were interchangeable.

They are not.

A high fraction of seeded clouds raining can coexist with a tiny seasonal precipitation gain. Modern cloud-seeding research makes this intuitive. Under carefully selected orographic conditions, investigators can directly observe seeded precipitation signatures with radar and gauges. Yet long-run operational yield can remain modest because suitable conditions occur infrequently and many seeding events contribute little.

That is not a contradiction. It is conditional probability.

The 2023 numerical evaluation of ten years of operational seeding over Wyoming's Wind River Range found that suitable conditions were rare and estimated an overall yield around one percent. The result does not prove Popeye ineffective; the meteorology, terrain, methods, and objectives differ. It proves something more useful: local process success and program-level yield belong to different scales.

The denominator problem also explains why believers and skeptics can talk past one another.

The believer points to an event: a seeded cloud produced snow.

The skeptic points to a season: total water gain is uncertain.

Both may be correct.

Popeye needs the same discipline. The historical evidence can establish that crews seeded clouds, that rain followed some seedings, and that officials believed the program useful. It does not automatically establish the counterfactual increment or military consequence.

This is where the classified nature of the program hurts twice.

First, secrecy limits the contemporaneous external scrutiny that might force clearer denominators.

Second, declassification often releases summaries more readily than complete operational datasets. Historians inherit the numerator: missions, claims, memorable outcomes. The denominator stays in a box, a missing annex, an unpreserved log, or an instrument network that never existed.

The result is an evidentiary ecology built to support mythology.

Not because anyone necessarily fabricated the rain.

Because success leaves stories and controls leave spreadsheets.
