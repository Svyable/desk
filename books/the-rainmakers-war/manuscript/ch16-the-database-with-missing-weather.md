# Chapter 16 — The Database With Missing Weather

In 2026, the Government Accountability Office reported something almost comic in light of Popeye.

The United States has had a weather-modification reporting law for more than fifty years, and the reporting system is still incomplete enough that GAO estimated that more than half of sampled reports likely contained errors or missing required information.

This is not a secret military operation.

It is peacetime paperwork.

Anyone conducting covered weather-modification activity in the United States is supposed to notify NOAA. NOAA is supposed to maintain records and make them available to the public. Yet operators may be unaware of requirements, forms are inconsistently completed, and NOAA does not actively search for every unreported activity.

The lesson is not corruption.

The lesson is that transparency is an engineered system too.

A law can exist while the dataset remains weak. A database can be public while being incomplete. A reporting regime can reduce uncertainty without eliminating it.

This matters enormously because weather modification has reentered a suspicious political environment. Some states have proposed or enacted bans. Public claims about aircraft, chemicals, hurricanes, and geoengineering spread faster than official records can answer them.

The obvious response is better information.

But **better information** needs specificity: standardized coordinates, dates, target areas, materials, quantities, platform, operator, purpose, meteorological conditions, and outcome measures. Without these, public disclosure becomes a list of intentions rather than an audit trail.

Popeye is the nightmare version of missing metadata.

Modern reporting should be the opposite.

If atmospheric intervention is legitimate, the burden is not only to operate safely. It is to leave enough evidence that outsiders can tell what happened.
