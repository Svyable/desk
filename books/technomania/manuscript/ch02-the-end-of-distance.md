# The End of Distance

The railway did not begin by shrinking the world. It began by making a particular journey faster.

That distinction is easy to lose because we inherit the network after it has become geography. A modern rail map looks like a diagram of places that naturally belonged together. London and Birmingham, Boston and New York, Paris and Lyon: lines join cities that now seem destined to be joined. The original decisions disappear. So do the failed routes, bypassed towns, competing gauges, speculative extensions, bankrupt companies, bitter land disputes, and the uncomfortable fact that a machine celebrated for abolishing distance made location newly valuable.

Nineteenth-century observers reached quickly for metaphors grander than transport. Steam movement seemed to annihilate space. Telegraphy would intensify the phrase, but rail gave it physical force. A coach road had made distance unpleasant. A railway made the same miles pass under a passenger with a regularity that belonged to machinery rather than weather, animal fatigue, or road conditions. Timetables turned movement into a schedule. The promise felt metaphysical because the old experience of space had partly been an experience of time.

If Edinburgh is two days away and then hours away, what exactly has happened to Edinburgh?

The city has not moved. The cost of reaching it has.

That difference is the beginning of technomania and the cure for it.

The historian who studies “annihilation of space” finds a phrase that refuses to die. Steamships, railways, telegraphs, automobiles, aviation, telephones, television, satellites, fiber optics, and the internet have all been said to shrink the world. The persistence tells us something. Every generation encounters a technology that changes the friction of distance and mistakes a change in one dimension of geography for geography’s disappearance.

Railways show what really happens: distance is not annihilated. It is repriced.

A mile beside a station is not the same mile as one twenty miles from the line. A town served by a frequent junction is not equivalent to a town the train bypasses. A port connected to interior markets can become more valuable because the railway reaches it. Agricultural land can enter distant markets and, for the same reason, face distant competition. Workers can commute farther, firms can enlarge their catchment areas, newspapers can circulate faster, perishable goods can travel where they could not before. None of that makes place irrelevant. It makes network position consequential.

A technology marketed as escape from geography produces a new geography.

This is the pattern I want to keep returning to because it corrects one of the laziest habits in technological prediction. We take the constraint a machine most visibly reduces and imagine a future organized around the absence of that constraint. We rarely ask which secondary constraint becomes more important once the first one falls.

Rail reduced travel time. It increased dependence on infrastructure.

The stagecoach could change route. A railway was steel committed to a corridor. The faster system was also, in one sense, less flexible. It required land, grading, bridges, cuttings, stations, signaling, maintenance, rolling stock, coal, water, repair depots, trained labor, capital markets, and a timetable coordinated across distances. The miracle was not the locomotive. The miracle was that all of these things could be made to behave like one machine.

That organizational fact is easy to miss because the locomotive has smoke.

The locomotive can be painted. It can arrive in a station in front of a crowd. It provides the photograph and the symbol. The institution that keeps two trains from occupying the same piece of track is more important to the passenger’s survival and nearly invisible. Technologies repeatedly acquire a mythology around the object while their social effects depend on the system.

Consider time itself. Railways helped force local time into standardized time because a timetable that passed through towns running on slightly different solar clocks was an operational nuisance. Time zones are not a poetic consequence of speed. They are an administrative solution to coordination. The faster the network became, the more intolerable temporal ambiguity became. An innovation that seemed to make time matter less made clock agreement matter more.

This is an excellent general rule: when a technology reduces one kind of friction, coordination often becomes the next scarcity.

You can see the same thing in modern software. Faster communication increases the number of people who can be included in a project and often increases the cost of deciding who decides. Shared documents reduce the effort required to distribute drafts and can multiply comments. AI systems reduce the cost of producing candidate answers and can increase the cost of verification. The railway version was tangible: more movement required more scheduling.

There was a financial version too.

Railway construction is one of the places where the word mania stops being metaphorical and begins showing up in economic history. Britain’s Railway Mania of the 1840s combined real technological promise, permissive capital, speculative promotion, parliamentary authorization, engineering ambition, and public appetite for shares. The fact that speculation occurred does not make the railway unreal. That is precisely why railway history is so valuable for thinking about later technology bubbles. A fraudulent perpetual-motion machine cannot create a durable speculative boom of the same kind because eventually there is no underlying infrastructure worth building. Railways had enough truth to support too much belief.

This is the anatomy of a serious bubble.

The asset is connected to a real discontinuity. Early winners demonstrate extraordinary returns. Capital arrives. Forecasts assume current growth rates persist. Promoters extend the addressable market. Infrastructure plans move from profitable corridors into marginal ones. The existence of demand in one place is used as evidence for demand everywhere. Financing conditions matter as much as engineering. A network effect gives every new line rhetorical value because it will supposedly make the whole system more useful. Construction itself becomes proof that the future is arriving. Then the arithmetic catches up.

What remains afterward is not zero.

That point separates a technology mania from a pure fraud. When railway companies failed, track could remain. Bridges remained. Rights of way remained. Engineering knowledge remained. Skilled labor remained. Survey data remained. Towns and industrial districts had already begun orienting themselves around the network. Later owners could acquire distressed assets. The speculative investor might lose money while society inherited useful capital.

The same pattern appears in later telecommunications booms. Fiber laid under optimistic demand forecasts can become cheap capacity for the next wave of users. Data centers built for one workload may be repurposed. Excess semiconductor fabrication investment can depress prices and broaden access. The presence of waste does not prove that the capacity was useless. Sometimes the mania is a terrible investment strategy and an effective infrastructure policy carried out accidentally through private loss.

The railway also reveals how “democratization” works in practice.

Faster transport was genuinely broadening. People who could not afford private carriages gained access to mechanically powered movement. Excursion travel expanded. Commuting patterns changed. Goods once confined to local markets circulated farther. But network access was not uniform. Fares mattered. Class mattered. Station placement mattered. Timetables mattered. Ownership mattered. A line might open a region to opportunity while also opening it to extraction.

Technologies do not distribute their consequences according to the elegance of their engineering.

The same rail connection that allows farmers to reach a distant market allows distant producers to compete with them. The same line that reduces isolation can move young workers away. The same standardized logistics that lower prices can centralize production. Connectivity is not an outcome. It is a condition under which many outcomes become possible.

This seems obvious when stated slowly. It becomes strangely invisible during a boom.

The rhetoric of connection tends to assign a moral sign to connectivity itself. Connected is good. Disconnected is backward. Faster is progress. Slower is obstruction. A railway line becomes more than infrastructure; it becomes proof that a place has entered modernity. Later technologies inherit this moral grammar. Electrification, telephone penetration, broadband, smartphone adoption, digital payments, and AI use can all become development metrics because access often does matter. The problem begins when the proxy replaces the human question.

A village can have connectivity and lose bargaining power.

A worker can have access to a labor platform and face worse terms.

A school can have devices and no evidence that the devices improve learning.

A hospital can buy an AI system and spend more clinician time resolving false alerts.

A city can have a high-speed line that increases land values near stations faster than wages.

None of these observations makes infrastructure undesirable. They restore the missing variable: institutions decide how capability becomes welfare.

Railway history is also a warning about forecasts that appear correct only because they are vague. “Rail will transform society” is almost impossible to falsify and was correct. “This particular line will produce enough traffic at these fares to service this debt” is testable and often was not. Technology discourse prefers civilizational claims partly because civilizational claims mature slowly and admit endless reinterpretation. Capital allocation cannot wait that long.

A useful forecast has to touch a constraint.

How many passengers? At what price? Between which places? With what construction cost? How much maintenance? Which competing routes? What happens if financing costs rise? What demand is induced by the line, and what demand is merely shifted from another carrier? What land value is created, and who captures it?

Those questions are dull. That is their virtue.

The railway promoters who were most wrong were not necessarily wrong about steam transport. They were wrong about a line, a traffic projection, a cost, a schedule, or a capital structure. Their error lived one level below the headline.

Modern AI forecasting often has the opposite problem. We are asked whether “AI will replace programmers” or “AI will transform medicine,” as if the relevant unit were a profession or a sector. The railway analogy suggests smaller questions. Which programming tasks become cheap? Which bottleneck follows? What happens to review? How does a firm reorganize when code generation accelerates? Does software demand expand because software becomes cheaper, as travel demand expanded when transport improved? Which parts of medicine are information processing and which are licensure, liability, physical access, reimbursement, patient trust, workflow, and authority?

The machine does not enter a vacuum. It enters a system already full of prices and vetoes.

Railways entered property law, municipal politics, capital markets, military strategy, postal systems, labor relations, class structures, and existing transport businesses. Their effects were not secondary to the technology. Those effects were the technology becoming social.

This is why “general-purpose technology” can be a misleading phrase when people hear “general” as “automatic.” A general-purpose technology has many possible uses. It does not implement those uses by itself. Electricity did not merely arrive and cause productivity. Factories had to be redesigned around motors. The internet did not merely arrive and cause e-commerce. Payment systems, warehouses, fraud controls, logistics, browsers, standards, advertising markets, and consumer habits accumulated around it. AI may be general-purpose in the same sense: broad possibility followed by specific institutional work.

The railroad did something else to imagination. It changed expectations of speed.

Once an express train exists, the old journey becomes not merely slow but intolerably slow. Technology creates dissatisfaction with the baseline it replaces. A letter that once arrived in a respectable number of days becomes absurd after telegraphy. A web page that loads in ten seconds becomes broken after broadband. A customer-service response within two days becomes unacceptable after instant chat. A software feature that once took a team two weeks may appear expensive if a model can prototype it in an afternoon.

This ratcheting matters because productivity gains are not experienced only as leisure. They can become new service expectations.

John Maynard Keynes famously imagined that technological progress might eventually reduce the hours required to satisfy human needs. In many sectors, however, productivity has partly produced more output, more variety, higher standards, and new categories of consumption rather than a proportional retreat from work. Railways did not cause society to say, “Travel is now fast enough; let us stop moving.” They helped create more movement.

Cheap capability can increase demand for the capability.

This is another reason the repeated “end of work” forecast is difficult. If AI lowers the cost of producing software, demand for software may rise. If it lowers the cost of translation, more translation may occur. If it lowers the cost of personalized tutoring, expectations around educational support may expand. Some workers will still be displaced. Some occupations will shrink. But an engineering calculation of labor saved is not identical to a macroeconomic calculation of labor no longer demanded.

Railways also made military movement faster, state administration more coherent, markets larger, diseases more mobile, tourism more accessible, extraction more efficient, and political control over territories sometimes easier. A machine can serve opposed purposes without contradiction. Its affordance is not its ideology.

This sounds like a warning specifically for communications technologies, but transport learned it first. A network does not choose what moves through it.

The cargo can be grain or troops.

The passenger can be a migrant or an occupying official.

The message can be scientific data or propaganda.

The model can draft a lesson or a scam.

The moral character belongs to the arrangement around the tool.

Railway enthusiasts were often right about magnitude. They were less reliable about direction. That is a recurring combination in technomania. A person can correctly perceive that a technology will be enormous and still misidentify which institutions it strengthens.

The internet was supposed to weaken centralized gatekeepers because publishing costs collapsed. It did weaken many old ones. Then search engines, app stores, hosting platforms, payment processors, social networks, cloud providers, recommendation systems, and a small number of device ecosystems became powerful new chokepoints. Decentralization occurred at one layer and centralization at another.

Rail did something structurally similar. Individual movement became faster and more accessible while dependence on network owners, timetables, terminals, and coordinated infrastructure increased. Greater mobility did not mean less system.

It meant more system.

This is perhaps the central correction the railway offers our current moment. We imagine automation as subtraction: remove the clerk, remove the driver, remove the coder, remove the intermediary. But a mature technology often adds layers of coordination even as it removes local effort. The railway replaced enormous amounts of animal and human transport labor while creating dispatchers, engineers, signalmen, maintenance crews, station staff, schedules, standards, regulators, financiers, and an industrial supply chain. The future was not a simpler version of the past. It was a more organized one.

AI may remove keystrokes and add governance.

It may remove first drafts and add evaluation.

It may reduce the cost of expertise-like output and increase the value of liability-bearing authority.

It may decentralize access to capabilities once held by professionals and centralize dependence on model providers, compute infrastructure, power contracts, proprietary data, identity systems, and distribution platforms.

The right historical analogy is not that AI is “like the railroad” in some sweeping sense. Analogies become stupid when asked to predict details. The useful inheritance is a question: which geography is being redrawn?

For rail, the answer became visible in stations and cities.

For AI, the new geography may consist of compute clusters, electrical grids, semiconductor supply chains, cloud contracts, data access, legal permissions, model interfaces, organizational workflows, and the distribution of people authorized to sign their names under machine-assisted work.

Distance did not end.

The map changed.

The next technology made that error even more tempting because, unlike rail, it could move a message without moving the messenger at all. The telegraph did not merely accelerate travel. It severed communication from transport. If any machine deserved to be called the destroyer of distance, it was that one.

And still distance survived.
