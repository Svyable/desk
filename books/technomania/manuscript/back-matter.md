# Back Matter

## A note on evidence

Technological history is unusually vulnerable to quotation mining because a vivid prediction can survive longer than its context. Wherever possible, this manuscript distinguishes the famous line from the institutional record around it. Lewis Strauss’s “too cheap to meter” prediction is therefore read against the Nuclear Regulatory Commission’s historical reconstruction rather than treated as a floating slogan. Apollo-era forecasting is read alongside NASA planning documents and post-Apollo decisions. Internet history is anchored in DARPA and NSF accounts rather than retrospective startup mythology. Current AI claims are bounded by the specific task-level, labor-exposure, and macro-financial evidence cited in the research trail.

The book uses four working evidence classes throughout its notes:

1. **Verified / documented** — primary records, official program histories, peer-reviewed or institutionally published empirical findings, financial or technical facts that can be directly checked.
2. **Credible but incomplete** — serious reporting, bounded extrapolation, or evidence that supports a claim but does not close it.
3. **Disputed / unconfirmed** — claims whose factual status is contested, weakly sourced, highly interested, or lacking independent confirmation.
4. **Speculative synthesis** — the book’s own attempt to connect cases, generate hypotheses, or identify recurring structure.

The categories are not moral grades. Class 4 ideas can be useful. Class 1 facts can be irrelevant. The purpose is to prevent an attractive synthesis from quietly borrowing the authority of a measured result.

## What remains unfinished

This draft is structurally complete but should still be treated as a working Desk manuscript. The historical chapters can absorb additional archival reconstruction, especially primary nineteenth-century railway and telegraph sources, early educational-radio records, primary personal-computing oral histories, and a deeper source trail for the early commercial web and social-media period. The AI chapters should continue to be refreshed because task-level evidence, investment conditions, and labor-market data are changing quickly.

The book would also benefit from a quantitative appendix comparing several technological booms on a common set of dimensions: infrastructure capital intensity, observed unit-cost decline, diffusion time, major complements, dominant bottleneck after diffusion, investment boom/bust characteristics, and eventual institutional settlement.

A second useful appendix would track famous predictions that were wrong about timing but right about direction, right about capability but wrong about institutions, and wrong in both dimensions. The point would not be prediction-score entertainment. It would be to test the book’s central claim that most serious forecasting errors occur at the transition between layers.

## Source practice

URLs in the research trail point to public, lawfully available materials. No classified access is claimed or implied. Where a contemporary institution summarizes academic studies, the manuscript treats the summary as an institutional synthesis and should, on later passes, continue replacing those summaries with the underlying papers where doing so changes the argument.

## Closing note

The strongest technological cultures do two things that look contradictory from outside: they become excited enough to build and skeptical enough to measure.

Losing either capacity is expensive.
