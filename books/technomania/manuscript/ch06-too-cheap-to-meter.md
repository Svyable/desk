# Too Cheap to Meter

Lewis Strauss’s phrase survives because it is compact enough to be unfair.

“Too cheap to meter” has become shorthand for technological overpromising, usually with nuclear power standing in for the entire class of machines that were supposed to make abundance trivial. The phrase is repeated so often that its historical ambiguity has become part of the story. Defenders have argued that Strauss meant fusion. Critics have treated it as a promise about commercial fission. The Nuclear Regulatory Commission’s historical account examined the record and found a less tidy answer: Strauss’s 1954 speech surrounded the prediction with discussion of fission and civilian atomic power, and when he was asked days later about commercial power from atomic piles, he again said he expected electricity too cheap to meter and suggested the day might be close.

The same NRC account also records an important complication. Utilities themselves did not uniformly embrace the slogan. Industry figures understood that fuel was only one part of an electric bill. Some called the idea a myth or delusion. Sober evaluations of nuclear economics were less ecstatic than the famous line.

This is useful because it separates technological enthusiasm into layers.

There was the physics.

There was the engineering.

There was the institution speaking publicly about the future.

There was an industry that would have to finance and operate the plants.

And there was a public that heard the memorable sentence rather than the footnotes.

Technomania is often produced in the gaps between those layers.

The physics of nuclear fission really is astonishing. A tiny mass of fuel can release energy at densities that make chemical combustion look crude. Anyone encountering that fact for the first time can understand the leap. If fuel has been one of civilization’s great constraints, and a new process extracts millions of times more energy per unit mass than familiar chemistry, abundance feels like the natural conclusion.

But a power plant sells electricity, not binding energy.

Between the nucleus and the wall socket lie reactors, control systems, steam systems, turbines, generators, containment structures, pumps, cooling systems, transmission, maintenance, security, fuel handling, financing, regulatory oversight, emergency planning, decommissioning, waste management, trained operators, and an expectation that the whole system will work safely for decades.

The energy can be dense while the institution is expensive.

This is one of the most durable errors in technology forecasting: extrapolating from the economics of the novel component to the economics of the whole service.

A solar module can become cheap while interconnection, land, storage, transmission, permitting, and financing remain consequential. A battery cell can fall in price while the installed storage system contains power electronics, thermal management, safety equipment, construction, and grid connection. A language model can make tokens cheap while a reliable enterprise system still needs data integration, evaluation, access control, workflow redesign, human review, compliance, and liability management.

Cheap intelligence-like output is not the same thing as cheap decisions.

The atomic story is particularly instructive because optimism and success coexisted. Nuclear power did not become too cheap to meter. It did become a major source of electricity in several countries, operating fleets for decades and supplying large amounts of low-carbon power. The technology neither fulfilled its grandest economic promise nor collapsed into a historical curiosity.

The middle outcome is common and badly represented in technology discourse.

We prefer triumph or fraud.

A technology can be consequential without being universal.

It can solve a physical problem and encounter an economic one.

It can work at scale and still fail to dominate.

It can be socially valuable while producing disappointing returns for particular investors.

It can be technically mature and politically contested.

Nuclear power is all of these things at once.

This matters because current debates around AI are already becoming binary in the same way. Either artificial intelligence is an epochal technology that will reorder most cognitive work, or the investment boom is a bubble and therefore the technology has been overhyped. Nuclear history suggests that these propositions are not opposites. Enormous technical significance does not guarantee the economic structure imagined by promoters.

A model can become ordinary infrastructure while some data-center investments disappoint.

Productivity gains can be real while valuations overshoot.

Automation can spread while aggregate employment effects remain more complicated than “jobs disappear.”

There is another atomic-era lesson: cost forecasts are political even when expressed as engineering.

A large infrastructure project is not built from a physical cost estimate alone. Interest rates matter. Construction duration matters. Regulatory change matters. Supply-chain competence matters. Standardization matters. Whether the same design is built repeatedly or reinvented matters. Public tolerance for rare catastrophic risk matters. Insurance arrangements matter. Waste rules matter. Local opposition matters. Geopolitics matters.

None of these variables changes the fission cross-section.

They can determine whether the plant is built.

Technological futurism often calls these “nontechnical barriers,” as if they are debris surrounding the real story. For society, they are the story. A bridge delayed by permitting is still a bridge not crossed. A drug that cannot pass safety testing is still a drug not prescribed. A model that cannot be used because an organization cannot establish data governance is still an unrealized productivity gain.

Calling a constraint nontechnical does not make it nonbinding.

The atomic age also reveals the way a successful military or state program can distort expectations about civilian deployment. Wartime nuclear development demonstrated that governments could mobilize extraordinary resources around a strategic objective. The Manhattan Project created a compressed timeline under conditions that did not resemble a normal commercial industry. The later civilian sector inherited some of the technological achievement and much of the aura.

Apollo would do something similar for space.

A state can make an expensive objective happen by concentrating money, authority, secrecy, talent, procurement, and political legitimacy. That achievement proves possibility. It does not prove that the same thing has a self-sustaining civilian business model.

This distinction is crucial whenever people invoke wartime mobilization to predict technological diffusion.

Governments can force a technology across a valley private markets will not cross. Sometimes that creates lasting infrastructure. Sometimes the strategic objective ends and the economics underneath it become visible.

The nuclear case is also a lesson in the asymmetry of visible failure. When a technology promises abundance, every cost overrun becomes evidence against the dream. Quiet decades of reliable operation do not produce the same image. Catastrophic accidents dominate public memory for understandable reasons. The result is a technology whose perception is shaped by rare dramatic events and slow invisible output.

This is not an argument about whether those perceptions are rational. It is a reminder that risk is social before it is statistical.

Engineers can estimate probabilities and consequences. Communities decide what kind of risk is acceptable, to whom, under whose authority, and in exchange for what benefit.

Technomania tends to assume that superior numbers settle those questions. Technophobia tends to assume that dread settles them. Mature technological societies have to do the tedious work in between.

The phrase “too cheap to meter” also captures a recurring confusion about abundance itself.

Suppose electricity had become almost free at the point of generation. Metering might still matter because the grid has capacity constraints. Peaks matter. Transmission lines congest. Distribution equipment has limits. Electricity consumed at one place and time is not identical to electricity consumed somewhere else. A commodity can become cheap while coordination around the commodity remains valuable.

Digital goods made this lesson unavoidable. The cost of copying a file is effectively negligible. That did not make music, movies, books, software, or journalism economically trivial. Rights, discovery, reputation, production, attention, subscription, and distribution systems remained. Cheap copying changed the business model rather than abolishing economics.

AI may do the same to cognitive artifacts.

The marginal cost of producing a plausible memo can collapse. That does not make the consequences of acting on the memo cheap. The marginal cost of generating a software patch can collapse. Testing, security, maintenance, product judgment, and responsibility remain. The marginal cost of creating an image can collapse. Audience attention, brand, legal rights, and cultural significance do not collapse in parallel.

Abundance in one object can increase competition for its complements.

The atomic era produced another recurrent rhetorical move: the merger of technological forecasts with moral progress. Strauss’s 1954 vision did not stop at cheap electricity. The larger passage imagined famine becoming history, effortless high-speed travel, and longer life as disease yielded to knowledge. The atomic age was presented as a package of human advance.

This is emotionally understandable. Major technologies arrive in clusters. The middle twentieth century contained antibiotics, aviation, electronics, nuclear physics, mass production, television, rocketry, expanding universities, and rapidly rising living standards in parts of the world. It was easy to treat the cluster as a single arrow called progress.

But technologies do not arrive with a common ethical direction.

The same nuclear physics produced reactors and weapons.

The same rocketry produced satellite launch and ballistic missiles.

The same chemistry produced medicine and pollutants.

The same computing infrastructure produces scientific discovery and industrial surveillance.

The same generative model can assist a researcher and industrialize phishing.

Technological capacity expands the action space. It does not tell us which action to choose.

This becomes especially important when people talk about “solving” social problems with abundance. Cheap energy can help desalination, fertilizer production, industrial heat, transportation, and climate mitigation. It does not decide who owns land, who receives infrastructure, which neighborhoods bear environmental burdens, how prices are regulated, or whether a government is competent.

A country can possess abundant resources and govern them badly.

A city can have electricity and unreliable service.

A school can have broadband and poor instruction.

A hospital can have advanced equipment and inadequate staffing.

A bureaucracy can have AI and still make cruel decisions faster.

The technology changes the feasible set. Institutions choose within it.

Nuclear power’s history also challenges the assumption that a powerful technology must spread uniformly. Countries made radically different choices. Some built large fleets. Some abandoned programs. Some paused and returned. Some treated nuclear power as a strategic national capability; others treated it as politically toxic. The physics crossed borders unchanged. The institutions did not.

This is a warning against global AI forecasts built from a handful of frontier firms and rich-country labor markets.

A model may be globally accessible while wages, languages, infrastructure, education systems, electricity reliability, regulation, and firm structures vary enormously. The same automation can be attractive where labor is expensive and uneconomic where labor is cheap. The same tool can substitute for a professional in one regulatory regime and remain an assistant in another because only a licensed person can act.

There will not be one AI labor market.

There was not one atomic age.

The long-run story of nuclear technology is also a story of learning and forgetting. Standardized operations, accumulated experience, regulatory institutions, supply chains, and workforce expertise matter. When construction pauses for decades, those capabilities can decay. Restarting is not simply a matter of retrieving old blueprints. Institutions have memory, and memory is perishable.

This is relevant to every technological civilization. We imagine knowledge as permanently stored once documented. But practical capacity depends on people, suppliers, routines, facilities, and tacit coordination. A society can know how something works and lose the ability to do it cheaply.

Technological progress is not a monotonic database.

It is maintained.

That is another reason the phrase “the technology exists” can be misleading. A prototype can exist without a supply chain. A paper can exist without manufacturing capability. A trained model can exist without affordable inference. A laboratory demonstration can exist without a regulatory pathway. A country can possess documentation for a reactor class and lack the experienced firms to build one on schedule.

Existence has layers.

The nuclear dream also reminds us that one of the strongest drivers of technological enthusiasm is moral urgency. If a technology promises to eliminate a serious problem, skepticism can begin to look unethical. In the atomic era, abundance meant relief from fuel scarcity and perhaps poverty. In the climate era, nuclear power is now defended by many people as a necessary low-carbon resource. In AI, advocates argue that accelerated science, medicine, education, and productivity could improve billions of lives.

These possibilities matter.

They also create pressure to compress uncertainty. If the stakes are enormous, the promoter can argue that delay itself is harmful. The skeptic can answer that irreversible deployment creates its own harms. Both sides can become addicted to worst-case opportunity costs.

Technomania is not merely greed. Sometimes it is moral impatience.

That makes it harder to criticize, because the person promising abundance may sincerely be thinking about people who lack it.

The right response is not cynicism. It is to demand a more complete causal chain.

The reactor produces heat. How does that become cheaper electricity?

The model produces an answer. How does that become better medical care?

The platform connects a worker to a job. How does that become economic security?

The satellite maps a field. How does that become food abundance?

The chain can be long, and each link can fail for a different reason.

Technomania shortens the chain until the technological step sits adjacent to the moral outcome.

The cure is to put the missing institutions back in.

Strauss’s phrase endured because electricity did not become too cheap to meter. But there is a more interesting way to read it. The phrase identified the wrong scarcity.

Fuel energy density was not the final bottleneck.

Building and governing the system was.

That is one of the themes of this entire book. The new technology attacks a scarcity. If it succeeds, something else becomes scarce. Sometimes the new scarcity is capital. Sometimes attention. Sometimes verification. Sometimes land. Sometimes trust. Sometimes coordination. Sometimes legitimacy.

A technological revolution does not end scarcity.

It moves the frontier.

By the late 1950s, another technology was making the frontier literal. Sputnik crossed the sky. Rockets rose on columns of fire. Human travel beyond Earth stopped being fantasy. And because the first steps came so quickly, a generation learned one of technomania’s most intoxicating lessons: sometimes the impossible really does happen on schedule.
