# The City Becomes a Circuit

Electricity is a dangerous case for anyone arguing against technological overreach because, by almost every ordinary measure, electrification justified enormous enthusiasm.

It changed lighting, factories, transport, communications, domestic labor, entertainment, medicine, refrigeration, computation, and the organization of the city. It made whole categories of later technology possible. It is difficult to imagine another infrastructure that entered so many parts of daily life while becoming so quickly invisible. A modern building loses electricity for ten minutes and suddenly reveals how much of civilization had been hiding inside the wall.

The lesson is not that the electrification enthusiasts were wrong.

The lesson is that even one of history’s clearest technological victories arrived less like a single invention than a long institutional conversion.

A generator is not an electric society.

The transition required power stations, transmission systems, distribution networks, meters, wiring codes, fixtures, appliances, motors, standards, rate structures, capital, municipal franchises, maintenance crews, and households able to pay for connection. It required factories to reorganize around a new power source. It required architects to treat wiring as part of a building. It required governments to decide which companies could occupy streets with poles and conduits. It required safety regimes because a force marketed as clean and modern could kill people and burn buildings.

This is the mundane machinery technomania removes from the picture.

The demonstration makes the future look compressed. A lamp turns on. A motor spins. A laboratory shows that a physical capability exists. The observer imagines the world after adoption and mentally skips the interval in which millions of independent objects and organizations have to be rebuilt around the capability.

Electricity’s history makes that skipped interval impossible to ignore.

Factories are especially revealing. Early electric motors did not instantly produce the productivity gains later associated with electric manufacturing. A factory built around a central steam engine had been designed for mechanical power transmission through shafts, belts, and line systems. Simply replacing the prime mover while retaining the old layout captured only some of electricity’s advantage. The deeper benefit emerged when machines could be powered individually and the factory floor reorganized. Production could be arranged around workflow rather than around proximity to a mechanical shaft.

The technology did not merely substitute for steam.

It changed architecture.

That distinction matters enormously for artificial intelligence. Much current AI adoption resembles an electric motor bolted onto a steam-era factory. A model drafts an email, summarizes a meeting, writes a fragment of code, or fills a form inside a workflow designed around human production of those artifacts. The immediate gain can be real. The larger question is what happens when organizations redesign the workflow around machine capability rather than insert the machine into the old process.

That may produce larger gains. It may also produce new failures nobody can see from the demo.

The electrification analogy is popular in technology writing because it flatters whatever new technology is being compared to electricity. Everyone wants to be the next general-purpose infrastructure. The analogy is useful only if we keep the inconvenient parts.

Electrification took time.

Complementary investment mattered.

Standards mattered.

Organizational redesign mattered.

The value of the technology varied by use.

Adoption was unequal.

Incumbent systems did not vanish on the day a superior technology was demonstrated.

And electricity created new dependencies alongside new freedoms.

The electric grid is a machine no household can build for itself.

That point complicates the standard story of technological empowerment. Electricity enabled individuals to use machines that would have been impossible to power domestically. At the same time it tied homes and businesses to a network whose reliability, pricing, fuel mix, governance, and maintenance were largely outside the user’s control. A person gained local capability by becoming dependent on a larger system.

This is not a contradiction. It is how infrastructure works.

The telephone increases an individual’s reach and depends on a network. Cloud computing gives a small firm access to computing capacity once available only to large organizations and makes the firm dependent on a provider. AI APIs can give a two-person company access to capabilities that would have required a research team and also create dependence on model pricing, terms, uptime, data policies, and provider decisions.

Technological empowerment often arrives as a trade: more power at the edge, more reliance on the center.

That trade becomes politically important when the center is privately controlled.

The early electric industry forced cities and governments to confront questions that would recur with communications networks and digital platforms. Who gets a franchise? What rate is fair? Is duplicate infrastructure wasteful competition or useful redundancy? When is a network a natural monopoly? Which parts of the system should be regulated? Who must be served? Can profitable urban customers effectively subsidize harder-to-reach users? What happens when access to the network becomes necessary for ordinary life?

The engineering does not answer these questions.

There is a persistent fantasy in technology culture that sufficiently good engineering dissolves politics. If the tool becomes cheap enough, efficient enough, distributed enough, or intelligent enough, institutional conflict will supposedly lose relevance. Electrification suggests the opposite. The more essential a technology becomes, the more political its governance can become because exclusion, reliability, pricing, and control now matter to everyone.

A toy can remain outside politics.

Infrastructure cannot.

This is why the phrase “software is eating the world” always deserved a second clause: and the world eventually regulates what it has to eat through.

Electricity also teaches a subtler lesson about measurement. Early technical improvements are often easy to measure at the component level. Generator efficiency can be measured. Lamp output can be measured. Motor performance can be measured. The effect of electrification on a business or economy is harder because it depends on reorganization and complementary investment. The productivity gain may arrive after the technology itself because organizations need time to learn what the technology is for.

This produces a recurring paradox in technological revolutions. Everyone can see the machines, while aggregate statistics may initially look disappointing.

The computers of the late twentieth century generated a similar argument. Firms bought increasingly capable machines, but economists debated why productivity growth did not immediately reflect the apparent revolution. One answer was that organizations had not yet completed the complementary redesign. Another was measurement. Another was that benefits were uneven. The important point for this book is that technological capability and realized social productivity can live on different clocks.

AI will almost certainly do the same.

A benchmark can improve in weeks. Procurement can take months. A profession’s liability rules can take years. Educational systems can take decades. Buildings and power infrastructure can take longer than software release cycles. Demography moves on another clock entirely.

Technomania places them all on the benchmark clock.

That is one reason every new general technology appears to be “moving faster than anything before it.” One layer really is moving faster. Software can be copied globally in seconds. But adoption is not copy speed. An AI model available in 190 countries tonight still enters 190 countries with different languages, wages, regulations, electricity prices, data regimes, labor institutions, firms, schools, hospitals, courts, and political systems tomorrow morning.

The executable travels instantly. The institution does not.

Electricity’s spread also complicates stories about abundance. Once a technology becomes much cheaper per unit of useful output, total use can rise rather than fall. Electrification did not cause households to decide that they had now satisfied their need for mechanical and luminous services. It created appliances, entertainment systems, air conditioning, refrigeration, industrial processes, and new expectations of comfort. Lower effective cost expanded the category.

This is crucial to thinking about automation.

If machine intelligence lowers the cost of a legal draft, software prototype, design variant, translation, tutoring session, or analysis, the world may demand more of those things. The amount of work embodied in each unit can fall while total production rises. Labor effects then depend on whether demand expands faster or slower than labor requirements shrink, which tasks remain complementary to human work, and who captures the gains.

There is no universal answer called automation.

Electricity is also a reminder that efficiency can become expectation. Air conditioning is a luxury until buildings, work schedules, urban settlement patterns, and social norms assume it. Electric lighting extends usable hours and eventually creates environments in which darkness feels like malfunction. Refrigeration changes supply chains until food systems depend on continuous cold.

A technology can begin by giving people an option and end by reorganizing life so the option is no longer optional.

The smartphone is the obvious modern case. A mobile computer once represented convenience. Then authentication systems, boarding passes, banking, maps, messaging, work scheduling, government services, medical portals, and social expectations accumulated around it. Refusing the device remains legally possible and operationally expensive.

AI may follow the same path in professions. The first lawyer to use machine assistance gets an advantage. The tenth uses it because competitors do. Eventually clients expect the turnaround time and a lawyer who abstains may need to justify slower work. The technology has shifted from tool to baseline without any legislature declaring it mandatory.

This is how technical progress becomes social compulsion.

It is also how gains are distributed unevenly. The person who owns the complementary asset can gain more from the new technology than the person who merely encounters it. Electrification made electric machinery valuable, but it also made factories designed to exploit that machinery valuable. It changed land values, industrial organization, and labor demand. A technology’s beneficiaries are not necessarily the people who directly operate it.

The same question applies to AI. If intelligence-like output becomes cheaper, who owns the scarce complement? Proprietary data? Customer relationships? Legal authority? Distribution? Brand trust? Capital? Compute? Energy? Regulatory licenses? Physical infrastructure? A profession’s protected right to act?

The value can migrate toward whatever the model cannot commoditize.

This is why “AI democratizes expertise” can be simultaneously true and incomplete. A person may gain access to medical information, legal reasoning, coding assistance, or design capability that would once have required an expensive professional. But expertise is not only the production of sentences. It includes access to records, tacit context, responsibility, physical intervention, credentialing, trust, and the authority to commit an institution to action.

Electricity democratized mechanical power without democratizing the power company.

The difference between capability and control survives.

The electric era also generated its own mythology of cleanliness and immateriality. An electric light produces no soot in the room. An electric motor can be cleaner at the point of use than an engine burning fuel locally. The pollution does not necessarily disappear. It moves to the generation system.

Technologies repeatedly externalize their visible mess.

Cloud computing made enterprise servers disappear from offices and caused giant data centers to appear elsewhere. Streaming made discs disappear from shelves and built a global delivery infrastructure. AI interfaces remove the sense of a machine entirely: type a sentence, receive an answer. Behind that smooth exchange sit semiconductor fabs, mines, chemical processes, logistics, transmission lines, cooling systems, diesel backup generators, construction crews, and increasingly contested power demand.

The user experiences intelligence as text.

The grid experiences it as load.

This matters because technomania loves interfaces. Interfaces are where friction has been deliberately hidden. The better the product, the less infrastructure the user has to think about. That success becomes a forecasting hazard. We infer that because the experience is effortless, the system is effortless.

Electricity taught the same illusion. The switch is simple because the grid is complicated.

The economic and political consequences of electricity also depended on reliability, not merely capability. A generator that works ninety percent of the time is a remarkable machine and a poor foundation for a hospital. Infrastructure technologies cross a threshold where reliability becomes more important than peak performance.

AI is approaching that threshold in some uses. A model that produces an astonishing answer ninety-five times out of a hundred can be delightful for brainstorming and dangerous for high-stakes autonomous action. As systems move from optional assistant to infrastructural component, the tail matters. Error detection, fallback systems, auditability, and responsibility become part of the product.

This is another stage technomania tends to skip. Demonstration culture rewards the peak case. Infrastructure culture fears the edge case.

Electricity had to become boring before it became civilization.

There is a useful dignity in that boredom. Wires in walls, breakers, meters, transformers, codes, linemen, planned maintenance, emergency crews: these are technologies of trust. They convert a dangerous physical phenomenon into an ordinary expectation. The user does not need to understand alternating current before turning on a lamp because institutions and standards carry some of that understanding on the user’s behalf.

Mature AI may require an analogous layer of boring infrastructure: provenance systems, logging, identity, authorization, monitoring, model evaluation, incident reporting, version control, fallback procedures, insurance, professional standards, and well-defined places where a human remains responsible.

That future is less dramatic than autonomous superintelligence.

It may be more consequential.

There is one final reason electricity belongs near the beginning of this book. It is a case where an apparently excessive technological optimism was, in broad form, justified. Electricity really did become foundational. It really did reshape ordinary life. It really did enable industries that early advocates could not have named.

That success should make us cautious about dismissing current technological enthusiasm merely because the rhetoric is embarrassing.

A person in 1900 saying electricity would enter nearly every home, factory, office, vehicle, hospital, communication network, and computational device would sound like a zealot if the listener focused on the incandescent bulb in front of them.

The zealot might be right about scale.

They could still be wrong about ownership, timing, equality, cost, institutions, labor, and politics.

Magnitude is only one dimension of a forecast.

Technomania takes a correct intuition about magnitude and spends it on conclusions it did not earn.

Radio would make that spending spree irresistible. Electricity had wired cities. Broadcasting seemed to offer something grander: the ability to place a voice, a concert, a sermon, a lesson, or a political speech inside millions of rooms at once. If ignorance depended on access to knowledge, the machine appeared to have found the cure.
