# The Machine That Was Going to Replace Trust

Cryptocurrency arrived with a more radical promise than cheap payments.

Payments were only the visible application. The larger claim was institutional: cryptography, distributed consensus, programmable assets, and open networks could reduce or eliminate the need for trusted intermediaries. Banks, clearinghouses, payment processors, registries, brokers, perhaps even governments occupied their roles partly because strangers needed someone to keep records and enforce rules. A sufficiently well-designed protocol might make some of those functions computational.

The phrase that mattered was not digital money.

It was trustless.

That word did enormous philosophical work.

Trust is expensive. Institutions spend fortunes establishing identity, policing fraud, settling disputes, maintaining records, enforcing contracts, managing collateral, complying with law, and convincing people that balances will still exist tomorrow. If software could replace those functions, the savings would not be incremental. A major layer of civilization would become cheaper.

The claim contained real technical substance. Public-key cryptography really does allow forms of verification that do not require parties to share a secret. Distributed ledgers really can maintain records across participants who do not place ordinary interpersonal trust in one another. Smart contracts really can execute programmed logic against digital assets. Tokens can move globally without the same operational pathway as a bank wire. Stablecoins can create dollar-like instruments that settle on blockchain rails around the clock.

By 2026, the Bank for International Settlements was publishing not dismissals of these systems but detailed work on their macroeconomic implications. BIS research described stablecoins as core components of blockchain-based financial systems and noted that roughly 98 percent of stablecoin value was dollar-denominated. The institution that exists near the center of the central-bank world was studying the possibility that stablecoins could reinforce dollar dominance, accelerate currency substitution in unstable economies, and affect monetary sovereignty.

The joke had become infrastructure.

That did not mean the ideology was correct.

The deeper history of crypto is a demonstration that removing one form of trust can increase demand for another.

A user may not need to trust a central ledger operator to know that a transaction appears on a blockchain. The user may still need to trust wallet software, hardware, smart-contract code, an exchange, a stablecoin issuer, a bridge, a price oracle, a custodian, a front end, an auditor, a governance process, and their own ability not to lose a private key.

Trust did not disappear.

It decomposed.

This is one of the most useful ways to think about technological disruption. An incumbent institution bundles functions. A new technology unbundles them. Some functions become cheap. Others remain scarce and are taken over by new actors.

A bank does not merely maintain a balance sheet. It handles identity, customer service, fraud, legal compliance, payments, credit, safekeeping, dispute resolution, and interfaces with the state. A blockchain can replace some recordkeeping and settlement functions without replacing the whole institution.

The mistake is to identify the most technologically visible function with the entire social role.

We saw this with education: content delivery is not learning.

We saw it with journalism: publishing is not reporting.

We saw it with money: ledger maintenance is not the whole financial system.

We will see it with AI: generating an answer is not the whole profession.

Crypto also exposed the difference between decentralizing a protocol and decentralizing an economy built on top of it. A base network can have distributed validation while users cluster around a small number of exchanges. Stablecoins can move on decentralized ledgers while being issued by centralized entities holding reserve assets. A decentralized-finance protocol can rely on a small set of developers, governance-token holders, or infrastructure providers.

Again: which layer?

The BIS’s 2025 analysis of cryptocurrency and decentralized finance made a point that cuts directly against the strongest novelty claims. DeFi aims to reproduce many functions of traditional finance, but the underlying economic drivers often remain familiar: information asymmetry, leverage, liquidity risk, market inefficiency, maturity transformation, and incentives do not disappear because software changes the wrapper.

Finance has old ghosts.

They learn new APIs.

The crypto boom also supplied a near-perfect example of technomania’s tendency to turn architecture into morality. Decentralization was often described as inherently fairer because no central institution could discriminate. Transparency was described as inherently safer because transactions were visible. Code execution was described as more reliable because rules were deterministic.

Each claim had a real case behind it.

Each also encountered the social layer.

A transparent ledger can coexist with opaque ownership structures. A deterministic smart contract can deterministically execute a bug. An open protocol can be dominated by participants with more capital. A decentralized system can make fraud harder to reverse because no trusted authority has power to undo it. A user can gain sovereignty over assets and gain sovereignty over irreversible mistakes.

Autonomy moves responsibility.

The phrase “be your own bank” sounds liberating until the bank’s boring functions are listed.

Custody is work.

Security is work.

Recovery is work.

Compliance is work.

Risk management is work.

Institutions sometimes exist because people prefer to outsource these burdens.

This is a recurring blind spot in anti-intermediary technology. Intermediaries do not survive only because they are rent-seeking. Some survive because coordination, trust, accountability, and service have value.

The correct question is not “Can we remove the intermediary?”

Which functions does the intermediary perform, which are technologically obsolete, which can be competitive, and which remain necessary?

Crypto forced this distinction through repeated failures. Centralized exchanges failed. Algorithmic stablecoins lost pegs. Bridges were exploited. Smart contracts contained vulnerabilities. Projects marketed as decentralized turned out to contain administrative keys or concentrated governance. None of these failures prove that distributed ledgers are useless. They reveal which institutional functions the architecture had not eliminated.

The failures were information.

Technomania treats failures as public-relations problems because the narrative requires inevitability.

Engineering treats failures as data.

That difference matters.

A serious technological culture should want the strongest claim to fail early if the failure reveals a missing assumption.

Crypto also demonstrates the persistence of state power. Some early rhetoric imagined money escaping national control through mathematics. By 2026, the most economically significant stablecoins were overwhelmingly denominated in dollars. A technology built partly around escaping fiat institutions had created a new rail for the dominant fiat currency.

The direction of disruption can reverse.

A decentralized architecture can reinforce an existing center.

The BIS warned that widespread stablecoin use could produce digital dollarization in emerging markets, allowing people to substitute into dollar-denominated instruments more easily. From the perspective of a user in a country with unstable money, this can look like useful financial access. From the perspective of a central bank trying to conduct monetary policy, it can look like erosion of sovereignty.

The same technology produces opposite evaluations because the observers occupy different institutions.

This is why technological politics cannot be inferred from architecture alone.

Crypto’s energy debate offers another lesson about hidden physicality. Digital assets felt immaterial. Proof-of-work systems made electricity consumption impossible to ignore. A consensus mechanism existed in software and manifested as industrial hardware, power demand, cooling, location decisions, and environmental controversy.

The digital world repeatedly returns to matter.

Clouds require buildings.

Tokens require networks.

Models require chips.

Chips require fabs.

Fabs require water, chemicals, energy, machinery, and geopolitical supply chains.

The more abstract the interface, the easier it is to forget the body.

This matters for AI because intelligence has historically been imagined as a property of minds, not power systems. A model accessed through a browser feels detached from physical scale. Yet the economics of frontier systems increasingly depend on data-center construction, energy procurement, semiconductor capacity, networking, and cooling. AI’s “trustless” equivalent may be “software”: a word that makes the system sound lighter than it is.

Crypto also revealed how quickly financial narratives can outrun technical distinctions. A blockchain, a token, a cryptocurrency, a stablecoin, a decentralized exchange, a non-fungible token, and a smart-contract platform are not the same object. During booms, the category flattens. Success in one part of the space lends legitimacy to another.

This is category contagion.

The same process occurs in AI. A language model’s success at code generation becomes evidence about autonomous scientific discovery. An image model’s success becomes evidence about general reasoning. A benchmark win becomes evidence about labor substitution. The category “AI” is large enough to transport optimism between systems that share branding more than economics.

Technomania likes umbrella nouns.

Umbrella nouns let evidence travel too far.

Crypto’s strongest contribution to this book may be the distinction between verification and trust.

Technology can make some facts cheaply verifiable. A cryptographic signature can establish that a key authorized a transaction. A public ledger can establish that a transaction was recorded according to protocol rules. Neither fact tells you whether the human behind the key understood the transaction, was coerced, was defrauded, or deserves legal relief.

Verification answers narrower questions than trust.

AI creates a similar temptation. A model can produce a prediction with impressive measured accuracy. That does not establish that the institution using it deserves trust. The institution may lack appeal procedures. The training data may encode an inappropriate target. The model may be deployed outside the population on which it was evaluated. The decision may involve values no accuracy metric captures.

A reliable component can sit inside an illegitimate system.

Crypto also teaches something about irreversibility. Traditional institutions are sometimes inefficient because they preserve the capacity to reverse error. Chargebacks, court orders, account freezes, fraud departments, bankruptcy, and human discretion create friction. They can also rescue people from mistakes.

A protocol that makes settlement final has improved one property by weakening another.

Finality is valuable.

Reversibility is valuable.

The right balance depends on the transaction.

Technomania often interprets friction as pure waste. Mature institutions know some friction is insurance.

We encountered this with telegraphy, where faster communication eventually required deliberate pauses and controls. We encounter it in finance, where settlement speed, fraud prevention, compliance, and dispute resolution trade against one another. We will encounter it with autonomous AI, where the ability to act quickly may be less valuable than the ability to stop, inspect, and reverse.

A system that can do something instantly needs a stronger answer to who can undo it.

Crypto also made “code is law” famous as an aspiration and then forced communities to confront situations where social consensus overrode or contested code outcomes. That conflict is not embarrassing. It is clarifying.

Law is not merely executable instruction.

Law contains interpretation, precedent, legitimacy, discretion, remedies, and procedures for changing the rules. Code can implement parts of a legal or economic arrangement with extraordinary precision. Precision does not resolve disagreement about what the arrangement should mean after an unforeseen event.

The unexpected case is where institutions reveal themselves.

This is one reason a fully automated society remains more difficult than a fully automated process. Processes can be bounded. Societies generate exceptions.

Crypto did not eliminate trust.

It made the components of trust visible by attempting to engineer around them.

That is a serious achievement even if the strongest ideology fails. Technologies often teach us about institutions by breaking them into parts.

The blockchain asks: which parts of a ledger require a trusted operator?

AI asks: which parts of cognitive work require a human producer?

The answer in both cases is likely to be less than incumbents claim and more than revolutionaries hope.

The story then returns to an object everyone already carries. The smartphone fused the internet’s network, social media’s graph, the camera’s sensor, GPS’s map, the payment terminal, the workplace, and the entertainment system into a personal device.

The personal computer had put computation on the desk.

The smartphone put the institution in the pocket.
