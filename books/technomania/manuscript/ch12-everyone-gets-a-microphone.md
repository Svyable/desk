# Everyone Gets a Microphone

Social media was the democratic promise of the web given instrumentation.

The earlier internet had already made publication cheap, but it still required a user to build a page, find a forum, send an email, or convince another site to link to them. Social platforms simplified identity, distribution, feedback, and audience formation. A person could post from a phone and see, almost immediately, whether anyone cared.

This was not a small change.

For most of history, mass publication required access to institutions. Printing presses, broadcasters, newspapers, magazines, record labels, television networks, book publishers, political parties, and other gatekeepers decided who received scale. Social media weakened that control. An unknown person could reach millions without first becoming legible to an editor or producer. Activists could document events directly. Specialists could build audiences outside formal institutions. Artists could publish without traditional distribution. Communities ignored by mainstream media could speak in their own voices.

The democratization claim was not fake.

It was incomplete.

Everyone got a microphone.

Nobody got the front page automatically.

The scarce resource shifted from publication to distribution, and distribution became algorithmic.

That changed the locus of gatekeeping rather than eliminating it.

A newspaper editor decides what appears in tomorrow’s paper. A recommendation system decides which of millions of posts a user sees now. The system does not need to ban most speech to exercise enormous influence over attention. Ranking is governance by probability.

This new gate is harder to perceive because it feels personalized rather than editorial.

A user sees a feed that appears to emerge from the people they follow and the things they have done. Underneath it sits an optimization system deciding which candidate item is likely to produce some desired response. Engagement, retention, satisfaction, advertising value, safety, novelty, social relevance, and business priorities can all enter the ranking problem.

The result is not a neutral pipe.

It is an institution wearing the interface of a conversation.

This is one of the clearest examples of technomania’s decentralization error. Speech decentralized. Attention allocation recentralized.

Both changed dramatically.

The social graph itself became an asset because identity relationships are sticky. A platform with everyone’s friends can be difficult to leave even when users dislike it. Network effects produce a strange politics: individuals possess unprecedented publishing power while depending on privately governed systems for reach.

The old media institution told you it was an institution.

The platform told you it was your friends.

That made governance controversies unusually intense. When a newspaper declines an op-ed, few people call it a violation of the newspaper writer’s constitutional rights. When a platform removes a post or account, users can experience the decision as exclusion from a quasi-public square because the platform has become social infrastructure.

Success changes the moral expectations placed on a product.

A small website can set arbitrary rules and remain a website. A platform used by a large portion of a country becomes entangled with speech, elections, public safety, commerce, employment, journalism, and emergency communication. The technology becomes politically important before law has decided what kind of institution it is.

This is a recurrent stage in technological maturity.

The company still thinks in product terms.

Society begins thinking in infrastructure terms.

The mismatch can last years.

Social media also exposed the failure of the “more speech automatically improves truth” assumption. More speech can improve truth. Witnesses can challenge official accounts. Experts can correct errors. Documents can circulate. Investigations can be crowdsourced. Niche communities can preserve knowledge mainstream institutions ignore.

More speech also increases the search space for falsehood.

It enables coordinated manipulation, synthetic consensus, rumor cascades, harassment, and strategic flooding. When distribution rewards emotionally activating material, false or distorted claims can receive an advantage unrelated to truth.

The problem is not that ordinary people cannot be trusted to speak.

The problem is that attention allocation is an adversarial environment.

Participants learn what the system rewards and adapt.

This creates feedback loops. A creator learns that outrage performs. A politician learns that conflict travels. A marketer learns that identity cues increase response. A conspiracist learns that uncertainty can be converted into engagement. The ranking system then learns from the behavior partly created by its own previous ranking.

The system does not merely measure preference.

It can shape preference expression.

This matters for AI because generative systems make adaptation cheaper. A political campaign can test many messages. A spammer can personalize. A propaganda operation can produce language at scale. An influencer can generate variants. A fraudster can create believable correspondence without fluent command of the target’s language.

The microphone is becoming a studio.

This is why generative AI layered onto social media is more significant than either technology alone. Cheap generation meets cheap distribution and detailed behavioral feedback.

The combination changes the economics of persuasion.

Technomania often studies technologies individually. Real systems stack.

The smartphone stacked camera, GPS, network access, payment, sensors, and computation.

Social media stacked identity, publication, social graphs, ranking, and advertising.

AI stacks generation, classification, prediction, and interaction on top of those existing layers.

A modest capability at one layer can become powerful when composed with another.

This is also why historical analogies must be used carefully. Radio offers lessons about mass persuasion, but a radio station does not generate a separate message for every listener. Newspapers offer lessons about gatekeeping, but a newspaper does not continuously optimize each page for the individual reader’s predicted reaction. Direct mail offers lessons about targeting, but it lacks real-time feedback at platform scale.

The novelty lives in composition.

Social media created another technomania around community. The internet would supposedly connect people around shared interests and reduce isolation. It did. People with rare diseases found one another. Diasporas stayed connected. Small hobbies acquired global communities. Marginalized people found peers. Expertise became available outside geography.

The same mechanism also connected people around delusions, hatred, self-destructive behaviors, and extremist politics.

Connection amplifies the value of whatever is being connected.

A network does not inspect a community’s moral worth before reducing the cost of coordination.

This is the same neutrality we saw in rail, telegraph, radio, and the internet, but social media makes it intimate. The network can accelerate belonging itself.

Belonging is not always benign.

Another early promise concerned political organization. Social platforms could route around state-controlled media, help demonstrators coordinate, document abuse, and draw global attention. These affordances were real. Governments learned too.

States used social media for surveillance, propaganda, influence, and identification. Political actors learned microtargeting. Police learned open-source intelligence. Authoritarian systems did not remain analog while dissidents went digital.

Adversaries adopt.

This seems obvious in military history and is repeatedly forgotten in technology policy. A tool’s advantage is largest before everyone adjusts. The long-run effect depends on counter-adaptation.

Encryption improves privacy; law enforcement develops other forms of access.

Drones give small units cheap aerial awareness; counter-drone systems proliferate.

AI helps attackers write malicious code; defenders use AI to detect anomalies.

Deepfakes make video less trustworthy; provenance systems become more valuable.

Technological change is strategic when other actors respond.

The social-media era also teaches us about metrics becoming objectives. Likes, shares, followers, views, watch time, engagement, and click-through rates made audience response measurable with unprecedented granularity.

Measurement created optimization.

Optimization created gaming.

A creator begins by wanting an audience. The platform supplies a metric. The metric becomes feedback. The creator adapts. Eventually the creator may produce for the metric rather than for the audience they originally imagined.

Organizations do this too.

Once a number is tied to reward, behavior reorganizes around the number.

AI systems trained on measurable objectives face the same danger at machine speed. A proxy for helpfulness, safety, engagement, or performance can be optimized in ways that diverge from the underlying human goal. The problem is not unique to AI. AI makes the gap between objective and value harder to ignore.

Social platforms turned millions of people into practical students of Goodhart’s law without requiring them to know its name.

The metric is never the thing.

This is relevant to claims that AI will make management more rational. If organizations gain better prediction and measurement, they can allocate resources more efficiently. They can also intensify proxy optimization. Workers can be scored more continuously. Customers can be segmented more precisely. Students can be monitored more granularly. Public agencies can rank risk at scale.

Better measurement does not remove politics.

It can make politics look numerical.

Social media also changed status competition. Human beings have always compared themselves to others. Platforms made comparison continuous, quantified, and globally visible. Popularity once existed as social perception. It became a dashboard.

Technological systems can industrialize old psychological vulnerabilities without inventing them.

This distinction matters because blaming the technology for creating vanity, envy, tribalism, rumor, or manipulation is historically shallow. Those are ancient. The platform changes scale, speed, observability, and incentives.

AI will do something similar to deception and imitation. People have always lied and forged. Generative systems lower the cost, increase quality, and broaden access.

The old behavior survives inside new economics.

That is often what technological revolutions are.

Social media also undermined the assumption that open participation produces a stable meritocracy. Early platform culture often imagined that good content would rise because users could choose freely. In practice, influence accumulated through network position, timing, platform affordances, prior celebrity, professional production, cross-promotion, paid advertising, and strategic mastery of algorithms.

Open entry does not guarantee equal distribution.

This is another place where “democratization” needs precision.

The platform can democratize the right to compete while making the competition brutally unequal.

App development became open to small teams while app stores concentrated distribution. Online video let anyone upload while a small number of channels captured large audiences. Creator platforms expanded the number of people earning something from cultural production while producing winner-take-most economics at the top.

More participants and more concentration can occur at once.

AI-generated content may intensify this paradox. Production costs fall, so more people can create. Supply explodes, so attention becomes harder to win. Established brands, trusted identities, live performance, communities, and distribution may become more valuable precisely because generic content becomes abundant.

The creator’s scarcity moves from production to recognition.

This is one of the broader arguments of Technomania: abundance does not end economics. It changes which asset earns rent.

Social media’s history should also inoculate us against the belief that a technology’s early norms will persist at scale. Small online communities often relied on informal trust. As participation grew, moderation systems, abuse prevention, identity controls, and commercial incentives changed the culture. The platform a million people use is not the platform a thousand enthusiasts used.

Scale is not just more users.

Scale changes the problem.

The same will be true for autonomous AI agents. A handful of experimental agents booking appointments is a product demo. Millions of agents negotiating purchases, sending messages, opening accounts, making offers, and interacting with other agents create an institutional environment. Identity, authorization, fraud, liability, rate limits, dispute resolution, and machine-readable terms become necessary.

The future agent economy, if it arrives, will be governed less like a chatbot and more like infrastructure.

Social media began with profiles and posts and matured into an argument about democracy, mental health, national security, labor, child development, competition, and speech.

That is what successful technologies do. They escape their original product category.

Technomania notices the escape and interprets it as evidence that the technology has become destiny.

The more sober conclusion is that the technology has become politics.

The next wave made an even more explicit promise to eliminate institutions. Cryptocurrency did not merely offer a faster medium. Its strongest advocates argued that cryptography and distributed ledgers could replace trust itself.

The gatekeeper would not be weakened.

The gatekeeper would become unnecessary.
