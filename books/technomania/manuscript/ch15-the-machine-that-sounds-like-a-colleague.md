# The Machine That Sounds Like a Colleague

Artificial intelligence became culturally explosive when it stopped looking like a specialist instrument and started answering in ordinary language.

Machine learning had already been embedded in search, advertising, fraud detection, logistics, recommendation, image recognition, speech recognition, translation, and industrial systems. Those applications mattered economically, but they mostly sat behind interfaces designed by other people. Generative models changed the experience. A user could type a request and receive prose, code, images, summaries, plans, explanations, classifications, and transformations directly.

The interface made capability legible.

That is why the current AI boom feels different even to people who had been living inside machine-learning systems for years. A recommendation engine is powerful and impersonal. A language model replies.

Conversation produces a dangerous psychological compression. The user supplies intent in the form humans use with one another and the machine produces an answer in the form humans associate with thought. This can make several layers appear fused: linguistic competence, factual knowledge, reasoning, judgment, agency, and understanding.

Some of the fusion is deserved. A system that can use language to solve unfamiliar problems is doing something more general than a rigid lookup table. Some of it is projection. Fluency can exceed reliability. A model can produce a useful analysis and invent a citation in the same register.

The interface has no facial expression for uncertainty.

That matters because technomania loves smooth interfaces. The better the system hides its machinery, the easier it is to treat output as a natural property rather than the result of training data, model architecture, prompting, retrieval, tool access, system instructions, post-training, policy constraints, and infrastructure.

The response appears in one box.

The causal stack does not.

Current evidence is strong enough that dismissal is no longer serious. In September 2026, the general manager of the Bank for International Settlements summarized task-level research showing generative-AI productivity gains ranging roughly from 10 to 65 percent and time savings on the order of 20 to 50 percent in studied tasks, particularly in coding, consulting-like work, and professional writing. These are not claims about the entire economy. They are evidence that the technology can materially alter the cost of specific cognitive work.

That distinction is essential.

Task productivity is not aggregate productivity.

The BIS itself makes the gap explicit. The open question is how micro-level gains translate into economy-wide total-factor productivity once adoption frictions, capital reallocation, labor shifts, competition, and demand are included. Estimates vary widely.

This is the exact point where technomania usually accelerates past the evidence.

A twenty-percent improvement in a task becomes a twenty-percent improvement in a job.

A twenty-percent improvement in a job becomes a twenty-percent improvement in a firm.

A twenty-percent improvement in a firm becomes a macroeconomic growth rate.

Each arrow hides a different system.

A worker may save time and be assigned more work. A firm may gain productivity and lower prices. Competitors may match the gain and pass it to customers. Demand may expand. Management may fail to redesign workflows. Errors may create review costs. The model may perform best on tasks that occupy only a small share of total labor. Adoption may be limited by data, regulation, or organizational politics.

No one percentage survives automatically through all those layers.

This does not reduce the significance of the task result. It tells us what has actually been measured.

The labor evidence is similarly easy to inflate. The International Labour Organization’s 2025 global index estimated that one in four workers was in an occupation with some degree of exposure to generative AI. Its conclusion was not that one in four workers would lose their jobs. Because occupations contain heterogeneous tasks and human input remains necessary in many of them, the ILO judged transformation more likely than outright redundancy for most exposed work.

Exposure is not displacement.

This seems obvious and is repeatedly ignored because displacement makes a cleaner headline.

An occupation can be highly exposed because many of its tasks can be assisted. The final employment effect depends on how much output demand expands, how complementary human work remains, whether regulations require human responsibility, how wages adjust, and whether the technology changes the number of firms or services the market supports.

The IMF’s earlier estimate that roughly forty percent of global employment was exposed to AI made the same distinction. Advanced economies had more exposure because more work involved cognitive tasks that AI could affect, but exposure included both complementarity and substitution. A technology can threaten some workers precisely where it helps others become more productive.

The distribution matters.

Several task studies have found larger gains for less experienced workers. If that pattern generalizes, AI could compress some skill gaps. The experienced worker’s tacit knowledge may partly be captured in systems that help novices perform closer to the expert frontier.

That sounds equalizing.

It can also erode the training ladder.

If junior workers become productive because a model supplies patterns previously learned through years of work, firms may need fewer juniors. Then fewer people accumulate the experience required to become senior. The short-term complement can weaken the long-term pipeline.

This is one of the book’s recurring institutional questions: which apparently inefficient tasks are actually reproductive tasks that create future capability?

Apprenticeships are expensive because novices are learning.

Automating the novice work can automate the apprenticeship accidentally.

Law, medicine, accounting, journalism, engineering, and software all contain versions of this problem. The routine case is not merely revenue. It is practice.

Technomania sees the task’s current output and asks whether a machine can produce it.

Institutions have to ask what else the task was doing.

AI also changes the economics of expertise by making something expertise-like abundant. A user can ask a model for a legal explanation, a medical differential, a programming approach, a financial model, or an academic summary at negligible marginal cost compared with hiring a specialist.

The obvious conclusion is that expertise gets cheaper.

The less obvious conclusion is that authority may get more expensive.

Who can sign the filing?

Who carries malpractice liability?

Who has access to the complete record?

Who can physically intervene?

Who can certify that the system was used appropriately?

Who is trusted when the recommendation is contested?

The more abundant analysis becomes, the more valuable accountable decision rights can become.

This is the same scarcity migration we saw in radio and the internet. Cheap content creates expensive attention. Cheap transactions create expensive trust. Cheap analysis can create expensive accountability.

The AI boom is also physical in a way its interface hides. BIS reports in 2026 described an investment surge in data centers, specialized semiconductor manufacturing, cloud infrastructure, and hardware large enough to affect global macroeconomic conditions. Its July bulletin described the productivity payoff as potentially large but uncertain and uneven. Its September speech noted that the five largest technology companies were expected to spend more than one trillion dollars on AI-related capital expenditure across 2025 and 2026, with industry projections reaching several trillion dollars by 2030.

A chat window has become a construction boom.

This is where the railway analogy becomes useful without becoming prophecy. Firms are racing to secure capacity in an environment where the eventual distribution of profits is uncertain. The BIS’s 2026 working paper on the AI investment race explicitly compares the current build-out with earlier canal, railway, and dot-com booms. Its model describes a winner-take-most contest in which firms may overcommit capital relative to social returns, particularly when debt and cross-holdings increase financial fragility.

The comparison does not tell us that a crash will happen.

It tells us that real technology and excessive investment are compatible.

That is the most important historical lesson investors repeatedly refuse to remember.

A data center can be useful and overpriced.

A model can be transformative and commoditized.

A company can build essential infrastructure and fail to earn the returns assumed when it financed the build.

A society can inherit valuable capacity from a capital cycle that destroyed equity.

The words bubble and revolution are not antonyms.

AI also revives the atomic-age temptation to infer system economics from a component cost curve. Inference costs can fall sharply. Model capability per dollar can improve. Hardware can become more efficient. Smaller models can perform tasks once requiring larger ones.

The surrounding system still has costs.

Data integration. Security. Evaluation. Human review. Power. Networking. Identity. Audit. Workflow redesign. Legal risk. Training. Change management. Monitoring. Incident response.

A token can be cheap and a decision expensive.

The distinction becomes most obvious in high-stakes work. A hospital does not benefit from an inexpensive diagnosis if false outputs create more review burden than they save. A bank does not benefit from cheap fraud analysis if the model cannot be audited or produces discriminatory false positives. A court cannot delegate legitimacy to a system merely because its predictions are statistically useful.

The last mile is institutional.

Artificial intelligence is also the first technology in this book that can participate directly in its own technology narrative at mass scale. Models can write investment memos about AI, produce software for AI companies, summarize AI papers, generate marketing about AI products, and advise organizations on AI adoption.

The technology can help amplify the discourse evaluating the technology.

That recursive feature is culturally novel even if its economic effects are uncertain.

It creates a risk of synthetic consensus. If organizations use similar models trained on overlapping corpora to produce strategy documents, market research, job descriptions, policies, and commentary, the world can begin to hear its own statistical average reflected back with high confidence.

This is not superintelligence.

It is institutional autocorrelation.

The danger is not that the model secretly controls society. It is that cheap plausible language lowers the cost of repeating assumptions until repetition looks like evidence.

This is another form of abundance requiring a new scarcity: independent judgment.

AI’s strongest advocates sometimes argue that recursive model improvement could eventually produce explosive growth. The BIS’s 2026 macroeconomic discussion treats transformative AI as one scenario among several, not an established forecast. Its September speech contrasted bounded productivity improvements with a scenario of self-reinforcing automation and another in which demand becomes a bottleneck as income shifts away from labor.

That is the appropriate epistemic posture.

Scenarios are not predictions.

A scenario asks what follows if a premise becomes true.

Technomania quietly changes the grammar from if to when.

The difference can move trillions of dollars.

The possibility of highly autonomous systems deserves serious preparation because the downside of being surprised can be large. That preparation does not require pretending the strongest capability forecasts are settled.

Uncertainty is not a reason for inaction.

It is a reason for reversible action.

Build evaluation systems before granting broad authority.

Separate recommendation from execution.

Log actions.

Require explicit permissions.

Design rollback.

Test on bounded domains.

Measure error under real distribution shift.

These are less glamorous than arguments about machine consciousness and more useful for the systems being deployed now.

The consciousness question belongs in philosophy and cognitive science. It is also a magnet for technomania because it turns performance into ontology.

A machine writes a poem; therefore it understands beauty.

A machine says it is afraid; therefore it feels fear.

A machine reasons through a puzzle; therefore it possesses humanlike consciousness.

Or the reverse: the machine is “just statistics,” therefore none of its capability matters.

Both arguments jump categories.

Behavioral capability can be studied without resolving subjective experience. Institutions have to govern what systems can do before philosophers agree on what systems are.

A forklift does not need a theory of consciousness before a warehouse sets safety rules.

AI does require more subtle governance because its behavior is less bounded, but the principle survives.

Technomania also produces a temporal distortion. Capability improvements are visible monthly. Institutions adapt yearly. Infrastructure adapts over longer periods. Demography changes slower still. Observers compare the fastest curve with the slowest system and conclude that society is permanently behind.

Sometimes it is.

Sometimes the slower pace is a feature.

A court should not change precedent at software-release speed. A medical standard should not update merely because a vendor shipped a new model. A school curriculum cannot reorganize every quarter. Democratic legitimacy requires deliberation that looks inefficient beside computation.

Speed is not the universal objective.

The telegraph already taught us that institutions sometimes need to put delay back into fast systems.

AI will make this more important. An autonomous system can act at machine speed across markets, networks, communications, and software. Human oversight becomes meaningless if the human sees the event only after thousands of actions have occurred.

The architecture of control must therefore change before autonomy scales.

“Human in the loop” is not a magic phrase if the loop moves faster than the human.

The future of AI will likely contain both substitution and complementarity, both infrastructure and bubble behavior, both democratization and concentration. Open models will coexist with giant data centers. Small firms will gain capabilities and become dependent on larger providers. Expertise will become more accessible and authority more contested. Some jobs will disappear. Others will grow. New tasks will be invented partly because machines made old tasks cheap.

That is not a refusal to predict.

It is a prediction that history will remain multi-layered.

The strongest claim of technomania is that a sufficiently powerful technology collapses those layers. Technical progress becomes economic progress, then institutional progress, then moral progress, along one accelerating curve.

Nothing in the evidence requires that conclusion.

Artificial intelligence may be one of the most important technologies of the century.

That sentence is compatible with almost every caution in this book.

The harder work begins after we accept it.

If a technology really is powerful, then the question is not whether to believe in it.

The question is how not to believe too much.
