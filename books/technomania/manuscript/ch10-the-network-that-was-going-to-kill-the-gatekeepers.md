# The Network That Was Going to Kill the Gatekeepers

The commercial internet arrived with a useful historical amnesia.

By the time ordinary users began encountering the web in the 1990s, the network could feel like an emergent property of personal computers: machines had simply discovered one another. The institutional genealogy was less romantic. The National Science Foundation’s own account traces the commercial internet through government-funded networking, DARPA’s early support for ARPANET, the development of TCP/IP, NSFNET, university networks, supercomputing centers, and a deliberate transition toward commercial infrastructure. NSFNET began in 1986 by linking supercomputer centers and academic networks. It grew from around two thousand connected computers to more than two million by 1993. As commercial services expanded, NSF retired its backbone in 1995.

The internet that would supposedly dissolve institutions was born from institutions building standards, funding infrastructure, and deciding how commercialization should happen.

This is not an argument against the private sector. Commercial providers scaled access in ways a research backbone was never designed to do. Browsers, search engines, hosting companies, e-commerce firms, content networks, software vendors, device makers, telecom carriers, and millions of entrepreneurs turned the network into something much larger than its early builders could have administered.

The point is that “decentralized” is not a creation story.

Networks require prior agreements.

DARPA’s history of ARPANET emphasizes the problem clearly. Computers from different manufacturers had to communicate. Packet switching had to route information without depending on one fixed circuit. Common protocols had to be developed so incompatible machines could internetwork. A network of networks becomes possible because participants agree on enough structure to disagree everywhere else.

Standards are the quiet constitution of decentralization.

This should make us suspicious whenever a new technology is described as escaping institutions merely because it lacks one obvious center. The absence of a central operator does not imply the absence of governance. Protocol design, client software, identity, naming, hardware supply, network access, security practices, and standards bodies can all distribute power in different ways.

The early web made the case for liberation irresistible. Publishing a page was radically cheaper than printing and distributing a magazine. A small merchant could theoretically reach customers outside a local market. An amateur could publish without convincing an editor. A researcher could share documents globally. Communities could form around interests too rare to support a local club. Email collapsed correspondence time. Search, once it improved, made information discoverable at scales libraries could not replicate through physical browsing.

Old gatekeepers really did weaken.

That fact made the next mistake plausible: gatekeeping itself would disappear.

It did not.

It migrated.

When publication becomes cheap, discovery becomes scarce.

When storefront creation becomes cheap, customer acquisition becomes scarce.

When communication becomes cheap, attention becomes scarce.

When copying becomes cheap, trusted provenance becomes scarce.

When everyone can reach everyone, filtering becomes infrastructure.

The firms that learned to control these complements became some of the most powerful institutions in the world.

Search engines did not re-create the newspaper editor. They created a different gate. Social platforms did not re-create the broadcast network. They created recommendation systems whose power came from ranking rather than ownership of every piece of content. App stores did not re-create software retailers. They became distribution chokepoints attached to operating systems. Cloud platforms did not re-create corporate mainframes exactly. They centralized enormous amounts of computing while selling that centralization as elastic access.

This is one of technomania’s recurring surprises: technical decentralization can create economic centralization.

The mechanism is not mysterious. Networks often reward scale. A search engine improves with data, infrastructure, engineering investment, and user habit. A marketplace becomes more useful as buyers and sellers gather. A social platform becomes valuable when a user’s friends are already there. A cloud provider can spread fixed costs across enormous demand. A device ecosystem attracts developers because users exist and attracts users because applications exist.

The open protocol can sit underneath a concentrated market.

This is why the political argument “the internet is decentralized” became less useful as the web matured. At the packet-routing layer, the statement captures something real. At the attention layer, a small number of systems can dominate. At the hosting layer, concentration can appear again. At the content-production layer, millions of independent creators can flourish. Different layers have different geometries.

The same mistake now appears in arguments about AI.

Open-weight models can decentralize inference and experimentation. Frontier training can remain concentrated because compute, talent, capital, data-center construction, and semiconductor access are expensive. Small firms can build products on top of APIs while becoming dependent on a handful of providers. Model capability can diffuse even as infrastructure concentrates.

The right question is never “Is AI centralized?”

Which layer?

The internet also promised the death of distance, again.

A website does not care whether the reader is across town or across an ocean. Email transmission cost is nearly indifferent to physical separation. Remote work can move certain kinds of labor outside offices. Online commerce can connect niche sellers to global demand.

Yet geography survived in mutated form.

Data centers cluster around power, fiber, land, tax regimes, and cooling. Technology firms cluster around labor markets, capital, universities, and professional networks. Fulfillment centers sit where logistics networks make sense. Cross-border commerce encounters customs and regulation. Remote workers care about time zones. Streaming rights stop at national boundaries. Governments can block networks. Undersea cables land at specific shores.

The internet made some distances cheap and made other locations strategic.

A person can video-call across the world while living in a neighborhood without affordable housing near a high-wage technology cluster.

Distance does not have one price.

The network also carried an implicit theory of truth. More information would improve decisions because ignorance was partly a distribution problem. This was reasonable. Restricted access to information had protected institutions from scrutiny. Online publication enabled whistleblowers, independent researchers, activists, journalists, and citizens to circulate evidence quickly. Search made obscure documents findable. Wikipedia demonstrated that distributed volunteers could build an extraordinary reference work. Open-source communities built software used across the world.

Information abundance did real democratic work.

It also removed scarcity faster than it removed uncertainty.

The human mind did not acquire proportional capacity to evaluate sources. Institutions did not automatically become more trusted. False information acquired the same replication advantage as true information. Cheap publication made it easier for experts to speak and easier for people pretending to be experts to do the same. Search made evidence accessible and made motivated reasoning more efficient: almost anyone could find a page supporting what they already wanted to believe.

The problem moved from access to evaluation.

This transition is now happening again with generative AI. Search asks the user to navigate sources. A conversational model can compress them into a single answer. That removes effort and can remove visible disagreement. The interface becomes smoother exactly where epistemic caution might require friction.

The solution is not necessarily to make the tool worse. It is to build provenance and uncertainty into the surrounding system.

The history of the web suggests this will happen after avoidable damage rather than before it.

Technologies are often governed in retrospect because the most important failure modes appear only at scale.

Spam is a useful example. Email’s low marginal transmission cost was a triumph until senders could cheaply externalize the cost of filtering onto recipients. A message that costs essentially nothing to send can be economically rational even if almost everyone ignores it. The result is a tragedy of cheap communication.

Cheap generation creates a similar structure.

If producing a marketing email, synthetic review, job application, legal threat, social-media comment, fake image, or personalized scam costs almost nothing, the sender can flood systems whose recipients still have finite attention. The value of defenses rises because the attack cost falls.

Abundance can be adversarial.

This is a general law worth taking seriously. When a productive capability becomes cheap, malicious or low-quality uses become cheap too. The cost of verification can become a tax on everyone else.

The internet also taught us that intermediate institutions can disappear for good reasons and be missed for reasons nobody priced.

Local newspapers lost advertising revenue as classified ads and commercial attention moved online. Readers gained enormous information access. Many communities lost local reporting capacity. The lost institution had bundled things together: profitable advertising supported costly civic reporting. The internet unbundled the package more efficiently than it replaced every cross-subsidy.

This is another recurring effect of technological disruption. An incumbent institution may be inefficient because it is doing several jobs at once.

When a new technology attacks the profitable job, the socially valuable but unprofitable job can die with it.

AI may do this inside professions. Junior work can look inefficient when measured only as output production. Drafting, research, document review, and routine analysis may also be the training ground through which novices become senior professionals. If automation removes the apprenticeship tasks, the profession may need a new way to produce expertise.

The immediate cost saving can create a long-term capability gap.

A law firm that no longer needs junior associates to do first-pass research may need fewer associates now and still need experienced partners later. A radiology department that automates routine reads still has to train people to handle difficult cases and supervise systems. A newsroom that automates commodity reporting still has to develop reporters capable of investigation.

Technomania counts the eliminated task.

Institutions have to count the developmental function hidden inside it.

The commercial internet’s most dramatic economic mistake was not believing the network would matter. It was believing that traffic, eyeballs, users, and growth could substitute indefinitely for business models.

The network was revolutionary.

Many companies built on the network were still bad investments.

This distinction is so important that it deserves its own chapter, because the dot-com crash is now remembered as evidence of hype even though the underlying technological thesis won more completely than many boom-era promoters dared to imagine.

That is what makes it the perfect Technomania case.

The bubble was real.

So was the future.
