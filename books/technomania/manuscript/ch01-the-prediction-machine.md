# The Prediction Machine

In October 1997, Nathan Myhrvold wrote a piece for The New Yorker called “The Dawn of Technomania.” It is useful now because it arrived before the vocabulary had hardened. The web was real enough to have changed offices and universities, young enough that most households still reached it through a modem, and lucrative enough that everyone had begun predicting which established institutions it would erase. Myhrvold’s word was not a diagnosis of insanity. It described a social weather system. People close to the machinery could see genuine acceleration. Investors could see fortunes forming around it. Reporters could see old hierarchies becoming interestingly unstable. Skeptics could see a sales pitch getting ahead of installation crews. All of them were looking at the same thing.

That last detail matters. Technomania is easiest to misunderstand when it is treated as simple gullibility. Most technological manias do not begin with a fake machine. They begin with a machine that works.

The railway really did collapse travel times. The telegraph really did separate communication from physical transport. Electric power really did reorganize the factory and the city. Radio really did put a live voice into distant homes. Nuclear fission really did release energy at a density previously available only in chemical imagination. Rockets really did cross the atmosphere. Personal computers really did put programmable machines on desks. Packet-switched networks really did allow incompatible computers to communicate. Smartphones really did compress cameras, maps, mail, newspapers, recording studios, ticket offices, bank counters, encyclopedias, and a respectable fraction of the twentieth-century office into something that fits in a pocket. Machine-learning systems really can perform classes of cognitive work that, not long ago, were assumed to require a person.

A book about technomania therefore has to resist an easy pleasure: the pleasure of finding old predictions that sound ridiculous and laughing at them from the future. That is too cheap. A prediction can fail in its total form while containing an accurate perception of what has changed. The interesting error is usually not “this machine matters.” The interesting error is the next sentence.

The railroad matters, therefore distance is ending. Radio matters, therefore education will become universally available and ignorance will retreat. Atomic energy matters, therefore electricity will become almost free. Automation matters, therefore labor will become optional—or unemployable. The internet matters, therefore gatekeepers are finished. Social media matters, therefore speech is democratized. Cryptocurrency matters, therefore trust and intermediaries are obsolete. Artificial intelligence matters, therefore expertise, office work, software, perhaps creativity itself have entered their terminal phase.

Each leap begins with evidence. Each leap then smuggles in a theory of society.

That is the subject here.

I do not mean that optimism is foolish. Pessimism has its own stupidities. A culture that had refused every technology because someone might lose a job or misuse a tool would still be arguing about whether movable type was too dangerous for the clergy. Nor is technological enthusiasm itself a pathology. Enthusiasm is often the emotionally correct response to a real extension of human capability. There are moments when a laboratory result, a rocket launch, a new network, or a machine doing something everyone had privately classified as impossible really does justify exhilaration.

Technomania begins later, when capability becomes destiny.

The distinction sounds philosophical until money arrives. Then it becomes practical. Railways are laid with capital. Reactors are financed with assumptions about future electricity demand and construction costs. Fiber is buried because someone has projected traffic. Data centers are built because expected inference demand justifies transformers, substations, debt, land, cooling water, and contracts for power that may not be needed on the original schedule. The prediction eventually becomes steel, concrete, regulation, debt, zoning, and stranded equipment. A bad forecast about a poem is harmless. A bad forecast about infrastructure can survive as a balance-sheet item for decades.

The current AI boom makes the pattern unusually visible because it has compressed an old sequence. In September 2026, the head of the Bank for International Settlements warned that AI’s investment boom was creating new financial-stability risks even while acknowledging real productivity gains in particular tasks. The BIS estimate cited in contemporary reporting put investment by the five largest technology firms above one trillion dollars across 2025 and 2026, with broader AI investment potentially running into trillions by 2030. Task-level productivity effects are not imaginary; neither is the financing. Those facts can coexist. A technology can work and a capital cycle around it can overshoot. The railway already taught this. So did fiber-optic cable.

A mania is not disproved by usefulness.

That sentence is the load-bearing beam of this book.

Consider one of the most famous technological promises of the twentieth century: electricity “too cheap to meter.” Lewis Strauss, chairman of the U.S. Atomic Energy Commission, used that phrase in a 1954 speech to the National Association of Science Writers. The quotation has been fought over ever since. Nuclear advocates have argued that Strauss had fusion in mind; critics have treated it as the emblem of fission-era overpromising. The Nuclear Regulatory Commission’s own historical reconstruction is more interesting than either slogan. Strauss’s speech did not explicitly name the generation technology in the famous sentence. But the surrounding material concentrated on fission, civilian reactors, and atomic applications; days later, when asked on Meet the Press about commercial power from atomic piles, Strauss again anticipated power too cheap to meter and said the day might be close at hand.

The useful point is not that Strauss was silly. He had seen something enormous. Nuclear binding energy really did change the scale on which engineers could think about fuel. A small quantity of uranium could release energy on a scale that made coal piles look primitive. If one stares only at fuel energy density, cheapness feels almost physically ordained.

But the price of electricity is not the price of fuel. It is a system price. Reactors require pressure vessels, turbines, containment, cooling, operators, financing, transmission, maintenance, regulation, decommissioning, waste management, security, and an institution capable of doing all of that for decades. The atom did not fail to contain energy. The social machine required to convert that energy into dependable electricity remained expensive.

Technomania repeatedly mistakes the cost curve of a component for the cost curve of a civilization.

We have done this with computation. The falling cost of a transistor became an intuition that information itself wanted to become free. Cheap storage became an intuition that memory would stop being scarce. Cheap communication became an intuition that geography would stop mattering. Cheap replication became an intuition that distribution would stop concentrating. Cheap digital publication became an intuition that attention would become abundant rather than more contested. Cheap prediction may now be becoming an intuition that judgment will become cheap too.

Sometimes the adjacent scarcity is the whole story.

A train reduces the time between cities and increases the value of being located near a station. A network makes communication cheap and increases the value of attention. A search engine makes documents accessible and increases the value of ranking. A social platform makes publication nearly free and increases the value of moderation, recommendation, identity, and distribution. A large language model can make first-draft text abundant and thereby increase the value of trusted provenance, editing, taste, access to proprietary context, and the authority to act.

The old scarcity does not always disappear. Often it moves.

That movement is difficult to see during a technological boom because new capability is photogenic and institutional adaptation is not. The first successful demonstration is a scene: a locomotive arriving, a voice coming from a radio set, a mushroom cloud, a man stepping onto the Moon, a browser loading a page, a machine writing code. The reorganization that follows is a spreadsheet, a standards committee, a rate case, a union contract, a training program, a procurement rule, a zoning hearing, a lawsuit, a classroom policy, a billing dispute, an insurance exclusion. Civilization is changed by the spectacular object and then governed by the boring machinery around it.

Technomania remembers the object.

History tends to remember both, but only after enough time has passed.

That is why this book moves backward before returning to artificial intelligence. AI is too close to us. Every fact is already recruited into an argument. A benchmark improvement is proof of imminent generality. A failed deployment is proof the entire field is hype. A layoff is attributed to automation, then disputed, then attributed to restructuring, then folded into another round of forecasts. Capital spending is read as confidence by one side and as bubble behavior by another. People who have spent their careers building the systems often possess the best technical intuitions and the strongest incentives to enlarge the category of problems the systems will solve. People threatened by those systems may see real limitations sooner and may also have incentives to deny capability. Everyone has a position before the evidence has finished arriving.

Older technologies are cleaner laboratories because the future already happened.

The railway chapter will not ask whether rail mattered. It asks what happened to the nineteenth-century claim that steam transport was annihilating distance. Recent historical work has shown how durable the “annihilation of space” metaphor became: every major transport and communications technology since the 1830s has been described as shrinking the world. Yet the history of rail is partly a history of newly important places—junctions, ports, terminals, industrial corridors—and newly peripheral ones. Faster movement does not abolish geography. It redraws it.

The radio chapter asks why a medium capable of reaching millions did not automatically produce universal education. The answer is not that broadcasting failed. Broadcasting succeeded so thoroughly that entertainment, advertising, propaganda, public service, politics, music, religion, and education all fought to inhabit the same spectrum. Technical reach was real. Social purpose was contested.

The atomic chapter returns to Strauss because his mistake, if we call it that, was not technological ignorance. It was a forecasting error about systems. The space chapter is even less comfortable for cynics. Apollo achieved a goal that seemed wildly ambitious on the announced schedule. If this book were merely a catalogue of overpromising, Apollo would break it. But Apollo helps make the actual argument. A society can do something astonishing when it concentrates money, prestige, engineering talent, procurement authority, and political attention on a finite objective. That tells us less than we think about whether the achievement naturally generalizes into lunar cities, routine Mars travel, or a permanent post-scarcity space economy.

Capability is not diffusion. Demonstration is not deployment. Deployment is not institutional settlement. Cost decline is not universal access. Technical decentralization is not political decentralization. Information abundance is not epistemic order. Automation is not liberation from work. Intelligence on tap is not authority on tap.

Those distinctions will recur, but I do not want to turn them into a branded framework. They are ordinary questions, and ordinary questions are harder to evade than slogans.

What, exactly, got cheaper?

What bottleneck moved somewhere else?

Who had to build the surrounding system?

Which old intermediary disappeared, and which new intermediary gained power?

Did the technology reduce scarcity, or did it change the object that was scarce?

Which forecasts were technically right but institutionally wrong?

Which were wrong on timing but right on direction?

Which were salesmanship dressed as inevitability?

Which sounded absurd and then came true?

Those last questions are important because hindsight is a corrupting drug. The past is full of confident people who underestimated technologies too. There were executives who thought computers would remain specialized machines, officials who underestimated the growth of networks, publishers who treated digital distribution as marginal, and institutions that dismissed open-source software, online commerce, smartphones, or generative models until the thing had already changed user behavior. Anti-mania can become a mania of its own: the belief that every boom will collapse back to the previous normal.

It does not.

Railway bubbles burst and railways remade countries. The dot-com bubble burst and the web swallowed commerce. Cryptocurrency bubbles destroyed fortunes and stablecoins became sufficiently important that central banks now model their effects on monetary sovereignty. AI valuations can overshoot while machine intelligence becomes ordinary infrastructure. The choice between “bubble” and “revolution” is one of the laziest binaries in technology writing because history routinely answers: both.

The harder question is which layer is revolutionary.

During the internet boom, it was possible to be wrong about a company and right about networking. During early aviation, one could be wrong about the economics of an airline and right that flight would reorder war, travel, logistics, and distance. One can be wrong about a cryptocurrency token and right that programmable settlement, digital bearer instruments, or globally available dollar substitutes matter. One can be wrong about an AI startup and right that the marginal cost of many cognitive operations is falling.

Technomania fuses layers that should remain separate: science, engineering, product, company, market structure, institution, culture, politics, and civilization. A breakthrough at one layer becomes proof of inevitability at all the others.

That fusion is emotionally efficient. It is much easier to say “AI changes everything” than to say that transformer-based models change some tasks quickly, some organizations slowly, some legal categories awkwardly, some professions unevenly, and perhaps some political structures only after decades of secondary effects. The total claim fits a stage. The conditional claim fits reality.

There is another reason total claims flourish: technologies acquire constituencies. Every mature technological system is surrounded by people whose careers, status, capital, identity, and hopes depend on a particular account of its future. This is not an accusation of fraud. A person can sincerely believe a forecast that also benefits them. In fact, sincerity may become easier when the forecast is financially useful. The engineer sees unsolved technical problems as solvable because solving technical problems is what engineering does. The investor sees a giant addressable market because giant markets justify risk. The regulator sees a threat category because threat categories justify jurisdiction. The incumbent sees limitations because limitations preserve incumbency. The displaced worker sees substitution because substitution is arriving at their desk before the complementary jobs have names.

Every witness is standing somewhere.

The archival record is therefore more revealing when read for incentives as well as quotations. Hugh Dryden, NASA’s deputy administrator, wrote in 1960 that forecasting astronautics was effectively prophecy and that the salesman and the engineer responsible for a successful launch would naturally see different futures. That observation deserves a place beside every modern technology forecast. The person seeking money and the person responsible for the failure mode inhabit different time horizons.

The salesman sells the terminal state.

The engineer meets Tuesday.

Society lives between them.

My argument is not that terminal states never arrive. Some do. A telephone network once sounded like an extravagant vision; now a child can video-call another continent. Computational abundance that would have seemed governmental in 1970 is discarded in consumer electronics. The world contains miracles made boring by repetition. That may be the most astonishing fact about technology: its successes vanish into the background. Nobody walks into a supermarket marveling at refrigeration logistics, fertilizer chemistry, containerization, barcodes, payment networks, weather forecasting, polymers, or global positioning. The technological sublime has become fluorescent lighting and a receipt.

Technomania is what happens before the miracle becomes plumbing.

It is the interval in which we mistake the direction of the capability curve for the direction of history.

That interval is dangerous and productive. It attracts money. It recruits talent. It tolerates foolish experiments that a sober cost-benefit analysis might never authorize. It builds too much. Some of the excess becomes waste; some becomes infrastructure. The American railway boom did not merely misallocate capital. It also left track. The telecom boom left fiber. A speculative frenzy can subsidize the next era’s boring utility.

So this will not be a book that asks us to stop becoming excited.

It asks for something more difficult: remain excited while refusing surrender.

A tool may become astonishingly capable without acquiring moral authority. A market may grow without becoming destiny. A cost curve may fall without abolishing institutions. A model may predict without deciding. A network may connect everyone and still produce centers. A machine may remove work from a task and create new work around verification, coordination, repair, safety, adjudication, and care. A genuine revolution may be underway while half the stories told about it are wrong.

Those are not compromises between technophilia and technophobia. They are descriptions of what technological change has repeatedly looked like when the smoke clears.

The first place to see it clearly is the railway, because the railway gave us one of technomania’s oldest promises: the end of distance.
