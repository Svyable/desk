# The End of Work, Version One

In March 1964, Lyndon Johnson sent Congress a letter proposing a National Commission on Automation and Technological Progress. The wording is useful because it could have been written yesterday with the nouns changed. Technological progress, Johnson wrote, had helped produce extraordinary living standards while also creating dislocations, lost jobs, and “the spectre of poverty in the midst of plenty.” The goal was to secure the maximum benefit from progress while reducing the hardship it imposed on workers.

The machine had become good enough to make abundance and unemployment imaginable at the same time.

This tension is older than computers. Mechanization had displaced tasks for centuries. Industrial machinery altered textile work, agriculture, mining, transport, and manufacturing. But mid-century automation added a new psychological ingredient. Control systems, computers, numerical control, and cybernetics suggested that machinery might not merely supply force. It might sense, compare, regulate, calculate, and decide.

The machine was moving into the office of the machine.

That produced a recurring forecast: the end of work.

Sometimes the forecast was utopian. Machines would perform drudgery and human beings would gain leisure. Sometimes it was catastrophic. Automation would create a permanent surplus population whose labor the economy no longer needed. Sometimes the same writer moved between both outcomes depending on politics.

The interesting fact is not that these forecasts proved entirely wrong. They were repeatedly right at the level of particular tasks and occupations.

Agricultural employment collapsed as a share of rich-country labor forces. Typing pools vanished. Telephone operators declined. Elevator operators mostly disappeared. Numerical-control machinery changed skilled manufacturing. Bookkeeping software removed clerical work. ATMs changed bank branches. Word processors changed secretarial labor. Industrial robots changed assembly. E-commerce reorganized retail and logistics.

A person can lose a job even if “employment” survives in the aggregate.

That distinction is where broad automation debates often become morally evasive. Economists are right to note that labor markets adapt, new tasks appear, demand expands, and previous waves of technology did not render the population permanently unemployable. A laid-off worker is right to observe that macroeconomic adaptation is not a job offer.

Both scales matter.

Technomania prefers the aggregate when defending progress and the anecdote when selling it.

A company demonstrates an automated workflow and generalizes toward a labor revolution. When workers worry, advocates generalize in the opposite direction: history always creates new jobs. The unit of analysis changes mid-argument.

The 1960s automation debate is useful precisely because we already know the long-run outcome. The United States did not run out of work. It did experience painful restructuring, deindustrialization in particular regions, changing skill premiums, occupational churn, and periods in which workers displaced from one system did not smoothly step into the better jobs promised by another.

The economy adapts through people.

That sentence sounds obvious and is strangely absent from technological optimism. “The labor market will adjust” can mean a forty-eight-year-old worker loses seniority, moves, accepts lower pay, retrains, or leaves the labor force while a twenty-four-year-old enters a new occupation elsewhere. At the aggregate level, one category shrinks and another grows. At the human level, there is no conservation law requiring the winner to be the same person as the loser.

Progress can be positive and distributionally brutal.

Automation also shows why the task is often a better unit than the job. Occupations are bundles. A lawyer researches, drafts, negotiates, counsels, appears, persuades, manages risk, signs, and carries professional responsibility. A nurse monitors, records, lifts, notices, comforts, administers, coordinates, and exercises judgment under uncertainty. A programmer interprets needs, writes code, debugs, reviews, communicates, maintains, tests, and makes trade-offs.

Technology rarely arrives with the exact shape of an occupation.

It attacks tasks.

That can reduce employment if enough tasks disappear, expand employment if lower costs increase demand, or change the occupation if remaining tasks become more valuable. The result depends on economics, not on benchmark capability alone.

This is one reason predictions about AI replacing whole professions swing so wildly. A model can perform a task associated with a profession, and observers silently promote task success into occupational substitution.

The missing variables are demand, reliability, authority, workflow, liability, and complementarity.

Suppose a model cuts the time required to draft a routine contract by ninety percent. If contract demand stays fixed and drafting is most of a lawyer’s work, employment pressure is obvious. If cheaper contracting causes firms to document agreements that previously went unwritten, demand may expand. If clients use the model directly for simple matters but seek lawyers for higher-stakes matters, the occupational mix changes. If insurers or courts require professional sign-off, authority remains scarce. If the model’s output increases the volume of documents that need review, some work migrates rather than disappears.

No slogan decides the answer.

The end-of-work prediction also misunderstands how societies manufacture new standards. When productivity increases, we do not necessarily consume the same output with fewer hours. We often increase the output expected.

A teacher with digital tools may be expected to communicate more with parents, document more, personalize more, and maintain more administrative systems. A physician with better diagnostics may practice within a system that demands more documentation and more sophisticated care. A software team with AI assistance may simply be asked to ship more software.

Efficiency gains can become workload gains.

This is not inevitable. It is institutional.

Who owns the saved hour?

That may be the most important labor question of any technological revolution.

If a worker becomes twice as productive, the gain can become leisure, higher wages, lower prices, higher profit, better quality, more output, or some mixture. Technology does not choose among them.

The politics of automation begins where the benchmark ends.

Cybernetics added another dream: not merely automated work, but automated governance. If feedback systems could stabilize machines, perhaps societies could be understood through information, control, and feedback too. Management science, operations research, systems analysis, and computational planning each offered versions of this aspiration. Some produced genuinely powerful tools. The danger came when an analytical method designed for a bounded system was mistaken for a complete account of a political one.

A thermostat has a target temperature.

A society does not have one uncontested target.

This seems like a small distinction and has wrecked many elegant theories.

Optimization requires an objective function. Public institutions often contain competing objectives because citizens disagree about what should be optimized and because values cannot be reduced to one unit without political choice. Efficiency can conflict with resilience. Growth can conflict with environmental protection. Security can conflict with privacy. Speed can conflict with due process. Equality can conflict with some forms of choice. A machine cannot make those conflicts disappear by calculating faster.

Technomania converts political disagreement into technical optimization because technical optimization feels soluble.

The dream survives today in algorithmic governance. If a model can predict which inspections are likely to find violations, allocate hospital beds, flag tax fraud, rank applicants, or forecast recidivism, it can improve specific decisions. But the prediction sits inside a policy: what false-positive rate is acceptable, which error harms whom, what due process is required, whether historical data encode previous discrimination, who can appeal, and what happens when people change behavior in response to the system.

The algorithm may be excellent and the institution unjust.

Or the institution may be legitimate and the model bad.

Or both may be better than the human process they replace.

Again, the layers must be separated.

Mid-century automation debates were unusually aware of this because machines were entering institutions that had spent generations organizing around human labor. The transition was not abstract. Unions negotiated it. Firms invested in it. Governments studied it. Workers experienced it.

The 1964 commission was an institutional response to uncertainty rather than a declaration that technology would stop. Johnson’s formulation was not anti-automation. It assumed progress would continue and asked how to capture benefits while limiting harm.

That is a more serious stance than either technological fatalism or technological denial.

Fatalism says the machine will do what the machine will do, so society must adapt.

Denial says the machine is hype, so society need not adapt.

Governance begins by noticing that technologies alter feasible choices without selecting all of them.

The labor market is especially important because people experience technology through bargaining power before they experience it through historical statistics. An AI assistant that lets one employee do the work of two can be liberating if the employee gains pay or time. It can be threatening if the employer uses the gain to reduce headcount and intensify work. The same capability supports both outcomes.

The architecture of the firm decides.

There is a historical reason technological unemployment predictions persist despite repeated aggregate adaptation. Each new wave reaches tasks previously treated as protected.

Mechanization reached muscle.

Automation reached routine control.

Computers reached calculation and clerical processing.

Software reached symbolic routines.

Generative AI reaches language, images, code, and other outputs associated with educated cognitive labor.

People who felt safely on the complementary side of automation now see their own work in the demonstration.

That changes the politics.

It does not guarantee mass unemployment, but it makes the old reassurance less emotionally credible. Telling a copywriter, junior programmer, translator, illustrator, analyst, or paralegal that “technology creates jobs” does not answer whether their bargaining power is falling now.

A serious book about technomania should not use history as anesthesia.

History does not say everything will be fine.

It says aggregate adaptation is compatible with concentrated pain and that institutions shape who bears it.

The more useful historical question is which policies and organizational choices turned productivity into broad gains rather than narrow ones. Education matters, but retraining is not magic. Geographic mobility matters, but people are attached to places. Social insurance matters, but benefits can preserve income without preserving dignity or local economic life. Labor institutions matter, but they can protect incumbents at the expense of entrants. Competition policy matters, because productivity gains captured by a few firms do not distribute like gains in competitive markets.

Technology changes the bargaining terrain.

It does not write the settlement.

The end-of-work debate also contains an ironic possibility. If AI does become capable enough to replace large quantities of labor, society may discover that employment was doing more than allocating tasks. Jobs distribute income, status, routine, social contact, identity, benefits, and political membership. An economy can solve production and still have a distribution problem.

The post-work future is therefore not an engineering milestone.

It is an institutional design problem.

A machine that can produce abundance does not determine how claims on that abundance are assigned.

This is the same mistake Strauss’s atomic vision made at a different layer. Cheap energy does not automatically produce cheap living. Cheap labor-like cognition does not automatically produce shared leisure.

The scarce object may become ownership.

By the 1970s and 1980s, another technological promise was beginning to invert the automation story. Mainframe computing had looked centralized. Personal computing would supposedly put power back into individual hands. The machine that threatened workers could also become the tool that liberated users from institutions.

Technomania was about to discover the personal computer.
