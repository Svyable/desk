# The Schoolhouse in the Air

Radio seemed to solve a problem educators had been trying to solve for centuries: how to put the best teacher in every room without putting the teacher there.

The machine could cross mountains, farms, neighborhoods, class boundaries, and school-district lines. One transmitter could reach thousands of listeners. A lecture no longer had to be repeated separately in every classroom. Music, languages, science demonstrations, current events, agricultural advice, public-health information, and university courses could be broadcast across a region. The marginal cost of one more listener was tiny compared with the marginal cost of one more seat in a lecture hall.

This sounded like educational abundance.

It was educational abundance, in one narrow technical sense. The scarce object called a live signal became much less scarce.

Then everything else remained.

A student needed a receiver. The receiver needed power. The student needed time, language, concentration, and a reason to listen. A curriculum needed to decide what the broadcast meant. A teacher often needed to contextualize it. A broadcaster needed spectrum and money. A regulator needed rules. Producers had to decide whether the audience wanted a chemistry lesson or a comedy show. Advertisers discovered that the same machinery that could distribute knowledge could distribute persuasion. Politicians discovered that a voice entering a home could do political work. Religious organizations understood the intimacy of the medium. Entertainers understood that mass attention had value.

The radio did not fail as a school because the radio was weak.

It succeeded as too many things at once.

This is an important category of technological disappointment. A general medium is often judged against the most morally flattering use imagined during its early period. When other uses dominate, observers say the medium failed to realize its potential. But potential is not a schedule. A technology that can educate can also entertain, advertise, coordinate, deceive, soothe, radicalize, and sell soap. The actual use emerges from institutions and incentives competing for the channel.

The internet would repeat the entire argument.

In its early public imagination, universal access to information seemed almost synonymous with enlightenment. Libraries would be everywhere. University lectures could be available to anyone. Scientific papers could circulate instantly. People previously excluded from publishing could speak. Experts could answer questions at global scale. Each statement was true in the sense of possibility. Many became true in practice.

The same network also produced clickbait, disinformation, spam, pornography, fraud, influencer marketing, targeted advertising, conspiracy communities, online harassment, algorithmic outrage, and an industrial contest to capture attention.

People sometimes describe this as if the internet became corrupted after a pure beginning.

Radio suggests something more ordinary. A medium with cheap distribution attracts whatever human institutions can benefit from cheap distribution.

The technology lowers the gate. It does not choose who walks through.

Educational radio also exposes a confusion between access and learning. Giving a person access to high-quality instructional material is often valuable. It is not equivalent to causing learning. Education contains feedback, practice, motivation, diagnosis, social pressure, assessment, sequencing, correction, and adaptation to the student. A broadcast does some of these things poorly or not at all.

The mistake is modular. We identify one expensive component—delivery of expert content—and imagine that reducing its cost reduces the cost of the whole system proportionally.

Sometimes it does. Often another component becomes binding.

This is exactly the question now facing AI tutoring. A model can explain algebra in several ways, generate exercises, translate material, answer questions at any hour, and adjust surface difficulty. That is a genuine capability. The question is not whether it can produce explanations. The question is how explanation interacts with motivation, curriculum, assessment integrity, misconceptions, developmental needs, teacher oversight, family environment, device access, privacy, and institutional incentives.

AI may transform education enormously. Radio could not tell us otherwise. It tells us where to look for the difference between reach and result.

A technology can democratize the input while leaving the output unequal.

This happens whenever the complementary resources are distributed unevenly. A motivated student with a quiet room, prior knowledge, and supportive adults can extract enormous value from free information. Another student with intermittent connectivity, household responsibilities, weak foundational skills, and no feedback may receive the same content and gain much less. The content is equally available. The capacity to use it is not.

This is why “democratization” is one of technomania’s slipperiest words.

It can mean reduced price. It can mean wider access. It can mean more producers. It can mean weaker credential barriers. It can mean a flatter distribution of outcomes. Those are different claims.

Radio democratized access to voices and performances in a way that would have been unimaginable before broadcasting. It also created national celebrities, network power, advertising markets, and concentrated ownership. A medium can broaden participation at one layer and concentrate power at another.

The apparent contradiction dissolves once we stop asking whether a technology is centralized or decentralized in the abstract.

Production can decentralize while distribution centralizes.

Distribution can decentralize while discovery centralizes.

Speech can decentralize while advertising centralizes.

Model use can decentralize while model training centralizes.

The first radio amateurs could experiment with transmitters. As the medium became commercially and politically important, spectrum allocation, interference, and licensing became more consequential. That is another recurring pattern. Early technological spaces often feel anarchic because the stakes are low enough that coordination failures are tolerable. Success makes rules necessary.

People then experience governance as the betrayal of the technology’s original freedom.

Sometimes it is. Sometimes it is the cost of scale.

The distinction has to be argued case by case.

A million people operating high-power transmitters on arbitrary frequencies do not produce a freer radio system. They produce interference. Coordination is not automatically oppression. But whoever controls coordination can acquire power. The problem is not solved by chanting either “regulation” or “decentralization.”

Technological maturity turns engineering constraints into governance questions.

Radio also showed how a new medium changes political intimacy. A newspaper gives the reader text. A radio gives the listener a voice. Tone, cadence, hesitation, warmth, anger, confidence, and theatrical control travel with the message. Political power can become parasocial before the word exists.

This matters for AI because synthetic media pushes the same transition further. A model can produce text in a voice, audio in a voice, video with a face, and personalized messages adapted to the recipient. The historical problem is not merely misinformation. It is mediated intimacy at scale.

A radio speech addressed millions simultaneously. An AI political system could theoretically generate millions of different speeches, each adapted to a person’s interests, fears, language, location, and prior behavior.

Broadcasting was one-to-many.

Algorithmic persuasion can be one-to-one at many-to-one scale.

That difference is real enough to justify concern. History should not be used to minimize novelty. “We have seen media change before” can become as lazy as “this changes everything.” The job is to identify what is actually new.

Radio lowered the cost of synchronous mass distribution.

The internet lowered the cost of asynchronous global publication and interaction.

Social platforms added identity graphs, behavioral feedback, recommendation, and measurable engagement.

Generative systems lower the cost of producing adaptive content.

Each layer compounds the previous one.

The lesson from radio is therefore not that society always panics about media and survives. It is that new media alter incentives around attention before institutions understand the consequences.

Attention is the scarce complement to cheap information.

This is obvious now and was less obvious when access dominated the rhetoric. If everyone can broadcast, nobody receives an audience automatically. If every university can publish lectures, the bottleneck becomes discovery and completion. If every student can ask a model for an explanation, the bottleneck may become knowing which explanation to trust or sticking with the problem long enough to learn.

Information abundance can create an attention shortage.

The economic system notices shortages.

Advertising is one way to monetize attention. Recommendation is another way to allocate it. Subscription is another. Reputation, credentialing, and brand are ways to reduce the cost of deciding where to spend it. Once information becomes abundant, institutions that help people not look at most information become valuable.

This is the opposite of the early dream. The dream is universal access. The mature problem is universal filtering.

Libraries understood this before networks did. A library is not merely a room where information is stored. It is classification, selection, indexing, preservation, and a set of social practices that make abundance usable. Search engines became enormously valuable for the same reason. Large language models may become another interface to abundance, which means their filtering decisions will matter even when those decisions appear as fluent conversation rather than ranked links.

An answer machine hides omission more elegantly than a search page.

That is not necessarily bad. It is a responsibility.

Radio history is full of responsibility questions because broadcasting created asymmetry. The broadcaster could reach millions; the individual listener had little comparable ability to answer. Social media appeared to correct this by making everyone a publisher. It created a new asymmetry around recommendation. Generative AI can make everyone a producer of sophisticated media while creating new asymmetry around models, compute, distribution, and verification.

The locus moves.

The power does not evaporate.

Educational broadcasting also teaches us to separate failure of prediction from failure of timing. Some visions associated with radio eventually became more plausible through later technologies. Remote lectures, language instruction, educational video, interactive tutoring, open courseware, and global access to experts are now ordinary enough that people forget how old the aspiration is.

A technological prophecy can be wrong about the machine that fulfills it.

This complicates scorekeeping. The dream of a schoolhouse in the air was not nonsense simply because broadcast radio did not replace schools. The aspiration migrated through television, correspondence courses, videotape, satellite education, personal computers, web courses, video platforms, videoconferencing, and AI tutoring.

The recurrent human desire was stable. The technical form changed.

That is why this book is interested in promises rather than gotchas. If a 1920s educator says radio will bring expert teaching to isolated students, the statement contains a real prediction about declining distribution cost. If the educator predicts that this alone will equalize education, the statement contains a theory of learning and institutions that deserves separate scrutiny.

Technomania bundles them.

The bundle is rhetorically powerful because the technical claim lends certainty to the social one.

We can receive the signal here; therefore education will improve.

We can automate this task; therefore the profession will disappear.

We can transact without a bank in this case; therefore intermediaries will disappear.

We can generate humanlike text; therefore human judgment is about to become obsolete.

The first clause may be experimentally demonstrable. The second can remain speculative while borrowing the first clause’s authority.

Radio made another promise that survived in a stranger form: national cohesion. A population hearing the same programs could share experiences. In an era before fragmented digital media, national broadcasts did produce common moments. The political and cultural consequences were real.

But cohesion is not inherently liberal or benign. A shared medium can support public culture and propaganda. The same reach that allows emergency information to spread allows centralized political narratives to spread. A technology that synchronizes attention increases the stakes of whoever captures the channel.

Again, the capability has no ideology.

This is an uncomfortable fact for both utopians and dystopians. A medium is not intrinsically democratic because many people can receive it. It is not intrinsically authoritarian because states can exploit it. Its political effects depend on ownership, access, competition, norms, law, and the larger institutional environment.

Technological affordances constrain possibilities. They do not mechanically select regimes.

That should make us suspicious of modern claims that a particular architecture—blockchain, open source, decentralized networks, encrypted messaging, artificial intelligence—has a built-in political destiny. Architectures matter. So do courts, money, police, culture, firms, standards, and people.

The radio did not become the universal classroom. It became radio.

That sounds trivial only because we know what radio became.

At the beginning, the category was open. The technology had not yet settled into institutions. Education was one plausible future among several. Commercial entertainment was another. Political broadcasting was another. Local experimentation was another. Network consolidation was another. The future was not encoded in the carrier wave.

This is the humility we need around new technologies before their institutions have hardened.

A language model may become tutor, assistant, bureaucratic layer, search interface, operating system, surveillance mechanism, creative partner, commodity utility, advertising surface, agent platform, or some mixture whose dominant forms have not been invented. The fact that a demonstration supports one use does not tell us which use wins.

The winning use may be the one with the strongest business model rather than the highest moral value.

Radio has already warned us.

The next great technological dream was different. Broadcasting promised cheap information. Atomic energy promised cheap power itself. The nucleus seemed to contain so much energy that the economics felt almost beneath the physics.

That is where a phrase entered history and refused to leave: too cheap to meter.
