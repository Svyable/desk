# Where the Scarcity Moves

Every major technology in this book begins by attacking a scarcity.

Railways attack travel time.

Telegraphs attack communication delay.

Electric grids attack local power constraints.

Radio attacks distribution cost.

Nuclear fission attacks fuel-energy density.

Personal computers attack access to computation.

The internet attacks the cost of copying and transmitting information.

Social media attacks the cost of publishing to an audience.

Crypto attacks some costs of settlement and recordkeeping.

AI attacks the cost of producing certain cognitive outputs.

This is why technological revolutions feel like abundance.

Something that was expensive becomes cheap enough to behave differently.

Then scarcity moves.

The railway makes travel faster and makes stations, rights of way, scheduling, and network access more important.

The telegraph makes transmission fast and makes trusted news gathering, authentication, and routing more important.

Electricity makes mechanical power accessible and makes grids, reliability, capital, and regulation more important.

Radio makes mass distribution cheap and makes spectrum, programming, and attention more important.

The internet makes publication cheap and makes search, ranking, trust, and attention more important.

Social media makes audience access easier and makes recommendation, identity, moderation, and reputation more important.

Crypto makes some settlement programmable and makes custody, interfaces, legal recourse, stable value, and secure code more important.

AI makes drafts, images, code suggestions, summaries, and classifications cheaper and makes verification, authority, proprietary context, judgment, and accountability more important.

Technomania stares at the scarcity being destroyed.

Strategy looks for the scarcity being created.

This is not cynical. It is how value migrates.

When one capability becomes a commodity, rents shift toward complements that remain scarce.

The history of computing is an obvious example. Hardware was once scarce and expensive. As computation became cheaper, software became more important. As software distribution became easier, customer relationships, ecosystems, data, and cloud infrastructure gained value. As information became abundant, search became valuable. As search matured, attention and recommendation became valuable.

The stack keeps moving.

The mistake is to assume the new abundance destroys the old economics without creating new economics.

Consider images. Before cameras, producing a realistic portrait required a person with specialized skill and time. Photography reduced one scarcity. Film, cameras, development, access, and later distribution became the complements. Digital photography reduced film and development costs. Smartphones reduced camera scarcity. Social platforms reduced distribution friction. Generative systems reduce some forms of image-production cost further.

What remains scarce?

Access to a unique event.

A trusted identity.

A recognizable artist.

A live performance.

A community that cares.

A brand.

A legal right.

A person whose presence in the image matters.

Taste.

Attention.

The object called “an image” becomes abundant. The social meaning of a particular image can remain scarce.

This is why economic predictions based on raw content volume are weak. Culture does not price bytes. It prices relationships around bytes.

The same is true of expertise. If an AI system can produce a competent medical explanation, legal memo, software prototype, or financial model, some scarcity has genuinely fallen.

The next scarce layer may be the right to act.

A diagnosis can be generated cheaply. A physician’s authority to prescribe, access the patient record, perform a procedure, coordinate care, and carry liability remains institutional.

A legal analysis can be generated cheaply. Representation, filing rights, client trust, negotiation, and professional responsibility remain.

Code can be generated cheaply. Product context, security, integration, maintenance, and organizational ownership remain.

The scarce complement can become more valuable because the abundant layer increases throughput.

If a model lets a physician review more cases, the physician’s attention may become the bottleneck.

If code generation accelerates, testing and product judgment may become the bottleneck.

If marketing content becomes almost free, customer attention may become more expensive.

If synthetic applications make applying for jobs cheap, employer screening becomes more expensive.

If AI makes legal filings easier to generate, courts may face more volume and verification burden.

Abundance can create congestion.

This is the part of technological optimism that standard productivity measures often miss. Lower production cost can increase total demand enough to move the system into a different regime.

Road building can induce traffic.

Email made messages cheap and created inbox overload.

Online forms made submissions easier and created form processing at scale.

Generative AI can make content cheap and create content saturation.

The system responds by building filters.

Filters then become power centers.

This is another recurring pattern: abundance creates intermediaries whose job is to say no.

Search engines say which pages matter.

Spam filters say which messages matter.

Credit systems say which applicants matter.

Recommendation engines say which posts matter.

AI evaluators may say which generated outputs matter.

Verification layers, identity systems, provenance standards, and trusted brands can all grow because creation became cheap.

The gatekeeper returns wearing a new uniform.

This does not mean decentralization is fake. It means every decentralization should be mapped across layers.

A technology can decentralize production and centralize discovery.

It can decentralize access and centralize infrastructure.

It can decentralize ownership and centralize standards.

It can decentralize publication and centralize ranking.

It can decentralize intelligence-like capability and centralize compute.

These are not paradoxes once the layers are separated.

The scarcity map also helps explain why some incumbents survive. An incumbent attacked at one layer may own a complement that becomes more important.

A newspaper threatened by cheap online publishing may still own trust, local reporting relationships, archives, and brand. A bank threatened by programmable payments may still own customer relationships, credit expertise, regulatory licenses, and deposit access. A software company threatened by code generation may still own distribution, enterprise integration, and proprietary workflows.

Incumbents fail when they defend the commoditized layer and neglect the complement.

Entrants fail when they attack the visible layer and underestimate the complement.

This is a better strategic question than “Will AI disrupt this industry?”

Which layer is becoming cheap, and who owns what stays scarce?

The same map clarifies labor markets.

If AI makes a task cheap, workers whose value is mostly the task face pressure. Workers who control complements can gain.

A designer who only executes routine variations may face competition from generation. A designer with customer relationships, brand, art direction, and taste may use generation to increase output.

A junior analyst who summarizes documents may face substitution. An analyst who owns context, client trust, data access, and decision authority may become more productive.

The distribution can be brutal because access to complements is unequal.

Capital owners often own infrastructure.

Senior workers often own relationships and tacit knowledge.

Credentialed professionals own authority.

Large firms own proprietary data and distribution.

When the commodity layer becomes cheap, those existing advantages can become more important rather than less.

This is why technological democratization can increase inequality even while broadening access.

Everyone receives the tool.

Not everyone receives the complement.

The same issue appears between countries. A model can be accessed globally, but countries differ in electricity, data centers, language coverage, wages, education, capital, legal systems, and industrial structure. A low-income country may face less immediate job substitution because wages are lower and occupations differ, while also capturing fewer productivity gains because complementary infrastructure is weaker.

The IMF and ILO have both warned about this asymmetry in AI exposure and readiness.

A global technology can widen a global divide if the complements are concentrated.

The policy question therefore changes. “Give everyone access to AI” may be useful and insufficient. Access to electricity, connectivity, education, local-language data, institutions, capital, and markets can determine whether the tool becomes capability or dependency.

The scarcity map also improves climate and energy debates. Suppose AI creates large productivity gains and large electricity demand. Compute becomes more efficient, but demand expands faster. Energy becomes a strategic complement to intelligence infrastructure.

Then power generation, transmission, transformers, land, and grid interconnection can capture value.

The technology most associated with software can produce a boom in heavy infrastructure.

This is not irony. It is complementarity.

Semiconductors create another scarcity map. Model algorithms can diffuse through papers and open-source implementations. High-end fabrication capacity, lithography equipment, advanced packaging, memory, and supply chains are physical and geopolitically concentrated.

Knowledge can be open while production remains scarce.

This distinction is central to national technology policy. Countries often imagine that possessing scientific knowledge means possessing capability. The nuclear chapter already showed otherwise. Tacit knowledge, manufacturing ecosystems, supplier networks, skilled labor, and capital equipment are part of technological sovereignty.

A PDF is not a factory.

A model architecture is not a chip fab.

A patent is not a supply chain.

The scarcity map also tells us where regulation is likely to migrate. Regulators historically focus on bottlenecks because bottlenecks are where power accumulates.

Railroads became regulated partly because essential routes had monopoly characteristics.

Utilities became regulated because network infrastructure had natural-monopoly properties.

Telecommunications became regulated because spectrum and networks were scarce.

Digital-platform regulation increasingly focuses on app stores, search, advertising, data, and marketplace power.

AI regulation may move toward frontier compute, model access, high-risk deployment, identity, provenance, and authority boundaries depending on where harms and concentration emerge.

The wrong policy regulates the old scarcity after the market has moved.

The right policy understands the complement.

This is hard because the scarcity map is dynamic. A complement that is scarce today can be commoditized tomorrow.

Model inference may be expensive, then cheap.

Chips may be scarce, then overbuilt.

Data may be proprietary until synthetic or public alternatives improve.

A credential may be valuable until institutions find another trust mechanism.

A platform may dominate until interoperability weakens lock-in.

Strategy based on permanent scarcity eventually fails.

Technomania makes the opposite mistake: it assumes every current scarcity falls because the new technology is improving rapidly.

Both positions confuse local change with universal change.

The scarcity map is also useful for human identity because some things resist commoditization for reasons unrelated to technical difficulty.

A handwritten note is easy to reproduce mechanically and can remain valuable because the sender wrote it.

A live concert matters even when recorded music is abundant.

A handmade object can command a premium despite machine alternatives.

A human therapist may remain valuable even if an AI can produce therapeutically competent language because the relationship itself is part of the service.

Authenticity can become a luxury good when simulation becomes cheap.

This is not guaranteed. People can form meaningful relationships with mediated or artificial systems. The point is that technical equivalence does not automatically produce social equivalence.

Value is partly symbolic.

Economics already knows this. Technology discourse often forgets.

The scarcity map also explains why trust keeps returning. Every wave claims to remove a trusted intermediary and creates new objects requiring trust.

The telegraph requires authentication.

The internet requires trusted certificates, domains, identities, and sources.

Crypto requires trust in code, interfaces, custody, and issuers.

AI requires trust in data, model behavior, provenance, evaluation, and operators.

Trust can be redistributed and partially mechanized.

It is difficult to abolish because uncertainty cannot be abolished.

Wherever someone can be harmed by a fact they cannot personally verify, trust enters.

The dream of a trustless society is really a dream of moving trust into mechanisms whose operation is easier to verify.

That can be progress.

It is not transcendence.

The final scarcity may be legitimacy.

A system can be accurate, efficient, secure, and unpopular because people reject the institution’s right to decide. Democratic governance contains procedures that do not maximize speed precisely because legitimacy depends on participation, appeal, transparency, and stable rules.

AI can optimize a decision.

It cannot make the decision politically legitimate merely by being correct more often.

That is not a permanent human monopoly on wisdom. It is a statement about authority.

Who gets to decide remains a different question from who can predict.

Technomania repeatedly merges them because a highly capable system feels deserving of authority.

History gives no reason to grant that inference automatically.

The practical value of a scarcity map is that it changes the forecast.

Instead of asking whether a technology will dominate, ask what it cheapens.

Then ask what becomes relatively more important.

Then ask who owns that complement.

Then ask whether the complement can itself be commoditized.

Then ask which institution governs the bottleneck.

That sequence produces less spectacular forecasts and better strategy.

It also produces a different politics. If AI cheapens cognitive production and makes verification scarce, invest in verification. If it makes authority scarce, clarify responsibility. If it makes energy scarce, build energy. If it makes attention scarce, design incentives that do not waste it. If it makes provenance scarce, develop provenance systems before synthetic media saturate institutions that require trust.

The future is not merely what the new machine can do.

It is what everyone else has to become because the machine can do it.

One chapter remains.

The temptation at the end of a book like this is to issue a doctrine: neither technophilia nor technophobia, a balanced middle path, a checklist for wise innovation. That would betray the argument. Technologies differ too much for one emotional posture.

What can be carried forward is a discipline.

Not moderation.

Revision.
