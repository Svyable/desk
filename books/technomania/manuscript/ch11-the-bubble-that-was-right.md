# The Bubble That Was Right

The dot-com crash is one of history’s favorite moral stories because everyone can use it.

Skeptics use it to show that investors lose their minds around new technology. Optimists use it to show that bubbles can surround technologies that later become even more important than the hype suggested. Investors use it to remind themselves that timing matters. Founders use it to explain why early losses may be strategic. Critics of current AI spending use it as a warning. Defenders of current AI spending use the same episode as evidence that today’s expensive infrastructure may become tomorrow’s foundation.

All of them are partly right.

That is why the dot-com era matters here.

The basic technological thesis—that networked computing would reorganize commerce, media, communication, software, advertising, and everyday life—was not merely correct. It was underpowered. The web became more central than many late-1990s users could imagine because the mature internet was not only a desktop publishing system. It became payments, maps, streaming, social networks, cloud software, logistics interfaces, video calls, software distribution, government services, employment marketplaces, education, entertainment, and the operating layer beneath mobile devices.

Yet an investor could believe all of that in 1999 and still lose almost everything.

This is the most important distinction in technology investing: being right about civilization is not the same as being right about a security.

A sector can grow while a company fails.

A technology can win while early firms are displaced by later entrants.

Demand can grow while margins collapse.

Infrastructure can become essential while the owners of overbuilt infrastructure earn poor returns.

A product category can become enormous while valuations paid during a boom imply an even more enormous future that never arrives.

Technomania collapses these layers because the civilizational story is emotionally powerful. If the internet will change everything, then an internet company feels valuable. If AI will transform knowledge work, then an AI company feels valuable. The gap between “important” and “worth this price” gets lost.

Markets eventually recover it.

The dot-com boom also shows how a new metric can become temporarily detached from the old discipline. Page views, registered users, traffic, and “eyeballs” mattered because the web genuinely changed the value of audience scale. The mistake was not measuring users. The mistake was sometimes treating user growth as if it automatically implied a durable mechanism for turning use into cash.

New technologies often require new metrics.

Manias occur when the new metric becomes an excuse to stop asking old questions.

Does the customer pay?

What does acquisition cost?

Does scale improve or worsen economics?

What happens when competitors copy the feature?

What is scarce?

Who owns the relationship?

How much capital must be reinvested?

What happens when the cost of money changes?

These questions sound embarrassingly basic after a crash and unnecessarily conservative during a boom.

That is exactly why they matter during a boom.

The infrastructure side is subtler. Telecommunications firms laid large amounts of fiber under optimistic forecasts. Some owners suffered badly when capacity exceeded near-term demand. Yet excess capacity helped reduce transmission costs and supported later internet growth.

This is where public welfare and investor welfare can diverge.

Overbuilding can be socially useful and financially disastrous.

A city benefits from a bridge even if the original bondholders were wiped out in a restructuring. Future users can benefit from cheap fiber installed by companies that overestimated their own returns. Consumers may gain from price wars that destroy supplier margins.

The current AI infrastructure boom may eventually produce the same divergence. Data centers, power connections, specialized chips, transmission upgrades, and model-serving infrastructure could become widely useful even if specific capital structures disappoint. Conversely, investors could earn extraordinary returns while broader social gains remain uneven.

Technology does not guarantee alignment between who pays, who loses, and who benefits.

This matters when policymakers treat private investment as a proxy for social value. A company builds what it can monetize, not necessarily what society needs most. Sometimes the two overlap. Sometimes a speculative boom accelerates infrastructure that later becomes broadly valuable. Sometimes it produces stranded assets.

The future cannot reimburse every bad forecast.

The dot-com era also shows how bubbles recruit labor. People leave stable jobs. Students choose fields. Cities reorganize around employers. Suppliers expand. Professional identities form. When capital disappears, the crash is not confined to stock charts.

A technology narrative is a labor-market signal.

AI is already operating this way. Students are deciding whether to enter software, design, law, translation, marketing, medicine, or research partly based on forecasts about automation. Companies are hiring and firing against assumptions about future productivity. Data-center construction is reshaping power planning. Governments are subsidizing semiconductor capacity.

The forecast is becoming physical before the outcome is settled.

This is what makes technological hype more than speech.

It coordinates bets.

Sometimes coordination is exactly what a nascent technology needs. A network becomes more useful when enough people adopt it. A semiconductor ecosystem needs fabs, tools, packaging, design skills, and customers to mature together. Electric vehicles need vehicles, batteries, charging, power, maintenance, and supply chains. AI needs models, chips, data centers, software integration, training, evaluation, and users willing to reorganize work.

No single actor controls the whole stack.

A shared narrative can cause complementary investments to arrive at roughly the same time.

Hype can be a coordination technology.

This is uncomfortable because it means some exaggeration may have productive effects. A sober forecast can under-mobilize. A visionary story can attract enough talent and capital to move a technology across thresholds that would otherwise take longer.

The cost is misallocation.

The benefit is acceleration.

That does not give promoters moral permission to lie. It explains why cultures repeatedly reward people who speak beyond the evidence. The exaggerated story can make itself partly true by recruiting the resources necessary to pursue it.

The dot-com boom left another important inheritance: interface changed demand before institutions understood the change.

Consumers learned that some transactions could happen online. Once that expectation formed, businesses that had treated the web as an optional channel faced pressure to adapt. The first e-commerce experiments did not need to replace all retail to change the baseline. They only had to demonstrate a superior experience in enough cases.

This is how technological disruption becomes compulsory.

A competitor adopts the tool.

Customers adjust expectations.

The old service is now judged against a new benchmark.

Non-adoption becomes a decision rather than the default.

AI is entering this phase in software, writing, customer support, search, and administrative work. A firm does not need to believe the strongest AI forecasts. It may adopt because competitors can produce drafts faster or serve customers at lower cost. The strategic pressure arrives before the macroeconomic evidence is clean.

That pressure can produce over-adoption too.

Companies buy systems because boards ask about them. Managers run pilots because nobody wants to appear behind. Vendors relabel ordinary software as AI. Internal projects survive because they signal modernity rather than because they create value.

This happened with the web. It happened with blockchain. It happened with big data. It happened with mobile apps.

A technology can be real and the local use case fake.

This is one reason enterprise technology cycles generate so much frustration. Executives hear a true sector-level story and force it into a company-level mandate. “The internet is the future” becomes “every company needs a portal.” “Mobile is the future” becomes “we need an app.” “Blockchain is the future” becomes “put this database on a ledger.” “AI is the future” becomes “every workflow needs a chatbot.”

The nouns change. The procurement ritual survives.

Technomania becomes expensive when strategic signaling substitutes for problem definition.

What problem are we solving?

What does failure cost?

Who uses the system?

What data does it require?

What is the baseline?

What gets better enough to justify the change?

Again, boring questions are defensive technology.

The dot-com collapse did not restore the old world. This is the part of the story people forget when they use “bubble” as a synonym for “fake.” After the valuations collapsed, users did not stop wanting email. Companies did not abandon networks. Browsers did not become curiosities. Universities did not disconnect. The infrastructure and habits remained.

The mania receded. The adoption curve continued.

This is one possible future for AI and should frighten both camps.

The strongest skeptics may be right that current capital spending and valuations overshoot and wrong that the technology is marginal.

The strongest optimists may be right that AI becomes foundational and wrong about which companies capture the value, how quickly organizations change, and how much current infrastructure earns its cost of capital.

History permits both errors at once.

The dot-com case also complicates “first mover advantage.” Early companies learned valuable things, but being early could mean paying high costs to educate customers, build infrastructure, and discover failure modes that later entrants exploited more cheaply. The company that proves a market exists does not automatically own the mature market.

This is a problem for any investor trying to convert technological foresight into financial foresight.

The future can arrive through someone else’s product.

It can also arrive in a form that destroys the original business model. Online news fulfilled the prediction that information would move digitally and undermined the economics of many organizations producing that information. Streaming fulfilled digital-distribution predictions while changing the economics of music and film. Cloud software fulfilled networked-computing predictions while reducing the relevance of some packaged software models.

The technology can win by killing the company that correctly predicted it.

There is a deeper psychological lesson. During a boom, skeptics are punished socially before they are proven financially. Prices rise. New fortunes become visible. Old expertise looks stale. People who insist on unit economics appear unimaginative. If the boom lasts long enough, prudence can look indistinguishable from incompetence.

This is why manias are not solved by intelligence.

Smart people can see the overvaluation and still be unable to time it. Smart people can see the technology and overestimate the business. Smart people can know that both are true and have no profitable trade because markets can remain unreasonable longer than their mandate, liquidity, or career can tolerate.

Technological bubbles are epistemic traps because several propositions with different time horizons become entangled.

Is the technology useful?

Will usage grow?

Will this company win?

Will it earn attractive margins?

Is the stock cheap relative to those future earnings?

Will the market agree before my capital runs out?

A person can answer four correctly and lose on the fifth.

Civilizational prediction is easier than portfolio construction.

The dot-com era should therefore discipline current AI debate in a specific way. Do not ask whether AI is a bubble as if the word resolves the technology.

Ask where expectations exceed realizable cash flows.

Ask which infrastructure has value under multiple future scenarios.

Ask what capacity can be repurposed.

Ask which firms possess scarce complements rather than merely model access.

Ask whether adoption creates switching costs or commodity competition.

Ask who benefits if inference gets dramatically cheaper.

Ask whether demand expands fast enough to use the installed capacity.

These are not bearish questions.

They are questions that survive either outcome.

The dot-com boom also created a cultural template for technology founders as public prophets. A founder no longer merely built a company. They narrated a future in which the company occupied a structurally necessary role. The business pitch became a civilizational pitch.

That style intensified through social media, crypto, and AI.

The founder’s ability to describe the future became part of the company’s asset base because narrative recruited employees, investors, partners, and customers. Technical competence and prophetic competence merged.

The danger is obvious. The person with the greatest incentive to enlarge the market becomes a primary source for the size of the market.

The benefit is also obvious. People need stories to coordinate around uncertain projects.

There is no clean escape.

The answer is not to stop listening to builders. Builders often see possibilities outsiders miss. The answer is to classify what kind of statement is being made.

A measured performance result is evidence.

A customer renewal rate is evidence.

A forecast of total addressable market is a model.

A claim that society will reorganize around the product is a thesis.

A moral claim that the reorganization will be good is politics.

Technomania lets one category borrow authority from another.

The dot-com crash separated some of them with brutal efficiency.

Then the network kept growing.

The next phase made the promise of democratic participation more literal. The web had lowered the cost of publication. Social media lowered the cost of distribution through a social graph and gave ordinary people an audience-measuring machine.

Everyone could speak.

What could go wrong?
