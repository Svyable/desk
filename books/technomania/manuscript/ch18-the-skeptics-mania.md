# The Skeptic’s Mania

Technological enthusiasm has an opposite pathology that looks more intelligent because it usually speaks in complete sentences.

Every boom attracts skeptics who point out the hype, the weak business models, the inflated forecasts, the failed pilots, the hidden costs, and the historical precedents. Often they are right. Sometimes being right about the hype becomes a substitute for understanding the technology.

This is the skeptic’s mania: the belief that because a story is exaggerated, the underlying capability must be unimportant.

The dot-com crash is the obvious warning. Investors overpaid for many companies. Business models were weak. Capital was wasted. The rhetoric was absurd. Then the internet kept spreading.

The skeptic who concluded in 2001 that the web had been exposed as a fad had correctly diagnosed a financial mania and incorrectly diagnosed civilization.

That is not a small error.

Railways produced speculative excess and changed countries.

Nuclear power produced overpromising and became a major electricity source.

Personal computing generated foolish forecasts and became ordinary infrastructure.

Social media generated utopian rhetoric and changed politics, media, culture, and advertising.

Crypto generated fraud, collapses, and ideological overreach while stablecoins and programmable settlement became important enough for central banks to study as macro-financial systems.

A bubble can contain a technology.

Hype is not the opposite of substance.

It is often a layer around substance.

This matters now because AI criticism can become as lazy as AI boosterism. A model makes an obvious error and the critic declares that language models do not reason. A company’s chatbot pilot fails and the critic declares enterprise AI useless. A benchmark is gamed and the critic treats all measured capability as fraudulent. An AI startup collapses and the critic treats the financing failure as evidence against the underlying technology.

These moves mirror the errors described in the previous chapter.

They jump layers too.

A failure mode at one layer becomes a universal conclusion at another.

Skepticism earns its value by staying specific.

The model fails under this distribution.

The productivity study measures this task and not that outcome.

The business has these unit economics.

The system creates this review burden.

The deployment lacks this control.

The valuation assumes this growth rate.

Those claims can be tested.

“AI is hype” is almost as empty as “AI changes everything.”

The skeptic’s mania also relies on a comforting historical story: society has seen technological panics before, therefore current concern is probably exaggerated. This is the inverse of technological exceptionalism. Instead of saying everything is unprecedented, it says nothing important is.

History supports neither.

New technologies recur. Their combinations can be novel.

Railroads changed transport but did not produce instantaneous global communication. Telegraphy did. Radio produced mass real-time audio but not individualized algorithmic feeds. Social media produced individualized feeds but not cheap adaptive generation. Generative AI added a new layer to an already networked, instrumented, mobile world.

The correct use of history is not to flatten novelty.

It is to identify where novelty actually resides.

A person who says “people worried about automation in the 1960s and employment survived” has established that aggregate labor markets can adapt to major technological change. They have not established that generative AI will have the same occupational distribution, speed, skill profile, bargaining effects, or political response.

The analogy generates priors.

Evidence updates them.

The skeptic’s mania can also arise from professional identity. People whose expertise is threatened have incentives to emphasize what machines cannot do. This does not make their criticisms wrong. In fact, domain experts often see failure modes technologists miss.

But incumbency has its own bias.

A profession can mistake its current bundle of tasks for a natural law.

Scribes could correctly identify things movable type did badly and still underestimate printing. Travel agents could correctly explain why complicated trips required expertise and still underestimate online booking. Taxi operators could correctly point to safety and local knowledge and still underestimate smartphone dispatch.

The current institution often has reasons for its structure.

It can still be disrupted.

The hard intellectual stance is to hold both facts at once: incumbents contain hidden functions, and incumbents are not immortal.

The skeptic’s mania is especially dangerous in institutions with slow procurement or weak competition because dismissal can become a strategy of delay. A university can call online education shallow until competitors learn how to combine digital delivery with credentials and support. A media company can dismiss online distribution until advertising moves. A manufacturer can dismiss new production methods until the supply chain shifts.

Sometimes caution is wisdom.

Sometimes caution is a business model that has not noticed it is expiring.

How do we tell the difference?

One answer is to track revealed behavior rather than rhetoric.

Are customers paying?

Are users returning?

Are firms changing workflows rather than running demos?

Are costs falling?

Are reliability metrics improving?

Are complementary investments appearing?

Are incumbents adopting the system privately while criticizing it publicly?

Are regulations being rewritten because the technology creates real cases, or merely because politics is reacting to headlines?

Behavior is not proof, but it is harder to fake than a keynote.

The skeptic’s mania also overuses edge cases in the opposite direction. Every complex system has edge cases. The existence of a failure does not establish that the system lacks value. The relevant question is frequency, severity, detectability, and comparison with the baseline.

Human professionals make errors.

Machines make errors.

The institution needs comparative evidence.

A diagnostic model does not need to be perfect to help if it improves outcomes under an appropriate workflow. It does need to be evaluated against the actual alternative, not against an imaginary flawless human.

Conversely, “humans make mistakes too” is not a defense of a system whose errors are correlated, scalable, or difficult to detect.

Error shape matters.

A human clerk can make one mistake at a time. A software rule can make the same mistake across a million cases before anyone notices.

Automation concentrates failure.

It can also standardize quality.

The baseline comparison has to include both.

This is one reason aggregate benchmark scores tell us less than deployment studies. The institutional question is not merely how often a model is correct. It is whether the human-machine system produces a better distribution of outcomes than the human system under realistic constraints.

That includes cost.

That includes time.

That includes rare failures.

That includes who is harmed.

The skeptic’s mania tends to compare the machine’s actual failure with the human institution’s ideal behavior.

Technomania tends to do the reverse.

Both comparisons cheat.

The same symmetry appears in debates about creativity. Enthusiasts point to images, music, prose, and code that would have required skill to produce manually and infer that creativity has been automated. Skeptics point to generic outputs, clichés, attribution problems, and derivative style and infer that nothing creative is happening.

The more interesting question is economic and cultural.

Which parts of creative production were scarce because of technical execution?

Which were scarce because of taste, access, identity, reputation, editing, performance, or trust?

If technical execution becomes cheaper, what moves?

We already know from photography that cheap image capture does not eliminate photography as culture. It changes who takes pictures, how many exist, what professionals do, and which images remain valuable.

Generative media may be more disruptive because it automates synthesis rather than capture. The historical principle still survives: abundance reorganizes scarcity.

The skeptic’s mania also mistakes current limitations for permanent ones. This is a classic failure in fast-improving technical domains. A model cannot perform a task reliably today, so a critic builds an institutional conclusion around the limitation. The model improves. The argument collapses.

Technologists make the mirror error by treating every current limitation as temporary.

Some limitations are engineering backlog.

Some are data limitations.

Some are economic.

Some are physical.

Some are legal.

Some may be conceptual.

The honest answer is often that we do not know which yet.

Uncertainty is not intellectually satisfying, but it is more useful than pretending every curve extrapolates or every wall is permanent.

A good skeptic therefore asks for defeat conditions for their own skepticism.

What evidence would change my mind?

If a model achieved a specified reliability in a prospective deployment, would I accept that the task is automatable?

If firms sustained productivity gains for three years across independent sectors, would I revise my view of macroeconomic significance?

If autonomous systems operated safely under defined authority limits, would I stop calling them demos?

The booster should do the same.

What would falsify the adoption thesis?

What return on capital would make the infrastructure build excessive?

What plateau would change the capability forecast?

What labor data would contradict the substitution story?

A belief without a defeat condition is identity.

Technomania and technophobia both become identity easily because technology now carries cultural affiliation. A person’s view of AI can signal politics, profession, age, class, optimism, fear, or membership in a technical community. Once the belief serves social functions, evidence has to fight more than a proposition.

This is why the tone of technology debate is often strangely moral.

Optimists accuse skeptics of wanting stagnation.

Skeptics accuse optimists of wanting dispossession.

Builders accuse regulators of not understanding technology.

Regulators accuse builders of externalizing risk.

Workers accuse executives of using technology as cover for cuts.

Executives accuse workers of resisting productivity.

All of these motives can exist in particular cases.

None explains the whole field.

The alternative is not bland centrism. Some technologies deserve aggressive acceleration. Some deployments deserve prohibition. Some companies are overvalued. Some professions are more exposed than they admit. Some risks are speculative. Some are already measured.

The position changes with the evidence.

That is not fence-sitting.

It is the discipline technomania destroys by demanding one emotional attitude toward “technology.”

There is no coherent attitude toward technology in the singular.

A person can support nuclear power, oppose autonomous lethal weapons, use generative AI, demand strict medical-AI validation, favor open technical standards, distrust cryptocurrency speculation, and welcome stablecoin competition in specific payment corridors. Those positions can arise from one principle: judge systems by evidence, incentives, and consequences rather than by whether they feel futuristic.

The skeptic’s mania is therefore not skepticism.

It is the refusal to update upward.

Technomania is the refusal to update downward.

A serious technological culture needs both directions.

The reason is simple. We are going to be surprised.

Some capabilities will arrive earlier than expected.

Some will stall.

Some will matter in applications nobody currently values.

Some enormous companies will become small.

Some ridiculous experiments will become infrastructure.

Some celebrated breakthroughs will become footnotes.

Some regulations will look foolish.

Some regulations will look painfully late.

The task is not to avoid surprise.

It is to remain revisable under it.

The next chapter gathers the pattern running beneath every case in the book: technology does not abolish scarcity as often as it relocates scarcity. If we can learn to look for where the bottleneck moves, we can forecast the institutional future more usefully than by staring at the capability curve alone.
