# Technomania

## When Tools Become Theories of History

Sven Hardy Benson

A railway shortens a journey and people announce the death of distance. A radio sends a voice across a continent and people imagine ignorance collapsing. Nuclear physics reveals extraordinary energy density and people imagine electricity too cheap to meter. The internet makes publishing cheap and people imagine gatekeepers disappearing. Artificial intelligence makes certain forms of cognitive work cheaper and people imagine the end of work, expertise, perhaps politics itself.

The pattern is not that people are foolish about technology.

They are often right first.

The machine works. The cost curve bends. The capability is real. Then the argument jumps a layer—from engineering to economics, from economics to institutions, from institutions to morality, from morality to destiny.

This book is about that jump.

It is not anti-technology. The opposite would be easier and less accurate. Railways, electricity, broadcasting, nuclear power, computers, networks, smartphones, cryptography, and artificial intelligence all expanded the range of what human beings could do. The mistake is not enthusiasm. The mistake is allowing a tool to become a theory of history.

The recurring questions here are deliberately ordinary. What exactly got cheaper? What bottleneck moved elsewhere? Which intermediary disappeared and which new intermediary gained power? Which claims are measured facts and which are forecasts? What happens when the first-order effect meets institutions that adapt? Who captures the gain? Who carries the loss? What becomes scarce after abundance arrives?

The evidence posture is conservative where the claim is controversial and aggressive where the documentation is strong. Historical speeches are treated as speeches, not predictions proven by quotation. Company forecasts are treated as interested forecasts. Government attention proves attention. Patents prove filing. Task-level productivity studies prove effects under their tested conditions, not automatic macroeconomic transformation. Scenarios remain scenarios.

The method is simple enough to be annoying: keep the layers separate until evidence earns the connection.

That does not make the future less strange.

It makes the strangeness easier to see.
