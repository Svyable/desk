# Living Inside the Revolution

The railway did not end distance.

The telegraph did not end misunderstanding.

Radio did not end ignorance.

Atomic energy did not end scarcity.

Automation did not end work.

The personal computer did not end institutions.

The internet did not end gatekeepers.

Social media did not automatically democratize truth.

Crypto did not end trust.

Artificial intelligence will not end politics.

This could sound like a list of disappointments. It is not.

Every technology on that list changed the world.

The mistake was asking the technology to do work that belonged to institutions, values, incentives, law, ownership, culture, and human choice.

That is technomania’s core error. A new capability appears. Because the capability is real, the surrounding story acquires borrowed certainty. The machine can do X; therefore society will become Y. The gap between X and Y disappears in rhetoric and returns later as politics.

The gap is where we live.

A civilization cannot avoid that gap by becoming more technologically advanced. Greater capability enlarges it. The more powerful the tool, the more consequential the decisions around it become.

A weak technology can be ignored.

A strong technology has to be governed.

This is why “let innovation happen” stops being a complete philosophy once innovation becomes infrastructure. Nobody says let bridges happen and leaves load standards, rights of way, maintenance, inspection, and liability to spontaneous discovery. The existence of rules does not make a bridge anti-innovative. The rules are part of what makes people willing to drive across it.

The same principle will eventually become obvious for AI.

Right now the culture is still close to demonstration. Models are compared by capability, speed, price, and benchmark performance. Firms release new versions rapidly. Users discover uses faster than institutions can document them. The frontier is moving enough that governance can feel premature.

That phase will end.

Not because progress stops, but because important systems will depend on the technology.

When dependence rises, reliability becomes a product feature.

When authority rises, audit becomes a product feature.

When liability rises, traceability becomes a product feature.

When autonomous action rises, permissioning and rollback become product features.

The boring layer arrives.

The lesson is not that we should wait until every risk is solved. That would freeze beneficial technologies behind impossible standards. The lesson is to match reversibility to uncertainty.

When evidence is weak, make the bet easy to reverse.

When harm can scale, limit authority before limiting capability.

When a system is hard to evaluate, do not give it irreversible power merely because the demo is impressive.

When evidence improves, expand deployment.

This sounds like ordinary engineering because it is.

The culture of technology often becomes strange exactly where ordinary engineering would be most helpful. We speak about destiny, acceleration, disruption, transformation, inevitability, superintelligence, post-scarcity, decentralization, and the end of work. These may be useful scenario labels. They are poor substitutes for specifying interfaces and failure modes.

A civilization that wants to live with powerful technology should become less impressed by nouns and more interested in verbs.

What does the system do?

Under what conditions?

To whom?

With what error distribution?

Who can authorize it?

Who can stop it?

Who gets paid?

Who bears the loss?

What becomes scarce afterward?

Those questions survive fashion cycles.

There is also a discipline for reading technology news. Start by identifying the evidence class.

A measured result is not the same as a company claim.

A company claim is not the same as independent reporting.

Independent reporting is not the same as a replicated deployment.

A patent proves filing, not efficacy.

A benchmark proves performance under the benchmark conditions, not a profession’s obsolescence.

An investment proves somebody made a bet, not that the bet will pay.

A government program proves interest, not success.

A demonstration proves possibility, not reliability.

A forecast proves someone is forecasting.

This sounds severe only because technology discourse routinely erases these distinctions.

The four evidence classes used in the research notes for this book are deliberately simple.

First: verified or documented facts.

Second: credible but incomplete evidence.

Third: disputed claims or claims without independent confirmation.

Fourth: speculative synthesis.

The point is not to rank every interesting idea as guilty until proven innocent. The point is to stop a Class 4 idea from borrowing the tone of a Class 1 fact merely because they sit in adjacent sentences.

A great deal of technomania is typography.

Everything appears in the same confident font.

A second discipline is to identify the unit.

Is the claim about a task, a job, a firm, an industry, an economy, or civilization?

Evidence does not scale automatically between them.

A model that improves call-center productivity is evidence about a task and workflow. It may contribute to a labor-market forecast. It is not by itself evidence that AI will add a specific number of percentage points to national growth.

A data center under construction is evidence of investment. It is not evidence of final demand.

A startup’s revenue growth is evidence about a business. It is not evidence that the market structure will remain concentrated.

A technology forecast becomes more honest when the unit is written into the sentence.

A third discipline is to find the complement.

If the capability became cheap tomorrow, what would still prevent the outcome?

If legal drafting were free, what would still make legal services scarce?

If software generation were free, what would still make software products hard?

If medical analysis were free, what would still make healthcare expensive?

If energy generation were free, what would still make electricity systems costly?

If education content were free, what would still make learning unequal?

The answer is often where institutions and investment should move.

A fourth discipline is to trace incentives.

Who benefits if this forecast is believed?

That question should never be used as a shortcut to dismissal. People with incentives can be right. Builders often have the best information. Regulators can have incentives to enlarge their jurisdiction and still identify real risks. Incumbents can have incentives to minimize disruption and still understand hidden functions outsiders miss.

The point is to know where the witness is standing.

An investor selling a future is a different source from an engineer reporting a measured failure rate.

A union warning about job loss sees a different layer from a customer reporting faster service.

A government promoting a national technology strategy has strategic reasons to describe capability in certain ways.

Location does not invalidate evidence.

It helps interpret it.

A fifth discipline is to look for the displaced cost.

Technology announcements tell us what became cheaper.

They rarely lead with what became expensive.

Online publication became cheap; attention became expensive.

Email became cheap; filtering became expensive.

Digital photography became cheap; curation became expensive.

Software distribution became cheap; discovery became expensive.

AI generation becomes cheap; verification can become expensive.

The displaced cost is often the mature business model.

A sixth discipline is to separate private return from social return.

A railway can be socially useful and financially disastrous for its original investors.

A platform can be financially extraordinary and socially damaging in some dimensions.

A data center can create local jobs and stress a power system.

An AI system can raise firm productivity while weakening worker bargaining power.

The word value is too vague unless we ask value to whom.

This is where technological politics becomes unavoidable. Efficiency gains are not self-distributing.

A seventh discipline is to respect adaptation.

People react.

Competitors copy.

Workers retrain or resist.

Regulators regulate.

Criminals exploit.

Security teams respond.

Customers change expectations.

Institutions reorganize.

The first-order effect is not the equilibrium.

This is why linear forecasts from current behavior fail so often. The technology changes the behavior used to forecast the technology’s impact.

A system that is valuable because nobody has defenses can lose value when defenses appear.

A system that initially looks threatening can become ordinary once norms and safeguards develop.

A technology that substitutes for labor can induce demand and create complementary work.

The world moves back.

An eighth discipline is to preserve the possibility of genuine surprise.

Not every future is a replay of a previous mania.

History provides structures, not scripts.

The railway analogy can help us think about overbuilding and network infrastructure. It cannot tell us the maximum capability of machine intelligence. The radio analogy can help us think about cheap distribution and attention. It cannot tell us whether synthetic media will destabilize authentication in a particular election. Automation history can inform labor expectations. It cannot settle whether AI will eventually automate a far wider share of cognitive tasks than previous systems.

The past deserves respect and not obedience.

This is where the title Technomania needs its final qualification.

Mania is not excitement.

Excitement is often appropriate.

A child seeing Saturn’s rings through a telescope, a researcher watching a protein structure emerge from a model, an engineer seeing a machine perform a task nobody had automated, a patient receiving a treatment created by new science—none of that needs to be apologized for in the name of sophistication.

Cynicism is not maturity.

The disease begins when excitement becomes authority.

The machine works, therefore the machine decides.

The company grows, therefore the company knows the future.

The curve rises, therefore the curve continues.

The old institution is clumsy, therefore every function it performs is obsolete.

The new system is decentralized, therefore power is decentralized.

The output is fluent, therefore the judgment is sound.

The task is automated, therefore the person is redundant.

The resource is abundant, therefore scarcity is over.

Those are not technological statements.

They are political, economic, and philosophical claims wearing a lab coat.

The correct response is not to remove the lab coat.

It is to inspect the seam.

Technologies deserve to be judged at full strength. The strongest case for nuclear power should include its real energy density and operating record. The strongest case for the internet should include its genuine democratization of access. The strongest case for crypto should include what programmable settlement and global digital dollars actually enable. The strongest case for AI should include measured productivity gains and rapid capability improvements.

A weak opponent produces a weak book.

Then the social claim should be forced to stand on its own evidence.

Will this lower prices?

Will it broaden ownership?

Will workers gain leisure?

Will power decentralize?

Will inequality fall?

Will truth improve?

Will institutions become more legitimate?

Those outcomes do not come free with the machine.

They have to be built.

This is the political responsibility technomania avoids by describing outcomes as inevitable. If a better future is inevitable, nobody has to organize for it. If automation naturally creates abundance, distribution can be postponed. If connectivity naturally produces democracy, platform governance can be ignored. If intelligence naturally produces wise decisions, authority can be delegated without redesigning accountability.

Inevitability is an excuse not to govern.

The historical record is more demanding.

Railways required rules.

Electricity required institutions.

Broadcasting required governance.

Nuclear power required safety cultures.

The internet required standards and security.

Social platforms required moderation whether their founders wanted the responsibility or not.

AI will require an institutional settlement we do not yet know how to build.

The fact that the settlement is unfinished should not be mistaken for evidence that the technology is fake.

It is evidence that the technology is young.

There will be bad laws.

There will be bad products.

There will be ridiculous valuations.

There will be genuine breakthroughs.

There will be workers harmed by transitions and workers empowered by them.

There will be companies selling ordinary automation under grand names and systems that quietly exceed what serious people thought possible a few years earlier.

There will be things called impossible that happen.

There will be things called inevitable that never do.

The useful stance is not balance.

Balance implies two fixed sides and a point between them.

The useful stance is revision.

Take the strongest evidence available.

Make the smallest claim that explains it.

Follow the consequence into the next layer.

Look for the complement.

Look for the new scarcity.

Ask who acquires power.

State what would change your mind.

Then update.

A civilization capable of technological miracles should also be capable of changing its opinion without humiliation.

That may be the rarest technology in this book.

Technomania is what happens when the tool becomes a theory of history.

We do not need fewer tools.

We need better theories of history.
