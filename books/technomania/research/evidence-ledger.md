# Evidence Ledger

This ledger is a working epistemic map, not a substitute for chapter-level sourcing. Claims can move between classes as stronger evidence arrives.

## Class 1 — verified / documented

### Historical technologies

- Rail, telegraph, electrification, broadcasting, nuclear fission, personal computing, the internet, smartphones, public-key cryptography, distributed ledgers, and modern machine-learning systems all produced real technical discontinuities in the domains discussed. The manuscript does not treat any of these as “mere hype.”
- U.S. government records document Lyndon Johnson’s 1964 concern with automation, dislocation, and poverty amid technological abundance and the creation of a National Commission on Technology, Automation, and Economic Progress.
- NASA records document the 1961 lunar goal, the geopolitical context around it, successful achievement by 1969, and ambitious post-Apollo proposals that were not funded on the trajectory some planners imagined.
- NASA / NTRS records document Hugh Dryden’s explicit warning that astronautics forecasting was closer to prophecy than established science and that fundraisers/salesmen and responsible engineers naturally see the future differently.
- NRC historical reconstruction documents Lewis Strauss’s 1954 “too cheap to meter” prediction, the later dispute over whether he meant fusion or fission, the speech’s fission-heavy context, and his later reply about commercial atomic piles.
- DARPA and NSF official histories document ARPANET, packet switching, the transition to TCP/IP, NSFNET’s role, its rapid growth, and the 1995 commercial transition.
- Computer History Museum materials document links among Whole Earth / People’s Computer Company / Homebrew-era cultures and early personal computing.

### Crypto / digital finance

- BIS publications document that decentralized-finance systems reproduce many familiar financial functions and risks even when technical architecture changes.
- BIS 2026 analysis states that approximately 98% of stablecoin value was dollar-denominated in its dataset/assessment and treats digital dollarisation as a plausible macro-financial scenario in some economies.
- BIS Project Atlas and related work document serious central-bank efforts to measure crypto and DeFi flows; this establishes policy relevance, not endorsement.

### Artificial intelligence

- ILO 2025 work estimates that roughly one in four workers globally are in occupations with some degree of generative-AI exposure and concludes that transformation is more likely than outright redundancy for most exposed jobs under its methodology.
- IMF 2024 work estimates roughly 40% of global employment is exposed to AI, with higher exposure in advanced economies and both substitution and complementarity channels.
- BIS 2026 publications document a macroeconomically significant AI investment boom in data centers, specialized semiconductors, cloud infrastructure, and related hardware.
- BIS’s 10 September 2026 synthesis cites task-level studies showing roughly 10–65% productivity gains and 20–50% time savings in selected studied tasks. The manuscript treats this as task evidence, not a measured aggregate productivity effect.
- BIS 2026 work documents increasing debt/private-credit financing around AI investment and models the possibility of contest-driven overinvestment. Modeling is not evidence that a bust is inevitable.

## Class 2 — credible but incomplete

- The claim that recurring technology manias often accelerate useful infrastructure build-out even when investors overpay. Historical rail and telecom cases support the pattern, but a rigorous cross-period quantitative comparison remains to be built.
- The claim that falling production cost systematically shifts economic rents toward complements such as attention, verification, distribution, authority, identity, energy, or trust. Strong case evidence exists; a general causal estimate does not.
- The claim that AI will create large productivity gains at firm and economy scale. Task evidence is substantial and investment behavior is strong, but aggregate total-factor-productivity effects remain uncertain.
- The claim that AI may compress skill gaps in some workflows because less experienced workers often receive larger measured task-level gains. Evidence exists in selected studies; whether this generalizes across occupations is open.
- The claim that automating junior tasks can weaken apprenticeship pipelines. Mechanism is credible and observable in some domains, but long-run profession-level evidence for generative AI is still developing.
- The claim that open models may decentralize inference while frontier training remains concentrated. Technically plausible and currently visible in market structure, but concentration can change as hardware and algorithms evolve.
- The claim that generative AI layered onto social-media recommendation materially changes persuasion economics. Capability and incentive logic are clear; robust population-scale causal evidence is still incomplete.

## Class 3 — disputed / weakly established / claims not treated as proof

- Specific dates for AGI, transformative AI, self-improving AI, post-scarcity, or the end of large occupational categories.
- Claims that AI has already caused a particular macroeconomic share of layoffs unless firm-level evidence separates automation from restructuring, demand changes, interest rates, offshoring, and other causes.
- Claims that blockchain or crypto eliminate the need for trust or intermediaries in a general social sense.
- Claims that the internet inherently decentralizes political power.
- Claims that social media inherently democratizes politics or truth.
- Claims that a benchmark result alone establishes professional replacement.
- Claims that large capital expenditure proves final demand sufficient to justify the investment.
- Claims that a patent, white paper, demo, or vendor benchmark proves scalable efficacy.

## Class 4 — speculative synthesis / open conjecture

- **Technomania thesis:** major technology hype cycles often begin with a correct perception of a real technical discontinuity and fail when that result is promoted into an unsupported theory of institutions, economics, politics, or morality.
- **Scarcity-migration thesis:** successful technologies more often relocate scarcity than abolish it; the durable economic power often moves to the complement that becomes binding after the breakthrough.
- **Boring-layer thesis:** the strongest sign a technological revolution is becoming durable infrastructure is growth of maintenance, standards, audit, liability, training, incident response, and other unglamorous institutional functions.
- **Infrastructure-after-bubble thesis:** some speculative investment booms can generate socially useful infrastructure even when private returns disappoint; this should be tested rather than assumed for AI data centers.
- **Authority scarcity thesis:** if AI makes expertise-like analysis abundant, accountable authority, provenance, proprietary context, and trusted responsibility may appreciate in relative value.
- **Synthetic-consensus thesis:** mass institutional use of similar generative systems may increase correlated assumptions in strategy, analysis, policy language, and organizational decision-making even without centralized coordination.
- **Automation-curriculum thesis:** removing routine novice work may create a hidden long-term training deficit unless institutions deliberately replace experience formerly accumulated through repetition.

## Evidence hygiene rules for later passes

1. Do not cite a forecast as evidence of the forecasted outcome.
2. Do not convert exposure estimates into displacement estimates.
3. Do not convert task-level productivity into aggregate productivity without an explicit causal bridge.
4. Do not convert technical decentralization into economic or political decentralization without layer-specific evidence.
5. Do not call an investment boom a bubble solely because it is large.
6. Do not call a technology socially successful because adoption is high.
7. Do not treat a failed company as disproof of the underlying technical capability.
8. Do not treat a successful company as proof its market structure is inevitable.
9. Prefer contemporaneous primary sources for historical predictions over later paraphrase.
10. Keep known incentives beside testimony: builders, investors, incumbents, regulators, labor groups, and governments all occupy interested positions without thereby becoming unreliable.
