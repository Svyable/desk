# Chronology

## 1830s–1840s — speed becomes a social theory

- **1830s onward:** railways become the first industrial technology in this book to make “annihilation of space” rhetoric culturally durable. The technical fact is faster overland movement; the larger claim is that geography itself is receding.
- **1840s:** Britain’s Railway Mania turns a real network technology into a capital-allocation frenzy. The episode is useful precisely because the railway survives the speculation.

## 1840s–1860s — information separates from transport

- Electrical telegraph networks sever message speed from the speed of a messenger. Authentication, routing, trusted gathering, and strategic control of cables become newly important complements.
- The recurring metaphor of a planetary nervous system begins to acquire technical plausibility without producing planetary political unity.

## Late nineteenth to early twentieth century — electricity becomes infrastructure

- Electrification spreads through cities, factories, homes, transport, and communications.
- Productivity gains depend not only on generators and motors but on complementary redesign: factory layout, standards, distribution, metering, safety, financing, and institutions.
- The technology becomes more consequential as it becomes less visible.

## 1920s–1930s — radio promises universal reach

- Broadcasting sharply lowers the marginal cost of reaching an additional listener.
- Educational radio becomes one early form of the recurring claim that cheaper distribution of expert content can transform education.
- Commercial entertainment, advertising, politics, public service, and propaganda compete for the same capability.

## 1940s–1950s — atomic abundance

- Nuclear fission demonstrates extraordinary energy density.
- **16 September 1954:** AEC chairman Lewis Strauss tells the National Association of Science Writers that future generations may enjoy electrical energy “too cheap to meter.” Later historical argument disputes whether the famous sentence primarily referred to fission or fusion; NRC reconstruction shows that the surrounding speech was heavily concerned with fission and civilian atomic power and that Strauss later repeated the basic forecast when asked about commercial atomic piles.

## 1957–1969 — the decade in which technological prophecy wins

- **1957:** Sputnik demonstrates Soviet orbital capability and intensifies U.S. political urgency.
- **1960:** Hugh Dryden writes that forecasting astronautics is effectively prophecy and explicitly distinguishes the future seen by fundraisers/salesmen from that seen by engineers responsible for successful launches.
- **12 April 1961:** Yuri Gagarin orbits Earth.
- **25 May 1961:** Kennedy asks Congress to commit the United States to landing a man on the Moon and returning him safely before the decade ends.
- **1968:** Apollo 8 orbits the Moon.
- **20 July 1969:** Apollo 11 lands. The success becomes a permanent cultural argument for audacious technological goals.
- **1969:** post-Apollo planning includes large stations, reusable transport, lunar-base concepts, and Mars planning that do not continue on Apollo’s budget trajectory.

## 1964 — automation becomes a national political problem

- **9 March 1964:** Lyndon Johnson proposes a National Commission on Automation and Technological Progress, explicitly pairing extraordinary living standards with dislocation, job loss, and poverty amid plenty.
- **19 August 1964:** Johnson signs the law creating the National Commission on Technology, Automation, and Economic Progress.

## 1970s–1980s — computation moves toward the individual

- Microprocessors and hobbyist computing reduce the institutional scarcity of computer access.
- Whole Earth / People’s Computer Company / Homebrew-era cultures join technical access to ideas of individual agency and institutional bypass.
- Personal computers make spreadsheets, word processing, programming, games, databases, and desktop publishing mass-market activities.

## 1969–1995 — a network becomes the commercial internet

- **1969:** ARPANET’s first four nodes establish a packet-switched research network.
- **1983:** ARPANET transitions to TCP/IP.
- **1986:** NSFNET launches as a research backbone connecting supercomputing centers and academic networks.
- **1993:** NSF reports more than two million computers connected, up from roughly two thousand in NSFNET’s early period; Mosaic accelerates public web adoption.
- **1995:** NSF retires its backbone as commercial internet infrastructure takes over.

## 1990s–2000s — the web and the bubble

- The commercial web lowers publication, communication, and storefront-creation costs.
- Search, discovery, identity, payments, hosting, and customer acquisition emerge as crucial complements.
- **1997:** Nathan Myhrvold publishes “The Dawn of Technomania,” useful here as a contemporaneous artifact of internet-era technological exhilaration.
- **1999–2001:** dot-com valuations and capital spending overshoot in many firms while the underlying network continues spreading after the crash.

## 2000s–2010s — the institution moves into the pocket

- Smartphones combine network access, sensors, cameras, maps, identity, payments, media, work, and social systems into an always-carried interface.
- Social platforms sharply lower publication friction while algorithmic recommendation becomes a new attention-allocation layer.
- The political argument moves from whether ordinary people can publish to who controls ranking, moderation, identity, and platform access.

## 2009 onward — cryptographic institutional substitution

- Blockchain systems demonstrate that some forms of transaction ordering and settlement can occur without a single conventional ledger operator.
- The strongest rhetoric expands from technical verification to generalized claims about eliminating trust and intermediaries.
- Repeated exchange, smart-contract, bridge, custody, governance, and stablecoin failures expose the institutional functions that protocol-level decentralization does not automatically remove.

## 2024–2026 — AI moves from capability shock to infrastructure cycle

- **2024:** IMF estimates roughly 40% of global employment is exposed to AI, while distinguishing substitution from complementarity.
- **20 May 2025:** ILO’s refined global exposure index estimates one in four workers are in occupations with some degree of generative-AI exposure and argues transformation is more likely than outright redundancy for most exposed jobs.
- **2025–2026:** frontier-model development becomes coupled to a very large data-center, semiconductor, cloud, networking, and power-infrastructure build-out.
- **7 January 2026:** BIS publishes on the shift from internally funded AI capital expenditure toward debt and private-credit financing.
- **14 July 2026:** BIS Working Paper 1367 models an AI investment race with possible contest-driven overinvestment and explicitly compares the dynamics with canals, railways, and the dot-com era. It is a model, not a forecast that a bust must occur.
- **28 July 2026:** BIS Bulletin 130 emphasizes potentially large but uncertain and uneven macroeconomic productivity effects from AI.
- **10 September 2026:** BIS General Manager Pablo Hernández de Cos summarizes task-level studies showing roughly 10–65% productivity gains and 20–50% time savings in selected domains, while also describing investment and financial-stability risks. The manuscript treats these figures as a synthesis of bounded studies, not evidence that the economy has already achieved comparable gains.

## The chronology’s caution

The recurring pattern is not “new technology appears, mania follows, reality disappoints.” Several forecasts are fulfilled, sometimes spectacularly. The more useful chronology is:

1. a capability becomes real,
2. a public narrative promotes it into a larger social outcome,
3. capital and institutions reorganize around the expectation,
4. complements and bottlenecks become visible,
5. the mature technology settles into a form that is often more consequential and less mythic than either early advocates or critics expected.
