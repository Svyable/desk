# Defeat Conditions and Research Priorities

A book about technological overclaiming should not protect its own synthesis from failure.

## Defeat conditions for the central thesis

### 1. Scarcity migration proves too flexible to predict anything

The “scarcity moves” thesis is weakened if it can only identify complements retrospectively. A stronger version should predict, before full market settlement, which complements become relatively more valuable when a capability becomes cheap.

**Test:** select several current AI workflows and preregister expected bottlenecks—verification, proprietary data, authority, energy, distribution, or integration—then compare those predictions with observed cost and profit migration over time.

### 2. Large technological discontinuities routinely produce proportional system-cost declines

The component-to-system warning is weakened if historical comparison shows that system costs usually track the breakthrough component closely once deployment begins.

**Test:** build common cost decompositions for electrification, computing, telecommunications, solar, storage, and AI systems across early and mature periods. Measure how much of the initial cost decline survives after complements are included.

### 3. Decentralization at the technical layer reliably predicts decentralization of economic power

The layer-specific power thesis is weakened if open protocols and edge capability consistently produce flatter ownership and market concentration over mature cycles.

**Test:** compare protocol openness with concentration measures across web hosting, search, app distribution, cloud, crypto exchanges/stablecoins, open-source software, and AI inference/training.

### 4. Technology narratives have little effect on capital allocation or adoption

The book treats hype partly as a coordination technology. That claim is weakened if investment, labor migration, complementary infrastructure, and policy show little measurable response to narratives after fundamentals are controlled.

**Test:** exploit identifiable forecast shocks, public demonstrations, policy announcements, and narrative shifts to look for changes in investment and hiring independent of measured capability.

### 5. AI task gains translate rapidly and proportionally into aggregate productivity

The book emphasizes the missing institutional middle between task studies and macroeconomics. If economy-wide productivity gains closely and quickly track measured task gains, that caution is overstated.

**Test:** follow sector-level adoption with matched firm and labor data; distinguish output gains, price changes, hours, employment, and capital deepening.

### 6. Automation does not materially damage apprenticeship pathways

The automation-curriculum thesis is weakened if professions successfully reduce novice routine work without later shortages of experienced workers or deterioration in expert performance.

**Test:** compare organizations with high and low automation of entry-level tasks while measuring promotion, retention, error rates, training cost, and later senior competence.

### 7. Authority does not become a scarce complement to cheap analysis

The authority-scarcity thesis is weakened if AI-generated professional-quality analysis commoditizes licensed or accountable professional services without preserving premiums around responsibility, execution rights, access, or trust.

**Test:** track price and employment changes separately for analysis tasks and authority-bearing tasks in law, medicine, accounting, engineering, and finance.

## Strongest disconfirming cases to seek

The next research pass should actively search for technologies where:

- the initial utopian social promise was fulfilled almost exactly,
- new bottlenecks did not become comparably important,
- decentralization remained decentralized after scale,
- a hype cycle accurately priced future demand rather than overbuilding,
- institutional resistance was mostly incumbent rent protection rather than hidden function,
- automation translated into durable leisure rather than increased output expectations,
- a general-purpose technology diffused with unusually little complementary organizational redesign.

A book that cannot absorb such cases is merely another narrative machine.

## Highest-priority historical research

### Railway Mania

- British parliamentary authorizations and railway bills from the 1840s.
- Contemporary prospectuses and shareholder material.
- Construction-cost and traffic forecasts versus realized outcomes.
- Which failed or restructured lines left infrastructure later used profitably.
- Distributional effects on towns bypassed versus connected.

### Telegraphy

- Contemporary primary uses of “annihilation of space/time” language.
- Cable company records and transatlantic-cable financing.
- Reuters / Havas / Associated Press formation as evidence of news-gathering scarcity after transmission cost fell.
- Military and diplomatic examples where faster communication increased central control rather than simply reducing distance.

### Electrification

- Firm-level evidence on productivity before and after factory-layout redesign.
- Historical rate regulation and utility-franchise disputes.
- Rural versus urban electrification timing and complement costs.
- Cases where local generation and network dependence evolved differently.

### Educational radio

- Primary school and university radio curricula.
- Contemporary claims about replacement or supplementation of teachers.
- Usage/completion evidence rather than only program existence.
- How advertising and entertainment economics competed with educational objectives.

### Nuclear power

- Direct Strauss speech/transcript preservation in addition to NRC reconstruction.
- Utility executive reactions in 1954–1960.
- Historical construction-cost series by country and standardized/non-standardized fleet.
- Financing-cost decomposition versus fuel cost.

### Apollo and post-Apollo

- Congressional appropriations and NASA budget share over the Apollo period.
- Post-Apollo Space Task Group alternatives and Nixon-era decisions.
- Compare bounded mission criteria with later “moonshot” policy programs whose goals are social rather than technical.

### Personal computing

- Homebrew Computer Club newsletters.
- People’s Computer Company primary publications.
- Whole Earth Catalog / Stewart Brand primary materials.
- Early trade-press forecasts about household and office computing.

### Commercial internet and dot-com boom

- Primary 1990s analyst forecasts for traffic, e-commerce, and advertising.
- Fiber build-out, bankruptcy, and later utilization data.
- Company-level examples where the technological thesis was right but value capture failed.

### Social media

- Platform-era primary claims about democratization and political participation.
- Research separating publication access from attention concentration.
- Longitudinal concentration of creator income/audience.
- Evidence on recommendation-driven behavior changes that avoids simplistic causation.

### Crypto and stablecoins

- Separate protocol-level decentralization from exchange, custody, issuer, and interface concentration.
- Stablecoin reserve and transaction evidence.
- Cross-border use cases where digital-dollar access changes currency substitution.
- Cases where programmable settlement measurably lowers cost versus merely changes the technical rail.

## Quantitative appendices worth building

### A. Boom comparison matrix

Columns:
- technology,
- breakthrough period,
- primary scarcity attacked,
- capital intensity,
- unit-cost decline,
- adoption time,
- key complement,
- financing boom,
- observed overbuild,
- investor outcome,
- social infrastructure legacy,
- eventual regulatory settlement.

Candidate rows: British rail, U.S. rail, electrification, commercial radio, nuclear power, telecom fiber, dot-com/web infrastructure, smartphones/mobile broadband, crypto infrastructure, AI data centers.

### B. Forecast error matrix

Classify predictions as:
- capability right / timing right / institution right,
- capability right / timing wrong,
- capability right / institution wrong,
- capability wrong / social outcome partly right through another technology,
- wrong in both capability and social outcome.

This would prevent the book from treating all “failed predictions” as one category.

### C. Scarcity migration table

For each case:
- what became cheaper,
- what became more valuable,
- evidence of price/rent migration,
- whether the new scarcity was predicted in advance,
- whether the complement was later commoditized.

## Manuscript priorities

- Bring every numbered chapter above the Desk 3,000-word minimum using primary cases, not repetition.
- Bring chapter-only manuscript above 65,000 words.
- Deepen Chs 2–5 with primary nineteenth/early-twentieth-century material.
- Add quantitative tables only where measurement earns them; keep them out of prose when they would create pseudo-precision.
- Audit repeated rhetorical constructions (“X did not end Y,” “scarcity moved,” “which layer?”) so the recurring concepts remain motifs rather than generated scaffolding.
- Preserve Apollo as a genuine counterexample rather than a fairness token.
- Preserve the possibility that AI capability advances faster than the book’s institutional caution expects.
- Preserve the possibility that the current AI capital cycle earns its investment rather than assuming historical overbuild.
