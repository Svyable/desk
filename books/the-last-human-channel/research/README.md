# Research Brief

## Thesis under test

*The Last Human Channel* tests the proposition that artificial intelligence changes statecraft less by replacing diplomats than by changing what is scarce inside diplomacy.

The strong, brittle version — "AI makes diplomacy too fast for humans and therefore causes war" — is not the book.

The durable version is:

> As analysis, translation, surveillance, drafting, simulation, and synthetic persuasion become cheaper, diplomatic advantage shifts toward verification, attention, authorized intent, institutional judgment, alliance interoperability, and trusted human channels. Governments that accelerate routine cognition while preserving deliberate friction around consequential decisions should outperform governments that confuse machine-speed output with political understanding.

The evidence must be allowed to weaken this claim.

## Core empirical questions

1. **Communications speed:** How did earlier communications technologies change diplomatic autonomy, centralization, and crisis timing?
2. **AI adoption:** Which AI capabilities are actually entering foreign-ministry, alliance, intelligence, and mediation workflows?
3. **Decision quality:** Does faster analytical production improve decisions, or can it create overconfidence, option overload, and pressure to act?
4. **Authentication:** Which technical and procedural methods can establish the provenance and authority of diplomatic messages in a synthetic-media environment?
5. **Crisis stability:** Under what conditions can AI-enabled warning or decision support compress time in ways that increase miscalculation risk?
6. **Negotiation:** Where can AI improve preparation, draft comparison, translation, and mediation support without changing the underlying bargaining problem?
7. **Alliances:** What does AI interoperability require beyond compatible software — shared data standards, review practices, confidence language, and political trust?
8. **Distribution of power:** Does widely available AI reduce the analytical advantage of large states, or do compute, security, proprietary data, and institutional capacity preserve it?
9. **Regime type:** Do authoritarian systems adopt AI faster because they face fewer procedural constraints, and if so, do information distortion and leader insulation offset the speed advantage?
10. **Human relationships:** Which diplomatic functions remain dependent on personal access and trust once open-source information and machine analysis become abundant?

## Falsification and counter-evidence

Every chapter should carry a live note titled **What would make this false?**

Important counterclaims include:

- Faster information can prevent escalation by reducing uncertainty rather than increasing pressure.
- AI systems can provide structured alternative hypotheses and improve deliberation rather than compress it.
- Most consequential state decisions may remain slow because institutions, law, politics, and military command already impose durable review.
- Deepfakes may become a manageable nuisance as publics and governments adapt to provenance and verification practices.
- Diplomacy may become more formal and protocol-driven, reducing rather than increasing the importance of personal relationships.
- Large states may gain more from AI than small states because they possess superior classified data, compute, cyber defenses, and technical talent.
- Shared commercial models can increase interoperability and common understanding rather than create correlated failure.
- Authoritarian governments may gain genuine speed advantages without suffering the decision-quality penalties democracies often assume.
- AI-supported mediation may uncover bargains that human teams routinely miss.

The book should distinguish evidence against the thesis from evidence that merely qualifies its scope.

## Evidence lanes

### I. Communications revolutions

Use the telegraph, telephone, radio, secure teletype, the Washington-Moscow direct communications link, satellite communications, and video conferencing to trace how speed changes diplomatic discretion and crisis practice.

Primary sources should dominate.

### II. Foreign-ministry AI adoption

Track real deployments in the U.S. State Department and other foreign ministries where reliable public evidence exists. Separate announced strategies, pilots, operational systems, and aspirational use cases.

### III. Crisis decision support

Use military AI governance, deterrence research, exercises, and historical crisis literature to examine how machine recommendations interact with human authority.

Do not infer live nuclear or military integration from generic strategy documents.

### IV. Authentication and provenance

Track deepfake detection, cryptographic provenance standards, secure official channels, call-back procedures, digital signatures, and institutional methods for distinguishing technical authenticity from political authorization.

### V. Negotiation and mediation

Study AI-assisted mediation, treaty comparison, translation, bargaining simulation, and drafting. Preserve the distinction between information-processing problems and distributional conflicts over interests.

### VI. Alliances

Use NATO's responsible-AI and interoperability work as a central case. Examine how allied systems share not only data but evidentiary standards, uncertainty, and authorization.

### VII. U.S.-China strategic communication

Follow official and Track II discussions where AI, military risk, or crisis stability are addressed. Avoid treating dialogue as agreement. Mutual interest can exist without mutual trust.

### VIII. Small states and middle powers

Look for foreign ministries where AI materially broadens language coverage, country monitoring, consular services, or negotiating preparation. Test whether these gains alter diplomatic leverage.

### IX. Regime type and information quality

Compare speed, control, censorship, internal dissent, leader access, and error correction. Avoid assuming either democracy or authoritarianism has an inherent general advantage.

## Planned figures

1. **Diplomatic latency** — message travel and decision cycles from sailing ship to telegraph to direct link to AI-assisted analysis.
2. **The speed budget** — routine tasks that can be accelerated versus decisions where preserved friction may protect options.
3. **The authentication stack** — provenance → identity → authority → intention → credibility.
4. **From source to principal** — transformations between raw information and an AI-assisted senior briefing.
5. **The alliance confidence problem** — how identical labels can conceal different evidentiary standards.
6. **Analytical abundance** — which capabilities diffuse to small diplomatic services and which remain scale-dependent.
7. **The human channel** — formal, military, intelligence, diplomatic, and backchannel paths used during crises.

## Source discipline

- Prefer official documents, archival records, primary strategy papers, treaty texts, transcripts, and original research.
- Mark archived government webpages as archived; do not imply they describe current policy without current confirmation.
- Distinguish strategy, announced intent, pilot use, and routine operational deployment.
- Never attribute an AI-generated recommendation to a government unless the government has documented that use.
- For synthetic-media cases, separate confirmed manipulation from early reporting that was later revised.
- Record publication date and access date for all web sources.
- Do not use an institution's description of its own AI program as proof of effectiveness.
- Treat think-tank simulations as research evidence, not descriptions of government behavior.
- Preserve disagreements between American and Soviet records rather than harmonizing them.
- Do not quote a historical figure or official without verifying the primary or archival source.
- For every major policy prescription, identify the failure mode it introduces.

2. Build a verified timeline of AI deployment inside foreign ministries beyond strategy statements.

4. Build a U.S.-China strategic-communication chronology focused on military and AI risk reduction.

6. Interview mediation practitioners about where AI helps and where confidentiality or political authority blocks it.
7. Develop comparative small-state cases rather than assuming diffusion from tool availability alone.
