# When Everyone Has an Analyst

For most of the modern diplomatic era, analysis was rationed by payroll.

A foreign ministry could care about many countries. It could not study all of them equally.

Some embassies had large political and economic sections. Others had a few officers covering several portfolios. Intelligence agencies concentrated resources on adversaries, wars, nuclear programs, terrorism, major economies, and whatever crisis had risen to the top of the morning brief. Expertise accumulated unevenly because people, language training, travel, secure systems, and time were expensive.

This scarcity shaped foreign policy more than governments liked to admit.

A country could matter and still receive little sustained attention because it was not currently urgent. A small state's internal politics could be known to a handful of specialists and almost no one else. A trade rule, provincial election, port concession, regulatory change, or military procurement decision might sit in open sources for months before someone with the right context noticed why it mattered.

The information existed.

The attention did not.

Artificial intelligence changes this equation before it changes anything more dramatic.

It lowers the cost of first-pass attention.

A system can read and translate material from hundreds of outlets. It can monitor speeches, regulations, corporate filings, parliamentary debates, social-media accounts, procurement notices, shipping data, and economic releases. It can group recurring themes, retrieve earlier statements, identify changes in language, and prepare summaries for officers who would never have had time to read the underlying volume.

The United States Department of State made this logic explicit in its first enterprise AI strategy, released in 2023. The strategy described uses ranging from foreign-policy analysis and translation to public diplomacy and administrative work. The Department's AI materials later highlighted Northstar, a system used to search and translate media content, generate summaries, and analyze the information environment.

These are not science-fiction applications.

They are the automation of work diplomatic services already do.

That makes them more important, not less.

The spectacular AI scenarios attract attention because they look like a break with the past: autonomous weapons, synthetic leaders, machine negotiators, systems that recommend escalation. But institutions are usually transformed first by tools that make ordinary work easier.

The spreadsheet changed governments without becoming head of government.

Search engines changed intelligence work without issuing orders.

AI can change diplomacy long before anyone delegates a treaty negotiation to a model.

The simplest effect is coverage.

Imagine a foreign ministry that previously assigned one officer to follow five countries. That officer had to choose what to read. Local-language newspapers competed with diplomatic reporting, economic data, meetings, cables, headquarters requests, and administrative obligations. The result was not negligence. It was triage.

Now imagine that officer receives a daily machine-generated map of developments across all five countries, with links to original sources, uncertainty labels, translations, and comparisons against the last year of official language.

The officer has not become omniscient.

The officer has acquired leverage.

A small diplomatic service can gain even more.

Large powers have historically enjoyed an analytical advantage because they could maintain broad networks of embassies, intelligence agencies, research organizations, military attaches, trade offices, and specialists. Smaller states often compensated through concentration. They knew their region deeply, cultivated relationships, and chose issues where their attention could matter.

AI does not erase the advantage of scale.

It can reduce the penalty for being small.

A ministry with fifty excellent diplomats may be able to monitor a world that previously required five hundred people to follow at the same level of surface coverage. The machine cannot attend a funeral, notice a room's social hierarchy, cultivate a source over ten years, or decide which apparently minor official will become a future president. But it can prevent human experts from spending half their week searching for documents and translating routine text.

This creates a geopolitical effect that is easy to overlook.

Analytical capability diffuses.

The countries that benefit most may not be those with the best frontier model.

They may be those that adapt their institutions fastest around capable models available to many users.

This is a familiar pattern in technology. Possessing the most advanced machine matters. So does knowing how to use a less advanced one throughout an organization.

For diplomacy, that means the important unit may be the workflow rather than the benchmark.

Can a ministry connect models to trusted archives?

Can it preserve source links rather than passing around untraceable summaries?

Can it use secure systems for sensitive material?

Can officers challenge a model's answer?

Can the system retrieve what the institution already knows rather than repeatedly rediscovering it?

Can analysts record why they rejected a machine-generated interpretation so the same mistake does not return tomorrow?

These questions are less dramatic than asking whether AI will replace diplomats.

They are also closer to where advantage is likely to accumulate.

The Department of State's own framing is revealing. Its strategy emphasized infrastructure, workforce, responsible use, and innovation. Those categories sound bureaucratic because they are bureaucratic. A foreign ministry does not become capable because someone purchases access to a model. It becomes capable when the tool fits into classification rules, records systems, training, authorities, procurement, accountability, and ordinary habits.

Institutions metabolize technology slowly.

Adversaries may not wait.

This creates the second effect of analytical abundance: the baseline rises.

When one government can answer a question in an hour that once required a week, other governments face pressure to match it.

A minister may ask why a briefing on a new coalition agreement is not ready when commercial tools can already summarize every party manifesto.

An embassy may be expected to respond immediately to an online narrative because the ministry can see that narrative spreading in real time.

Negotiators may arrive at a meeting with machine-generated comparisons of every proposal the other side has made in the previous decade.

The tool that reduces workload can increase demand.

This is one of the central paradoxes of abundance.

When analysis becomes cheaper, governments do not necessarily do the same amount of analysis with fewer people.

They ask for more analysis.

The number of questions expands.

A country that previously received a weekly summary may receive a daily one. A minister who once asked for three options may ask for ten. A negotiating team that once compared five precedents may search five thousand. Every public statement can be scored, translated, contrasted, and modeled.

More analysis can improve decisions.

It can also bury the decision-maker.

The old scarcity forced prioritization. Someone had to decide which facts were worth carrying into the room.

AI weakens that constraint.

The result is a new professional skill: choosing what not to know right now.

This sounds perverse. Foreign policy failures are often explained as failures of information. Warning signs were missed. Expertise was ignored. A relevant historical case never reached the principal. Why would anyone defend less information?

Because information and attention are different resources.

The amount of material that can be generated for a president, prime minister, foreign minister, ambassador, or commander is effectively unlimited.

Their attention is not.

A machine can create a hundred plausible scenarios before breakfast. The principal can still only think seriously about a few.

Abundance therefore increases the importance of editorial judgment.

Which uncertainty matters enough to put on the first page?

Which alternative explanation is dangerous to omit?

Which apparent trend is merely a statistical artifact?

Which claim comes from a source the institution has reason to distrust?

Which issue can be delegated, and which one requires the minister to sit with ambiguity?

These are analytical questions, but they are also questions of institutional character.

A healthy organization can use AI to widen the field of view while preserving the distinction between signal and possibility.

An unhealthy one can turn possibility into paperwork.

The danger is not only volume.

It is fluency.

Machine-generated analysis often arrives in complete sentences. This sounds trivial until one considers how much bureaucratic authority is communicated through form.

A rough note signals incompleteness.

A finished memorandum signals that someone has done the work.

A polished machine output can look like the second while containing the epistemic status of the first.

This is especially dangerous in foreign affairs because many questions do not have clean answers.

Why did a government move forces near a border?

Is a leader's threat intended to deter, intimidate domestic opponents, reassure a military faction, improve a bargaining position, or prepare public opinion for action?

How much economic pain will a country accept before changing policy?

Will an ally honor a commitment under a scenario the treaty never anticipated?

Does a faction inside an authoritarian system have influence or merely visibility?

These questions invite inference.

A model trained on enormous amounts of language is very good at producing inferences that sound like answers.

The discipline of diplomacy is partly learning which questions are not answered merely because someone can state a view confidently.

Human analysts fail this test too.

Institutions have spent decades developing devices to manage that fact: confidence levels, sourcing standards, dissent channels, estimative language, red teams, intelligence community coordination, legal review, interagency processes, and the informal practice of asking the person in the room what the sentence actually rests on.

AI does not eliminate the need for those devices.

It increases the number of outputs that may need them.

This changes the value of expertise.

There is a common fear that when a model can explain a country, language, treaty, or political movement, the specialist becomes less necessary.

Some specialist tasks will become less scarce. A diplomat no longer needs to spend half a day producing a rough translation of a routine speech if a machine can generate a usable first pass in seconds. An analyst does not need to remember exactly which archive contains a ten-year-old communique if search can retrieve it instantly.

But expertise is not merely stored text.

An expert knows where the text lies.

That is a different form of knowledge.

An expert knows which official statistics are politically sensitive, which newspaper is used for signaling, which translation of a constitutional phrase carries the least distortion, which historical analogy local elites consider insulting, and which apparent contradiction is ordinary because two agencies have never agreed on the issue.

Expertise is a map of exceptions.

AI systems can learn many exceptions.

The problem is knowing when the current case is one of them.

The best outcome is not the replacement of expertise but its multiplication.

A specialist who can interrogate a large corpus quickly may spend less time retrieving facts and more time examining the deviations that matter. A negotiator can compare proposed language against decades of agreements without delegating the decision about which precedent is politically usable. An ambassador can ask a system to surface local commentary outside the mission's normal reading list and then decide which voices are actually influential.

The human role moves upward only if the institution allows it to.

There is no law requiring saved time to become better judgment.

It can become more meetings.

This matters for another reason: analytical abundance can encourage leaders to bypass institutions.

A president or minister can ask a general-purpose model for a view directly.

That may sometimes be useful. It can also create the illusion of independent analysis when the model is recombining public material without access to classified intelligence, private diplomacy, institutional history, or the uncertainties that specialists would place around the answer.

The ease of asking becomes part of the risk.

Before AI, a leader who wanted a country assessment usually had to ask someone whose professional identity was attached to the response. The chain could be slow and political, but responsibility was visible.

With a model, the answer can appear without an accountable author.

Responsibility then migrates to the user.

The leader must know what kind of question the system is capable of answering.

That is a demanding standard to place on busy people.

It is why AI literacy in diplomacy cannot mean teaching officials a few prompting techniques.

It has to include institutional skepticism.

What data could this system have seen?

What data could it not have seen?

Is it retrieving sources or generating from memory?

Does the answer distinguish a confirmed fact from a plausible inference?

Is the system optimized to be helpful, and if so, does helpfulness make it too willing to complete a theory the user already prefers?

What would make the answer false?

These questions should become as ordinary as asking who wrote a cable.

The final effect of ubiquitous analysis may be the most geopolitical.

If many governments gain capable analytical tools, information advantages become harder to preserve at the surface level.

Commercial satellite imagery already reduced the monopoly governments once held over some forms of strategic observation. Open-source investigators can track ships, aircraft, fires, construction, and military equipment. Machine translation reduces language barriers. Models make large document collections searchable by people who do not know the archive's internal vocabulary.

Secrecy remains powerful.

But obscurity becomes weaker.

A government can no longer assume that a complicated public record will remain effectively invisible because no rival has enough analysts to read it.

This changes the value of the things that cannot be cheaply reproduced from open information.

Private access.

Institutional trust.

Human sources.

Secure communications.

The ability to distinguish a real signal from a performance.

The ability to ask a counterpart what a move means and believe, within limits, that the answer carries information.

Analytical abundance therefore leads back to diplomacy.

The more governments can know from machines, the more valuable it becomes to know what the machines cannot settle.

This is not a defeat for AI.

It is the normal consequence of a technology becoming widespread.

Once everyone has a calculator, arithmetic stops being the advantage.

Once everyone has a search engine, finding a public document stops being the advantage.

Once everyone has an analyst, the advantage moves to asking the question that the analyst cannot answer alone.

The distribution of analytical abundance also creates a less obvious vulnerability: dependence on common tools.

If dozens of governments use the same commercial models for translation, summarization, coding, document search, and first-pass analysis, they may gain capability while acquiring correlated weaknesses.

The old intelligence failure was often local.

One agency misunderstood a source. One embassy missed a political shift. One analyst selected the wrong analogy.

The new failure can be shared.

A widely used model can contain the same blind spot everywhere it is deployed. A translation system can normalize an ambiguous phrase in the same direction across several ministries. A retrieval system can rank the same public sources as authoritative. A model fine-tuned around one country's assumptions can quietly carry those assumptions into another country's workflow.

This is not a reason to build every system domestically.

Most countries will not, and should not, attempt to recreate the entire AI stack as an exercise in symbolic sovereignty.

It is a reason to understand dependency.

Governments already manage dependence on telecommunications vendors, cloud providers, semiconductor suppliers, software companies, and satellite systems. AI adds another layer because the dependency can shape interpretation rather than merely transport information.

A foreign ministry needs to know what it is renting when it rents intelligence amplification.

The answer includes the model.

It also includes update policy, data residency, security controls, logging, retention, availability, pricing, censorship behavior, model routing, tool permissions, and the provider's ability to change the product faster than the bureaucracy can revise its procedures.

This gives large technology companies a peculiar position in international affairs.

They do not become sovereign states.

But some of their systems become part of the cognitive infrastructure through which sovereign states see the world.

That is strategically important.

It creates incentives for governments to diversify tools, maintain independent validation, preserve local expertise, and retain the ability to operate when a provider is unavailable.

The ideal architecture may look less like one national super-model than a diplomatic portfolio.

Different systems for different classes of work.

A secure internal model or retrieval system for sensitive archives.

Commercial frontier systems for unclassified research where appropriate.

Specialized translation tools.

Open-source models for tasks requiring greater control.

Human review concentrated where error carries political consequence.

The portfolio approach is less elegant than the dream of a single machine assistant.

Real institutions are usually less elegant than dreams.

They contain layers because failure has layers.

Analytical abundance also changes the relationship between headquarters and the public.

A foreign ministry can now generate public explanations faster, in more languages, and in more formats. It can monitor narratives across regions and respond to false claims quickly. These capabilities are attractive because diplomacy is partly a competition over interpretation.

But the same tools can tempt governments to mistake message volume for influence.

Public diplomacy is not customer support.

The objective is not to answer every mention.

A government that responds automatically to every hostile narrative may amplify marginal claims, exhaust credibility, or produce a voice so constant that nothing sounds important.

Abundance creates a need for silence as strategy.

The valuable public statement may be the one delivered rarely enough that people notice it.

This is another form of scarcity moving upward.

When text is expensive, producing text is power.

When text is cheap, restraint becomes part of power.

The same is true inside negotiations.

Generative systems can produce possible language for an agreement, identify clauses that have appeared in earlier treaties, compare competing drafts, and expose inconsistencies. A mediation team can use AI to track dozens of unresolved issues and model where compromise might exist. Recent work on AI-assisted mediation has emphasized precisely these possibilities while warning that trust, confidentiality, spoiler management, and political judgment remain central.

This is where the phrase "negotiation advantage" needs care.

A model may improve preparation.

It does not change the fact that the other side has interests.

There is no prompt that converts an unacceptable concession into an acceptable one.

There is no simulation that guarantees a leader will survive the domestic politics of a compromise.

There is no optimizer that can make a security guarantee credible if the state offering it lacks the will or capability to honor it.

Diplomatic problems are often not puzzles waiting for a sufficiently intelligent solver.

They are conflicts among people who understand the problem and prefer different outcomes.

AI can help locate the bargaining space.

It cannot create bargaining space where none exists.

The distinction is essential because the language of intelligence encourages a solver's mentality.

If a model can beat humans at difficult games, diagnose patterns in medical images, and write complex software, why should a border dispute or cease-fire be different?

Because international agreements are not evaluated against a fixed objective function.

The objective function is part of the dispute.

One party may value territory.

Another may value regime survival.

Another may value alliance credibility.

Another may care about a domestic constituency whose preferences are not stable.

The weights can change while the negotiation is underway.

A human mediator spends time discovering what can be traded, what cannot, what can be renamed, what can be delayed, and what can be left deliberately ambiguous.

A model can assist that work.

It cannot declare the political meaning of the result.

This is where the abundance of analysis can become a form of humility if institutions use it well.

A machine can generate five explanations for an adversary's behavior instead of allowing one explanation to harden too quickly.

It can retrieve cases that contradict the preferred analogy.

It can identify where experts disagree.

It can show that the evidence supports several narratives rather than one.

This use of AI is less glamorous than prediction.

It may be more valuable.

The system does not tell the diplomat what will happen.

It reminds the diplomat how many things could be happening.

That is a powerful capability in a profession where catastrophe often begins with excessive certainty.

The countries that learn to use AI this way may acquire an advantage that is difficult to measure on conventional leaderboards.

They will not necessarily produce the fastest briefing.

They may produce the briefing that leaves the right question open.
