# AI Arms Control Without AI Arms Control

The most ambitious version of AI arms control begins with the technology.

Define the dangerous systems.

Limit them.

Verify compliance.

Punish violations.

That sequence is attractive because it resembles familiar arms-control logic.

The problem is that artificial intelligence is not one weapon.

It is a general-purpose capability embedded in sensing, logistics, communications, cyber defense, intelligence, planning, vehicles, software development, translation, targeting support, maintenance, and countless civilian systems.

The same model family can assist a diplomat, a programmer, an engineer, an intelligence analyst, or a military planner.

The object is difficult to bound.

That does not make risk reduction impossible.

It suggests that the first useful agreements may need to govern the seam where machine capability becomes dangerous behavior rather than the machine in the abstract.

## The category problem

Traditional arms control benefits when the controlled object is legible.

A missile has dimensions.

A launcher has a location.

A warhead can be counted under particular arrangements.

A test produces observable signatures.

Software resists this clarity.

A model can be copied.

Updated.

Fine-tuned.

Wrapped in another system.

Used for several purposes.

Moved between civilian and military environments.

Combined with tools that change its effective capability.

Even the word *autonomous* can describe several different degrees of machine initiative depending on the system and mission.

If diplomacy waits for universal definitions, the technology will keep moving while the negotiation debates nouns.

There is another route.

Start with verbs.

What behavior do we want to make less likely?

## Govern the behavior

Arms control has always contained behavioral rules as well as object limits.

States notify tests.

Avoid certain deployments.

Maintain communication channels.

Permit inspections.

Observe geographic constraints.

Follow procedures around dangerous encounters.

The same logic can apply to machine-enabled military risk.

Two rivals can disagree about what counts as artificial intelligence and still agree that an emergency leader channel should not be used to transmit synthetic impersonation.

They can disagree about autonomy and still establish a contact procedure after an unmanned system behaves unexpectedly near contested forces.

They can disagree about model transparency and still agree that strategic warning systems require human-confirmable procedures before conclusions become executable action.

The agreement attaches to the politically dangerous behavior.

That is often easier to identify than the technology producing it.

## A behavior contract needs five parts

A narrow rule becomes operational when five things are clear.

Trigger.

Required behavior.

Prohibited behavior.

Evidence.

Recovery.

What event activates the rule?

What must the parties do?

What must they avoid doing while uncertainty remains?

What evidence shows that the rule was followed?

What happens after suspected violation or technical failure?

This is more useful than a broad statement that states should use AI responsibly.

For example, a crisis-authentication arrangement can define what counts as an emergency message, which channel establishes political authority, what parties do when a synthetic message appears elsewhere, what evidence confirms the authorized communication, and how compromised credentials are replaced.

The model behind the fake may be impossible to classify.

The behavior around the fake can still be governed.

## Risk-reduction islands

The useful image is an archipelago.

Not one comprehensive AI treaty.

Several risk-reduction islands.

One island might be crisis authentication.

Official emergency channels preserve human-verifiable identity and authority.

Synthetic impersonation of heads of government or senior military leaders is treated as a particularly dangerous act.

Backup verification routes are maintained.

Another island might be nuclear command and control.

Governments make declaratory commitments that nuclear-use decisions remain under human political responsibility and that machine support does not become an independent authorization path.

Another might concern strategic warning.

States discuss categories of cyber or space interference that could be misinterpreted as preparation to blind nuclear forces.

Another might concern military autonomy.

Parties establish incident channels for unmanned systems crossing boundaries, behaving unexpectedly, or losing command links in contested environments.

Another might concern testing.

Certain large exercises involving new machine-enabled systems could be notified in ways that reduce surprise without revealing technical secrets.

None solves AI governance.

Each reduces one pathway to catastrophe.

## Islands need bridges

An archipelago can become incoherent if each narrow arrangement invents different terminology, authorities, and incident processes.

The islands should therefore share some infrastructure.

Common authentication principles.

Common incident severity classes.

Common distinction between preliminary and confirmed reporting.

Common rules for preserving original messages versus machine transformations.

Common escalation contacts where appropriate.

Common expectations about acknowledging receipt before substantive response.

These bridges let learning travel across narrow agreements without turning the agreements into one comprehensive treaty.

The objective is reusable risk-reduction infrastructure.

The first protocol should make the second one cheaper to negotiate and easier to exercise.

## Verification has levels

The strongest objection is verification.

A state cannot easily inspect another government’s software stack.

Models change quickly.

Commercial services may be part of military workflows.

A system can behave differently depending on prompts, tools, data, or configuration.

This makes treaty-style verification difficult.

But verification is not binary.

There is a spectrum.

At one end is direct inspection of hardware, code, or facilities.

Farther down are demonstrations, test observations, logs, incident reports, cryptographic attestations, agreed exercises, reciprocal technical briefings, or third-party evaluation.

Farther still are observable behavioral commitments.

Did the emergency channel remain human-authenticated?

Was the required notification sent?

Did a state respond through the agreed incident mechanism?

Did the declared exercise conclude on schedule?

Did an autonomous platform follow the stand-down procedure?

A weakly verifiable agreement can still be useful if the behavior is narrow, the stakes are high, and violation creates political or operational evidence.

The relevant comparison is not always a perfectly verified treaty.

It may be no shared procedure at all.

## Evidence should match the claim

A narrow agreement should specify what kind of evidence can support what kind of conclusion.

A successful exercise demonstrates that a procedure can work under exercised conditions.

It does not prove it will work routinely.

A signed declaration establishes public policy.

It does not prove every internal system implements that policy correctly.

A cryptographic attestation can prove something about a software or hardware state.

It does not prove political intent.

An incident report can reveal a failure class.

It may not establish complete attribution.

Matching evidence to claim prevents confidence-building measures from becoming propaganda about capability.

That discipline is especially important in AI governance because the underlying technology changes faster than political descriptions of it.

## Declaratory commitments are not nothing

Declarations are often dismissed because they are cheap to make.

That skepticism is healthy.

But declaratory policy can still alter expectations.

A government that publicly commits to meaningful human responsibility for nuclear use creates a standard against which later doctrine, procurement, and exercises can be evaluated.

A state that says it will not use synthetic impersonation on an emergency channel creates political cost if evidence of violation emerges.

The declaration is not self-enforcing.

It creates a reference point.

Reference points matter in diplomacy because they make some forms of behavior more legible as deviation.

The stronger version combines declaration with procedure.

What does “human responsibility” mean operationally?

Which decisions require human authorization?

How is that authorization authenticated?

What happens if communications are degraded?

What safeguards prevent a decision-support system from turning into an execution path by default?

The closer a declaration gets to process, the more useful it becomes.

## Declarations need implementation traces

A state need not reveal sensitive internal architecture to make a declaration more credible.

It can describe categories of procedure.

Named political authority remains necessary.

Certain machine outputs are advisory.

Loss of a model does not remove the human authorization path.

Selected safeguards are exercised periodically.

Changes to the decision-support environment trigger renewed review.

The point is not to expose secrets.

It is to create observable implementation traces that make the declaration more than a sentence.

The evidence will remain incomplete.

Incomplete evidence can still improve interpretation if its limits are clear.

## The incident-reporting opportunity

Aviation, maritime safety, cybersecurity, and other high-risk fields learn from incidents because unusual failures reveal what ordinary testing misses.

Military AI creates the same need and a harder secrecy problem.

Governments will not share every failure involving sensitive systems.

They may still benefit from exchanging selected incident information when the failure mode itself creates common risk.

A system lost communication and behaved unexpectedly.

A model misclassified an object under unusual sensor conditions.

An autonomous platform crossed a boundary after navigation degradation.

A machine-generated message was mistaken for an authorized instruction.

The useful exchange does not always require source code or classified mission details.

It may require only enough information to identify the failure class and the behavior expected if it happens again.

This creates a possibility for anonymized or sanitized incident exchanges, military-to-military technical workshops, or third-party repositories focused on high-consequence failure patterns.

The aim is not transparency for its own sake.

It is shared learning where repeated surprise is strategically dangerous.

## Incident classes should have severity

A useful incident architecture distinguishes severity.

Technical anomaly with no external effect.

Loss of expected human supervision.

Boundary crossing.

Unintended interaction with foreign forces.

Synthetic message entering an official workflow.

Interference with strategic warning or communications.

Machine output materially shaping a high-consequence decision under disputed conditions.

Different classes should trigger different reporting and contact requirements.

Without severity classes, governments face a choice between reporting everything and reporting almost nothing.

A graded system preserves attention for the incidents most likely to create reciprocal strategic risk.

## Testing the seam

Joint or reciprocal exercises can provide another form of confidence building.

The parties do not need to expose internal models.

They can test crisis procedures around outputs.

What happens when an autonomous system approaches a boundary unexpectedly?

What happens when a synthetic leader message appears during an exercise?

What happens when an early-warning system produces a machine-assisted high-confidence anomaly at the same time a hotline is degraded?

What happens when one side believes an AI-enabled cyber tool has affected strategic communications?

A tabletop exercise can reveal terminology gaps before the real event does.

One side says *autonomous incident* and means loss of human control.

The other hears *autonomous incident* and thinks merely machine-assisted navigation.

Those semantic differences sound small until decisions depend on them.

## Exercises need red-team violation

A protocol should be tested under suspected noncompliance, not only cooperative use.

One side sends the required notification late.

One side claims its autonomous system lost command while the other side sees behavior suggesting deliberate control.

A synthetic message appears and the emergency channel is temporarily unavailable.

A party believes the other side has manipulated logs.

The exercise should ask what happens next.

Does every suspected violation terminate the mechanism?

Can the procedure continue in degraded mode?

Which evidence is enough for provisional restraint?

What can be investigated later?

Risk reduction matters most when trust is already damaged.

The protocol should be designed for that state.

## Suspension is different from collapse

Agreements need a suspension state.

A party may believe one mechanism has been compromised without wanting to abandon the entire risk-reduction relationship.

Suspend one data exchange.

Quarantine one credential.

Pause one exercise category.

Retain the hotline.

Retain incident acknowledgement.

Retain the prohibition on unauthenticated emergency instructions.

This segmentation is strategically useful.

A system that can only be fully active or fully abandoned is brittle.

The same design principle that helps digital networks helps diplomatic networks.

Limit the blast radius of failure.

## Unilateral measures can be reciprocal without a treaty

Not every useful restraint requires negotiated symmetry.

A government can impose internal rules against letting machine recommendations become nuclear-use authorization.

It can preserve independent warning channels.

It can require senior review before cyber operations against strategic command systems.

It can publish enough about those safeguards to make its posture legible.

Another state may adopt comparable measures for its own reasons.

This is reciprocal restraint without a formal bargain.

The weakness is obvious: either side can change policy unilaterally.

The advantage is speed.

When formal negotiation is politically impossible, parallel unilateral measures can establish norms of behavior that later become easier to discuss.

Diplomacy sometimes follows practice rather than creating it.

## Parallel measures need observability

Reciprocal restraint becomes stronger when each side can observe enough to know that a comparable practice exists.

Not the exact internal system.

The policy category.

The relevant exercise behavior.

The presence of a contact channel.

The fact that certain safeguards have been reviewed.

The fact that a declared procedure was used during an incident.

Observability helps unilateral restraint create expectation.

Expectation can later create political cost for reversal.

That is how practice can harden into norm without pretending it has already become law.

## The dual-use excuse

There is a temptation to treat dual use as a reason not to regulate anything.

If the same AI methods appear in civilian and military systems, perhaps control is futile.

That conclusion confuses control of capability with control of context.

Encryption is dual use.

Satellites are dual use.

Cyber tools are dual use.

Communications networks are dual use.

Governance still attaches special rules to particular environments and consequences.

A machine-learning model used to optimize warehouse inventory and the same model architecture embedded in a strategic warning workflow do not create the same political risk.

The relevant unit is system-plus-mission.

Diplomacy does not need to govern every copy of the capability to govern selected uses around catastrophic decisions.

## Mission transitions are the dangerous seam

A system can move from low-risk to high-risk use without changing its underlying model.

A general analytic tool gains access to strategic warning data.

A logistics model begins making recommendations about nuclear-force movement.

A translation system becomes the default interpreter for emergency leader communication.

An autonomy stack used for navigation receives authority over weapons employment.

The governance trigger should therefore include mission transition.

When a system crosses into a higher-consequence context, what new review, authentication, logging, and human-control requirements activate?

This is more durable than trying to classify the model once and assume the classification follows every future use.

## The attribution problem

Synthetic media and cyber operations create another hard case: attribution.

A forged crisis message may be produced by a state, proxy, criminal actor, activist, or model operating with limited supervision.

A protocol that bans state impersonation does not automatically tell the recipient who created a fake.

This is where procedure again becomes more useful than broad prohibition.

The emergency channel should have a way to confirm or deny an apparent message quickly.

Governments should know whom to contact after suspected spoofing.

The initial task is not to prove attribution beyond doubt.

It is to stop an unauthenticated artifact from driving irreversible action.

Accountability can follow.

Crisis stabilization cannot always wait for forensic certainty.

## Stabilization and accountability need different clocks

This distinction should be explicit.

Stabilization clock: what must happen now to prevent a dangerous misinterpretation?

Accountability clock: what evidence is required before assigning responsibility and imposing consequences?

Conflating them creates bad choices.

If stabilization waits for courtroom-quality attribution, the crisis may outrun the investigation.

If accountability uses the low evidentiary threshold appropriate for provisional restraint, the state may punish the wrong actor.

A mature protocol lets the clocks run separately.

Act cautiously under uncertainty now.

Investigate rigorously before irreversible attribution later.

## Competitive incentives

Why would rivals agree to any of this?

Because some risks are genuinely reciprocal.

A state may believe it has an advantage in AI research and still fear a forged leader message.

It may want faster autonomous systems and still fear an accident with another nuclear power.

It may invest heavily in cyber operations and still prefer that strategic warning not become indistinguishable from a battlefield target.

Risk reduction works when the parties can separate a shared danger from the broader competition.

That separation will never be complete.

States will worry that transparency reveals weakness or constrains an advantage they hope to exploit.

The negotiation therefore has to stay narrow enough that mutual interest survives.

The question is not: do we trust each other’s AI strategy?

It is: is this particular failure mode bad for both of us?

## Reciprocal vulnerability is the bargaining asset

The strongest candidate measures are those in which both sides can imagine being the victim of the same failure.

False leader message.

Uncontrolled boundary crossing.

Misinterpreted strategic cyber effect.

Warning-system anomaly.

Loss of human authorization trace.

A narrow negotiation should make that reciprocity visible.

The agreement is not a concession to the other side’s technology policy.

It is self-protection against a shared failure class.

This framing can matter politically because it separates risk reduction from broader judgments about who is more responsible for the rivalry.

## The role of unofficial dialogue

When official relations are poor, technical and track-two channels can do preparatory work.

Researchers, former officials, military experts, standards bodies, and technical practitioners can compare terminology, identify failure modes, and develop candidate procedures before governments are willing to negotiate.

These discussions should not be romanticized.

They do not bind states.

They can still reduce the cost of later diplomacy by producing a vocabulary and a menu of narrower options.

In a fast-moving field, vocabulary is infrastructure.

Governments negotiate faster when they do not have to invent every category during the crisis itself.

## Vocabulary should have version control

The technology changes.

The diplomatic vocabulary should be allowed to change too.

Definitions need revision dates.

Examples.

Known ambiguities.

Terms retired because they became misleading.

New categories added after incidents.

This is version control for strategic language.

It avoids the illusion that one treaty definition can freeze a fast-moving technical field permanently.

The agreement can preserve the behavioral objective while updating the vocabulary used to describe the systems around it.

That is institutional adaptability.

## Do not wait for the perfect treaty

The history of dangerous technology contains a recurring policy error.

A comprehensive solution appears so difficult that narrow solutions are dismissed as inadequate.

Then nothing is built.

AI risk reduction should resist that trap.

A hotline protocol is not an AI treaty.

A nuclear-human-responsibility declaration is not a verification regime.

An autonomous-incident procedure is not a ban.

A cyber restraint around strategic warning is not general cyber peace.

Good.

Their value comes from being narrower than the entire problem.

A small agreement can be tested, practiced, revised, and expanded.

An impossible grand bargain cannot.

## Narrow agreements need retirement criteria

Small measures should not live forever simply because they were once politically difficult to create.

A technology may make the procedure obsolete.

A better mechanism may replace it.

A rule may create perverse incentives.

A notification category may produce too much noise.

A verification method may become insecure.

Each measure should have a review cycle and a retirement condition.

What evidence would show that it no longer reduces risk?

What failure rate is acceptable?

Which technology change triggers reevaluation?

What mechanism replaces it if retired?

This keeps the archipelago adaptive rather than turning it into another layer of inherited bureaucracy.

## A risk-reduction state machine

Narrow arrangements should have operating states.

Proposed.

Pilot.

Active.

Degraded.

Suspended.

Recovery.

Retired.

The state should depend on evidence.

A pilot proves the procedure can be exercised.

Active status requires agreed authorities and working channels.

Degraded status acknowledges partial failure without pretending the mechanism is healthy.

Suspension limits reliance after suspected violation.

Recovery requires a defined test.

Retirement removes obsolete rules instead of leaving them as paper commitments.

This state machine makes risk reduction more realistic.

Agreements are systems.

Systems need lifecycle governance.

## The risk-reduction ledger

A serious program should be able to answer:

What behavior is being governed?

What event triggers the rule?

What evidence shows compliance?

What evidence would show violation?

What can be verified directly and what remains declaratory?

Which mission transition activates higher safeguards?

What incident severity class requires contact?

How long is the stabilization clock?

How long is the attribution clock?

What happens after suspected noncompliance?

Can one mechanism be suspended without collapsing the others?

How is trust restored?

When was the procedure last exercised under adversarial conditions?

What vocabulary version is current?

What would cause the measure to be revised or retired?

The ledger makes narrow agreements cumulative rather than ceremonial.

## Governing the political seam

The principle is practical.

Govern the seam where risk becomes political.

The moment a machine output can be mistaken for authorized intent.

The moment an autonomous system can create an international incident.

The moment a cyber operation can be interpreted as an attack on nuclear warning.

The moment decision support can compress human deliberation into rubber-stamp approval.

Those seams are where a technical capability becomes a strategic event.

They are also where governments have the strongest reason to care.

AI arms control without AI arms control is therefore not a retreat from ambition.

It is a sequencing strategy.

Stabilize behavior where the stakes are clearest.

Build procedures that can be observed.

Give them failure states.

Exercise them under suspected violation.

Preserve the distinction between stabilization and attribution.

Revise the vocabulary as the technology changes.

Retire measures that stop working.

Create evidence about which restraints survive real pressure.

Then decide whether broader agreements are possible.

The technology may move too quickly for diplomacy to classify it once and for all.

Diplomacy can still decide what humans and machines should never be allowed to make ambiguous together.
