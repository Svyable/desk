# AI Arms Control Without AI Arms Control

Comprehensive treaties governing military AI face verification nightmares and dual-use porosity. Waiting for them may be a way of doing nothing under solemn language.

Risk reduction can still proceed through narrower bargains: commitments around nuclear command authority remaining human; notification of certain autonomous tests; shared evaluation benchmarks for crisis decision-support tools; agreements not to spoof specified official channels.

Why comprehensive control stalls.

Military AI is dual-use at the root. The same classes of models that aid logistics, intelligence fusion, and targeting research appear in civilian industry. Verification of "AI arms" lacks the counting metaphors that made certain nuclear and conventional arms control workable. Definitions of autonomy are fought over because definitions allocate legal exposure. A treaty that tries to govern "AI weapons" as a class invites word games and breakout.

UN discussions and responsible military AI declarations create normative pressure and vocabulary. They are not verification regimes. Distinguishing the two prevents false comfort. Vocabulary can still be useful: it gives diplomats and militaries shared nouns for talks that would otherwise dissolve into metaphors.

Pragmatism at the AI-nuclear nexus.

SIPRI's October 2025 work on pragmatic approaches to governance at the artificial intelligence–nuclear nexus emphasizes a practical point: states broadly agree on the importance of human control over nuclear decision-making, yet lack consensus on how to define or operationalize it. Without clarity, debates risk slowing norms while capabilities evolve. Function-specific talks — warning, targeting support, decision support, NC3 interfaces — are more promising than metaphysics of machine agency.

SIPRI's later survey work on AI in Chinese, Indian, and U.S. nuclear postures, norms, and systems (2026) underscores asymmetries of transparency and the fact that seeming convergence on "human control" language can coexist with integration of AI into nuclear-related systems. Dialogue that treats language as enough will be disappointed. Dialogue that maps functions and fears may still reduce risk.

U.S. and Chinese officials have, at times, jointly affirmed human control over nuclear weapons decisions while continuing to compete across conventional AI-enabled military applications. Affirmation is not inspection. It can still be a political hook for narrower technical talks.

Track II and the mapping of shared fears.

Brookings and other accounts of Track II discussions on AI and security — including Geneva-centered dialogues — show mutual interest in avoiding unintended escalation amid low mutual trust. Track II is not official agreement. It can map shared fears: misclassification of incidents, compressed decision time, synthetic spoofing of orders, and correlated model failures. Mapping fears is a precondition for protocols.

CSIS commentary urging common crisis-management benchmarks and model-behavior testing belongs in the same pragmatic family: treat evaluation practices as confidence-building measures, not as soft substitutes for hardware arms control. Methodology matters; vendor demos are not benchmarks.

Theater, checklists, and harm.

The counterclaim is blunt: verification may be too difficult and dual-use boundaries too porous for meaningful commitments. Some proposed rules would be theater.

Theater that changes bureaucratic checklists can still have marginal value — a required human signature, a required second path, a required test log. Theater that substitutes for operational safety engineering does harm by creating complacency. The diplomatic task is to prefer the first and reject the second.

Export control coalitions are a form of arms control by supply chain. They create frictions, workarounds, and diplomatic workload. They are not irrelevant merely because they are incomplete. They also create alliance seams discussed earlier; pragmatism includes managing those seams rather than pretending controls are costless.

Interruptibility as a negotiable design goal.

Interruptibility — the ability to halt machine-accelerated processes — is a design goal worth negotiating about. Technical talks about firebreaks in command-and-control systems, kill switches for certain autonomous functions, and human confirmation gates for nuclear-related recommendations are more concrete than debates about whether a model "wants" war.

Insurance markets for autonomous systems may impose discipline where treaties lag, especially for civilian and dual-use deployments that touch critical infrastructure. Academic benchmarks of model escalation tendencies are research signals, not deployment proof, and still useful for agenda setting if their limits are stated.

Regional forums and allied red lines.

Regional forums can incubate norms among states that will not wait for consensus among all nuclear possessors. Allied coordination on red lines prevents adversaries from exploiting split messaging. Ceasefire monitoring technology can be a confidence builder if parties accept shared sensor feeds with authentication — linking this chapter to the protocols chapter.

Whistleblower protections inside military AI programs are domestic governance with international externalities. A state that cannot hear internal warnings about unsafe integration will export risk.

What would validate pragmatism.

What would validate this chapter's pragmatism? Measurable adoption of narrow practices that reduce incident rates or clarify crises — answered authentic channels, exercised notification, published evaluation methods for crisis decision-support tools, durable human-control operationalizations. What would falsify it? Evidence that narrow deals systematically distract from necessary harder constraints — or that they are always broken without cost when convenient.

Diplomacy's comparative advantage is packaging mutual fear into procedures short of friendship. AI arms control without AI arms control is that packaging: not a joke, not a comprehensive treaty, but a portfolio of narrow bargains that keep machine-speed competition from becoming unplanned catastrophe.


The verification problem without metaphors.

Nuclear arms control could sometimes count launchers and warheads. Chemical weapons control could sample and inspect. Military AI offers fewer countable objects. Weights can be copied; models can be fine-tuned offline; inference can move to edge devices; civilian APIs can be redirected. A verification regime that pretends otherwise will train cynicism.

That cynicism should not end diplomacy. It should redirect it toward behaviors and interfaces: how humans authorize nuclear use; how warnings are confirmed; how tests of autonomous systems near crisis thresholds are notified; how official channels are protected from spoofing. Behaviors leave different evidence than "AI arsenals," and sometimes leave enough for confidence-building.

Political declarations as hooks, not ceilings.

Responsible AI in the military domain declarations, including U.S.-led political declarations and national statements, create hooks for follow-on work. Treating them as ceilings — "we declared, therefore we are safe" — is the failure mode. Treating them as openings for function-specific talks is the pragmatic mode.

Language about human control over nuclear decisions should be operationalized: which recommendations may be machine-drafted; which actions require two-person human rules; how warnings are displayed; how disagreement among systems is shown to leaders. Operationalization is where engineering and diplomacy meet.

Benchmarks as confidence-building measures.

Shared evaluation benchmarks for crisis decision-support tools sound utopian until one remembers shared interest in not misreading a demonstration. Rivals need not share model weights to discuss test scenarios: ambiguous incidents, spoofed orders, conflicting sensors, time pressure. Track II can pilot scenarios; Track I can later adopt sanitized versions.

CSIS proposals for common crisis-management benchmarks fit here if methods are transparent and limits stated. Benchmarks that only flatter sponsors will poison the well.

Export controls as incomplete arms control.

Chip and model export controls slow some diffusion pathways and create workarounds through third countries, smuggling, and indigenous substitution. Diplomacy must manage the coalition that enforces controls, the partners harmed by collateral effects, and the false comfort that a control list equals capability denial. Incomplete tools can still be useful if their incompleteness is priced in.

Domestic governance with externalities.

Fail-safe reviews, inspectorates, and whistleblower protections inside military AI programs reduce the chance that a state surprises itself — and everyone else — with an unsafe integration. Encouraging adversaries to maintain such domestic institutions is delicate; demonstrating one's own is partly exemplary diplomacy, partly self-interest.

Insurance and liability regimes for autonomous systems in civilian domains can create de facto standards that militaries notice. Diplomats who track those standards may find negotiation fodder that did not originate in Geneva.


Regional incubators.

Not every norm must be born universal. Regional groups facing shared risks — maritime incidents, missile warning ambiguities, critical infrastructure interdependence — can incubate notification and evaluation practices. Success stories can later travel. Failure stories should travel too, to prevent copy-paste of empty forms.

Allied red-line coordination remains essential. An adversary that hears three different AI escalation thresholds from three allies will probe the softest. Cognitive interoperability includes normative interoperability about machine-involved force.

Interruptibility talks.

Concrete agenda items for interruptibility talks include: human confirmation gates before nuclear recommendations become options of record; time delays that cannot be silently skipped; display standards that show machine confidence and dissent; and exercises that grade teams on successful interruption, not only on speed.

These items will be resisted as constraints on readiness. The counter is that uninterruptible speed is not readiness if it produces unplanned war. Packaging that counter as mutual fear, not as moral lecture, is diplomacy's job.

Measuring pragmatic success.

Count answered authenticated crisis messages; count joint or parallel exercises of notification; count published (even if high-level) evaluation methods; count durable operational definitions of human control. Do not count pages of draft treaties that never leave committees. Pragmatism without metrics becomes another form of theater.

AI arms control without AI arms control will look unsatisfying in headlines. It may still be the portfolio that fits the technology we actually have.


Historical humility about "impossible" controls.

Observers once argued that certain nuclear verification problems were impossible until politics produced enough shared fear to fund inspectors and instruments. Military AI may never yield neat counting. It may still yield inspectable interfaces and auditable authorization logs for specific functions. Diplomacy should look for those inspectable surfaces rather than declaring the entire domain ungovernable.

Conversely, history also shows decorative agreements that changed little. The pragmatic portfolio must be judged by behavior change, not by signature counts.

The nuclear interface as priority surface.

If diplomats can only work a few surfaces, the nuclear interface deserves priority: human control operationalization; warning confirmation; protection of official nuclear and missile warning channels from spoofing; and interruption gates for decision-support tools that touch NC3-adjacent functions. Conventional military AI competition will continue. Preventing that competition from cascading through nuclear interfaces is a narrower, higher-value goal.

Scenario exercises on AI and NC3 — including Institute for Security and Technology workshop reports — are research evidence about pathways, not proof of deployment. They are still useful for agenda setting if treated as scenarios.

Spoofing, deepfakes, and ordered chaos.

A forged order to a military unit, a deepfake of a leader announcing surrender or nuclear use, or a synthetic allied "request" for action can compress politics into panic. Narrow agreements and domestic protocols that treat such events as authentication tests first — not as facts — are risk reduction. Public education that leaders may be faked is delicate (it can also undermine real messages) and still increasingly necessary.

Ceasefire tech and shared feeds.

Where parties to a conflict accept shared authenticated sensor feeds for ceasefire monitoring, they create a micro-example of arms-control-like cooperation without settling larger AI rules. Mediators should treat authentication of those feeds as central, not as a technical afterthought. Spoiled feeds are spoiled peace processes.

Portfolio diplomacy.

The portfolio approach means multiple small bargains across bilateral, allied, regional, and domestic tracks, reviewed annually for breakage and benefit. It looks messy because the technology is messy. Comprehensive elegance that cannot be verified is a different kind of mess — one that fails quietly while speeches succeed.

Diplomacy packages mutual fear into procedures. That comparative advantage does not disappear because the feared object is software. It becomes more necessary.


What not to negotiate first.

Avoid opening with attempts to define "AI weapon" for a global ban. Avoid moralizing lectures that ignore dual-use civilian stacks. Avoid demanding transparency that no intelligence service will grant. Start with shared fears that officers on all sides already whisper: inadvertent escalation, spoofed orders, overconfident decision support, compressed time.

Those whispers are the raw material of pragmatic control. Treat them as such.

Closing the pragmatic case.

AI arms control without AI arms control is not a shrug. It is a refusal to let the perfect comprehensive treaty become the enemy of interruptible, authenticable, human-authorized practice. Build the narrow bargains. Measure them. Replace theater with checklists that change engineering. Keep talking about fears even when trust is low. The alternative is to wait for a verification utopia while machine-speed competition writes facts on the ground.


Definitions as battlegrounds.

Definitions of autonomy, "human control," "decision support," and "AI-enabled weapon" will be contested in every forum because each definition allocates legal exposure, export-control coverage, and domestic procurement pathways. Diplomats should expect definitional combat and still pursue function-specific practices that do not wait for perfect nouns.

A workable approach is to bind behaviors at interfaces: what must be true before a nuclear-related recommendation is shown to a principal; what must be true before an autonomous conventional system may continue an engagement beyond a time or geographic box; what must be true before an official channel message is treated as authentic. Interface rules can be clearer than ontological debates about whether a system is "truly" autonomous.

Lessons from earlier dual-use regimes.

Dual-use export controls on nuclear, chemical, and biological materials never produced perfect denial. They raised costs, forced choices, and created diplomatic workload. Military AI controls will be similar. Judging them by purity standards guarantees a verdict of failure. Judging them by marginal delay, coalition signaling, and leverage in wider talks is more honest.

Nonproliferation history also warns against overclaiming. Controls that are sold as absolute create credibility crises when leaks appear. Sell incompleteness honestly.

Track I hooks from Track II maps.

When Track II maps shared fears — compressed time, spoofing, overconfident tools — Track I diplomats need a conversion path: sanitized agendas, agreed experts who can brief officials, and pilot exercises that do not require full political trust. Without conversion paths, Track II becomes a perpetual seminar.

Geneva-centered and other dialogues on AI and escalation should be read for fear maps, not for illusory consensus. Mutual interest can exist without mutual trust; protocols chapter already made that point. This chapter adds: mutual interest can be packaged into evaluation and interruptibility talks without packaging into friendship.

Allied messaging discipline.

Allies that advertise different red lines for AI-involved escalation invite probing. Coordination does not require identical military systems; it requires enough normative interoperability that adversaries cannot cheaply split the coalition with a single synthetic incident. Public messaging should be pre-agreed for classes of fakes and ambiguous attacks.

Domestic fail-safes as offerings.

States can strengthen pragmatism by publishing, at useful altitude, how they keep humans in nuclear authorization chains and how they test decision-support tools. Publication has OPSEC costs. Selective transparency can still reassure rivals that certain nightmares are taken seriously — a form of diplomacy that does not require access to code.


Autonomous test notification.

Notification of certain autonomous system tests — especially those near borders, in contested domains, or involving swarm behaviors that could be misread — is a candidate narrow bargain. It will be resisted as revealing capability. So were other notification regimes. The diplomatic question is whether mutual misread risk outweighs secrecy preference for a defined class of tests.

Start small: notify tests in agreed geographic boxes or of agreed categories. Expand if habits hold. Collapse if used as deception. Design for misuse from day one.

Academic benchmarks and their limits.

Benchmarks that claim to measure "escalation tendencies" of models are research tools. They can mislead if treated as deployment certificates. Used carefully, they give diplomats nouns and graphs for conversations that would otherwise be pure anecdote. Insist on methodology disclosure; reject vendor-controlled "safety scores" as substitutes for government evaluation.

The portfolio, restated.

Narrow bargains on human nuclear control operationalization, anti-spoofing of official channels, autonomous test notification, shared crisis-tool evaluation methods, interruptibility gates, export-control coalitions, and domestic fail-safes — reviewed annually — compose a portfolio. It is not AI arms control in the comprehensive sense. It is how adults govern a dual-use general-purpose technology under rivalry.

Waiting for the comprehensive treaty while doing nothing else is a policy of speech. Packaging mutual fear into procedures is a policy of practice. This chapter chooses practice.


The temptation of solemn delay.

Comprehensive treaty processes can become ways of performing seriousness while capability races continue. Committees meet; definitions multiply; operational risk grows in parallel. Diplomats who care about risk reduction need permission structures at home that reward narrow bargains, not only grand initiatives that never close.

Legislatures and publics may prefer grand initiatives because they are legible. Educating them about portfolio risk reduction is part of the diplomatic task. So is refusing to overclaim what narrow bargains do.

NC3-adjacent tooling in public view.

Public records do not establish that nuclear states have surrendered launch authority to models. The relevant public conversation is about assistance that reshapes time and confidence: warning fusion, option generation, display design. Pragmatic talks can address those surfaces without demanding access to the most sensitive code. Display standards and human-confirmation gates are discussable even among rivals who will not share weights.

RAND and related research on AI and nuclear risk has long flagged tempo and decision-support pressures. Scenario workshops extend that tradition. Use them as prompts for diplomacy, not as evidence of what any particular state has fielded.

Anti-spoofing as a first bargain.

Among first bargains, anti-spoofing of specified official channels ranks high because the harm pathway is clear and the norm is easy to explain to publics: do not fake the other side's nuclear or diplomatic command messages. Verification will be imperfect. Naming the behavior still changes the political cost of being caught and creates a basis for domestic criminalization and alliance response.

Pair the norm with technical cooperation on detection where possible, and with domestic drills where cooperation is not.

Evaluation exchanges.

Rivals might exchange, at high altitude, how they evaluate crisis decision-support tools: what scenarios they use; whether they test spoofing; whether they grade interruption success. Full sharing is unlikely. Partial sharing can still reduce the chance that one side misunderstands the other's tool-induced behaviors in a crisis.

Allies should go further with each other: joint evaluation, shared uncertainty language, and annexes for machine-generated assessments — linking back to the alliance room.

When pragmatism fails.

Pragmatism fails if narrow bargains become excuses to avoid harder constraints that later become necessary, or if they are broken without consequence whenever convenient. Build consequences: publicity, reciprocal suspension, alliance responses, domestic legal penalties for spoofing. Without consequences, portfolio diplomacy becomes portfolio theater.

It also fails if it ignores conventional military AI competition entirely. Competition will continue; the goal is to keep it from cascading through nuclear and crisis-authentication interfaces. Border-drawing is the work.

A workable near-term agenda.

For the next few years, a workable agenda is: operationalize human control language at nuclear interfaces; negotiate or declare anti-spoofing norms for official channels; pilot autonomous test notification in limited boxes; run Track II evaluation scenarios that convert to Track I briefings; align export-control coalitions while managing allied seams; strengthen domestic fail-safes and selective transparency; measure answered authenticated messages and exercised notifications.

That agenda will not satisfy anyone who wants a single AI treaty. It may keep more people alive while the technology and the politics continue to move. Diplomacy has done harder things with uglier tools.
