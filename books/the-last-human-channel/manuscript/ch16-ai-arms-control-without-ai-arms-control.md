# AI Arms Control Without AI Arms Control

Comprehensive treaties governing military AI face verification nightmares and dual-use porosity. Waiting for them may be a way of doing nothing under solemn language.

Risk reduction can still proceed through narrower bargains: commitments around nuclear command authority remaining human; notification of certain autonomous tests; shared evaluation benchmarks for crisis decision-support tools; agreements not to spoof specified official channels.

Responsible military AI declarations and UN discussions create normative pressure and vocabulary. They are not verification regimes. Distinguishing the two prevents false comfort.

U.S.-China dialogues on AI and security, including Track II work, show mutual interest in avoiding unintended escalation without mutual trust on broader order questions. Dialogue is not agreement. It can still map shared fears.

The counterclaim is blunt: verification may be too difficult and dual-use boundaries too porous for meaningful commitments. Some proposed rules would be theater.

Theater that changes bureaucratic checklists can still have marginal value. Theater that substitutes for operational safety engineering does harm.

Pragmatic approaches at the AI-nuclear nexus emphasize function-specific talks—warning, targeting support, decision support—rather than metaphysics of machine agency.

Regional forums can incubate norms among states that will not wait for consensus among all nuclear possessors.

Export control coalitions are a form of arms control by supply chain. They create frictions, workarounds, and diplomatic workload. They are not irrelevant merely because they are incomplete.

What would validate this chapter's pragmatism? Measurable adoption of narrow practices that reduce incident rates or clarify crises. What would falsify it? Evidence that narrow deals systematically distract from necessary harder constraints—or that they are always broken without cost when convenient.

Definitions of autonomy will be fought over because definitions allocate legal exposure.

Tests and evaluations shared among rivals sound utopian until one remembers shared interest in not misreading a demonstration.

Whistleblower protections inside military AI programs are domestic governance with international externalities.

Insurance markets for autonomous systems may impose discipline where treaties lag.

Academic benchmarks of model escalation tendencies are research signals, not deployment proof, and still useful for agenda setting.

Allied coordination on red lines prevents adversaries from exploiting split messaging.

Ceasefire monitoring tech can be a confidence builder if parties accept shared sensor feeds with authentication.

The perfect must not be the enemy of the interruptible.

Interruptibility—ability to halt machine-accelerated processes—is a design goal worth negotiating about.

Diplomacy's comparative advantage is packaging mutual fear into procedures short of friendship.
