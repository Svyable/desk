# The Principal and the Machine

A foreign minister used to receive options after an institution had argued with itself.

The argument was often slow, incomplete, and shaped by bureaucratic interest. It was also a filter. Analysts disputed facts. Lawyers flagged authorities. Regional bureaus warned about second-order effects. Intelligence services assigned confidence levels. By the time a paper reached the principal, it had been bruised by people who would have to live with the consequences of being wrong in different ways.

Artificial intelligence compresses that sequence.

A senior official can now ask a system for options on a sanctions package, a demarche, a military signaling problem, or a negotiating brief and receive a polished answer before the relevant offices have finished clearing a memo. The output can cite open sources, summarize classified material that has been made available to it, propose talking points, and present dissent as neatly labeled alternatives. The temptation is obvious: why wait for the interagency if the machine already knows the file?

The question misunderstands what the wait was doing.

Some delay was waste. Some was the political work of forcing tradeoffs into the open.

When a principal receives machine-polished options early, the institution’s argument does not disappear. It arrives later, if at all, as commentary on a frame the leader has already seen. First frames matter. Cognitive research and bureaucratic experience alike suggest that early articulation of a choice set constrains what later looks reasonable. A model that writes well can therefore exercise power without formal authority—by defining the menu.

This is decision support in the strong sense.

Not a search tool buried in a staff office, but a presence near the person who can commit the state.

Intelligence communities have lived with a related problem for generations. Estimative products try to separate what is known, what is inferred, and what is guessed. Good estimates preserve dissent. Bad ones launder uncertainty into consensus prose. Generative systems are exceptionally good at consensus prose. They can make a thin evidentiary base sound like a brief. They can also, if designed for it, surface alternative hypotheses and neglected indicators. The technology does not dictate which use dominates. Institutional demand does.

If leaders reward speed and confident synthesis, systems will optimize for that.

If leaders reward calibrated uncertainty and rival explanations, systems can be pointed that way.

The medical and aviation analogies are useful and incomplete.

In those fields, automation often improves routine performance while creating new failure modes when humans overtrust fluent systems, undertrust them after rare errors, or lose the skills needed to intervene when the unusual case appears. Diplomacy has fewer clean outcome metrics than a stabilized approach path. A “good” foreign-policy decision may be one that avoids a war that never becomes visible as a counterfactual. That makes automation bias harder to measure and easier to miss.

There is also a status problem.

A memo from a named office carries institutional fingerprints. A model output can look authorless even when it was prompted, filtered, and selected by particular people. Authorlessness is politically convenient. It allows a recommendation to circulate without an obvious bureaucratic owner. It also weakens accountability when the recommendation fails. Diplomatic systems that care about responsibility should treat model output as speech by someone—because someone chose to bring it into the room.

The Cuban Missile Crisis remains instructive here for a reason that has little to do with silicon.

ExCom’s value was not that it produced a single correct algorithm for Soviet intentions. It was that multiple senior people were forced to argue in front of the president while events were still ambiguous. The process created a record of disagreement. It slowed some impulses and accelerated others. A modern principal with a powerful assistant model could recreate a simulation of that debate in minutes. The simulation might be illuminating. It would not be the same as having officials with careers, constituencies, and departmental knowledge contesting one another in real time. The machine can role-play the secretary of defense. It cannot put the secretary’s institutional weight behind the dissent.

What, then, is the right place for principal-level AI?

A workable answer begins with role separation.

Use models to retrieve, compare, and stress-test.

Be wary of letting them close.

Retrieval means making sure the principal sees the cable, the treaty clause, the prior commitment, the allied concern, or the contradictory intelligence report that a hurried staff might omit. Comparison means placing current options beside historical analogues without pretending the analogues decide the case. Stress-testing means asking what would falsify the preferred course, which assumptions are load-bearing, and how an adversary could misread the chosen signal. These are assistive functions. They expand the principal’s effective attention.

Closure is different.

Closure is the moment when a government decides that this demarche goes, that this red line is stated, that these forces move, that this bargain is accepted. Closure requires a person who can be answerable after the fact. A system can recommend. It should not become the repository of the decision’s legitimacy.

Nuclear and high-intensity military settings make the distinction non-academic.

Research on AI and strategic stability has repeatedly returned to the same pressure: conventional operations moving faster, warning systems processing more data, and decision makers fearing that delay equals vulnerability. In that environment, decision-support tools that compress options can look like survival equipment. Scenario exercises on AI and nuclear command, control, and communications have explored how assistance in warning, targeting, and option generation could alter time lines and confidence. The public record does not establish that any particular nuclear state has surrendered launch authority to a model. The relevant risk is subtler. Assistance that is usually helpful can still reshape what leaders treat as the available human interval for deliberation.

Governments that say humans remain in the loop should specify what the loop still contains.

Does the human see the raw indicators or only the machine summary?

Can the human reconstruct why the system ranked options as it did?

Is there a pause rule for actions that are hard to reverse?

Are dissenting institutional views still required to reach the principal even when the model is confident?

Without those details, “human in the loop” is a slogan covering automation of judgment.

The counterclaim is serious and should not be caricatured.

AI can improve senior decisions by forcing alternatives into view, retrieving neglected evidence, translating material the principal would not otherwise read, and reducing dependence on a single charismatic briefer. Some human advisory processes are captured by hierarchy, groupthink, or exhaustion. A model that can generate the case against the preferred option on demand may be a healthier irritant than a deferential staff. In complex sanctions design, alliance consultation, or large documentary negotiations, machine assistance can make the principal less ignorant without making the principal less responsible.

The issue is design under incentives.

If the system is used to skip institutional conflict, it will amplify centralization.

If it is used to prepare the principal for institutional conflict, it can improve it.

Centralization deserves a hard look because communications revolutions have always tempted it. The telegraph allowed capitals to supervise ambassadors more tightly. Secure video now lets leaders speak directly and constantly. AI adds a further centralizing lure: the belief that the center can understand the periphery in real time without relying as much on the periphery’s judgment. Sometimes that is true. Often it produces a center that is flooded, overconfident, and less aware of what its own dashboards cannot see.

A principal who receives a brilliant morning brief generated overnight may feel informed.

The brief’s fluency can hide the absence of a phone call that should have been made to an ambassador, an ally, or an adversary’s emissary. Knowing about a country is not the same as being in a relationship that can carry a difficult message. Decision support can crowd out the slower work of maintaining those relationships if leaders come to think the file is the terrain.

There is also the problem of correlated judgment.

If several governments, or several departments inside one government, rely on similar commercial models trained on overlapping public data, their “independent” analyses may share blind spots. Diversity of advisory systems then becomes a strategic asset. This is one reason some states will invest in sovereign models, specialized classified systems, or deliberately heterogeneous toolchains. It is also a reason to keep human dissenters who are not merely paraphrasing the same latent space.

What would make the worry in this chapter excessive?

If principal-level AI remains a staff convenience rather than a gatekeeper of choices; if institutions successfully require model outputs to arrive with evidence trails, confidence statements, and rival hypotheses; if leaders continue to insist on hearing named officials argue before irreversible acts; if faster retrieval mainly lengthens the time available for diplomacy by clearing underbrush. Those outcomes are possible. They are not automatic. They depend on habits that have to be trained and defended when speed becomes a status symbol.

The briefing room itself changes when machine drafts become normal.

Staff still prepare papers, but the principal may arrive having already queried a system overnight. The meeting then begins downstream of a private conversation between leader and model. Advisers who do not know what the leader has already seen are arguing uphill. Some governments will formalize this by logging principal prompts and requiring that overnight machine briefs be shared with the relevant principals’ committees. Others will treat the private query as an informal prerogative, the digital equivalent of reading the newspaper before the morning meeting. The informal path is comfortable. It is also a quiet transfer of agenda-setting power to whatever system the leader happens to trust that week.

Personnel politics follows.

Officers who are skilled at prompting, verifying, and challenging model output will become more valuable. Officers who only summarize what the machine already summarized will become less so. That shift can improve quality if verification skill is rewarded. It can degrade quality if fluency with tools is mistaken for judgment about politics. Training systems should therefore teach skepticism as a professional habit: how to ask for sources, how to force alternative frames, how to notice when a recommendation is optimized for sounding complete.

Legal advisers have a special role.

Many foreign-policy choices are constrained by authorities, treaty commitments, and domestic law. A model that proposes elegant options without flagging legal gates can create the illusion of freedom where none exists. Conversely, a model integrated with reliable legal corpora can prevent principals from wasting time on paths that counsel would later block. The integration has to be governed carefully. Legal interpretation is not a retrieval problem alone. Still, the principle stands: decision support that ignores institutional constraints is not support. It is fantasy generation adjacent to power.

Allied consultation adds another layer.

A principal who has already converged on a machine-shaped preference may enter calls with counterparts less curious and more evangelical. Alliance management depends on the appearance, and often the reality, of mutual influence. If leaders use private AI preparation to harden positions before hearing partners, consultation becomes theater. If they use it to understand partners’ constraints better—by reviewing prior statements, parliamentary pressures, and known red lines—it can improve listening. Same tool, opposite diplomatic effect.

Historical decision pathologies remain relevant.

Groupthink, dominance by a single adviser, wishful pattern recognition, and the suppression of dissenting intelligence did not wait for neural networks. AI can mitigate some of them by generating dissent on demand. It can worsen others by giving the dominant frame a veneer of computational inevitability. The difference often lies in whether the principal wants to be challenged. Technology rarely cures a leader’s appetite for confirmation. It can feed it more efficiently.

Classification and data boundaries shape what the machine is allowed to know.

A system confined to open sources may miss the cable that changes the picture. A system connected to sensitive material creates new espionage and leakage risks. A hybrid process—human analysts deciding what the model may see for a particular question—reintroduces friction that impatient users will try to bypass. Those friction points are not bugs. They are where accountability lives. A government that cannot explain what data informed a principal-facing recommendation will have trouble defending the decision later, to legislators, courts, allies, or history.

Time-of-day effects are mundane and consequential.

Overnight generation means that the most fluent synthesis may arrive when human challenge capacity is lowest. Weekends, holidays, and travel days create similar gaps. Adversaries do not schedule crises for the convenience of staffing models. Institutions need surge doctrines for human review when machine output is about to inform irreversible acts outside ordinary business hours. Without that, “human in the loop” becomes “human wherever the loop happened to catch someone awake.”

The economics of attention at the top are brutal.

Principals can give deep attention to only a few issues. AI promises to widen the set of issues on which a leader can sound briefed. The promise is partly true and partly dangerous. Sounding briefed is not the same as having judgment. A leader who can discuss twelve crises with equal fluency may have twelve shallow grips rather than three serious ones. Diplomatic systems should beware of using AI to create the appearance of omniscience at the center. Strategic concentration—deciding what the principal must personally carry—remains a human allocation problem.

Failure libraries would help.

Aviation improved partly by studying accidents without requiring each crew to rediscover the same error. Foreign ministries could maintain carefully governed records of cases in which machine-assisted advice misled, oversimplified, or omitted a decisive local fact. Such libraries are politically awkward because they create paper trails of fallibility. They are also how professions learn. Absent them, each office will treat its surprises as unique, and the same failure modes will recur under new interface designs.

What would falsify the chapter’s caution more strongly than earlier?

If controlled comparisons showed that principals using well-governed AI assistance systematically produced better-calibrated decisions—fewer neglected alternatives, faster recognition of contradictory evidence, no increase in irreversible acts taken on thin confidence. That evidence base does not yet exist in public form at diplomatic scale. Until it does, prudence looks like expanding assistive uses while refusing to let fluency replace institutional argument at the point of closure.

Succession and continuity create further exposure.

Models change. Vendors change. Prompt libraries become obsolete. A decision rationale that lived mainly in a chat history may be unrecoverable when investigators, legislators, or successor officials ask what the government thought it was doing. Document management rules written for memoranda must be extended to machine-assisted deliberative traces without creating surveillance theater that drives real work into private channels. The balance is difficult. Avoiding it is worse.

Smaller cabinets and presidential systems may feel the effect sooner than large bureaucratic states, simply because fewer people stand between the leader and a tool. That can be an advantage in clarity. It can also remove the accidental safeguards of delay. Constitutional design rarely contemplated a world in which the head of government could generate a private national-security staff of infinite patience at two in the morning. Norms will have to do some of the work that organograms cannot.

International comparisons will be politicized.

Observers will claim that one system decides faster because it uses AI more aggressively. Speed will be treated as strength. The historical record of crisis decision-making suggests that speed without comprehension is a familiar path to error. The right comparison is not who generated options first. It is who retained the ability to reverse course when new evidence arrived, and who could explain the decision to those who had to implement it.

A practical discipline follows.

Every consequential machine-assisted option put before a principal should answer four questions in human language: What is the claim? What is the evidence? What is missing? Who owns the recommendation if it is wrong? If those questions cannot be answered, the output is not ready for the top of government. It is still a draft inside a staff process—which is where most machine output should live.

The implementation problem is the final filter.

A principal may accept a machine-assisted option that looks coherent on the page and then discover that no ministry owns the tasks required to carry it out. Diplomatic instructions that are elegant in summary can be operationally empty. The old staff process, for all its flaws, often forced someone to ask who would call which ambassador, which legal instrument would be used, which ally would be notified in what order, and what would be said if the first démarche failed. A model can list those steps. It cannot recruit the officials who must feel responsible for them. Governments that skip that recruitment step will produce decisions that exist as language without existing as action.

This returns the argument to scarcity.

Analysis is becoming cheaper.

Ownership remains expensive.

The difference is subtle enough to be missed in demonstrations where a model produces a brilliant memo. Demonstrations measure fluency. Statecraft measures whether someone will still defend the choice when the costs arrive. A principal who understands that distinction will use the machine as a staff multiplier. A principal who confuses the two will eventually discover that the state cannot be run as a conversation with a system that never has to face a legislature, an ally, a budget committee, or the families who must attend a funeral.

Principal-level AI is most valuable when it helps leaders see ownership problems earlier—when it reveals that an attractive course has no plausible implementing coalition, or that two departments’ preferred options rest on incompatible facts. It is most dangerous when it helps leaders enjoy the feeling of decision without the burden of assembling the people who must execute and defend it.

The last human channel, at the level of the principal, is not a romantic preference for intuition over analysis.

It is the insistence that the person who can bind the state remain able to explain the decision after the model that helped prepare it has been updated, replaced, or forgotten.

A principal who cannot explain a decision without reopening the chat history has not yet made a decision the state can inherit. Inheritance is the quiet test of authority in a tool-saturated government.
