# The Principal and the Machine

Every bureaucracy has a speed limit.

The limit is not the rate at which paper can move. It is the rate at which an institution can turn disagreement into advice.

A foreign ministry, intelligence service, defense department, treasury, and leader’s staff all see the world differently. Their internal processes are slow partly because information has to travel and partly because people with different responsibilities have to argue.

Artificial intelligence can bypass part of that process.

A leader can ask for options and receive them before the institution has finished producing its own.

That is a remarkable capability.

It is also a constitutional problem inside the executive branch.

The issue is not whether the machine is allowed to decide. The issue is what happens when the machine changes who gets to frame the decision.

A polished briefing can create agenda power.

If an AI assistant presents three options, the room begins with those three. If it summarizes the intelligence, disagreements may appear as footnotes. If it predicts reactions, uncertainty can look like a single forecast. If it drafts a speech, the policy can begin to harden before the departments responsible for implementing it have agreed on the premise.

The principal gains informational independence from the bureaucracy.

That can be healthy.

Leaders are often trapped by the way institutions package choices. Bureaucracies can suppress unconventional options, protect organizational interests, and overwhelm senior officials with detail. A machine that retrieves dissenting views, searches precedent, identifies assumptions, and generates alternatives can help a principal escape bureaucratic tunnel vision.

But independence from bureaucracy is not independence from structure.

The machine has a structure too.

It has training data, retrieval sources, system instructions, tool permissions, ranking choices, security filters, and an interface. Someone decides what it can see. Someone maintains the data. Someone writes the prompt templates. Someone determines whether the model produces one recommendation or several.

The staff has not disappeared.

Part of the staff has become software.

This makes governance unusually important at the top of government.

A senior decision-support system should not be optimized for decisiveness. It should be optimized for disciplined challenge.

That means separating facts from judgments. It means marking uncertain claims. It means surfacing the strongest argument against the apparent recommendation. It means showing which evidence changed the answer. It means identifying which agencies or experts disagree.

Most of all, it means refusing to confuse fluency with institutional consent.

A model can synthesize the positions of multiple agencies. It cannot manufacture the authority those agencies carry.

If the military believes an option cannot be executed, the issue is not that its view was missing from the text. If treasury officials believe sanctions will create a specific market reaction, their responsibility is not fulfilled because a model summarized a memo. If diplomats believe a proposed threat will destroy a negotiation, that disagreement is part of the decision.

Governance requires accountable dissent.

This is why analogies to aviation and medicine are useful but incomplete.

In both fields, automation can support expert judgment. Checklists, decision aids, diagnostic systems, and cockpit automation can reduce workload and catch errors. Yet the underlying environment is often governed by clearer performance criteria than statecraft.

A diplomatic decision may succeed tactically and fail strategically. A threat can work today and damage an alliance over five years. A concession can look weak in public and prevent a war in private. There is rarely a clean label in the historical data telling a model which option was correct.

Politics is full of counterfactuals.

The principal therefore needs a machine that can reason about uncertainty without pretending the uncertainty is solvable.

One useful design is adversarial briefing.

Instead of asking for the best option, ask the system to construct competing cases. What is the strongest argument for waiting? For acting? For signaling privately? For making a public commitment? Which assumptions differ? What observation tomorrow would make today’s preferred option look wrong?

The machine becomes a generator of structured disagreement.

That is much safer than a generator of synthetic consensus.

It is also more valuable.

The highest-level decisions rarely suffer from a shortage of eloquent recommendations. They suffer from missing assumptions, hidden tradeoffs, and compressed time.

A machine can help make those visible.

The institutional rule should be simple: AI may shorten the time required to assemble an argument, but it should not shorten the time allowed to challenge one.

This distinction protects the principal from one of the most seductive properties of machine intelligence.

Instant completion.

A question is asked. An answer appears. The interface suggests that the process is finished.

Government cannot operate that way at the point where responsibility becomes personal.

A president, prime minister, foreign minister, or national-security adviser is not merely choosing the answer with the highest expected value. The principal is authorizing risk on behalf of people who did not participate in the calculation.

That act cannot be delegated by formatting.

The machine can prepare the room.

It should not become the room.
