# Diplomatic Intelligence After OSINT

The embassy used to have a geographic monopoly.

If a government wanted to know what was happening in another capital, it needed people there. Diplomats read local newspapers, met officials, attended receptions, traveled, cultivated sources, watched demonstrations, and reported what headquarters could not see.

That monopoly is gone.

A desk officer thousands of miles away can watch local television, search social media, read translated documents, inspect commercial satellite imagery, follow ship movements, analyze financial data, and use AI to summarize streams of public information.

The capital can see the field.

So what is the embassy for?

The wrong answer is that the embassy becomes less necessary because information is abundant.

Abundant information changes the embassy’s comparative advantage.

It moves from collection toward interpretation, access, and verification.

Headquarters can see that a minister gave a speech.

The embassy may know why the minister gave it, who wrote it, which faction opposed it, how business leaders interpreted it, and whether the language reflects a real policy change or domestic positioning.

Headquarters can see a protest.

The embassy may know which organizations are participating, which are absent, whether local officials are worried, and how the event fits into a longer political pattern.

Headquarters can read every public statement.

The embassy can ask questions.

This distinction becomes more valuable as AI increases the volume of surface-level analysis.

If every capital can generate competent summaries of public information, the scarce product is not another summary.

It is privileged context.

That context does not have to be secret. Much diplomatic insight comes from relationships whose value lies in candor rather than classification.

A business leader, civil servant, journalist, opposition politician, military officer, academic, union organizer, or local official may say something in conversation that they would never publish because the relationship permits nuance.

The diplomat’s job becomes partly a sensor for social reality.

AI can support that job.

It can prepare meeting briefs, retrieve previous conversations, compare statements over time, and help an officer identify contradictions worth exploring. It can turn a small mission into a more capable analytical node.

But the system must not substitute machine-generated certainty for field judgment.

There is a temptation at headquarters to privilege what is measurable. Public data can be placed on dashboards. Human reporting arrives as prose and often contains caveats. The dashboard can look more objective even when it captures less of the political reality.

This creates a new version of an old bureaucratic problem.

What the center can see directly, it may overvalue.

The embassy’s answer should not be to compete on volume.

It should compete on questions the center cannot answer remotely.

Who trusts whom? Which commitment is real? What rumor is changing behavior? What does the local elite misunderstand about us? Which public issue is actually negotiable? What is everybody too polite to say in the formal meeting?

These are expensive questions because they require presence.

Presence means time, language, repetition, and the willingness to remain in a place long enough for relationships to become informative.

This can clash with modern staffing systems built around frequent rotations and broad generalist careers.

AI may therefore strengthen the case for deeper specialization.

If the machine can carry more of the transferable administrative and analytical workload, the human can invest more in what is not transferable: local memory and trust.

The embassy of the future should be smaller only if it becomes denser.

Denser in expertise. Denser in relationships. Denser in technical ability. Denser in institutional memory.

Otherwise, a government may save money while losing the one capability open-source abundance cannot replace.

Someone who is there when the map stops matching the country.
