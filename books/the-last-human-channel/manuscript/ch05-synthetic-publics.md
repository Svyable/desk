# Synthetic Publics

Public diplomacy used to be constrained by the cost of producing a message that looked official.

A ministry could publish a statement, hold a press conference, brief reporters, place an op-ed, fund a cultural program, or circulate talking points to embassies. Those instruments still matter. What has changed is the marginal cost of producing material that looks like journalism, testimony, eyewitness video, local commentary, or spontaneous public reaction. Generative systems make persuasive content cheap at a scale that older propaganda apparatuses could not sustain without large staffs and obvious coordination.

The first temptation is to treat this as a pure influence opportunity.

If messages are cheap, send more of them.

That logic misunderstands how influence works under abundance.

When every actor can flood a platform, the scarce goods are not volume and polish. They are credibility, restraint, and the capacity to make a particular statement count. A government that speaks through a thousand synthetic voices may discover that none of them can settle an argument, reassure an ally, or commit the state in a way the other side will trust.

Synthetic publics are the environments created when governments, parties, firms, activists, and covert operators can generate audiences, commentary, and media that appear social without being social in the older sense. The posts look like people. The videos look like events. The comments look like consensus. Some of the material is authentic. Some is machine-assisted amplification of real grievances. Some is fabricated end to end. The audience often cannot tell which category it is looking at, and the inability to tell becomes part of the political effect.

This is not merely a media story.

It is a diplomatic one, because foreign policy increasingly has to operate inside publics that can be simulated.

Consider the sequence that now accompanies many contested events.

An incident occurs—an explosion, a border clash, a sanctions announcement, a leaked document, a military strike. Within minutes, images circulate. Some are genuine. Some are old footage labeled as new. Some are synthetic reconstructions that feel more complete than the fragmentary real evidence. Accounts that appear local begin narrating the meaning of the event. Influencers repeat the most emotionally efficient version. Official accounts arrive late, sounding defensive even when they are accurate, because they are answering a story that has already hardened.

The diplomatic task in that environment is not only to state a position.

It is to keep a channel open to people who still care whether the statement is authorized and true.

That is harder when the information space rewards speed and emotional clarity more than provenance.

European monitoring of foreign information manipulation has documented recurring patterns in election periods: early attempts to shape the information environment, campaigns to delegitimize particular candidates or institutions, and later bursts of content designed to raise doubt about the fairness of the process itself. Researchers tracking Russia-linked networks have described operations that wrap false claims in layers of reshared material so that the origin becomes difficult to recover. Platform companies periodically announce removals of covert influence networks. None of this proves that any single election was decided by synthetic media. It does show that electoral calendars have become routine targets for message production at industrial scale.

The important diplomatic implication is not that publics are helpless.

It is that governments now negotiate under conditions in which the appearance of public reaction can be manufactured faster than institutions can verify it.

A foreign ministry preparing a demarche may face screenshots of “popular outrage” that did not exist the previous evening. An embassy reporting local sentiment may have trouble separating organic anger from seeded amplification. A mediation team may be told that a constituency will never accept a compromise, with evidence consisting largely of online volume. A military command may worry that a fabricated atrocity video will force political leaders into premature statements.

In each case, the machine does not have to persuade everyone.

It only has to raise the cost of waiting for verification.

Public diplomacy offices are adapting, unevenly.

Some are using AI tools for translation, media monitoring, audience segmentation, and rapid rebuttal. That can be legitimate. Knowing what narratives are spreading is part of situational awareness. Being able to answer a false claim in multiple languages quickly can reduce harm. Measuring which messages travel is not the same as flooding the zone with synthetic personas.

The line is crossed when the state begins to imitate the methods it fears.

A democracy that builds covert synthetic audiences to support its foreign policy may win short-term narrative skirmishes while spending down the asset that makes official speech valuable: the expectation that this account, unlike the swarm, can be held responsible. Authoritarian systems face a different calculation. They may care less about domestic reputational costs and more about degrading the information environment of rivals. Even then, the strategy has limits. If everything can be fake, the regime’s own preferred story becomes harder to stabilize abroad. Ambiguity is a weapon that cuts in more than one direction.

This is why the deepfake debate, left at the level of “can voters be fooled,” is incomplete.

People can be fooled. People can also become cynical. Cynicism is not the opposite of deception. It is often deception’s long-term product. A public trained to assume fabrication may dismiss authentic evidence of an atrocity, a troop movement, or a negotiated concession. Diplomats then confront not only false belief but exhausted belief—an audience too uncertain to update.

Alliance politics feels the effect quickly.

Partners do not only share intelligence about weapons and troop locations. They share judgments about what is happening in the information space. If one ally treats a video as decisive and another treats it as probable fabrication, consultation becomes an argument about epistemology before it becomes an argument about policy. NATO’s revised artificial intelligence strategy explicitly flagged AI-enabled disinformation and information operations as alliance concerns, not merely national media problems. That institutional recognition matters because it places synthetic publics inside collective defense thinking rather than leaving them to communications shops alone.

There is a counterclaim that deserves to be stated plainly.

Audiences may adapt. People learn heuristics. Platforms label content. Provenance standards spread. Newsrooms hire verification teams. Electoral commissions issue guidance. After enough scandals, the novelty premium of synthetic media may fall. A flood of low-quality generated content could even make carefully authenticated official communication more valuable by contrast.

Adaptation is real.

It is also uneven and slow relative to production capacity.

The same public that discounts crude fakes may still be moved by a sophisticated one arriving at the right emotional moment. The same journalist who checks reverse-image search may lack time to investigate a synthetic audio clip before a political deadline. The same voter who claims not to trust social media may still absorb its framing through friends, messaging apps, and partisan media that launder the original claim. Adaptation raises the quality bar for manipulators. It does not restore a shared factual floor.

Diplomatic services therefore need a doctrine for public environments they cannot control.

The doctrine should begin with triage.

Not every circulating claim deserves an official response. Responding to everything teaches adversaries that volume works. Silence can be strategic when a claim is self-consuming or confined to marginal networks. Response becomes necessary when a narrative is moving into institutions that matter—parliaments, markets, allied capitals, military commands, or diaspora communities positioned to pressure a negotiation.

Second, response should carry provenance.

An official denial that looks like any other post is weaker than an official denial that can be traced through known channels, signed statements, verified accounts, and, where possible, content credentials. Technical provenance will not persuade committed partisans. It can still help the undecided and the institutional actors who need a reason to wait.

Third, governments should separate monitoring from manufacturing.

Using AI to understand a synthetic public is different from becoming one. The first is analysis. The second is a decision to compete on terms that degrade the value of authorized speech. There will be hard cases, especially in wartime, where covert influence looks attractive. The book’s claim is not that democracies will never do it. It is that they should count the cost in future diplomatic capital, not only in immediate narrative position.

Fourth, embassies remain sensors that headquarters dashboards cannot fully replace.

Local officers can still ask whether anger in a capital is visible in streets, unions, parties, and elite conversation, or mainly in metrics. That distinction is crude and imperfect. It is often better than mistaking engagement counts for politics. Open-source abundance makes the embassy’s comparative advantage less about being first to see a post and more about knowing which posts correspond to power.

Historical analogy helps only up to a point.

Radio propaganda, leaflet campaigns, and active measures all sought to manufacture opinion. What is new is not the desire to shape publics. It is the collapse in cost and the rise in realism of the fabricated social surround. A leaflet could be recognized as a leaflet. A synthetic neighbor in a group chat may not be recognizable as an operation. The intimacy of the channel changes the epistemic problem.

Mediators feel this acutely.

Cease-fire talks often depend on leaders’ ability to sell a compromise to constituencies that can spoil it. If those constituencies can be simulated, or if spoilers can simulate mass rejection, the negotiation table becomes haunted by publics that may not exist in the claimed form. A party can say, sincerely or strategically, that its hands are tied. Distinguishing a real constraint from a manufactured one becomes part of mediation tradecraft.

The same problem appears in sanctions politics, alliance burden-sharing debates, and crisis signaling. Leaders look for evidence of what “the public” will bear. Synthetic publics make that evidence easier to fake and harder to trust. The result is not necessarily that propaganda always works. It may be that genuine consent becomes harder to measure, which in turn makes democratic accountability and authoritarian claims of popular support alike more difficult to evaluate from outside.

What would make this chapter’s warning overstated?

Evidence that audiences discount synthetic volume quickly enough that influence returns to slower, higher-trust institutions; evidence that provenance and platform defenses keep pace with generation; evidence that most foreign-policy decisions remain insulated from online weather. Some of that evidence exists in partial form. Elections continue to turn on inflation, war, identity, organization, and candidate quality. Not every deepfake matters. Many collapse. Treating every generated clip as strategically decisive would itself be a form of panic useful to manipulators.

The residual risk is still structural.

As long as message production is cheaper than verification, actors who benefit from confusion have a production advantage. Diplomacy cannot abolish that advantage. It can refuse to confuse influence with noise. It can invest in channels whose authority does not depend on winning the hour’s engagement metrics. It can teach officers to ask what a circulating public is made of before treating it as a constraint. It can preserve the difference between speaking as a state and sounding like a crowd.

Election periods make the structure visible because deadlines compress verification.

A campaign cannot wait six days for a forensic lab if a damaging clip appears forty-eight hours before polls open. A foreign ministry watching another country’s vote cannot always separate domestic dirty tricks from external operations in time to brief its own leadership usefully. Researchers examining AI-enabled influence have noted that non-AI falsehoods still matter enormously—old-fashioned editing, misleading context, and ordinary lies remain potent. The novelty is not that truth disappeared. It is that the supply of plausible false packaging increased while the calendar of democratic decision stayed fixed.

That mismatch is the operational core of the problem.

India’s multilingual information environment has shown how synthetic video can be tailored across languages and distributed through messaging groups at volumes that outrun ordinary press cycles. European institutions have described phased logics in foreign information manipulation aimed at elections: early delegitimation of leaders, later attacks on the integrity of the process, and continuous attempts to keep preferred narratives ambient through proxies and recycled accounts. British electoral administrators have warned publicly about deepfake risks around local contests. None of these observations requires claiming that AI alone decides national outcomes. It requires noticing that diplomatic reporting, alliance consultation, and crisis communication now occur inside electoral weather systems that can be seeded artificially.

Wartime information environments raise the stakes further.

During active conflict, publics are hungry for images that confirm what they fear or hope. Synthetic or mislabeled footage of destroyed infrastructure, mistreated prisoners, or miraculous weapons effects can travel because it fits an existing emotional template. Investigations after the fact may establish manipulation. By then, diplomatic positions may already have been stated, sanctions debated, and partner capitals lobbied on the basis of material that should have remained provisional. The repair work is not only forensic. It is relational: explaining to audiences and allies why yesterday’s certainty was premature without sounding like a government that only questions evidence when inconvenient.

This is where official restraint becomes strategically legible.

A government that rushes to authenticate every emotionally useful clip teaches its public that speed equals solidarity. A government that refuses to endorse unverified material, even when the material supports its preferred narrative, spends political capital in the short run to preserve a longer-run ability to say “this one is real” when authenticity matters for escalation control. That trade is hard to make in real time. It is one of the quiet disciplines of serious public diplomacy.

Platforms sit awkwardly in the middle.

They are not foreign ministries, yet their moderation, labeling, and distribution choices shape what diplomats must answer. Removals of covert networks can help. Inconsistent enforcement, slow responses in some languages, and the migration of operations onto encrypted or fragmented channels limit the help. Diplomats who outsource the entire problem to platform trust-and-safety teams will be repeatedly surprised. Diplomats who ignore platforms will be equally blind. The workable posture is institutional literacy: knowing how amplification works well enough to avoid mistaking it for society, and knowing how to route authenticated official content through channels that partners and journalists can recognize.

There is also a domestic feedback loop that foreign services underestimate.

Leaders watch the same feeds their citizens watch. Synthetic outrage can therefore enter the principal’s morning sense of political constraint even when embassy reporting is calmer. The machine-generated crowd becomes a phantom constituency. Foreign ministries that cannot compete with that phantom in the leader’s attention will find their careful cables discounted as out of touch. The remedy is not for diplomats to become louder influencers. It is for them to become better at showing where online weather and offline power diverge.

Commercial incentives complicate the picture.

Tools that generate persuasive media are dual-use by design. The same systems that help a museum localize an exhibit help an influence network localize a smear. Export controls, model-access rules, and watermarking proposals all try to raise the cost of abuse without pretending the tools can be uninvented. Diplomacy enters because standards, law enforcement cooperation, and shared definitions of covert manipulation are international problems. A purely national content policy cannot police a cross-border synthetic campaign.

Yet international cooperation here collides with speech politics and sovereignty claims.

States disagree about what counts as illegitimate interference versus ordinary advocacy. They disagree about platform power. They disagree about whether provenance mandates are consumer protection or censorship infrastructure. Progress is more likely in narrow lanes—election-period information sharing among trusted partners, rapid notification when official accounts are impersonated, joint forensic work on high-stakes military deepfakes—than in a grand treaty on digital speech.

Those narrow lanes matter for the rest of this book.

They are early forms of the protocols of trust discussed later: limited arrangements that do not require broader political alignment. A hotline about nuclear risk and a channel for authenticating whether a defense minister really issued an order are cousins. Both assume that adversaries or rivals sometimes share an interest in preventing certain kinds of confusion.

Synthetic publics also change how soft power is measured.

Cultural institutes, scholarships, and long-form journalism still shape attitudes over years. They now compete with bursts of content engineered for hours. States that abandon slow instruments because they cannot win the hourly metric may discover they have no reservoir of credibility when a crisis requires belief rather than attention. Conversely, states that invest only in slow instruments and ignore the synthetic surround may find their carefully built reputation bypassed by a week of manufactured narrative. The strategic portfolio needs both tempos.

What should a working diplomat do differently on Monday morning?

Read local power before reading trends.

Ask which circulating claims have entered institutions that can bind policy.

Demand provenance for material used in briefing seniors.

Resist the urge to match covert volume with covert volume unless the costs have been weighed in writing.

Keep a human channel to counterparts who can say, privately, whether a public storm is being treated as decisive in their system.

Preserve records of what was known when a public narrative was accepted or rejected, because retrospect will be cruel and forgetful.

These are crafts of attention.

They do not defeat synthetic media. They keep diplomacy from mistaking a generated crowd for the polity it is supposed to understand.

Commercial media markets interact with the synthetic surround in ways governments only partly control.

Outrage and novelty attract attention regardless of origin. A fabricated clip that fits a channel’s ideological brand may be repeated with thin caveats that audiences ignore. Corrections travel more slowly than initial claims. Diplomats who assume that “the press will sort it out” are relying on an institution under its own economic stress. Serious outlets still do verification work that matters. They cannot be the sole backstop for statecraft.

Diaspora communities complicate the map further.

They are often genuine political actors with legitimate stakes, and also frequent targets of tailored influence. A message that fails in a capital may succeed among expatriates who then pressure that capital through remittances, media, and lobbying. Synthetic content localized to diaspora languages and grievances can therefore move foreign policy indirectly. Embassies that maintain real relationships in those communities are better positioned to notice when online intensity does not match offline organization.

Finally, the ethics of response deserve more than a slogan.

Exposing an operation can inoculate a public; it can also amplify the operation. Ignoring it can starve it; it can also allow it to set terms unopposed. There is no universal rule. The choice depends on the claim’s trajectory, the audience’s prior beliefs, and whether authoritative silence would be read as guilt. Officers should be trained to make that choice consciously rather than by temper.

Taken together, these pressures do not prove that public diplomacy is finished.

They prove that public diplomacy’s scarce resource has shifted from the ability to speak toward the ability to be believed when speaking is easy for everyone else.

In an age of synthetic publics, restraint is not passivity.

It is a method for keeping official speech scarce enough to mean something.

Legislatures will demand metrics that prove influence operations were blunted. Metrics that count takedowns alone will miss whether belief among decisive constituencies actually moved. Diplomatic services should help political leaders understand measurement limits without sounding evasive. Evasion destroys oversight trust. Honest uncertainty preserves it longer than false precision about narrative victory.
