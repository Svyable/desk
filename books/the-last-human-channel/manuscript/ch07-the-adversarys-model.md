# The Adversary's Model

Strategic empathy has always been partly an act of imagination. A government tries to see its own moves as the other side might see them.

It asks what the rival fears, what the rival needs to show domestic audiences, which signals the rival is primed to misread.

Done well, the exercise reduces surprise. Done badly, it becomes mirror-imaging: the assumption that the other side wants what we would want, fears what we fear, and calculates as we calculate.

Artificial intelligence makes a seductive promise here. Feed the system doctrine, speeches, order of battle, economic data, and prior crises.

Ask it how the adversary will respond. Receive a fluent brief that sounds like a mind on the other side of the hill.

The promise is not empty. Models can retrieve more of an adversary's public corpus than any single desk officer.

They can keep track of inconsistencies across years of statements. They can generate alternative explanations for an ambiguous deployment.

They can role-play a counterpart ministry's likely objections in a negotiation. Used as a challenge function, they can make lazy mirror-imaging harder to sustain.

The danger is that fluency is mistaken for contact with the adversary's actual decision process. A model of an adversary is not the adversary.

It is a compressed story about the adversary, trained on data the adversary chose to make available or failed to hide, shaped by the questions the user thought to ask, and biased by whatever political assumptions were latent in the training mixture.

When that story is delivered in confident prose, it can feel more real than the messy reporting coming from an embassy or a human source who only partially understands what is happening.

Strategic gaming already knew versions of this problem. War games and crisis simulations have long been used to explore branching futures.

Their value often lies less in prediction than in exposing participants' hidden assumptions. Players discover that a logistics constraint mattered more than a speech, or that an ally's parliamentary calendar blocked an option everyone in the room had treated as available.

The game is a structured argument. AI crisis simulations can multiply such arguments quickly.

They can also manufacture a false sense that the future has been sampled adequately because the interface produced twenty scenarios before lunch.

Quantity of scenarios is not coverage of political reality. Some of the most important adversary behaviors are rare precisely because they are politically extreme.

Decisions to back down after public commitment. Decisions to use force despite economic self-harm.

Decisions driven by small-group dynamics invisible in open sources. A model trained on the public record may assign those paths too little weight until after they occur.

Humans make the same mistake. The machine can scale the mistake and give it a dashboard.

Red teaming is the right instinct and an incomplete solution. A red team that uses AI to generate hostile options can surface attacks a friendly staff would prefer not to imagine.

That helps. If the red team and the blue team are using closely related systems, however, they may share blind spots.

Correlated creativity is still correlation. Diversity of models, diversity of human expertise, and diversity of data access remain forms of insurance.

Cultural and doctrinal bias enters at every layer. Translation choices shape what counts as a threat verb.

Training mixtures drawn heavily from one strategic literature may over-weight certain theories of deterrence and under-weight others. An adversary's own propaganda may dominate the open-source record and train the model to take theatrical statements more seriously than internal constraints.

Alternatively, cynicism about propaganda may cause the model and its users to discount declarations that were, this time, operationally sincere.

There is no stable escape from interpretation. There is only the discipline of treating model output as hypothesis.

Consider a concrete diplomatic use. A sanctions team asks a system to predict how a target government will evade a new measure.

The system lists trade rerouting, front companies, alternative payment systems, and domestic substitution. Some of that list will be useful.

Some will be generic. The team still needs people who know which banks, which ports, and which politically protected firms matter this quarter.

Without those people, the model's list becomes a checklist that creates the feeling of foresight while missing the actual channel.

Contingency planning illustrates the same trap at larger scale. A model can outline possible blockade variants, cyber operations, and information campaigns.

Planners already do this. The risk is that a particularly vivid generated narrative becomes the reference scenario against which all preparations are judged.

Less cinematic paths get crowded out. Prolonged gray-zone pressure that never crosses a neat threshold may matter more than the dramatic scenario everyone rehearsed.

Vividness is not probability. Generative systems are engines of vividness.

Nuclear and high-intensity crisis research has begun to probe how generative decision support might shape escalation judgments. Public studies and scenario exercises caution that machine-generated foresight can normalize extreme pathways, misattribute intent under ambiguity, or create feedback loops in which simulated expectations start to drive real postures.

Those findings are research evidence, not proof of current operational deployment. They are still relevant to diplomacy because diplomats inherit the timelines and fears that military and political leaders bring into consultation.

The empathy problem deserves a careful statement. Modeling an adversary can improve empathy when it forces attention to constraints the user finds inconvenient: elite bargains, historical trauma, logistical limits, coalition politics.

It manufactures false confidence when it produces a complete psychological portrait from incomplete data and invites the user to stop listening.

The tell is often conversational. Officials begin speaking about what they will do as if the pronoun referred to a known person rather than a contested institution.

Adversaries will also model us. Mutual modeling can stabilize if it improves each side's understanding of red lines.

It can destabilize if each side's model tells it that the other side will fold, or that the other side has already decided on war, or that delay is more dangerous than action.

When both sides use similar commercial tools, they may even share distorted images of one another derived from the same media weather.

That is a new form of common-mode failure in strategic assessment.

Institutions can respond without pretending to solve uncertainty. Separate generation from judgment in adversary analysis.

Allow models to propose hypotheses. Require humans with regional accountability to rank and reject them.

Keep a record of what was rejected and why, so that later surprise can be studied without mythmaking.

Privilege indicators the adversary would have to pay costs to fake. Public rhetoric is cheap.

Certain military logistics moves, legal changes, and elite personnel shifts are less cheap.

Preserve inconvenient human sources. An agent, a diplomat, a defector, a business contact—any of these may contradict the model.

The institutional pathology to fear is discarding the dissonant human report because it does not fit the machine's neat distribution of likely behaviors.

Dissonance is often where learning lives. Run deliberate model disagreement.

If several systems disagree sharply about adversary intent, treat that disagreement as analytically valuable rather than as a software annoyance to be averaged away.

Beware of using adversary models as substitutes for communication. The whole point of channels—formal and informal—is to test models against responses.

A government that becomes too impressed with its simulation may underinvest in asking the other side a direct question when asking is still possible.

The Kennedy-Dobrynin meetings remain a reminder that some information only exists inside a relationship.

The counterclaim is strong enough to keep on the table. Structured model disagreement may outperform unstructured human mirror-imaging.

Humans are also trapped in analogy, prestige, and fatigue. A well-designed system can force consideration of paths a hierarchical meeting would suppress.

In commercial negotiations and some mediation settings, simulation of counterpart preferences has helped teams prepare. The technology's usefulness in preparation does not automatically extend to high-stakes political-military forecasting, but neither is the technology useless there.

Wargame practice offers a usable analogy if it is not romanticized. Good games appoint controllers who can inject reality when players' plans ignore politics.

They debrief ruthlessly. They treat the outcome as a prompt for questions, not as a forecast to brief the minister as fact.

AI-assisted games that skip debrief and export a glossy most-likely adversary course of action into planning documents are not modernizing strategy.

They are laundering speculation.

The bureaucratic incentives around such products are predictable. A vivid adversary model helps a briefing win attention.

Attention wins resources. Resources hire more modelers.

The loop can improve capability. It can also create an analytic monoculture in which career incentives punish people who say the model is elegant and insufficient.

Managers should promote officers who can kill a beautiful scenario with a stubborn fact.

Open-source intelligence intensifies both the opportunity and the trap. Never before could a small team watch so much of an adversary's visible activity.

Never before could a team confuse visibility with understanding of decision. A train movement can be imaged.

The meeting that authorized it may leave no digital trace the model can see. Diplomatic reporting and clandestine collection exist precisely for the residue of politics that sensors do not capture.

Alliance settings multiply models. Each ally may bring its own representation of the shared rival.

Harmonizing those representations becomes part of consultation. If harmonization means everyone adopts the largest ally's model, smaller allies lose the analytic dissent that sometimes warned the group.

If harmonization is impossible, the alliance may argue about the adversary's mind instead of about the joint action.

Cognitive interoperability begins here: shared language about confidence and evidence, not only shared software.

Track II dialogues with rivals create another awkward use case. Participants may be tempted to ask models what the other side really thinks before entering the room.

Preparation helps. Entering the room already certain that one has simulated the counterpart's soul does not.

The history of unofficial U.S.-China and U.S.-Soviet dialogues suggests that surprise remains available even among experienced specialists. Preserving the capacity to be surprised is not naivete.

It is a precondition for learning during the conversation.

Domestic politics of the adversary are often the hardest layer. Outsiders overstate the leader's freedom or understate it.

They miss succession anxieties, regional bargains, and institutional vetoes. A model fed on national media may absorb the regime's own theory of itself.

Analysts who have lived in the country, spoken its languages deeply, and watched its local fights may still be wrong.

They are wrong in ways that can be interrogated. A generated political sociology is harder to interrogate because it has no biography.

The falsification test is empirical and uncomfortable. If governments that integrate adversary-modeling tools show better calibrated predictions—fewer missed invasions, fewer false alarms, better anticipation of bargaining ranges—without a matching rise in overconfident irreversible acts, then the caution in this chapter should be narrowed.

Public evidence of that quality gain at state level remains thin. Until it thickens, the right posture is experimental seriousness without epistemic surrender.

There is a deeper philosophical temptation to resist. It is the idea that the adversary is a system to be solved.

Some aspects of state behavior are systemic. Others are contingent, personal, and path-dependent.

A leader's health, a factional insult, a misrouted cable, a local commander's initiative—these have shaped crises before. Models can include random shocks.

They cannot abolish contingency. Diplomacy's comparative advantage has often been attentiveness to the contingent fact that did not fit the briefing.

Machine models of the adversary will improve. They will ingest more languages, more satellite data, more financial flows, more leaked corpora.

Their graphs will look more objective. The political task will remain the same.

Decide when to believe a representation of the other side. Decide when to test it.

Decide when to act despite not knowing. That decision cannot be fully delegated, because the cost of being wrong is not borne by the model.

None of this is an argument against tools. It is an argument about the dignity of uncertainty.

Governments will use adversary models. They should.

They should also keep a human channel to the possibility that the other side is about to do something the model ranked as unlikely for excellent reasons the model cannot see.

In the end, the adversary's model is a mirror with better lighting. It shows more detail.

It can still show your own face where you expected to see someone else.

Commercial vendors will sell adversary-modeling suites the way earlier vendors sold open-source dashboards. The sales pitch will emphasize coverage, update speed, and the ability to brief a minister in minutes.

Procurement officers should insist on evaluation against withheld historical cases. How often did the system assign high probability to what actually happened next in past crises?

How often did it miss?

How stable are its judgments across prompt wording?

Without those tests, governments will buy fluency and call it foresight.

Intelligence tradecraft already contains methods for scoring sources and expressing confidence. Those methods are imperfect and sometimes theatrical.

They are still better than a paragraph that sounds certain because the interface rewards completeness. Integrating model output into estimative products should force the model into the same confidence grammar humans use, even if the grammar feels clumsy.

Clumsy uncertainty is preferable to elegant false precision.

There is a temptation to personalize adversary models around a single leader's psychology. Leader-focused analysis has a long pedigree and occasional predictive value.

It also fails when institutions constrain the leader, when the leader is opaque, or when succession changes the center of gravity overnight.

A system that writes compelling psychobiography from speeches can deepen the personalization bias. Desk officers should be trained to ask what the model would say if the leader were replaced tomorrow.

Economic statecraft offers a partial laboratory. Sanctions design, export controls, and investment screening already use data-heavy analysis.

Machine assistance can map corporate ownership networks and trade substitution paths faster than manual teams. Even here, political protection of particular firms and the willingness of third countries to enforce rules decide outcomes.

The model can show the network. It cannot show who will dare to anger which patron.

Military exercises by rivals are another rich input. Public imagery and announcements create large corpora for pattern analysis.

Analysts have always debated whether an exercise is rehearsal, signaling, routine training, or cover for something else. Models can compare the current exercise to prior ones along many dimensions.

They still cannot settle the intention question without a theory of politics. Intention remains the scarce object.

Diplomatic services should also model their own predictability as seen by others. If a government always responds to a certain provocation with a demarche and never with a cost-imposing measure, adversaries learn that pattern.

AI can help a state see its own revealed preferences in the historical record. That self-modeling function may be more valuable than adversary portraiture, because the data about one's own bureaucracy is better.

Legal and ethical constraints shape what may be simulated. Some states will refuse to allow models to generate attack plans against named civilians or to produce highly realistic deception material even for training.

Those refusals create capability gaps relative to less restrained actors. They also protect against the normalization of planning practices that erode legal firebreaks.

The tradeoff should be made explicitly rather than by silent tool drift.

Education pipelines will determine whether the next generation of officers treats adversary models as oracles or as instruments.

War colleges and diplomatic academies should require students to break a model in graded exercises: find the missing constraint, the bad analogy, the overfit to last year's war.

A profession that cannot assign homework against its own tools will eventually be captured by them.

Finally, humility has operational content. After any major surprise, governments should ask which model-supported assumptions failed and which ignored human reports would have helped.

The answers should change collection priorities and briefing norms. If the only response is to buy a newer model, the institution has learned the wrong lesson.

What would make this false in a stronger sense than earlier?

Evidence that adversary-modeling systems, under real operational conditions, consistently improve calibration and reduce both false calm and false alarm across multiple governments and crises.

Until such evidence is public and robust, the working rule remains simple. Use the model to widen the hypothesis set.

Do not let it close the case.

The last human channel, in adversary analysis, is the person willing to say that the elegant picture is incomplete.

That sentence rarely wins the meeting. It sometimes prevents the next war's first mistake.

Personnel systems will reveal whether adversary modeling is a craft or a fashion. If promotion boards reward officers who ask hard questions of the tools, quality will rise. If they reward officers who produce the most cinematic brief, quality will fall under the appearance of modernity.

Allied intelligence sharing agreements may need new annexes for machine-generated adversary assessments. Partners should know whether a shared conclusion came from sensors, humans, models, or a blend. Without that meta-data, consultation becomes an argument about ghosts.

The chapter's caution can be summarized without slogan. Models widen what a government can imagine about a rival. Imagination is not knowledge. Knowledge still requires contact with reality through sensors, sources, and conversations that can surprise the brief.

Regional specialists remain the awkward conscience of adversary modeling.

They are slower than dashboards.

They argue about historiography.

They remember the last three times a confident capital brief was wrong about the same country.

Their resistance can look like gatekeeping.

Sometimes it is.

Sometimes it is the only thing standing between a government and a beautiful mistake.

Collection managers face a parallel choice.

Do they fund sensors that feed the model more of what it already processes well, or human networks that produce the dissonant facts models undervalue?

Budgets reveal epistemology.

A service that only buys what machines digest will eventually know only what machines can digest.

Third-party states complicate portraits further.

An adversary's choices often depend on what a neighbor, a patron, or a client will tolerate.

Models that treat the rival as a closed system miss the coalition politics around it.

Diplomats who work the surrounding capitals still supply causal links that no unilateral data lake contains.

Private-sector risk firms will publish adversary outlooks that officials read whether or not they admit it.

Those outlooks can discipline thinking.

They can also synchronize elite opinion around a commercial narrative optimized for subscribers rather than for national interest.

Governments need enough internal analytic capacity to disagree with the market's favorite story about the rival.

Otherwise policy becomes a derivative of someone else's product roadmap.

In coalition wars and sanctions coalitions, divergent adversary models become diplomatic disputes in their own right.

One partner insists the rival is cornered.

Another insists the rival has room to escalate.

Both may have models.

The argument is then about whose representation governs joint action.

Settling that argument is alliance statecraft, not software configuration.

Imagination about the adversary is necessary.

Delegation of belief to a fluent representation is optional.

The optional part is where professional courage still has work to do.
