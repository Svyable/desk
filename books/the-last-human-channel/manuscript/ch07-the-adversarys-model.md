# The Adversary's Model

Strategists have always built imaginary enemies.

War games, intelligence estimates, red teams, diplomatic cables, and academic models all try to answer the same question: what will the other side do?

Artificial intelligence makes the imaginary enemy interactive.

A model can be prompted with speeches, doctrine, historical behavior, economic constraints, military capabilities, and political institutions. It can generate likely responses to sanctions, deployments, negotiations, or crises. It can simulate factions. It can run the same scenario thousands of times with different assumptions.

This feels like a new form of access.

It is not access to the adversary.

It is access to a model of the adversary.

The distinction sounds obvious until the model begins to speak confidently in the adversary’s voice.

Humans are vulnerable to anthropomorphism. We treat coherent language as evidence of coherent thought. A simulation that says, “Beijing would interpret this as…” or “Moscow is likely to…” can feel more authoritative than a paragraph full of caveats, even when both rest on the same uncertain evidence.

The danger is mirror-imaging with better prose.

A model trained primarily on one side’s public analysis may reproduce that side’s assumptions while wearing the costume of the other. It may infer rationality from outcomes. It may flatten internal disagreements. It may treat doctrine as behavior and rhetoric as intention.

The result can be exquisitely detailed and strategically wrong.

This does not make adversary models useless.

Used correctly, they can be excellent engines of surprise.

The right question is not, “What will the adversary do?”

It is, “How many plausible ways could the adversary interpret this?”

That turns prediction into hypothesis generation.

A government considering a military exercise, export control, diplomatic statement, or sanctions package can ask a system to construct multiple interpretations from the perspective of different institutions and factions. One response might see deterrence. Another might see preparation for attack. Another might see domestic politics. Another might see an opportunity to split an alliance.

The value comes from disagreement among models, not agreement.

This suggests a different architecture for strategic simulation.

Do not build one adversary model.

Build an adversary ensemble.

Use different data sources, model families, assumptions, and institutional framings. Force the outputs to expose why they differ. Ask what evidence would discriminate among them. Preserve minority scenarios when their probability is low but their consequence is high.

The goal is not to create a digital twin of a government.

Governments are not unitary objects. They are coalitions of institutions, leaders, habits, incentives, fears, and incomplete information. Their behavior changes under stress. They deceive. They misunderstand themselves. They make mistakes.

A model accurate in routine competition may fail in the crisis where it matters most.

This is a classic problem of strategic intelligence.

The rare event is precisely the event with the least training data.

AI can still improve red teaming because it lowers the cost of generating alternatives. A human team that once had time to explore three scenarios may examine thirty. Historical analogies can be retrieved faster. Contradictory evidence can be surfaced. Analysts can ask the model to attack their own assumptions.

But the institution must reward contradiction.

If leaders use the model to validate an existing policy, simulation becomes theater. A system that reliably produces the answer the principal prefers is more dangerous than no system at all because it converts preference into apparent analysis.

The governance problem therefore begins before the prompt.

Who owns the model? Who can challenge it? Are the assumptions recorded? Can analysts reproduce the output? Does the system show uncertainty? Can senior officials see when a conclusion depends on one contested premise?

These are mundane questions.

They are the difference between a tool and an oracle.

There is a diplomatic benefit as well.

Adversary modeling can improve empathy without producing sympathy. Understanding how another government may perceive a move does not require endorsing that perception. It simply creates a wider map of possible reactions.

That map can expose avoidable escalation.

A signal intended as reassurance may be read as weakness. A signal intended as deterrence may look like preparation. A private warning may be more credible than a public threat. A concession designed to create an off-ramp may be invisible to the faction that needs to defend it domestically.

The machine can help generate these possibilities.

Only the human system can decide which are politically real.

The adversary’s model is therefore most useful when it makes certainty harder.

If it makes certainty easier, something has gone wrong.
