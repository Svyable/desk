# Translation Without Understanding

The oldest fantasy about translation is that language is a wall.

Remove the wall and the people on either side will finally understand one another.

Artificial intelligence makes that fantasy more plausible than it has ever been. A diplomat can receive a speech in seconds, compare versions, search unfamiliar terminology, draft a response, and follow a meeting in a language the diplomat does not speak. Small foreign ministries can work across more languages. Leaders can read more source material without waiting for a human translation pipeline.

This is an enormous gain.

It is not the same thing as understanding.

Language carries information. Politics carries stakes.

A sentence can be translated perfectly and still be misread because the listener does not know who was expected to hear it, which faction demanded it, which bureaucratic compromise produced it, what could not be said publicly, or which historical phrase has been chosen precisely because it means more than its dictionary definition.

Diplomacy is full of statements that are technically clear and politically ambiguous.

That ambiguity is often deliberate.

Treaties are negotiated word by word because words create obligations. Joint communiqués can be engineered so that two governments can endorse the same sentence while explaining it differently at home. Leaders use formulaic language to signal continuity without reopening an argument. Translators and interpreters do more than substitute vocabulary. They protect register, rhythm, hierarchy, and the possibility that a phrase is doing institutional work.

A machine can help with all of this.

The danger begins when fluent output hides what it does not know.

Machine translation tends to produce a finished sentence. A finished sentence feels settled. The roughness that once warned a reader that context might be missing can disappear.

This creates false confidence.

Imagine a crisis statement whose literal content is ordinary but whose phrasing departs from a formula used for years. A language model may produce a smooth translation while normalizing the novelty. Or it may flag the novelty without knowing whether it matters. The technical question is not only whether the words were rendered accurately. The diplomatic question is whether the departure was intentional, authorized, and politically costly.

Those questions require institutional memory.

A human expert who has followed a government for years may notice who signed the statement, which ministry published it first, which sentence moved, which adjective disappeared, and which domestic audience is being reassured. None of those observations is mystical. Many can be encoded, retrieved, or modeled. But they depend on a continuous understanding of institutions rather than isolated linguistic competence.

The right comparison is therefore not human translation versus machine translation.

It is thin understanding versus thick understanding.

Thin understanding asks: what does this sentence say?

Thick understanding asks: why this sentence, from this person, through this channel, at this time?

AI makes thin understanding cheap. That can free diplomats to spend more time on the thick kind.

But institutions have to choose that outcome.

If translation becomes cheap, governments may decide they need fewer language specialists. Training pipelines may shrink. Rotations may become shorter because software appears to cover the gap. The system then gains linguistic reach while losing the people most able to recognize when the machine’s fluent answer is politically incomplete.

This is a familiar pattern in automation. Once a tool performs the normal case well, organizations become tempted to remove the expertise needed for the abnormal case.

Diplomacy is mostly normal cases until the one case that matters.

The answer is not to preserve every old practice. Some traditional language work should become automated. Routine document translation, searchable archives, first-pass summaries, terminology consistency, and multilingual drafting are exactly the kinds of tasks machines can improve.

The scarce resource should move upward.

Foreign ministries need fewer people spending hours converting text mechanically and more people capable of asking what the conversion hides. Language depth becomes more valuable when surface fluency is abundant because the value shifts from access to interpretation.

This suggests a training principle.

Use the machine to expand exposure, not excuse ignorance.

A diplomat learning Arabic, Mandarin, Russian, Turkish, Hindi, French, Spanish, or any other relevant language can use AI as a tutor, a corpus, a pronunciation partner, and a rapid comparison engine. That should make deep learning easier. It should not become a reason to stop before cultural and institutional competence begins.

The same principle applies at the negotiating table.

Real-time translation can widen participation. It can reduce the privilege of delegations able to field large interpretation teams. It can allow working groups to move faster across languages.

Yet some negotiations benefit from the pause created by interpretation.

The pause gives a speaker time to observe the room. It gives a delegation time to think. It makes each sentence costly enough to choose carefully. If simultaneous machine translation removes that rhythm, speed can change behavior even when the translation itself is excellent.

Again, the issue is not whether technology works.

It is what institutional function disappears when it works.

The diplomatic future will be multilingual by default. Most language barriers that once limited access to information will weaken dramatically.

That is progress.

But a world in which everyone can understand the words may create a new advantage for the people who understand why the words were chosen.

Translation solves the sentence.

Diplomacy begins after that.
