# Translation Without Understanding

In April 1972, Henry Kissinger objected to a change in the Soviet draft of a short statement about his talks in Moscow.

The Americans wanted the discussions described as frank and useful.

Andrei Gromyko explained that the Soviet side could not use the word in quite the same way. In their diplomatic lexicon, “frank” implied disagreement.

The disagreement was about one adjective.

It was also about the meaning of the meeting.

That is the translation problem in diplomacy.

Words do not move between languages as sealed containers. They arrive with histories, institutional habits, registers, legal implications, domestic audiences, and expectations about what people in a particular room are trying to signal without saying directly.

A machine can translate the sentence.

The harder task is translating the situation.

Diplomacy has always depended on people who know that these are different jobs.

The U.S. Department of State maintains an Office of Language Services responsible for official interpreting and translating support to the State Department, White House, National Security Council, and other senior officials. Its rules distinguish interpreting from written translation, official work from informal work, and now machine translation from human translation. The distinction is not administrative trivia. Official language acquires consequences because governments stand behind it.

The State Department’s current language-services rules explicitly define machine translation as translation produced by artificial intelligence, including neural and generative systems. They also require human post-editing of machine translations and human verification when AI interpreting is used.

That requirement is revealing.

The institution is not rejecting the machine.

It is refusing to let fluency become authority by itself.

Machine translation is becoming extraordinarily useful because diplomacy produces a quantity of language no human service could process equally fast. Embassies monitor newspapers, social media, parliamentary debates, regulations, broadcasts, speeches, diplomatic notes, intelligence reporting, court decisions, technical standards, and local conversations. A foreign ministry can now ask a machine for a first-pass translation of material that would once have waited in a queue or never been translated at all.

This changes what can be seen.

A desk officer who does not read a language can search more local reporting. A small embassy can monitor more media. A crisis team can compare statements across countries in minutes. Negotiators can generate working versions of drafts in several languages before professional translators begin the official text.

Translation abundance creates intelligence abundance.

It also creates a new temptation.

The translated sentence looks finished.

That appearance can hide the fact that the most consequential uncertainty was never lexical.

Consider a phrase used in military signaling.

One government describes an exercise as routine.

Another calls the same activity unprecedented.

A third says it is defensive.

The words may be translated perfectly. The diplomatic question remains: what does each government expect the others to infer from choosing that word now?

“Routine” can be descriptive.

It can also be an attempt to normalize behavior that was not previously routine.

“Defensive” can describe doctrine.

It can also create political space for an operation whose physical effect looks coercive to the other side.

“Final” can mean no further negotiation.

It can also mean the speaker needs the other side to make a new offer privately so the public position does not have to change.

The machine sees language.

The diplomat sees position, audience, sequence, and room for movement.

The gap does not make machine translation useless. It tells us what kind of tool it is.

Translation is an input to understanding.

It is not understanding itself.

Professional interpreters know this because they work in real time where the cost of a wrong register becomes visible immediately. Their task is not to improve what a principal says. It is to render the meaning and register faithfully enough that the other side encounters the statement the speaker actually made.

That can require restraint.

A clumsy sentence may need to remain clumsy.

An ambiguity may need to remain ambiguous.

A threat should not become stronger because the target language offers a more dramatic synonym.

A joke cannot be repaired so aggressively that the interpreter becomes its author.

A leader who speaks indirectly may be doing so intentionally.

The translator’s temptation is clarity.

Diplomacy sometimes requires preserving uncertainty.

This becomes important with generative AI because generative systems are rewarded for useful completion. If a phrase is elliptical, the model may infer the missing relationship. If an idiom is culturally specific, the model may choose a target-language expression that captures the general sense while losing the political resonance. If a sentence contains an awkward legal compromise, the model may smooth it into language that sounds more natural and therefore changes what the negotiators fought to leave unresolved.

A polished translation can be less faithful than an uncomfortable one.

This is not a hypothetical weakness unique to AI.

Human translators also interpret. They can make errors, import assumptions, miss references, or unconsciously improve prose. Diplomatic institutions have long used professional standards, comparison, review, and official records because people are fallible too.

AI changes scale.

One human error affects one translation.

A systematic model tendency can affect thousands of translations before anyone notices the pattern.

The problem may be especially difficult when the translations are individually plausible.

A system that occasionally outputs nonsense is easy to distrust.

A system that is almost always elegant can be harder to audit.

This produces a new form of linguistic automation bias.

The reader may stop asking what was in the source because the target text reads as if a competent person has already settled the ambiguity.

Foreign ministries should resist that shortcut in high-consequence work.

The most important diplomatic translations should preserve a path back to the source.

That means more than storing the original document somewhere.

It means making transformations legible.

Was the text directly translated or summarized?

Did a machine translate it before a human edited it?

Which phrases received substantive human revision?

Was the source an official transcript, a news report, an audio recording, or a social-media clip?

Did the translation preserve uncertainty in titles, pronouns, legal terms, military terminology, or idioms?

Were several source-language versions circulating?

These questions can sound pedantic until a government needs to know exactly what another government committed itself to doing.

Treaties make the issue obvious.

When multiple language versions are authoritative, a translation dispute is not merely editorial. It can become a dispute about obligations. Even when one language controls legally, negotiations may depend on how a phrase is understood in another capital. Domestic ratification can hinge on words chosen for political audiences that do not share the negotiating language.

Diplomatic translation therefore has two directions at once.

It carries meaning outward to the counterpart.

It carries consequences inward to the government’s own institutions and public.

A phrase acceptable in a negotiating room may become explosive when translated for a parliament. A formulation that sounds neutral in English may carry colonial, ideological, or legal associations elsewhere. A technical term used casually by one ministry may be a defined term of art in another country’s doctrine.

The translator is standing at a border between political systems.

AI can help map that border.

A capable model can retrieve prior uses of a phrase across thousands of documents. It can compare how the same official term was translated in earlier communiqués. It can flag that a word has several legally significant renderings. It can show where two draft versions diverge. It can search diplomatic archives for precedent much faster than a human team.

This may be one of the best uses of language models in diplomacy.

Not choosing the final word.

Showing the human why the word is difficult.

A model can become an ambiguity detector.

That role is strategically different from automatic translation.

The objective is not to make every text instantly smooth. It is to direct scarce professional attention toward places where smoothness is dangerous.

This suggests a triage model for foreign ministries.

Low-consequence language can move quickly.

A public article needed for background research can be machine translated with appropriate caution. Routine administrative material can receive automated first passes. Large media corpora can be searched through approximate translation. Speed creates enormous value here.

Medium-consequence material needs review.

A policy paper, intelligence summary, ministerial statement, sanctions regulation, or military announcement may require a linguist or subject-matter expert to inspect terminology and context.

High-consequence communication needs accountable language.

A leader-level message, crisis hotline exchange, treaty clause, ceasefire text, military notification, or final negotiating instruction should have a known human chain of responsibility even if AI assists every stage around it.

The more consequential the words, the more important it becomes to know who stands behind the translation.

That is not nostalgia for human craft.

It is command responsibility applied to language.

The distinction becomes even more important with simultaneous interpreting.

Machine systems are improving at live speech recognition, translation, and synthetic voice generation. It is easy to imagine meetings in which each participant hears another language almost instantly in a familiar voice.

The convenience will be extraordinary.

So will the new ambiguity.

Did the foreign minister actually use that phrase?

Did the software resolve a pronoun?

Did it convert a culturally specific joke into a more direct insult?

Did the system miss a correction spoken over another participant?

Did a latency spike remove a qualifier?

Did the synthetic voice preserve hesitation that mattered to an experienced negotiator?

A human interpreter in the room is visible as an intermediary.

An AI voice can make the intermediary disappear.

That disappearance may create false intimacy.

The listener hears the counterpart apparently speaking directly in the listener’s language. The technological achievement is impressive precisely because it hides the translation step.

Diplomacy should sometimes do the opposite.

It should preserve awareness that a translation occurred.

This is not because the machine will always be worse than a person. In many settings it may be more consistent, faster, and less fatigued. It can provide a second channel against which a human interpretation is checked. It can catch numbers, names, and repeated technical terms. It can produce transcripts that let teams reconstruct a difficult exchange afterward.

The safest design may be redundancy rather than substitution.

Human interpreter plus machine transcript.

Machine draft plus human final translation.

Automated comparison plus accountable reviewer.

The systems can challenge one another.

This resembles the architecture of warning and authentication elsewhere in this book. High-consequence institutions become safer when no single representation of reality automatically becomes reality for everyone downstream.

Language is one of those representations.

The diplomatic record proves how much can rest on it.

Foreign Relations of the United States volumes are filled with interpreters whose names often appear quietly in participant lists. Viktor Sukhodrev stood between Soviet leaders and American officials through some of the Cold War’s most important conversations. William Krimer and other State Department interpreters rendered the same exchanges from the American side. Their presence was not ceremonial. The historical record itself depends partly on the quality of their work and on the memoranda later drafted from meetings conducted across languages.

The interpreter can disappear from the photograph while remaining inside the history.

AI will make disappearance easier.

A future archive may contain a transcript produced automatically from audio, translated by one model, summarized by another, and incorporated into a policy memorandum by a third. If the chain is not recorded, historians may not know where the first consequential interpretation entered the document.

Neither will decision makers at the time.

Provenance therefore belongs to language services too.

A diplomatic institution should know which version is original, which is machine generated, which was human reviewed, and which one is official.

The State Department’s current requirement for human post-editing of machine translation points in this direction. It does not treat AI output as worthless. It treats it as unfinished until a qualified person assumes responsibility for accuracy and completeness.

That is a useful model beyond translation.

Artificial intelligence can make first drafts abundant.

Diplomatic authority begins when someone is willing to own the final version.

There is another reason to keep humans close to the process.

Negotiation often depends on discovering that two sides are using the same word differently.

A machine trained to maximize semantic equivalence may erase the discrepancy.

A diplomat can use it.

The 1972 disagreement over “frank” is a small example. The Soviet and American officials were not merely searching for the closest dictionary equivalent. They were negotiating the public signal created by a conventional diplomatic adjective. Gromyko’s explanation supplied information about how Moscow expected readers to interpret the word.

That information was itself part of the negotiation.

Misunderstanding, when surfaced early, can be useful.

It tells the parties where their conceptual maps differ.

The dangerous form is misunderstanding hidden by fluent language.

That risk will grow as AI makes cross-language conversation feel effortless.

Effortless communication is not the same as shared meaning.

Two governments may use the same translated words while disagreeing about the legal framework, historical analogy, military threshold, or domestic commitment attached to them. The better the translation, the easier it may be to postpone noticing that deeper disagreement.

This is why diplomats will need a new habit in the age of machine language.

After asking what the other side said, ask what the translated sentence may have made too easy.

Which ambiguity disappeared?

Which cultural cue was normalized?

Which technical term carries different doctrine?

Which phrase sounds agreed because the target language has one word where the source distinguishes two?

Which apparent disagreement is linguistic rather than political?

Which apparent agreement is political theater concealed by lexical harmony?

The questions require human judgment because they are not purely about language.

They are about the relationship between language and power.

Translation technology can reduce one of diplomacy’s oldest transaction costs. That is a real gain. More people can access more foreign material. Smaller states can process larger information environments. Negotiators can prepare faster. Public diplomacy can reach audiences in more languages. Alliances can coordinate across linguistic boundaries with less delay.

The gain should not be confused with the abolition of interpretation.

Diplomacy begins again after the sentence has been translated.

Someone still has to decide what the words mean here, from this person, to this audience, at this moment, after what happened yesterday, with what room left for tomorrow.

The machine can render the language.

The last human channel remains responsible for the meaning.
