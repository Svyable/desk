# The Protocols of Trust

Authentication becomes institutional design when rivals need to prevent certain confusions without becoming friends.

Hot lines, incident-at-sea agreements, cyber notification norms, digital signatures, and provenance standards are narrow protocols. They do not require shared ideology. They require shared interest in not stumbling into unwanted war.

The 1963 ancestor.

The 1963 Washington-Moscow Direct Communications Link remains the ancestor case: a technical path built after leaders discovered that ordinary diplomacy was too slow for nuclear danger. U.S. documentary records from the period show officials arguing, before and after the Cuban Missile Crisis, about whether direct leader communication would reduce war by miscalculation. The crisis made the latency problem undeniable. The link was a protocol: a designed path with procedures, not a friendship ceremony.

Modern equivalents must solve authenticity under speed, not only speed. A link that can be spoofed is a hazard. Voice cloning, fabricated video, and forged documents change the threat model around any channel that once relied on obscurity or on the difficulty of imitation. Prearranged challenge-response phrases sound antique and still beat cleverness when voices can be cloned. Cryptographic authentication of devices and messages belongs beside human confirmation routines.

INCSEA and the navy-to-navy habit.

The 1972 Agreement on the Prevention of Incidents On and Over the High Seas (INCSEA), signed in Moscow on 25 May 1972 by U.S. Secretary of the Navy John Warner and Soviet Admiral Sergei Gorshkov, is a second ancestor worth studying without romance. It did not end the Cold War. It created rules for ships and aircraft operating near one another: limits on dangerous maneuvers, signaling practices, notice for certain hazardous activities, information exchange through naval attachés, and annual reviews.

INCSEA mattered because it was functional, navy-to-navy, and backed by professional incentives to avoid collisions that could become political crises. It assumed rivalry. It did not require trust in the thick sense. It required a shared preference for not dying by accident. That is the template for narrow protocols in an AI era: design for adversaries who remain adversaries.

Naval encounters today still need practiced radio protocols that survive partial connectivity and language friction. Machine translation on bridge-to-bridge communications can help and can create false confidence if doctrinal understatement is flattened into apparent clarity. Human officers who understand the other service remain part of the protocol stack.

Provenance is not intent.

C2PA and related Content Credentials standards offer tamper-evident history for media — digital signatures and metadata about source and editing. They do not prove political intent. A video with perfect provenance can still be authorized propaganda, selective truth, or a deepfake of events that is simply not using the credentialed pipeline. Diplomacy still needs procedures that connect technical provenance to authorized speakers: which keys belong to which office; who can bind the state; what to do when provenance is missing.

Provenance mandates in commercial media ecosystems support diplomacy indirectly by raising baseline verification. They are not a substitute for government authentication channels. Third-party custodians of keys and timestamps may help enemies authenticate without trusting each other broadly — an institutional design problem as much as a cryptographic one.

Cyber notification and deliberate ambiguity.

Incident notification regimes in cyber conflict remain immature relative to the need. Ambiguity is sometimes deliberate policy: states may prefer not to clarify attribution thresholds or response ladders. Protocols fail when adversaries most want ambiguity — exactly when they are most needed. Design should assume attempted misuse: false notifications, delayed notifications, and notifications used as deception covers.

The counterclaim is thus built in. Narrow protocols may be abandoned under prestige pressure or exploited as covers. Redundancy remains the design principle — cryptographic, procedural, and human confirmation layered so that no single failure authenticates a catastrophic order.

U.S.-China scaffolding for bad days.

U.S.-China military communication rebuilds, when they occur, matter less as friendship symbols than as protocol scaffolding for bad days. Hotlines that are not answered are worse than theater if leaders believe they provide safety they do not. Exercises should rehearse failed authentication and the decision to wait. Public claims about hotline use should be careful; theatrical hotline politics can cheapen the instrument.

Space and undersea domains need notification habits before the first ambiguous incident becomes doctrine by panic. Domestic law must authorize officials to use channels quickly without awaiting perfect interagency clearance on every syllable — another place where democratic procedure can be strength or delay depending on pre-authorization design.

Small states as protocol entrepreneurs.

Small states can champion protocols as security contributions even without large force structure. Hosting talks, proposing notification templates, and offering third-party technical custody are roles that fit limited scale. They also expose small hosts to pressure when great powers want the protocol to bend. Clear mandates and transparent after-action reviews help.

What would make narrow protocols enough — or futile.

What would make narrow protocols sufficient? Repeated crises resolved through them without major breach. What would make them futile? Repeated spoofing or refusal to answer when answer would prevent escalation. Both futures are possible; policy should hedge by building layered systems and by refusing to confuse the existence of a channel with the assurance it will work under pride and fear.

Trust here means verified procedure, not affection. In a world of synthetic media and machine-speed claims, that thinner trust is not a consolation prize. It is often the only kind rivals can build — and the kind that may keep a night crisis from becoming a war that nobody planned to start.


Layering: crypto, procedure, human.

A serious authentication stack for diplomatic and military crisis channels has layers. Cryptographic identity establishes which device or key spoke. Procedural rules establish which office may use which key for which class of message. Human confirmation — call-backs, challenge phrases, second-path verification — establishes that a living authorized person intends the message now. Provenance standards for media sit beside this stack; they do not replace it.

Governments that invest only in the cryptographic layer will be surprised by stolen keys and coerced operators. Governments that invest only in human recognition will be surprised by clones and fatigue. Governments that skip procedural clarity will discover in a crisis that nobody knows who may speak. Layering is expensive. Single-layer trust is a bet against adversaries who study the seams.

Hotlines as instruments that can be emptied.

Hotlines fail in several documented ways across modern crises: they are not answered; they are answered by people without authority; they are used for propaganda after the fact; they are so surrounded by talking-point clearance that they lose speed. AI does not fix those failures. It can worsen them if leaders believe a "secure AI-assisted link" somehow guarantees political willingness to use it.

Pre-authorization is the democratic answer to clearance paralysis: statutes and directives that allow designated officials to send certain classes of crisis messages without waiting for a full interagency choir. Oversight can be retrospective for those classes. Perfect prior clearance of every syllable is incompatible with the purpose of a crisis channel.

Incidents at sea as a living practice.

INCSEA's annual review habit matters as much as its rules. Protocols that are never exercised and never reviewed become archival curiosities. Navy-to-navy professional culture sustained INCSEA through political weather that would have killed a purely diplomatic ornament. AI-era protocols will need analogous professional communities: operators who meet, argue about near-misses, and update procedures without requiring a summit.

Similar logic applies to air intercepts, space proximity operations, and undersea cable incidents. Each domain needs notification templates and practiced communications before the first ambiguous event teaches doctrine by panic. Machine assistance can help operators draft notifications quickly; it should not invent facts to fill templates when sensors are unclear.

Cyber: the hard case for notification.

Cyber incidents combine attribution difficulty, civilian entanglement, and incentives to preserve ambiguity. Notification norms proposed in Track 1.5 settings often stall on definitions: What counts as an incident worth notifying? How fast? With what evidence? Does notification create legal exposure?

Partial regimes — bilateral clarity about certain critical infrastructure categories, or agreements not to spoof specified official diplomatic channels — may be more achievable than comprehensive cyber peace codes. Spoofing official channels is a particularly diplomatic hazard in a synthetic-media environment; narrow anti-spoofing protocols are cousins of the authentication stack.

Digital signatures and the bureaucracy of keys.

Digital signatures are only as good as key custody. Ministries need inventories of which keys bind which offices, rotation schedules, compromise procedures, and training that treats a signature as a political act. A signed PDF from a compromised mid-level account can move markets and militaries if recipients have been taught to trust the seal more than the voice.

Alliance key interoperability raises further issues: can partners validate one another's official signatures quickly? Do they share revocation information? An adversary who can forge or steal across the slowest partner exploits the coalition's weakest custody.

Media provenance in the wild.

C2PA-style credentials will spread unevenly. Some governments and platforms will adopt them; others will not; adversaries will mix credentialed and uncredentialed content strategically. Diplomats should train for mixed environments: when to demand provenance, when its absence is suspicious, when its presence is insufficient. Public communication strategies should avoid implying that credentials equal truth.

Designing for misuse.

Every protocol should be written with a misuse section: how an adversary could send a false notification; how a domestic actor could leak a hotline transcript for political gain; how a spoofed "official" message could be made to look procedurally correct. Mitigations follow: dual control, delayed public acknowledgment rules, second-path confirmation, and exercises that practice ignoring a fluent wrong message.

The thin trust worth building.

Affection between rivals is not the goal. Verified procedure is. In machine-speed conditions, thin trust protocols — hotlines that authenticate, incident rules that professionals practice, provenance that raises the cost of certain lies, anti-spoofing commitments for official channels — are how states buy time for politics to catch up with sensors. They will fail sometimes. Building them anyway is adult behavior in a world where confusion can be manufactured on demand.


From telegraph latency to authentication latency.

Chapter 1's telegraph story was about message travel time and the loss of accidental cooling-off delays. Today's bottleneck is often not travel time. It is the time required to know whether a message is authorized. Authentication latency — how long it takes to confirm that a claim binds a state — becomes a strategic variable when adversaries can generate fluent false claims instantly.

Protocols that reduce authentication latency without reducing standards are therefore as important as faster cables once were. Challenge-response drills, dual-path confirmation, and pre-shared codebooks for crisis categories are boring until the night they prevent a mobilization based on a fake order.

Nuclear signaling and the cost of theatrical channels.

Nuclear-armed rivals have special reasons to keep thin communications alive. They also have special incentives to posture. A channel used primarily for theater teaches operators that the channel is not for truth. Rebuilding seriousness after theatrical use is harder than maintaining it. Leaders who wish to keep protocols for bad days should avoid spending them for good headlines.

Human control commitments over nuclear use decisions — affirmed in various political declarations and bilateral statements in recent years — are normative protocols. They are not verification regimes. Pairing them with technical and procedural work on warning and decision-support tools is the pragmatic path discussed in the next chapter. Alone, the sentence "humans remain in control" can become a lullaby.

Accidents, attachés, and the unglamorous middle.

INCSEA's use of naval attachés as information channels for incidents is a reminder that middlemen matter. Attachés, liaison officers, and duty officers are the human layer of many protocols. Training them for synthetic-media environments — including the possibility that a "message from headquarters" is forged — is now part of professional education. Fatigue rules matter; tired humans accept fluent lies more easily.

Third-country facilitators sometimes host talks when direct channels are frozen. Their protocol role should be defined: what they can convey, what they cannot bind, how notes are kept. AI briefing packs for facilitators should not mint false neutrality.

Domestic law, oversight, and speed.

Democracies need legal pathways that allow rapid use of crisis channels while preserving later accountability. Retrospective oversight, classified notifications to legislative leaders, and inspector-general reviews after the fact can substitute for prior clearance of every phrase. Designing those pathways in peacetime is protocol work as much as diplomacy with rivals.

If domestic law makes authenticated crisis communication illegal or impossible without impossible clearances, the state has disarmed itself in the authentication domain. That is a self-inflicted vulnerability in a synthetic age.

Building inventories before the night.

A practical government program follows from this chapter. Inventory crisis channels and their authentication methods. Inventory keys and signature authorities. Exercise failed authentication. Negotiate or update narrow incident protocols in domains where near-misses cluster. Align commercial provenance efforts with government needs without pretending credentials equal intent. Fund the boring middle: attachés, duty officers, translators who understand doctrine.

Protocols of trust are not poetry. They are procedures that assume enmity and still prefer accident prevention. In that thinness lies their realism — and their necessity when machines make confusion cheap.


Authentication latency as strategy.

When false orders, false incident reports, and false leader videos can be generated in minutes, the scarce resource is not bandwidth. It is confirmed authorization. States that pre-invest in reducing authentication latency — without lowering standards — buy decision time. States that assume "we will know it when we see it" will discover that seeing is easy and knowing is hard.

Drill programs should include cloned voices of known officials, fabricated "signed" directives, and deepfaked news of allied betrayal. The correct response in many injects is to wait for second-path confirmation. Rehearsing waiting sounds passive. It is an active skill under manufactured urgency.

Hotline anthropology.

Operators of crisis communications develop cultures: dark humor, superstition about which phrases "work," quiet knowledge of which counterparts are serious. Those cultures are part of protocol effectiveness. Swapping people too quickly, or surrounding operators with political minders who punish candor, empties the channel even if the hardware remains.

AI assistants that listen to hotline traffic for "summarization" should be treated as sensitive as the channel itself. Default transcription can kill candor. Candor is sometimes the only reason the channel exists.

INCSEA's lesson for non-naval domains.

What made INCSEA durable was professional ownership, annual review, and rules that mapped onto real near-miss patterns. Copying the brand without the professional ownership produces paper. For space, undersea, and cyber, the question is which community will own the annual review and have incentives to tell the truth about near-misses.

Cyber may lack a single professional community with INCSEA-like incentives. That is an argument for narrower bilateral regimes among states that operate critical infrastructure, not for waiting for a universal cyber INCSEA that never arrives.

Provenance diplomacy.

Diplomats will increasingly ask counterparts whether a clip is credentialed, whether a government seal is current, whether a signature key has been revoked. Those questions can be posed without accusing the other side of lying — as procedural hygiene. Making hygiene normal is easier in peacetime visits than in the first hour of a crisis.

Commercial platforms' uneven adoption of provenance tools will create forum-shopping for deception. Public diplomacy strategies should prepare publics for mixed environments rather than promising a future in which every true image wears a badge and every false one does not.


Legal pre-clearance of crisis speech.

Ministries of justice and foreign affairs should jointly draft templates: which officials may authenticate which message classes; what records must be kept; how legislative notification works after the fact. Without templates, lawyers invent process under adrenaline, and operators wait.

International agreements can reference those domestic authorities so counterparts know a message was sent under law. Transparency about authority is itself an authentication aid.

Spoofing official channels as a red line worth naming.

Among possible narrow bargains, commitments not to spoof specified official diplomatic and military channels have unusual clarity. Verification is imperfect, but the norm names a behavior that is particularly destabilizing in a synthetic-media environment. States that reject even that narrow norm are revealing something useful about their risk preferences.

Failure as a design input.

Protocols should be designed with expected failure rates. A hotline that works eight times out of ten is still valuable if the other layers catch the misses. Magical thinking about perfect channels leads to underinvestment in redundancy. Redundancy — second paths, attachés, challenge phrases, delayed action rules — is the adult response to imperfect instruments.

Thin trust is built by practice, review, and misuse analysis. It will not feel like peace. It may still keep a manufactured confusion from becoming an unplanned war.


Alliance authentication and the slowest verifier.

Allies that cannot share provenance judgments quickly become the soft target for synthetic wedges. A practical protocol among allies is a duty roster for verification cells that can issue holding statements within agreed times, even when full forensic certainty is unavailable. Holding statements are not conclusions. They are ways to keep publics from being stampeded while authentication completes.

NATO and partner networks that invest in TEV&V for models should invest similarly in authentication drills for public and military communications. The two investments answer related questions: can we trust a machine claim, and can we trust that a human claim is authorized?

Undersea and space near-misses.

Cable cuts, satellite proximity operations, and ambiguous sensor events in space will generate machine-speed narratives. Notification templates drafted now — who calls whom, with what minimum data, under what time goal — are cheaper than improvisation after markets panic. Small states hosting cable landings or spaceports have standing to propose such templates as security contributions.

Ending on procedure.

Protocols of trust will never feel sufficient to people who want friendship or final disarmament. They are not trying to be. They are trying to keep authentication latency from becoming a cause of war. Build them, exercise them, redesign them after misuse, and refuse to confuse their existence with safety. That refusal is part of the protocol.
