# The Protocols of Trust

Authentication becomes institutional design when rivals need to prevent certain confusions without becoming friends.

Hot lines, incident-at-sea agreements, cyber notification norms, digital signatures, and provenance standards are narrow protocols. They do not require shared ideology. They require shared interest in not stumbling into unwanted war.

The 1963 Washington-Moscow direct communications link remains the ancestor case: a technical path built after leaders discovered that ordinary diplomacy was too slow for nuclear danger.

Modern equivalents must solve authenticity under speed, not only speed. A link that can be spoofed is a hazard.

C2PA and related provenance standards offer tamper-evident history for media. They do not prove political intent. Diplomacy still needs procedures that connect technical provenance to authorized speakers.

Incident notification regimes in cyber conflict are immature relative to the need. Ambiguity is sometimes deliberate policy. Protocols fail when adversaries most want ambiguity—exactly when they are most needed.

The counterclaim is thus built in: protocols may be abandoned under prestige pressure or exploited as deception covers. Design should assume attempted misuse.

Redundancy remains the design principle—cryptographic, procedural, and human confirmation layered so that no single failure authenticates a catastrophic order.

U.S.-China military communication rebuilds, when they occur, matter less as friendship symbols than as protocol scaffolding for bad days.

What would make narrow protocols sufficient? Repeated crises resolved through them without major breach. What would make them futile? Repeated spoofing or refusal to answer when answer would prevent escalation. Both futures are possible; policy should hedge.
