# Crisis Time

A crisis is an argument with a clock running.

The clock may be physical: missiles moving, aircraft approaching, ships converging, a cyberattack spreading, markets opening.

It may be political: a leader must speak, an ally must decide, a legislature must vote, an adversary must interpret silence.

Artificial intelligence changes the clock by changing how quickly information can be processed and options can be generated.

That sounds like more time.

It can become less.

If a warning system detects an anomaly earlier, officials gain minutes or hours. If a model translates communications, fuses sensor data, retrieves precedent, and drafts options immediately, the institution can understand faster.

But faster understanding is not guaranteed.

The system may instead create faster confidence.

That difference is central to crisis stability.

Suppose an ambiguous military movement appears on several feeds. An AI system correlates satellite imagery, public data, logistics activity, and communications patterns. It produces a clear assessment and generates likely response options.

The leader now has an answer sooner.

The leader also knows the adversary may have a similar system.

This creates strategic reflexivity. Each side begins to act not only on what it sees but on what it believes the other side’s machines will infer.

A routine movement may be modified to avoid triggering a model. A deception may be designed to trigger it. A reassurance signal may be optimized for machine visibility rather than human interpretation.

The crisis becomes partly an interaction among inference systems.

That is new in degree, not in kind. Militaries have always tried to shape enemy intelligence. What changes is the speed and scale of the inference.

The safest use of AI in a crisis may therefore be to widen the space of interpretation before narrowing it.

A well-designed system should ask: what else could explain this?

What evidence is independent? Which feed could be compromised? What does the adversary know about our collection? Which conclusions depend on intent rather than observable action? What would we expect to see next under each hypothesis?

This creates cognitive time.

Cognitive time is not measured only in minutes. It is the room inside a decision process for uncertainty to remain legitimate.

Bad crisis systems destroy cognitive time even when they save clock time.

A dashboard with one red probability can do this. So can an AI-generated memo that collapses ten conflicting indicators into one narrative. Once the narrative is coherent, dissent begins to look like delay.

That is dangerous because crisis decisions are often irreversible in sequence.

A state can raise readiness, move forces, issue warnings, impose restrictions, evacuate personnel, or conduct cyber operations. Each step changes what the other side sees. The response to ambiguity becomes new evidence in the adversary’s model.

This is how a crisis can become self-confirming.

The solution is not paralysis.

Governments sometimes need to act quickly. Delay can be fatal. The point is to distinguish reversible actions from irreversible ones and informational actions from escalatory ones.

AI can help here too.

It can generate response ladders, identify actions that preserve options, simulate alliance reactions, and flag measures likely to be misread. It can help staff compare the time required for a move with the time required to communicate its purpose.

That last comparison matters.

Military action can now be planned faster than diplomatic explanation can be trusted.

A deployment may occur in minutes. A private message may take longer because the recipient has to authenticate it, interpret it, consult allies, and decide whether to believe the stated purpose.

Technology therefore increases the importance of preexisting channels.

A crisis is a terrible time to invent trust.

Hotlines, defense contacts, diplomatic backchannels, notification agreements, and shared terminology do not guarantee restraint. They create infrastructure through which restraint can be attempted.

The most valuable protocols are often narrow.

A government may not trust its adversary’s intentions but can still agree on how to authenticate an emergency message. It may not agree on the cause of a cyber incident but can still communicate that a particular military movement is precautionary. It may not accept the other side’s legal position but can still clarify whether an exercise has ended.

These are modest achievements.

Modesty is a feature.

The grand bargain is rare in crisis time. The useful question is often smaller: what fact, if understood correctly by both sides, reduces the chance that the next action is taken under a false premise?

AI can help discover those facts.

It cannot make the other side believe them.

That still requires a channel.
