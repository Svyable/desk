# Crisis Time

Crises compress the distance between perception and action.

A missile warning, a cyber intrusion into critical infrastructure, a clash at sea, a sudden refugee surge across a border, an assassination, a nuclear plant accident in a war zone—each can force governments to interpret incomplete information while audiences, markets, and militaries demand clarity.

Artificial intelligence enters this environment as both a promise of faster understanding and a mechanism that can further compress the clock.

The governing question is narrow and difficult.

Can AI shorten the time to understand a crisis without shortening the time to miscalculate it?

Faster fusion of sensors, open sources, and diplomatic reporting can, in principle, give leaders a clearer picture sooner. Anomaly detection can flag patterns humans would miss. Translation can make foreign statements available in minutes. Damage assessments can be drafted continuously. These are not trivial gains. In some accidents and natural disasters, speed of comprehension saves lives.

In strategic crises between armed states, speed has a more ambiguous moral valence.

Understanding sooner helps if the understanding is accurate and if the extra minutes are used to communicate, verify, and avoid irreversible steps. Understanding sooner hurts if the picture is wrong, or right but incomplete in a decisive way, or framed so confidently that leaders treat a precaution as a necessity.

The same system that detects a missile launch early may also misclassify a test, a sensor fault, or debris. Historical nuclear false alarms are reminders that warning without wise interpretation is not safety.

Research on AI and strategic stability returns repeatedly to decision tempo. As conventional operations and information attacks accelerate, leaders fear being second. Decision-support tools are offered as a way to keep humans inside the loop of a faster fight.

The loop metaphor can mislead.

Being inside a loop that completes every few seconds is not the same as having time to ask a rival capital what it thinks it is doing. Diplomacy needs intervals. AI can create intervals by clearing noise. It can destroy intervals by feeding a continuous stream of actionable-looking updates that make waiting feel like negligence.

U.S.-China military and diplomatic communication has illustrated how channels themselves become crisis infrastructure.

When talks are suspended, the time needed to clarify an incident lengthens. When channels reopen, they do not automatically carry trust. They carry the possibility of asking. AI does not replace that possibility. It can, however, change what leaders believe they already know before they ask.

A polished incident assessment delivered before the first call may harden talking points prematurely. The call then becomes a performance of positions rather than a search for shared facts.

Cyber incidents have a particularly nasty time structure. Attribution is often slow. Effects can be fast. Publics demand names. Allies demand consultation. Markets demand reassurance. A model that produces an early attribution narrative will be politically useful and often underdetermined.

Diplomacy's job includes resisting premature certainty without becoming paralyzed. That balance is older than AI. AI changes how cheap premature certainty has become.

Military alerting systems add another pressure. If AI-enhanced indication-and-warning shortens the time from anomaly to alert, diplomatic systems must shorten the time from alert to authentic contact with the other side—or deliberately refuse to treat every alert as a political emergency.

Both responses are costly. The first requires maintained channels and practiced protocols. The second requires leaders willing to absorb the domestic risk of not visibly reacting.

The counterclaim deserves full steelmanning.

Faster fusion and anomaly detection may create more time for diplomacy, not less, by reducing the period of pure confusion in which fear dominates. If leaders previously spent hours establishing basic facts, and now spend minutes, the saved hours could be used for calls, coalition management, and de-escalatory signaling. Some crisis failures are failures of ignorance. Tools that reduce ignorance are pro-diplomatic in those cases.

The empirical problem is that saved time is not automatically allocated to diplomacy. It can be allocated to military options development, public messaging, or simply to more meetings that consume the surplus. Institutional design determines whether speed becomes slack for judgment or fuel for action.

A crisis playbook that pairs each acceleration in warning with a required human communication step is safer than one that pairs warning directly with pre-authorized moves.

What would a diplomatic speed budget look like in practice?

Identify decisions that are hard to reverse: use of force, public ultimata, alliance-triggering statements, certain cyber responses, evacuation orders that signal expected war. Surround those with mandatory verification and consultation gates even when dashboards are green.

Identify decisions that are reversible and information-producing: requests for clarification, quiet notifications, temporary pauses, technical check-backs. Allow those to move faster with machine assistance.

The point is not to be slow for its own sake. It is to spend speed where speed buys options and refuse speed where speed spends them.

Exercises should rehearse the ugly case in which the AI assessment is wrong. If every exercise assumes the system's picture is correct, participants learn to obey the picture. If exercises inject false positives, delayed corrections, and conflicting allied assessments, participants learn to value channels and dissent.

NATO and national exercises that incorporate AI tools should treat corrupted or contested machine input as a standard inject, not as an unfair trick.

Domestic politics will punish some forms of wise delay. A leader who waits for verification while a rival's narrative fills the public square may look weak. Synthetic publics worsen that penalty. Crisis time is therefore not only measured in military minutes. It is measured in narrative minutes.

Governments that cannot authenticate their own statements quickly will feel driven to speak before they know. Authentication infrastructure becomes part of crisis tempo management.

Small states face a distinct compression. They may receive allied warning products generated by systems they do not control and do not fully understand. Their diplomatic services must decide whether to defer to the allied picture or to insist on local verification. Deference is faster. It can also import someone else's misclassification. In crisis time, that choice is felt as a demand to decide whose dashboard counts.

Human endurance remains a hidden variable. Crisis teams fatigue. Models do not. A system that keeps producing crisp options at hour thirty-six can outlast the humans who know why the crisp options are wrong. Shift discipline, enforced sleep, and second-team review are then safety systems as real as any software patch. Treating stamina as a soft concern is a good way to let automation dominate by attrition.

Records made under time pressure become the history that later judges the decision. If machine recommendations are not logged with their uncertainty and the human overrides beside them, after-action reviews will mythologize. Either the machine will be blamed for what humans chose, or humans will be blamed for ignoring what the machine supposedly knew. Honest logging is a form of temporal justice. It also improves future training by preserving how ignorance actually felt.

A workable checklist for foreign ministries is deliberately unromantic.

Before a crisis: map which machine systems feed senior briefs; require known failure modes to be briefed alongside capabilities; pre-clear authentication paths for public statements; practice adversary notification templates; decide in advance which categories of action need ministerial voice even if a model is confident.

During a crisis: label every machine-derived claim with confidence and age of data; assign a human devil's advocate shift; keep at least one channel warm solely for clarification questions; resist publishing attribution theater that closes diplomatic offramps; watch for allied dashboard divergence and treat it as an emergency of meaning, not only of politics.

After a crisis: compare what the systems said to what turned out to be true; punish fluent wrongness if it was rewarded in the moment; update playbooks; tell the public what can be told without destroying future sources.

These steps will be skipped under pressure unless they are drilled. Drills are how institutions store time.

Incident-at-sea agreements and military hotlines exist because earlier generations learned that technical proximity without political protocol produces accidents. AI-enabled sensing increases technical proximity again. The protocols must thicken in response, not thin out under the illusion that better sensors abolish politics.

Market crises interwoven with security crises add another clock. Capital flight, energy price spikes, and shipping insurance rates can move faster than cabinets. Machine analysis of markets can help leaders see cascading risk. It can also panic them into premature security measures that validate the panic. Coordination between finance ministries and foreign ministries under machine-speed information is part of modern crisis statecraft, not an afterthought.

The falsifying evidence for this chapter's worry would be a pattern of crises in which AI-assisted states clearly used speed to widen diplomatic windows—more successful clarifications, fewer shots fired under ambiguity, better allied coordination—without a corresponding pattern of accelerated error. Parts of humanitarian response and disaster diplomacy may already show benign acceleration. The strategic-military domain has less public proof.

Until it does, the prudent working theory is that speed is dual-use inside the crisis itself.

Crisis time is not only a technical parameter. It is a political allocation of attention under fear. Machines can move information through that interval faster than any previous staff. They cannot decide, on their own, whether the interval should be used to load weapons or to place a call.

That allocation remains the last human channel's burden. In a crisis it arrives without the courtesy of a seminar timetable.

The deepest irony of machine-speed crisis support is that the scarcest preparation is still human rehearsal of the decision to wait.
Humanitarian corridors and evacuation diplomacy show the benign face of speed.

When the task is to move civilians, match hospital capacity, or coordinate relief flights, faster translation and logistics optimization are almost unambiguous goods. The same government that should refuse to automate a nuclear alerting response can rightly automate parts of a disaster cell. The skill is not a general attitude for or against speed. It is classification of decision types under stress.

Classification fails when everything is labeled urgent.

A dashboard that paints every anomaly red teaches leaders that waiting is always negligence. Threshold design—what rises to the principal, what stays with a watch officer—is therefore as important as model accuracy. Many crisis errors begin as escalation of ordinary variance into political emergency.

Alliance consultation clauses interact badly with machine tempo if left unexamined.

Treaties and political agreements often require talking before certain acts. Those clauses assumed human staffing rhythms. If national systems now generate a shared picture in minutes, consultation must either speed up correspondingly or become a bottleneck that members bypass. Bypassing destroys the alliance's political function. Speeding up without shared confidence standards produces consultation that is quick and empty.

Empty consultation is worse than slow consultation because it creates the documentary appearance of agreement without the substance.

Legal advisers in the crisis cell deserve explicit time allocations.

Machine drafts of notifications, Article 51 letters, sanctions designations, and public statements can be produced instantly. Lawyers who must stake professional reputations on those texts cannot. A process that treats legal review as latency to be minimized will eventually produce a fluent illegal act—or a fluent act whose illegality becomes a second crisis.

Public international law already struggles with facts that move faster than courts. Adding generative fluency without protected review time intensifies the mismatch.

Subnational actors complicate crisis time further.

Governors, mayors, port authorities, and private critical-infrastructure operators may act on their own machine-assisted pictures before national diplomats have chosen a line. Foreign ministries need liaison habits with those actors in peacetime, not improvised phone trees after the dashboard lights up. Otherwise the state's external voice will be overtaken by its internal fragments.

Historical cases still discipline the imagination.

The Able Archer war scare, various nuclear false alarms, and accidental clashes at sea show how technical systems and political fear interact. None of those episodes is a perfect analogy for generative AI. Together they warn against assuming that more information automatically stabilizes. Sometimes more information, poorly timed or poorly authenticated, raises the perceived cost of restraint.

Restraint needs advocates in the room who are not penalized for being right too early.

Career incentives during crises favor visible action. A model that constantly surfaces options for action allies with that incentive structure. Institutions that want restraint to remain available must staff and protect roles whose job is to argue for verification and delay. Without those roles, the machine's option list becomes a menu that leaders feel obliged to order from.

Journalists and legislators will demand after-action transparency that sits uneasily with classified model logs.

Democracies need enough disclosure to maintain public trust without handing adversaries a manual for the next deception. That tension is old. What is new is the volume of machine-mediated steps that may need explaining. Preparing redacted explanation templates in peacetime is part of crisis readiness.

Partner capitals in different time zones create sequential rather than simultaneous decision windows.

A model that briefs Washington at peak staff hours may confront Asian allies at the worst staffing moment, or the reverse. Human channel maintenance includes knowing who can speak authoritatively at 03:00 local time and how to avoid treating silence born of clocks as silence born of policy.

The chapter's falsification standard remains. If AI-assisted crises show systematically wider diplomatic windows and fewer miscalculation shootouts, the argument narrows. Until then, treat speed as a resource to budget, not a trophy to maximize.

Crisis time ends when someone with authority decides that the next act can wait for a question to be asked across a channel that still works.

That decision is small on a flowchart.

It is large in history.

Technical debt becomes strategic debt in a crisis.

A ministry that never documented which model version feeds which alert board will spend the first hour of an emergency arguing about its own plumbing. Configuration management is not glamorous. It is how a state avoids discovering its tools in public.

Vendors and cloud dependencies introduce foreign jurisdictional risk into national crisis response.

If a critical analytic service can be degraded by another state's legal process, network control, or corporate decision, continuity plans must include degraded modes that still allow authentication and communication. Sovereignty theater that ignores these dependencies will fail at the worst moment.

Training audiences matter as much as training operators.

Principals who only see successful demos will overtrust. Principals who are occasionally shown failure cases in tabletop form will ask better questions when the stakes are real. Scaring leaders is not the aim. Calibrating them is.

Finally, remember that adversaries are reading open discussions of AI crisis risk and may craft operations to exploit known worries—flooding sensors, spoofing indicators, or forcing human verification bottlenecks. Resilience includes refusing to let the fear of deepfakes itself become a tripwire. Verification discipline must be steady rather than spasmodic.

The last human channel in crisis time is the person who can say: we do not know enough to do the irreversible thing, and we still have a way to ask.

A crisis cell that cannot explain its own data sources will eventually brief fiction with confidence.

Officers should be able to point to the sensor, the cable, the ally report, or the model inference behind each load-bearing claim.

If they cannot, the claim is not ready for the principal.

Interface design is therefore strategy.

Vendors who optimize for cinematic clarity without audit trails are selling a hazard.

Governments that buy those interfaces without modification are volunteering for future regret.

Parallel human watch teams remain useful even when machines monitor the same feeds.

Their job is not to race the machine to first detection.

Their job is to notice when the machine's ontology misses a political meaning.

A protest that looks like noise in a clustering algorithm may be a signal to a diplomat who knows the city's unions.

A quiet parliament may matter more than a loud network.

Hybrid discovery—machine breadth plus human depth—beats either alone.

Cross-border infrastructure incidents will test these habits repeatedly.

Undersea cables, pipelines, satellites, and payment systems create crises that are simultaneously technical, commercial, and diplomatic.

AI can help locate faults and propose attributions.

Diplomacy must still manage the narrative of blame before evidence matures.

Premature blame can close the cooperation needed to repair the infrastructure.

Delayed blame can look like weakness.

The narrow path is provisional public language paired with private technical channels.

Machine drafts often push toward definitive verbs.

Editors in the crisis cell should be rewarded for keeping verbs provisional until the facts earn certainty.

Nuclear adjacent scenarios deserve special institutional insulation.

Even research literature that merely explores generative support near nuclear command raises the question of how advice might compress deliberation.

States that publicly commit to retaining human authority over nuclear employment should specify what information products may and may not automate.

Ambiguity on that point invites both domestic complacency and adversary misreading.

Diplomatic signaling about those limits can itself be stabilizing if it is credible and specific.

Vague reassurances convince no one who studies the incentives.

Regional organizations and the United Nations bring additional clocks into major crises.

Resolutions, briefings, and fact-finding missions move on procedural calendars that ignore dashboards.

AI can help missions process material faster.

It cannot abolish the politics of mandates.

Foreign ministries that treat multilateral time as pure friction misunderstand a venue where legitimacy is still manufactured.

Sometimes legitimacy buys more de-escalation than a faster national option memo.

The interaction between national machine speed and multilateral procedural speed will be a recurring twenty-first century tension.

Managing it is part of crisis diplomacy's craft.

Stock markets close and open; security crises do not.

Overnight hours remain dangerous because staffing thins while models continue to write.

On-call rosters should include people empowered to freeze irreversible recommendations until a quorum of human review exists.

A freeze rule feels inefficient in a drill.

It can be lifesaving in a false alarm.

False alarms deserve trophies when they are caught.

Cultures that only celebrate successful rapid response will underinvest in successful non-response.

Non-response is often the correct diplomatic act.

Teaching that without breeding passivity is a leadership problem, not a software problem.

After enough machine-assisted crises, publics will ask whether humans were actually deciding.

The answer must be documentable.

If it is not, democratic control of security policy will erode in practice while remaining intact in theory.

That erosion would be a slow-motion strategic failure independent of any single miscalculation.

Crisis time, properly understood, is therefore also constitutional time.

It tests whether a political system can move quickly without forgetting who is allowed to bind it.

Artificial intelligence sharpens the test.

It does not grade the exam.

People still do.

They will do it under fear, fatigue, and incomplete knowledge, as they always have.

The tools will be new.

The requirement to keep a channel open long enough to prevent a mistake from becoming a fate remains the same.
