# The Authentication Problem

The most dangerous fake message in a crisis may be the one nobody believes.

For years, public discussion of deepfakes centered on a vivid scenario: a fabricated video of a national leader appears online, says something inflammatory, and causes panic before anyone can prove it is false.

That risk is real.

It is not the only one.

A world full of convincing synthetic media creates a second problem. Authentic recordings become deniable.

A government confronted with embarrassing evidence can claim fabrication. A political leader can dismiss a real statement as machine-generated. Citizens who already distrust institutions can treat every inconvenient image as suspect. Intelligence officers can spend precious time proving that a piece of media is genuine rather than asking what it means.

The technology creates falsehood.

The expectation of falsehood creates ambiguity.

For diplomacy, ambiguity about authorship is not a peripheral information problem. It touches the basic unit of statecraft: the authorized message.

Diplomatic systems are elaborate machines for answering a simple question.

Who is speaking for whom?

An ambassador's importance comes partly from the fact that the ambassador is not merely another informed person. The government has authorized that person to represent it.

A treaty requires signatures because words acquire different consequences when an authorized party attaches itself to them.

A demarche matters because one government intentionally delivers it to another.

A hotline matters because the path itself helps establish that the message comes from the leadership.

Authentication is built into diplomatic form.

Artificial intelligence makes that form more valuable.

The technical ability to imitate a voice or face has improved steadily. By the early 2020s, researchers and national-security institutions were already warning that synthetic audio, video, images, and text could be used to falsify orders, imitate officials, create confusion, and undermine trust. DARPA invested in programs to detect and characterize manipulated media. Researchers proposed provenance standards that could attach tamper-evident information to digital content. Governments and technology companies began experimenting with ways to distinguish material whose origin could be verified from material circulating without a reliable chain of custody.

These efforts matter.

They do not solve the diplomatic problem by themselves.

A perfect detector would tell you whether a file had been manipulated.

It would not tell you whether the president meant what the president said.

Diplomacy has always operated one layer above literal authenticity.

A statement can be genuine and still be strategic deception.

A government can publicly threaten an action it does not intend to take.

A negotiator can accurately describe a position while leaving out an internal disagreement.

A leader can authorize language designed for domestic politics and privately ask another government not to overreact to it.

The message can be authentic while the meaning remains contested.

AI adds a new layer beneath that old problem.

Now the message itself may not be authentic.

This creates what might be called an authentication stack.

At the bottom is technical provenance.

Did this file come from the claimed device, account, network, or institution? Has it been altered? Is there a valid signature? Is the chain of custody known?

Above that is identity.

Did the claimed person actually say or authorize the message?

Above identity is authority.

Was the speaker empowered to commit the government, or merely expressing a view?

Above authority is intention.

What did the government want the other side to believe or do?

Above intention is credibility.

Will the government carry out the promise or threat?

Diplomats already spend their careers moving up and down this stack, though they rarely describe it this way.

Synthetic media makes the bottom layers unstable.

That instability can contaminate everything above them.

Suppose an audio recording appears during a military crisis. It sounds like a defense minister instructing units to prepare for an attack.

A government has several questions to answer.

Is the audio real?

If it is real, was the instruction issued in an operational context or a training exercise?

If it was operational, did the minister have authority?

If the minister had authority, does preparation indicate imminent attack or precaution?

If it is precaution, what action by the other side produced it?

Every minute spent on the first question is a minute not spent on the rest.

This is where speed returns to the story.

Synthetic information is most dangerous when the verification cycle is slower than the decision cycle.

A false video that takes six hours to debunk may be harmless in a dispute unfolding over six months.

The same video can matter enormously if a commander has six minutes to interpret it.

This is why deepfakes belong in a book about diplomacy rather than only in a book about misinformation.

Diplomacy is one of the institutions designed to buy verification time.

A secure channel allows one government to ask another: Did you issue this order? Is this statement authorized? Are your forces moving because you intend to attack, or because you believe we do?

The answer may be deceptive.

But the ability to ask changes the problem.

During the Cold War, direct communications links were built because leaders understood that miscalculation could arise from delays and uncertainty. The 1963 U.S.-Soviet hot line emerged from the experience of the Cuban Missile Crisis, when ordinary communications had struggled to keep up with events.

The next generation of crisis communications needs to solve a related problem.

Not merely speed.

Authenticity under speed.

This suggests a simple but consequential principle: the most important diplomatic messages of the AI era should become harder to fake than ordinary public content.

That does not mean every conversation should become technologically burdensome.

It means governments need known, rehearsed, redundant methods for proving that a message is theirs.

Some of those methods will be cryptographic.

Digital signatures can help establish that a message came through an authorized system and has not been altered. Provenance frameworks such as the standards developed by the Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity attempt to make the origin and editing history of digital media more visible and tamper-evident.

Some methods will be procedural.

A government can agree that certain crisis messages are valid only through designated channels. Officials can establish call-back procedures. Embassies can confirm statements through secure networks. Leaders can prearrange authentication phrases or use multiple independent paths.

Some methods will remain stubbornly human.

A diplomat who has spoken to the same counterpart for years may detect that a message does not fit the person's language, habits, or authority. That intuition is not proof. It is a reason to verify before acting.

The important point is redundancy.

No single authentication system should become a new single point of failure.

Cryptographic credentials can be stolen.

Secure networks can be disrupted.

Officials can be compromised.

A genuine account can publish a false statement.

A real leader can lose control of subordinates.

The correct architecture therefore resembles aviation more than social media.

Critical systems assume that individual components fail.

They layer checks.

Diplomacy has often done this informally. An embassy cable, an intelligence report, an allied message, and a public statement are compared. A foreign minister calls another foreign minister. An ambassador is summoned. A military hotline is used. The redundancy feels inefficient until the sources disagree.

AI increases both the need for this redundancy and the ability to manage it.

A machine can compare thousands of media items for inconsistencies, trace reposting patterns, retrieve earlier language, and flag anomalies. It can help forensic teams detect manipulation. It can monitor whether a supposed official statement appeared through expected channels.

The machine can defend the human channel.

It can also attack it.

A government conducting an influence operation does not need to create one perfect fake if it can create enough conflicting artifacts to slow verification.

This is a different strategy from persuasion.

The objective is not to make everyone believe the same falsehood.

It is to make coordinated belief expensive.

A society in which every faction carries its own evidence has difficulty acting collectively.

An alliance in which members cannot agree on what happened has difficulty responding quickly.

A military chain of command in which every unexpected order might be synthetic has difficulty operating.

The attack surface is trust itself.

This makes provenance a geopolitical resource.

Countries and alliances that can authenticate their official communications quickly will have an advantage over those that cannot.

The advantage will be partly technical and partly institutional.

Consider an alliance responding to a fast-moving crisis. Intelligence arrives from several member states. Some of it is classified. Some comes from commercial providers. Some comes from social media. Some has been processed by national AI systems with different standards, training data, and security controls.

The alliance does not merely need information sharing.

It needs confidence sharing.

What does one country's "high confidence" mean to another?

Was a machine-generated assessment reviewed by a human?

Which source is primary?

Can the receiving government inspect the evidence?

Has the content been altered in transmission?

Does the sender stand behind the conclusion or merely pass it along?

These questions existed before AI.

AI makes the volume of material larger and the chain of transformation longer.

An image may be collected by a commercial satellite, processed by an automated system, combined with open-source reporting, summarized by a model, translated by another model, and placed into a briefing generated for a minister.

Each step can add value.

Each step can also add distance from the underlying evidence.

The final sentence may be excellent.

The reader still needs to know how it was made.

This is why source provenance should become part of diplomatic literacy.

A future diplomat may need to understand not only the origin of a claim but the transformations between origin and presentation.

Was this a direct translation or a summary?

Did the model retrieve from a closed archive or generate from its parameters?

Was the satellite image enhanced?

Was the audio cleaned?

Which parts of the final assessment came from machine inference rather than observed fact?

The questions sound technical.

Their purpose is political.

Governments make commitments based on what they believe.

If the chain of belief becomes opaque, accountability weakens.

A minister who acts on a human analyst's memorandum can ask the analyst to explain the judgment.

A minister who acts on an AI-assisted briefing may need to ask a more distributed question: which people designed the workflow, which sources entered it, which model transformed them, which official reviewed the result, and where the uncertainty changed along the way?

That is not an argument for paralyzing audit.

It is an argument for making the consequential parts inspectable.

Diplomacy has always depended on records.

Treaties, cables, memoranda of conversation, signed notes, diplomatic pouches, official seals, classified networks, and archives all exist partly because states need to know what was said and who was responsible.

The AI era does not abolish this documentary instinct.

It makes it more necessary.

There is another reason.

Authentication can itself become a form of escalation control.

Imagine two adversaries agree, before a crisis, on a protocol for verifying leader-level messages. The protocol does not resolve their political conflict. It does not require trust in the other government's intentions. It requires only mutual interest in preventing an unauthorized or fabricated message from starting a war.

This is a narrow agreement.

Narrow agreements are often where diplomacy works.

The United States and Soviet Union did not need to agree on the nature of the Cold War to agree that a direct communications link reduced the risk of catastrophic misunderstanding.

Future rivals may not need to agree on AI governance in general to agree that no machine-generated voice message should be treated as a valid leader communication unless it passes designated authentication procedures.

They may be able to establish rules for confirming anomalous military orders, high-consequence cyber notifications, nuclear incidents, or sudden changes in alert status.

The specific protocols will vary.

The diplomatic opportunity comes from separating shared risk from shared values.

Rivals can disagree about almost everything and still prefer not to be tricked into war by a third party.

This is where the authentication problem intersects with middle powers, nonstate actors, and alliances.

Synthetic media lowers the cost of impersonation.

That gives smaller actors a potential way to create disproportionate effects.

A fabricated statement attributed to a major power could be designed not to fool the major power itself, which may authenticate its own communications quickly, but to influence an ally, a market, a local military commander, or a public audience before verification travels.

The weakest link may sit outside the principal channel.

Crisis communication therefore cannot be protected only at the top.

Governments need a hierarchy of trusted channels extending into the institutions that might act on a message.

That is difficult.

It is also familiar.

Military organizations already use authentication procedures because they cannot assume every received order is legitimate. Financial systems use signatures and controls because money cannot move safely on appearance alone. Software systems use certificates and keys because identity on a network is not self-evident.

Diplomacy is entering the same world.

The cultural adjustment may be harder than the technical one.

For much of the television age, seeing a recognizable leader speak on video carried a strong presumption of reality. That presumption is weakening.

People will adapt.

They already have.

The question is what replaces the old shortcut.

The worst replacement would be generalized disbelief.

If citizens and officials respond to synthetic media by concluding that nothing can be known, the fabricator gains even when every individual fake is detected.

The better replacement is structured trust.

Do not believe a message because it looks real.

Believe it because the channel, provenance, institution, and accountable people behind it make a claim about its reality.

That is less convenient than sight.

It may be more durable.

The phrase "trust but verify" has been used so often that it has nearly lost its content.

The AI era reverses the order.

Verify so that limited trust remains possible.

A diplomat does not need to believe everything a counterpart says.

The diplomat needs to know that the counterpart actually said it.

That modest certainty may become one of the foundations on which every more ambitious negotiation rests.

There is a further complication.

Authentication can become political theater.

A government may possess technical proof that a piece of media is fabricated and still fail to persuade the audience that matters. Citizens may distrust the institution presenting the proof. Allies may demand access to the underlying evidence. Adversaries may publish competing forensic claims. Platforms may label content differently. Experts may disagree about detection methods.

Technical verification is therefore necessary without always being sufficient.

Diplomacy has an advantage here because it can operate in private.

Two governments do not need to convince the entire internet in order to prevent a misunderstanding between them.

They need a channel in which each can establish what it has and has not authorized.

That private certainty can exist even while public argument continues.

This is one reason backchannels survive every communications revolution.

Public diplomacy and private diplomacy solve different problems.

Public statements create commitments, reassurance, deterrence, and political legitimacy.

Private messages allow governments to test meanings that would be too costly to announce.

Synthetic media increases the distance between those functions.

A government may need to say publicly that a fake is false while privately telling the adversary that it does not interpret the incident as authorized behavior.

It may need to ask whether a real but inflammatory statement was intended as policy or domestic performance.

It may need to preserve room for the other side to disown an unauthorized actor without forcing a public humiliation.

These are old diplomatic moves under new technical conditions.

The technology changes the evidence.

It does not eliminate face-saving.

This matters because verification protocols can fail if they are designed only by engineers.

A technically perfect system that forces a leader to admit error publicly may be less useful in a crisis than a less elegant channel that allows both sides to step away from escalation.

The protocol must fit politics.

The Cuban Missile Crisis is instructive again.

The secret understanding over the Jupiter missiles in Turkey was useful partly because it was not packaged as a public concession extracted under pressure. The political form of the agreement mattered to whether the substance was possible.

Authentication systems in the AI era will need the same sensitivity.

There may be situations in which a government needs to confirm privately that a message is genuine without publicly endorsing the interpretation circulating around it.

There may be situations in which a government needs to confirm that an official account was compromised without revealing how it knows.

There may be situations in which two rivals agree that a particular incident was unauthorized while both avoid describing which internal actor caused it.

These arrangements can look evasive from outside.

They can also keep a mistake from becoming a war.

This is why the human channel cannot be replaced by a universal public ledger of truth.

International politics contains secrets for reasons beyond deception.

Some compromises require discretion.

Some intelligence cannot be exposed without destroying the source.

Some military vulnerabilities cannot be disclosed merely to prove that a warning is sincere.

Some private assurances work because they allow each government to tell a different domestic story about the same outcome.

A functioning authentication architecture must therefore support selective disclosure.

Prove enough.

Do not assume that proving everything is either possible or desirable.

There is also a temporal problem.

Provenance systems are strongest when content begins inside a trusted chain.

They are weaker when governments confront material that has already escaped into the open internet without reliable provenance.

That means the future information environment may divide into two classes.

Content with a trusted origin.

Content without one.

The second class will not automatically be false. Much valuable evidence begins outside official systems. A citizen's phone video, an anonymous leak, a local journalist's recording, or a copied document may be genuine and strategically important.

The absence of provenance cannot become a synonym for fabrication.

That would hand governments an easy way to dismiss inconvenient evidence.

So institutions need two skills at once.

They need to value provenance when it exists.

They need forensic and contextual methods for evaluating evidence when it does not.

This is harder than a binary authenticity label.

It is also more honest.

The goal is not to create a world in which every piece of information receives a green check or red cross.

The goal is to prevent consequential actors from pretending that uncertain provenance and falsehood are the same thing.

Diplomats are unusually well positioned for this kind of uncertainty because the profession has always worked with incomplete attribution.

A report from an opposition figure may be biased and useful.

A rumor in a capital may be false in literal detail and revealing in political effect.

A statement by a junior official may not represent policy but can still expose a faction's preferred direction.

Information is rarely valuable only because it is verified.

The challenge is attaching the right weight to it.

AI systems can help assign that weight if they expose their reasoning and sources.

They can make the problem worse if they collapse a messy evidence field into a single confident sentence.

The authentication problem therefore reaches all the way into model design.

A system used for diplomacy should be able to say: the video is unverified, the account that posted it is newly created, the language differs from the ministry's normal phrasing, two established outlets report that the official is elsewhere, and no authorized channel has confirmed the statement.

That is useful.

A system that simply says "likely fake" hides the structure of the judgment.

The first helps a human decide.

The second asks to be trusted.

This distinction should shape procurement.

Governments will be tempted to buy models for accuracy.

They should also buy them for inspectability.

Can an analyst see the source?

Can a reviewer reconstruct the path?

Can a mistaken judgment be corrected in a way that changes future behavior?

Can the institution distinguish model output from official assessment?

Can records survive long enough for an after-action review?

These are not merely compliance questions.

They determine whether an institution can learn.

International crises generate myths quickly.

After the fact, every successful decision can look inevitable and every warning can look obvious. Records are what allow governments to recover the uncertainty that existed at the time.

AI-generated decisions need records for the same reason.

A future historian should be able to tell not only what the machine recommended but what evidence it used, what humans changed, and who accepted responsibility.

Without that trail, states may become faster while becoming worse at understanding their own mistakes.

The ultimate purpose of authentication is therefore larger than proving media.

It is preserving responsibility.

A state is a political entity because actions can be attributed to authorized institutions and people.

If consequential messages become detached from accountable authors, diplomacy becomes harder at exactly the moment technology makes message production easier.

The response should not be nostalgia for wax seals.

It should be a modern equivalent of the seal's political function.

This came from us.

This did not.

This person had authority.

This person did not.

This commitment is real.

This output is advisory.

The categories sound basic.

In a machine-generated information environment, basic categories become infrastructure.

And infrastructure becomes visible only after it fails.
