# Prologue — The Channel

On the evening of October 27, 1962, the machinery of the American government had already produced more information than any one person could comfortably hold.

Reconnaissance flights had photographed Soviet missile sites in Cuba. Military officers were updating readiness. Intelligence analysts were trying to determine which launchers were operational and how quickly the remaining sites could become so. Diplomatic messages were moving among Washington, Moscow, the United Nations, and allied capitals. Newspapers were reporting a crisis whose private dimensions they could only partly see. A U-2 reconnaissance aircraft had been shot down over Cuba, killing Major Rudolf Anderson. Another U-2, flying an air-sampling mission near Alaska, strayed into Soviet airspace before returning.

The danger was not that the United States lacked information.

The danger was that events were moving faster than the governments involved could reliably interpret one another.

That evening Robert Kennedy met Soviet Ambassador Anatoly Dobrynin at the Justice Department. The attorney general was not a professional diplomat. His authority in the room came from a more primitive institution: his brother was the president of the United States, and Dobrynin knew he could carry a message to him without passing through the full machinery of either government.

The meeting has been reconstructed from American and Soviet records. The accounts are not identical. That is part of what makes it useful.

Dobrynin reported to Moscow that Kennedy described a situation that could slip out of political control. American military pressure was rising. If the missile sites were attacked and Soviet personnel were killed, the Soviet Union might retaliate in Europe. One action would call forth another. Kennedy conveyed that Washington could accept a settlement built around the removal of the Soviet missiles from Cuba, a public assurance against an American invasion of the island, and a confidential understanding that the United States would later remove Jupiter missiles from Turkey.

The Turkish part could not be public.

There was no digital signature attached to the promise. No public communique described the bargain. No treaty text carried it. The men were operating inside the most dangerous international crisis of the nuclear age with an arrangement whose usefulness depended, in part, on not writing all of it down.

A few days later the discomfort became explicit. Khrushchev sent a letter that described the understanding more directly than the White House wanted. Robert Kennedy returned it. According to Dobrynin's report, Kennedy reaffirmed that the American side would keep the oral promise while resisting a written record that could expose the linkage. His own memorandum to Secretary of State Dean Rusk framed the exchange differently and denied a formal deal over Turkey. Historians would spend decades arguing over what precisely had been promised, by whom, and on what terms.

The ambiguity did not make the channel useless.

It made the channel human.

The two sides were trying to solve more than a problem of information transmission. They were trying to communicate intention under conditions in which every sentence could alter military behavior, domestic politics, alliance cohesion, and personal credibility. A phrase that was reassuring in one room could be humiliating in another. A written commitment could become politically impossible precisely because it was written. An oral assurance could be useful precisely because both men understood the danger of pretending it was something more formal.

The Cuban Missile Crisis is often remembered as a story about nuclear weapons, reconnaissance, resolve, and presidential judgment. It is also a story about communications technology.

Messages between Washington and Moscow were too slow for the pace of the crisis. The two governments could not always tell which letter represented the latest Soviet position. Diplomatic traffic, public statements, radio broadcasts, and private approaches overlapped. The following year, the United States and Soviet Union established a direct communications link between their capitals. The so-called hot line was not initially a red telephone sitting on a president's desk. It was a secure textual connection designed to move written messages faster and reduce the risk that ordinary diplomatic communications would fall behind events.

The logic was simple enough to become permanent.

When the consequences of misunderstanding are extreme, communication itself becomes part of deterrence.

Sixty years later, governments confront the opposite technical problem.

Messages are no longer scarce.

A foreign ministry can receive satellite imagery, commercial data, financial flows, social-media posts, intercepted communications, news reports, machine translations, diplomatic cables, economic models, and military assessments in the same hour. Artificial intelligence can summarize thousands of pages, compare statements across languages, generate possible negotiating positions, search archives, classify images, identify anomalies, draft talking points, simulate an adversary's argument, and produce a plausible answer before the human user has finished describing the question.

The analyst shortage does not disappear, because verification and judgment remain expensive. But the production of analysis becomes cheaper.

So does the production of deception.

A government can now face a recording that appears to show a president speaking words the president never spoke. It can face ten thousand accounts repeating a claim whose original source cannot be identified. It can receive machine-generated assessments written in the measured tone once associated with institutional expertise. It can be offered a model-generated negotiating option that is coherent, polished, and wrong in a way no one notices until another government reacts to it.

Even authentic information arrives inside an environment polluted by the possibility of fabrication.

This changes diplomacy in ways that are easy to underestimate.

The first temptation is to imagine that diplomacy becomes a contest over who has the better models. There is some truth in that. A foreign ministry with superior language systems, open-source analysis, economic modeling, cyber defenses, and decision support will often work faster and see more. An alliance whose members can safely share data and machine-readable assessments will have advantages over one whose systems cannot speak to one another.

But diplomacy has never been reducible to the speed of analysis.

The Cuban Missile Crisis did not end because Robert Kennedy possessed a better spreadsheet than Anatoly Dobrynin.

It ended, in part, because both men could carry a message whose meaning exceeded its literal words.

That is the problem this book is about.

Artificial intelligence makes many of the supporting functions of statecraft abundant. It can help governments see, sort, translate, compare, draft, predict, and rehearse. It can lower the cost of maintaining expertise on countries and issues that once suffered from bureaucratic neglect. It can give small diplomatic services capabilities that previously required large staffs. It can help negotiators search records in real time and identify where positions have moved.

Those are genuine gains.

They create a second set of pressures.

If every side can generate more options, leaders may receive more choices than they can responsibly evaluate. If every side can create persuasive synthetic media, authentic messages need stronger channels of verification. If military systems use AI to shorten targeting and warning cycles, diplomats have less time to prevent one side's precaution from being read as the other's preparation for attack. If public diplomacy becomes automated, governments can flood the information space while making it harder for any single statement to carry weight.

The scarcity moves.

In an earlier era, the scarce thing was information.

In the next one, it may be confidence about what information means, where it came from, who stands behind it, and whether the person on the other side can be trusted to carry an unwritten implication without distorting it.

This is why the phrase human channel matters.

It does not mean that diplomacy should reject machines. That would be sentimental and strategically foolish. Diplomats adopted the telegraph, telephone, radio, aviation, encrypted networks, satellite communications, databases, and video conferencing because each changed what could be known and how quickly governments could act. Artificial intelligence belongs in that lineage, though its reach is wider because it acts on language and analysis themselves.

Nor does the human channel mean that personal relationships can rescue governments from incompatible interests. John F. Kennedy and Nikita Khrushchev did not become friends. Robert Kennedy and Anatoly Dobrynin did not dissolve the Cold War. Trust in diplomacy is usually narrower than affection. It can mean believing that a counterpart has accurately described the limits of a position. It can mean believing that a message is authorized. It can mean knowing which threat is a bargaining move and which warning reflects a real loss of control.

That limited trust can be enough to matter.

The modern state spends enormous effort building systems that move information quickly.

The next task is building systems that preserve judgment when information moves too quickly.

That requires technology. It also requires protocol, institutional memory, alliance habits, trained skepticism, authentication, and something less programmable: people who know enough about one another to understand what cannot safely be left to the machine.

On October 27, 1962, there was no shortage of official channels between the United States and Soviet Union.

The channel that mattered most that night was two men in a room.
