# The New Diplomat

When routine cognitive work becomes cheap, the diplomatic profession must say which skills remain scarce enough to deserve deep training.

Judgment under uncertainty, language depth beyond tourist fluency, relationship building, technical literacy about tools without captivity to them, verification habits, negotiation closure, institutional memory, and writing that preserves doubt—these remain expensive.

Some traditional skills genuinely lose relative value. Memorizing almanac facts that machines retrieve instantly is not a career. Nostalgia is not a curriculum.

The new diplomat is not a prompt technician with a flag pin. Technical literacy matters because otherwise officers cannot challenge fluent nonsense. Literacy is a means to judgment, not a replacement for it.

Writing under uncertainty becomes more important as machines produce confident prose. Humans must be trained to leave uncertainty visible where it is real.

Language depth still matters because political resonance lives in register, history, and silence. Machine translation expands access; it does not abolish the need for people who feel when a word is wrong.

Relationship building cannot be automated without ceasing to be relationship. Calendars of cultivation—who is visited when no favor is needed—remain craft.

Verification skill spans media forensics awareness, source criticism, and the social tact to ask a counterpart whether a message is authorized without insult.

Institutional memory is under assault from staff churn and tool churn alike. Mentorship and disciplined archives are competitive advantages.

What would falsify this skills list? Labor market and promotion evidence that different skills predict diplomatic effectiveness in the AI era. Curricula should update against evidence, not against fashion.
