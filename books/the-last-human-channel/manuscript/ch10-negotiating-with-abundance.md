# Negotiating With Abundance

Negotiation used to be constrained by how many drafts a small team could prepare and compare before talks began.

Artificial intelligence lowers that constraint. Teams can generate alternative clauses, compare treaty language across precedents, translate working texts, and simulate counterpart objections at a volume that would once have required a large secretariat.

The tempting conclusion is that bargaining space expands automatically when proposal generation becomes cheap.

Sometimes it does. A mediator who can show parties overlooked package deals may unlock movement that pride and fatigue had blocked. A sanctions negotiator who can map many exemption designs may find a politically saleable middle path.

Often the bottleneck was never search. It was willingness to accept distributional pain, to trust verification, to manage spoilers, and to survive domestic audiences who punish compromise.

Abundance of proposals can then become a new obstacle: option overload that lets parties avoid closure by requesting one more variant.

CSIS and other research on AI in mediation emphasizes information management help for small teams while stressing that trust, confidentiality, spoilers, and verification remain political constraints. That distinction should guide procurement and practice.

Preparation is where AI gains are most reliable. Searching prior commitments, aligning translations of key terms, tracking where each side's public red lines have shifted, and stress-testing one's own proposal for unintended legal effects are assistive tasks with clear value.

Live bargaining is different. The room still responds to timing, face, fear, and the identity of the person making a concession. A machine that suggests the optimal trade cannot absorb the humiliation cost on behalf of a leader.

Cease-fire talks illustrate the gap. Drafters can produce elegant sequencing for withdrawals and monitoring. Spoilers with guns can ignore elegance. Private assurances and enforcement capacity still decide whether text becomes quiet.

Treaty drafting tools can highlight inconsistencies and missing definitions. They can also smooth away constructive ambiguity that negotiators left in place so that multiple audiences could accept the same sentence. Smoothness is not always fidelity to the bargain.

Sanctions negotiations often fail as much on coalition maintenance as on target design. Generating clever designations does not keep partners aligned when their firms lobby for relief. Human coalition management remains central.

The counterclaim deserves weight. Some bargains are missed because humans cannot search the option space effectively. Structured generation and comparison may find Pareto-improving packages that bilateral muddling would never surface. Experiments in complex multi-issue negotiation support that possibility in controlled settings.

Controlled settings are not Gaza, Donbas, or the South China Sea. External validity is the open question. Until field evidence is stronger, treat generative bargaining support as a preparation amplifier and a closure risk if mismanaged.

Process rules help. Cap the number of machine-generated variants that enter a session. Require a human owner for each proposal on the table. Separate brainstorming mode from commitment mode. Keep a parking lot for clever ideas that are not yet politically live.

Authentication matters at the table too. Parties must know which text is the working text, which is a staff doodle, and which is authorized. Version control becomes diplomatic infrastructure.

Mediators using AI should disclose enough about their tools to parties that confidentiality expectations remain realistic. Feeding sensitive offers into inappropriate external systems can destroy a process faster than a hard line.

What would falsify skepticism? Repeated cases where AI-supported mediation produced durable agreements that comparable human-only processes missed, with transparent methods. Until then, humility about abundance is strategic, not Luddite.
