# Negotiating With Abundance

Negotiation used to be constrained by how many drafts a small team could prepare and compare before talks began.

Artificial intelligence lowers that constraint. Teams can generate alternative clauses, compare treaty language across precedents, translate working texts, and simulate counterpart objections at a volume that would once have required a large secretariat.

The tempting conclusion is that bargaining space expands automatically when proposal generation becomes cheap.

Sometimes it does. A mediator who can show parties overlooked package deals may unlock movement that pride and fatigue had blocked. A sanctions negotiator who can map many exemption designs may find a politically saleable middle path.

Often the bottleneck was never search. It was willingness to accept distributional pain, to trust verification, to manage spoilers, and to survive domestic audiences who punish compromise.

Abundance of proposals can then become a new obstacle: option overload that lets parties avoid closure by requesting one more variant.

What mediation research actually says.

In April 2026, a Center for Strategic and International Studies Futures Lab report with the Doha Forum — *AI and the Future of Mediation*, by Yasir Atalan, Benjamin Jensen, and Ian Reynolds — drew on a workshop with more than forty-five mediators and technology experts. The practical finding was not that algorithms invent peace. It was that mediation teams are small, time-poor, and drowning in fragmented information, while their core tasks remain political: building trust, tracking shifting negotiation space, designing confidence-building measures, anticipating spoilers, and inventing verification that parties will accept.

The workshop ranked pressure from complex actor environments and fragmented information highest. Trust, confidentiality, and misrepresentation ranked as the central adoption risks. The report's operational recommendation matches the argument of this chapter: use AI to augment mediator-controlled workflows in pre-mediation preparation, during-session information management, and post-agreement monitoring — not to replace judgment or process ownership.

That distinction should guide procurement. A ministry that buys a "negotiation AI" as if it were a deal-closing appliance will fund the wrong thing. A ministry that buys secure document synthesis, translation with human post-edit pathways, stakeholder mapping, and draft-diff tools for named officers is buying preparation capacity.

CSIS further recommended mediation-specific evaluations, training and operational rules, partnerships among NGOs, international organizations, and foreign ministries for secure adoption, and a narrow pilot scenario-simulation tool before broader deployment. Those are institutional recommendations, not product slogans. They treat evaluation as part of the craft rather than a post-purchase afterthought.

Preparation is where the gains are reliable.

Preparation is where AI gains are most reliable. Searching prior commitments, aligning translations of key terms, tracking where each side's public red lines have shifted, and stress-testing one's own proposal for unintended legal effects are assistive tasks with clear value.

Consider a sanctions negotiation. The political fight is often less about whether a target person or entity "deserves" designation than about how partners' firms, banks, and courts will implement exemptions, licenses, and wind-downs. Mapping those designs across jurisdictions once required a small army of lawyers and economic officers. Models can now draft comparative matrices quickly: which partner already uses a similar licensing path, which court doctrine will resist a novel theory of control, which humanitarian carve-out language has survived prior litigation. A human still has to judge which matrix row will hold when a finance ministry faces domestic lobbying. The matrix, used honestly, shortens the time to that judgment.

Treaty and cease-fire drafting show the same pattern. Comparing how earlier agreements defined "withdrawal," "heavy weapons," "observers," or "good offices" is documentary labor. Machines accelerate the labor. They do not decide whether a party that broke the last definition will accept a tighter one, or whether constructive ambiguity is being requested because politics cannot yet bear precision.

U.S. Foreign Service practice already distinguishes machine translation from finished diplomatic product. The Foreign Affairs Manual's language-services rules require human post-editing of machine translation and human verification of AI interpreting for official use. Negotiation teams that ignore analogous discipline for generative drafting — treating a model's clause as ready for the table — import a translation error into a political instrument. The FAM rule is a useful analogy even where it does not formally bind a mediation cell: fluency is not clearance.

Live bargaining is different. The room still responds to timing, face, fear, and the identity of the person making a concession. A machine that suggests the optimal trade cannot absorb the humiliation cost on behalf of a leader.

Cease-fires, spoilers, and the elegance trap.

Cease-fire talks illustrate the gap between textual elegance and political quiet. Drafters can produce sequencing for withdrawals, monitoring corridors, and staged releases of detainees that looks internally consistent on a whiteboard. Spoilers with guns, or factions that gain from continued fighting, can ignore elegance. Private assurances and enforcement capacity still decide whether text becomes quiet.

The temptation under abundance is to answer spoiler risk with more annexes. More annexes can help when they specify who verifies what, with what access, on what timetable. They can also become a form of avoidance: parties polish monitoring language while refusing to name the local commander who will actually order a halt. Models are especially good at polishing. They are indifferent to whether a named person exists who can execute the sentence.

Audience costs in digital conflict environments make this worse. A leaked draft, a synthetic clip of a mediator "favoring" one side, or a fabricated "secret annex" can collapse a process that was barely holding. The CSIS mediation report flags disinformation and amplified audience costs as features of the contemporary environment, not edge cases. Mediators using AI should therefore treat confidentiality architecture as first-order design: which systems may ingest offers, which clouds are forbidden, who logs access, and how parties are told that a tool is in use.

Disclosure need not reveal model weights. It does need to keep confidentiality expectations realistic. Feeding sensitive offers into inappropriate external systems can destroy a process faster than a hard line spoken in the room.

Constructive ambiguity is sometimes the bargain.

Treaty drafting tools can highlight inconsistencies and missing definitions. They can also smooth away constructive ambiguity that negotiators left in place so that multiple audiences could accept the same sentence. Smoothness is not always fidelity to the bargain.

Ambiguous verbs — "appropriate measures," "due regard," "in a manner consistent with" — are sometimes load-bearing political technology. They let two capitals tell different domestic stories about the same instrument while still creating a shared floor for behavior. Optimizing them into crisp obligations can improve enforceability on paper and kill ratification in practice. A responsible drafting assistant should flag ambiguity and ask whether it is accidental or intentional, not silently "fix" it.

Historical negotiators learned this the hard way without silicon. Lexical fights in the documentary record — including Cold War arguments over seemingly ordinary adjectives in joint statements — show that political resonance can live inside words that look interchangeable in a bilingual glossary. Machine translation and clause generation make those fights easier to overlook because the surface text looks finished. Officers who still feel when a word is wrong remain part of the security architecture of a deal.

Sanctions coalitions and the maintenance problem.

Sanctions negotiations often fail as much on coalition maintenance as on target design. Generating clever designations does not keep partners aligned when their firms lobby for relief. Human coalition management remains central: calls to counterparts before a package drops, quiet adjustments that preserve a partner's face, timed briefings for legislators who will otherwise discover the measure from a headline.

AI can help maintain the coalition's paperwork — tracking which partner reserved on which annex, which license category is drifting, which court case could unravel a shared theory. It cannot replace the relationship labor that makes a partner willing to absorb commercial pain for another week. When both sides of a negotiation have similar commercial models, power asymmetry does not disappear; the stronger party may simply generate coercive packages and talking points faster. Speed then becomes another face of leverage, not a neutral public good.

The counterclaim: missed bargains in a large option space.

The counterclaim deserves weight. Some bargains are missed because humans cannot search the option space effectively. Structured generation and comparison may find Pareto-improving packages that bilateral muddling would never surface. Experiments in complex multi-issue negotiation support that possibility in controlled settings.

Academic testbeds for proactive mediation agents — including simulation environments that score consensus change and intervention timing — report measurable gains for socially adapted mediators over generic baselines in hard multi-party settings. Those results matter. They also remain laboratory results. They do not yet show that the same tools, under gunfire, leaked chats, and leaders who must deny they are talking, produce durable peace agreements that comparable human-only processes missed.

External validity is the open question. Gaza, Donbas, and the South China Sea are not conference rooms with shared utilities and agreed scoring metrics. Until field evidence is stronger, treat generative bargaining support as a preparation amplifier and a closure risk if mismanaged.

What would falsify skepticism? Repeated cases where AI-supported mediation produced durable agreements that comparable human-only processes missed, with transparent methods, disclosed tool constraints, and follow-through that survives spoilers. Workshop enthusiasm and demo days are not that evidence. Neither is a model's fluency at restating the parties' public positions as a "bridging proposal."

Process rules that keep abundance from eating the clock.

Process rules help. Cap the number of machine-generated variants that enter a session. Require a human owner for each proposal on the table. Separate brainstorming mode from commitment mode. Keep a parking lot for clever ideas that are not yet politically live.

Deadlines interact badly with generative abundance because parties can always ask for another overnight draft. Chairs should set explicit draft freezes before decision sessions. A freeze is not anti-technology. It is recognition that closure is a political act, and that optimization without a stop rule becomes a method of delay.

Version control becomes diplomatic infrastructure. Parties must know which text is the working text, which is a staff doodle, and which is authorized. Shared repositories with permissioning matter in multi-party talks as much as coffee for delegates. Authentication at the table is continuous: a fabricated "latest draft" circulating on messaging apps can move a coalition as effectively as a real one if chairs have not designated a single authoritative channel.

Constituency briefings can be assisted by models that translate technical clauses into plain language without promising what leaders have not authorized. The failure mode is familiar from public diplomacy: a staff-generated explainer that overclaims, leaks, and then forces negotiators to walk back a commitment that was never made. Human clearance of outward-facing summaries is not bureaucracy for its own sake. It is how a state keeps its voice singular.

Cultural constraints and the speakable package.

Cultural theories of face and honor still shape what packages are speakable. A trade that looks mathematically balanced can be domestically unsellable because of who appears to have blinked. Models trained on prior agreements may propose recyclable packages that ignore those constraints, especially if the training corpus over-weights Western legal instruments. Human regionalists who know which symbols cannot be traded remain necessary editors of the option set.

Younger officers who grow up generating options must still learn how to recommend a single course under uncertainty. Evaluation systems that reward volume of variants will produce staff who never close. Evaluation systems that reward a clear recommendation, with dissent logged and assumptions stated, will produce staff who can use abundance without drowning in it.

Verification annexes deserve more human time than colorful preamble language, even if models prefer polishing preambles. Preambles flatter. Verification allocates risk. In an environment where synthetic incident reports can arrive within minutes of a clash, the annex that specifies how parties authenticate an allegation may matter more than the paragraph that celebrates peace.



How earlier abundance problems foreshadow this one.

Diplomacy has met proposal abundance before, just at slower speeds. Multilateral secretariat drafts, alternate bracketed texts, and "non-papers" have long allowed parties to defer closure by multiplying paper. The difference now is cost and latency. What once required a week of lawyers can arrive before lunch. The old pathology — mistaking textual motion for political motion — therefore arrives earlier in a process and can colonize stages that used to be reserved for listening.

Arms-control history is instructive without being romantic. Verification annexes and definitional schedules often consumed more negotiation time than political headlines suggested, because definitions allocated real risk. Teams that shortchanged those schedules in favor of prettier chapeau language produced agreements that looked finished and then failed in implementation. Generative tools that default to polishing the chapeau recreate that mistake at higher throughput.

Trade negotiations show a parallel. Side letters, tariff schedules, and rules-of-origin annexes are where distributional pain lives. A model that proposes "balanced" packages by averaging prior deals may miss the firm, region, or union that makes a package unsellable in one capital. Human coalition maps — who must be bought in, who can be outvoted, who can spoil ratification — remain the binding constraint.

Track II, confidentiality, and the false precision of unofficial notes.

Track II workshops and academic facilitators increasingly arrive with AI briefing books. Used carefully, those books help small teams master complex files. Used carelessly, they create false precision: a neatly scored "zone of possible agreement" that neither principal has authorized. Precision without authority is a hazard already familiar from unofficial intermediaries carrying over-specified private notes. Models make that hazard cheaper to produce.

Confidentiality norms exist because spoilers use publicity as a weapon. Synthetic publics make spoilers stronger. Quiet trial balloons therefore become more necessary, not less, as stages for testing what publics might later accept. AI does not remove the need for private paths between mediator and parties; it raises the cost of sloppy information handling around those paths.

A practical rule used by careful mediation cells is phase separation. In pre-mediation, broader tool use for open-source mapping may be acceptable. Once confidential offers exist, tool use shrinks to systems under mediator control, with logging, and with party consent where appropriate. Crossing that boundary because a commercial chatbot is convenient is how processes die.

Domestic audiences, legislators, and the overnight explainer.

Even when negotiators in the room could accept a package, domestic audiences may not. Legislatures, party factions, diaspora lobbies, and courts can unwind a bargain. Models can draft overnight explainers for those audiences. The risk is that staff publish or leak an explainer that commits the executive beyond the text, or that frames a concession as victory in a way the other side's public cannot bear if mirrored.

Chairs and principals should treat outward-facing summaries as negotiated products, not as free derivatives of the working text. Clearance chains that already exist for talking points should cover model-assisted explainers. Where partners will see each other's domestic messaging, coordination among coalition members belongs in the process design, not as an afterthought when a tweet lands.

What field evidence would have to show.

Skepticism here is provisional. Field evidence could change it. The bar should be stated plainly so that enthusiasm does not move the goalposts.

Useful evidence would include: mediation or negotiation processes where AI tools were documented; comparable cases without those tools or with different tool regimes; outcomes measured not only by signature but by durability against spoilers; and transparent accounts of confidentiality incidents, version conflicts, and near-misses. Practitioner memoirs and institutional after-action reviews will matter more than vendor case studies.

Partial positive evidence already exists in adjacent domains — document review, translation throughput, scheduling, and open-source monitoring — where foreign ministries report time savings. Those gains support the preparation thesis. They do not yet demonstrate that generative bargaining changes the underlying distributional conflict.

Until that harder evidence arrives, humility about abundance is strategic. Use the tools to search. Keep humans to choose. Freeze drafts when it is time to decide. Fund verification language as if the war could restart tomorrow, because sometimes it can.

Multi-party geometry and the repository problem.

Bilateral talks already strain version discipline. Multi-party talks multiply it. Each capital generates variants overnight; each coalition partner reserves on a different clause; each mediator produces a "compromise bridge" that none of the principals yet owns. Shared repositories with clear permissioning, named editors, and freeze points are not IT niceties. They are how a process prevents accidental negotiation with a ghost draft.

Chairs should designate, in writing, the single authoritative working text and the channel through which amendments enter. Everything else is non-paper or parking-lot material. Models that auto-merge conflicting edits without human review recreate the worst habits of collaborative software inside a political act. Merge conflicts in code are inconvenient. Merge conflicts in a cease-fire text can restart shooting if a party believes it accepted language that another party never saw.

Power asymmetry shows up here too. A well-resourced delegation can flood a repository with variants that exhaust a smaller team's review capacity. Process rules that cap machine-generated submissions per session are fairness mechanisms as well as anti-overload devices. Smaller mediation teams — precisely the teams CSIS identified as most in need of analytical help — are also the most vulnerable to being buried by abundance from better-funded parties.

Training the next cohort against the wrong metric.

If academies and ministries grade officers on how many options they can generate, they will get fluent option generators. If they grade on the ability to recommend one course, state what would falsify it, and own the recommendation in front of a skeptical principal, they will get negotiators. The tools make the first skill cheap. Institutions must keep the second skill expensive in the right way: expensive in judgment, not in clerical labor.

A useful training exercise is to give students a machine-generated package that looks balanced, then require them to kill it for a politically real reason — a domestic veto player, a spoiler with guns, a verification hole — and to write the one-page recommendation that remains. Another exercise is to take a constructively ambiguous clause and decide, under time pressure, whether to clarify it or protect it. Those drills teach the difference between textual optimization and political closure.

Humanitarian corridors and the monitoring illusion.

Humanitarian access negotiations are a specialized case of the elegance trap. Parties can agree on corridor maps, notification windows, and inspection rights that look complete in a document viewer. Implementation fails when the local unit that controls a checkpoint is not bound by the capital's signature, or when "inspection" means a staged visit that models of compliance will cheerfully score as success if fed the wrong inputs.

Remote sensing and commercial imagery can support monitoring after an agreement. They can also create an illusion that verification is automatic. Imagery shows trucks and roofs. It does not show whether aid was diverted, whether a tax was levied at a checkpoint, or whether a quiet threat emptied a neighborhood the night before. Human monitors, local sources, and authenticated reporting paths remain part of any serious verification design — which is why verification annexes deserve more human time than preamble adjectives.

Post-agreement monitoring is one of the use cases the CSIS mediation work flags as promising for AI assistance: organizing incident reports, flagging inconsistencies across sources, and helping small teams track commitments over months. That promise holds only if someone remains responsible for deciding which inconsistency is noise and which is a breach. Automation that "scores compliance" without a named owner recreates the authorlessness problem from senior decision support in a different costume.

Closing without optimizing forever.

Mediators and lead negotiators should leave a session with fewer live texts than they entered, not more. Abundance is a resource for the preparation room. The decision room needs scarcity: a small set of owned proposals, a known working draft, and a human willing to recommend a stop.

That allocation of attention is the practical meaning of negotiating with abundance. Artificial intelligence expands what a small team can prepare. It does not expand, by itself, what a polity can accept. The political work — accepting distributional pain, trusting verification, managing spoilers, surviving domestic audiences — remains the hard part. Tools should serve that work. They should not postpone it indefinitely under the banner of optimization.
