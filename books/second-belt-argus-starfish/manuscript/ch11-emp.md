# EMP Before EMP Became a Word

Starfish Prime is now inseparable from electromagnetic pulse, but later terminology makes the history look tidier than it was.

A high-altitude nuclear burst produces prompt gamma rays. Interactions in the upper atmosphere generate energetic electrons, and their motion in Earth’s magnetic field creates a fast electromagnetic disturbance. Other, slower components arise through different processes. Modern HEMP literature divides these effects into regimes because equipment does not respond to all of them the same way.

In 1962 the practical lesson arrived before the mature vocabulary. Electrical systems in Hawaii, far from the detonation, experienced disruptions. The effects were not civilization-ending. They were evidence that altitude radically changes the footprint of a nuclear effect.

The history is often abused in two directions. One camp treats Starfish as proof that any modern high-altitude detonation would instantly erase an entire continental grid. Another points to the limited Hawaiian damage and concludes that EMP concern is theatrical. Both arguments smuggle in assumptions about infrastructure.

Hawaii in 1962 was not the contemporary continental United States. Line lengths, protection equipment, solid-state electronics, grid topology, satellite dependence and communications architecture have all changed. At the same time, modern systems include surge protection, hardened military equipment and engineering knowledge that did not exist then.

Starfish is therefore a calibration point, not a drop-in forecast.

It also helps separate EMP from the artificial belt. The prompt electromagnetic disturbance happened quickly. The trapped-electron environment persisted. A satellite can survive the first and accumulate damage from the second. A ground system may care enormously about the first and almost not at all about the second.

Conflating them creates a monster effect more dramatic than any single mechanism. The real story is more technically interesting: one burst coupled into multiple physical systems on different timescales, each demanding a different model of vulnerability.
