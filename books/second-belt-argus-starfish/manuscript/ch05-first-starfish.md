# Starfish Fails Once

Starfish Prime has the retrospective smoothness of a famous event: a name, a date, a yield, an altitude, a photograph. The program itself was not smooth.

The first Starfish launch on June 20, 1962 failed. The Thor missile was destroyed after launch. Debris fell around Johnston Island. The successful shot on July 9 is called Starfish Prime because there had been a Starfish that never reached its intended detonation point.

That failed launch deserves more than trivia status. It is a reminder that the apparatus behind planetary-scale effects was still an unreliable stack of rockets, warheads, telemetry, range safety and hurried Cold War logistics. The same program capable of changing the radiation environment around Earth could also lose a vehicle in the first minutes of flight.

This is the technological texture that disappears when high-altitude tests are described only through their spectacular consequences. There was no clean separation between advanced physics and ordinary engineering failure. A calculation could be right and a booster wrong.

After the June failure, the second attempt proceeded. The device that detonated on July 9 had a yield of roughly 1.4 megatons and burst at about 400 kilometers altitude above the Pacific near Johnston Island. That was dramatically larger than the low-yield ARGUS shots.

Scale changed the character of the evidence. Starfish Prime produced immediate electromagnetic and optical effects, then a longer-lived population of trapped energetic electrons. The prompt event and the persistent environment are often blended in popular retellings. They should not be. Different mechanisms operated on different clocks.

A flash can be over in moments. A radiation belt can remain after the people who watched the flash have gone home.
