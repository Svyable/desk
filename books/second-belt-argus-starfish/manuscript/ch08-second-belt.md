# The Second Belt

Within hours of Starfish Prime, spacecraft measurements showed that the near-Earth radiation environment had changed.

The phrase “artificial radiation belt” is literal. Energetic electrons from fission-product beta decay became trapped in geomagnetic field lines and formed a population superimposed on the natural belts. W. N. Hess’s 1963 review combined measurements from Injun, Telstar, TRAAC and Ariel to construct a picture of that new population. The agreement among instruments was not exquisite. Hess described consistency to within about a factor of three. For a planetary experiment assembled from different satellites, that was enough to establish the main fact.

The belt did not behave like smoke spreading evenly from an explosion. Particle motion followed magnetic coordinates. Flux depended on energy and L-shell. Different spacecraft sampled different parts of the structure. A satellite could therefore be hit hard while another at a different orbit experienced less severe exposure.

This is why “Starfish radiation” should not be treated as a single number. Dose depended on orbit, shielding, particle energy, duration and device sensitivity. The artificial environment evolved over time as particles were lost, scattered, transported and replenished across regions of phase space.

The persistence was perhaps the most consequential surprise. Portions of the artificial population remained measurable for years. NASA technical work traced the artificial electron belt from 1963 into 1966. Decay was not a simple matter of particles falling out on one obvious clock.

The second belt also turned satellites into involuntary experiments. Solar arrays degraded. Detectors saturated or recorded enhanced fluxes. Electronics accumulated dose. Machines built to exploit the new space age were suddenly flying through an environment altered by a nuclear test from the old one.

That collision between eras is the book’s center of gravity. Starfish Prime did not merely demonstrate a military effect in space. It imposed a military-created environment on civilian and scientific spacecraft. From that moment onward, high-altitude nuclear effects and space-system survivability were no longer separable questions.
