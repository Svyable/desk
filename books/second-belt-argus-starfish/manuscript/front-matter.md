# The Second Belt

*ARGUS, Starfish Prime, and the Day We Learned to Rewrite Near-Earth Space*

Sven Hardy Benson

There is a category of experiment that looks different after it works.

Beforeward it is a proposal, a calculation, a military requirement, a diagram on a chalkboard. Afterward there is a thing in the world that did not used to be there.

In 1958 the United States detonated three small nuclear devices hundreds of kilometers above the South Atlantic under Project ARGUS. The point was not to destroy a city or sink a fleet. One objective was stranger: test whether charged particles from nuclear explosions could be trapped by Earth’s magnetic field and create an artificial radiation belt. Explorer IV and other instruments watched. The belt appeared.

Four years later Starfish Prime, far larger, did it again with consequences nobody could file neatly under one heading. Streetlights failed in Hawaii. Radio systems behaved oddly. Auroras appeared where auroras did not belong. Satellites crossed an energetic-electron environment that had been abruptly changed by a bomb. Some suffered accelerated solar-cell and electronic degradation. Telstar, launched the next day, became both a communications triumph and an unwilling dosimeter.

The easy version of this story is that the military discovered how to weaponize the magnetosphere. That is too clean. The opposite easy version is that these were crude Cold War stunts with little scientific meaning. That is also wrong.

What actually happened is harder to classify. Nuclear weapons laboratories, military planners, satellite engineers, and geophysicists briefly shared the same sky. A weapons-effects program produced planetary-scale plasma experiments. Those experiments created hazards for the very space infrastructure then beginning to emerge. The resulting measurements fed back into magnetospheric physics, radiation-belt models, satellite hardening, and later thinking about high-altitude nuclear effects.

This book stays inside the public record. It uses official histories, contemporaneous papers, NASA technical records, later peer-reviewed reconstructions, and declassified material where available. It does not claim access to classified survivability data. It does not turn patents into proof or program names into capability. Where later authors disagree about which satellite failed for which reason, the disagreement stays visible.

The central fact is already strange enough: for a brief period, human beings deliberately manufactured radiation belts around Earth.
