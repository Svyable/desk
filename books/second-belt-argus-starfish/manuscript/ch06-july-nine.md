# July 9, 1962

At 09:00 UTC on July 9, 1962, Starfish Prime detonated high above the Pacific. The altitude was roughly the orbital height of the International Space Station today, though there was no ISS and barely an orbital civilization to inconvenience.

The burst was visible across a huge region. Its charged particles and electromagnetic disturbance did not respect the geometric intuition of a bomb hanging over one point on a map. Magnetic field lines connected distant regions. Ionization changed radio conditions. Electromagnetic fields coupled into long conductors and electrical systems.

In Hawaii, about 1,400 kilometers from the burst, some streetlights failed. Communications systems experienced disturbances. The details matter because the phrase “Starfish knocked out Hawaii” is false. The island chain did not go dark. The documented effects were real but limited, and they occurred in an electrical infrastructure much smaller and differently wired than modern grids.

That boundedness is scientifically useful. A phenomenon can be consequential without being apocalyptic. The high-altitude electromagnetic pulse associated with Starfish Prime became a foundational historical case because it showed that a nuclear detonation far away could induce damaging voltages in unshielded systems over a broad area.

At the same time, optical phenomena appeared in the sky. Artificial auroral displays were observed far from the burst region as energetic particles followed geomagnetic field lines. Again the language can run away from the mechanism. Nothing mystical had happened. A nuclear explosion had injected charged particles into a planetary magnetic structure whose geometry carried their effects elsewhere.

The lasting consequence arrived more quietly. Fission products beta-decayed, producing energetic electrons. A fraction became trapped. Satellites began to encounter radiation levels well above the previous background in parts of their orbits.

A single event had created three histories at once: the history of EMP, the history of artificial aurora and ionospheric disturbance, and the history of an artificial radiation belt. Popular accounts tend to pick whichever history best suits the mood. The actual experiment was inconveniently plural.
