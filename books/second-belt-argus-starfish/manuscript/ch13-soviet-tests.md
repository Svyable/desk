# Soviet Bombs in the Same Sky

The United States was not alone in detonating nuclear devices at high altitude. The Soviet Union conducted its own high-altitude tests in 1961 and 1962, including powerful shots over Kazakhstan whose electromagnetic effects reached ground infrastructure.

That parallel history matters for two reasons. First, it prevents Starfish from becoming a uniquely American myth. High-altitude nuclear effects were part of a bilateral strategic problem. Second, Soviet tests created a second empirical record showing that coupling into electrical and communications systems was not peculiar to Hawaiian infrastructure.

Public descriptions of the Soviet K-series tests include damage to power and telephone systems at substantial distances from the bursts. Precise technical reconstruction is harder because the open documentary record is uneven and later English-language summaries often recycle one another. This chapter therefore keeps the evidentiary bar high: a reported failure is not promoted into a quantified EMP effect unless the underlying record can be traced.

The broader convergence is secure. Both nuclear powers were interested in how detonations behaved outside the ordinary atmosphere. Both discovered that the relevant target was not only an object. It was an environment—ionosphere, magnetic field, radio paths, electrical networks and orbital hardware.

The existence of parallel programs also complicates secrecy. Classification can conceal details, but strategic competition itself creates clues. If each side worries that the other may exploit a new effect, exploratory work becomes rational before anyone has demonstrated an operational system. Programs can therefore be evidence of concern without being evidence of success.

That rule will matter whenever this book approaches claims about radiation-belt weapons, anti-satellite effects or electromagnetic attack. Funding is not proof. Testing is not deployment. A real physical effect is not automatically a mature weapon.

The Soviets and Americans did, however, agree on one thing through behavior: near-Earth space had become part of the nuclear battlespace before most civilians had any reason to think of it as infrastructure at all.
