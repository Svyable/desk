## Reader promise

This book follows a narrow and strange historical claim: in the first years of the space age, nuclear-weapons laboratories did not merely test devices in the atmosphere. They deliberately altered the magnetosphere, created artificial radiation belts, damaged satellites, and generated data that became part of space physics. The story is not that governments possessed secret control of the sky. It is that they briefly conducted experiments on the sky itself, and the consequences were measurable enough that the distinction between weapons effect, scientific instrument, and planetary intervention became hard to maintain.

The strongest competing explanation is less dramatic and in some ways more interesting: these tests were improvised weapons-effects programs whose scientific afterlife has been inflated by hindsight. The manuscript treats that explanation seriously. It does not infer capability from ambition, and it does not treat every later satellite failure as Starfish damage.

## Evidence posture

1. **Verified/declassified/documented:** ARGUS detonations and instrumentation; Starfish Prime yield, altitude and date; artificial trapped-electron populations; published satellite observations; documented electrical effects in Hawaii; Partial Test Ban Treaty restrictions.
2. **Credible but incomplete:** attribution of individual satellite failures to Starfish dose; exact partition among prompt radiation, artificial-belt dose and pre-existing faults; some operational motives and classified survivability conclusions.
3. **Disputed or unestablished:** claims that the tests demonstrated practical long-duration control of the magnetosphere, that every 1962 satellite anomaly was nuclear-test damage, or that later exotic aerospace phenomena derive from these programs.
4. **Speculative synthesis:** high-altitude nuclear testing as the first deliberate human perturbation of a planetary-scale plasma environment, and the possibility that weapons-effects research unintentionally supplied some of the space-weather science needed to understand the damage it created.
