# The Beam

The first useful fact is also the one most likely to be ruined by enthusiasm: **there really was a signal.**

Not a rumor about a signal. Not a retired officer remembering that somebody had once mentioned a signal. Not a patent for a hypothetical emitter, or a congressional witness saying he had been told that one existed somewhere behind a classification wall. American officials measured electromagnetic energy directed at the U.S. Embassy chancery in Moscow. They complained about it to Soviet officials. They studied it. They worried about it. They tried to understand what it was doing.

Then they made the problem much harder by keeping much of that worry secret.

That is why the Moscow Signal matters now. It gives us something anomalous history rarely gives us: a case in which the extraordinary-sounding first premise is not the weak part. A foreign power really was illuminating an American diplomatic building with microwave energy. The epistemic trouble begins one step later, when the question changes from *was there a beam?* to *what was the beam for?*

Those are different questions. History has spent half a century trying to glue them back together.

In June 1967, during the Glassboro summit, Secretary of State Dean Rusk raised what a State Department memorandum called an “electro-magnetic signal” directed at the Moscow chancery with Soviet Foreign Minister Andrei Gromyko. Rusk said the United States did not know the purpose and wanted the activity investigated and stopped. Gromyko was skeptical. Soviet ambassador Anatoly Dobrynin suggested that the United States did similar things to Soviet facilities.

There is a small discipline hiding in that exchange. The American government was confident enough about the **observation** to make a diplomatic protest and uncertain enough about the **explanation** to say it did not know the purpose.

Keep those two states in your head at the same time. They are going to try to escape.

The signal had already generated a research program. Project PANDORA, and work later associated with BIZARRE, asked whether low-level microwave exposure could produce biological or behavioral effects. The question was not idiotic. A state does not need to believe a weapon exists before asking whether a foreign exposure might be harmful. Intelligence services are paid, among other things, to investigate low-probability explanations before the evidence is polite enough for a textbook.

But an investigation has a peculiar afterlife. Decades later, the verb *studied* begins to sound like *found*. The existence of a classified program starts functioning as evidence for the hypothesis that caused the program to be created. A question becomes a finding by archival osmosis.

PANDORA is especially dangerous in this respect because the underlying event was real. There was no need to invent a Soviet beam. The beam was available to carry whatever explanation later narrators placed on it.

The surviving record is less obliging.

A declassified ARPA update summarized by the National Security Archive describes an early primate experiment conducted using exposure assumptions that turned out to be too high. The CIA’s earlier estimate had put the Moscow power density around 0.5 to 1 milliwatt. By 1967, the corrected figure was reported as no greater than about 0.05 milliwatt per square centimeter. At the corrected level, the ARPA account said the modulated microwave exposure did not cause the primate to degrade in its work tasks.

That correction is more interesting than a clean positive or negative result. It shows a classified research program behaving like science under bad boundary conditions: a measurement of the real-world stimulus was wrong, an experiment was built around the wrong number, and the interpretation changed when the number changed.

The glamour disappears. Good.

This is the part of secret history that deserves more attention. Classification does not suspend metrology. A TOP SECRET number can still be the wrong number. An intelligence agency can know that a foreign state is doing something and misunderstand the magnitude, purpose, mechanism, or consequence. Secrecy may protect information from an adversary. It does not protect information from error.

There was another complication. Some embassy personnel were medically sampled without being told the real reason. Contemporary records describe a cover story around blood collection while officials investigated possible effects of the microwave exposure. That choice is understandable in the narrow bureaucratic grammar of the Cold War: protect a sensitive counterintelligence problem, avoid alarming personnel, avoid telling Moscow exactly what Washington knows, keep diplomacy from being hijacked by an unresolved technical question.

It is also almost perfectly designed to create suspicion later.

Imagine learning, years afterward, that the government knew a foreign power had been irradiating your workplace, secretly drew blood while telling you another story, funded animal experiments on possible biological effects, debated health consequences internally, and did not initially give you the complete explanation.

Now imagine being told that none of this proves the beam harmed you.

Both statements can be true.

That is the Moscow problem.

The usual argument splits into two camps because two camps are easier to sell. In one version, alarmed officials discovered a Soviet directed-energy weapon and buried the health consequences. In the other, excitable Cold Warriors chased a harmless low-level signal and later conspiracy culture inflated the episode into mind control. Each version makes the same mistake in reverse. It tries to make one answer do the work of three.

There was a **signal**. Did it cause a **biological effect**? What was the Soviet **intent**?

Call this the signal/effect/intent triangle.

The edges do not fill themselves in.

A measured signal can exist without a demonstrated adverse effect. An effect can exist without having been intended. Intent can be hostile without the hypothesized mechanism working. A device meant to activate or interrogate passive electronics could incidentally expose people. A harassment signal could be biologically trivial. A bioeffects research program could fail to demonstrate the feared effect while remaining entirely rational to have commissioned. Even a statistically unusual health outcome, if one were found, would not by itself identify the source, mechanism, or purpose.

This sounds elementary when written slowly. It becomes surprisingly difficult when secrecy is added.

Secrecy creates what I will call **causal overhang**: a verified hidden event leaves more explanatory room than the public evidence can occupy. The blank space does not remain blank. Institutions fill some of it with classified hypotheses. Journalists fill some with sources. witnesses fill some with memory. Advocates fill some with analogy. Skeptics fill some with ordinary explanations. Years later, the archive opens unevenly and everyone discovers documents that appear to resemble the story they already preferred.

The Moscow Signal is a near-perfect machine for producing causal overhang because the government’s own behavior was visibly consequential. Senior officials protested to Moscow. Researchers exposed animals. Doctors collected samples. Engineers considered shielding. Diplomats debated what employees should be told. President Gerald Ford eventually wrote to Leonid Brezhnev about the transmissions and health concerns. Henry Kissinger discussed the problem with Dobrynin. Whatever the beam was for, Washington did not treat it as imaginary.

But institutional concern is evidence of concern.

That sentence is almost insultingly obvious, which is why it needs to be written down.

The same mistake appears everywhere in anomalous research. The Air Force investigated UFOs; therefore the Air Force knew UFOs were extraordinary craft. The CIA funded remote viewing; therefore remote viewing worked. Intelligence agencies collected foreign parapsychology reports; therefore the foreign program had operational capability. NASA tested a disputed propulsion claim; therefore the effect was promising. A patent was classified; therefore the device functioned. A special-access program existed; therefore every capability attributed to it existed.

No. Programs are nouns. Findings require verbs.

What was measured? Under what conditions? Against what control? With what denominator? What changed when the measurement improved? Did another group reproduce it? Did the effect survive the transition from laboratory apparatus to operational environment? Did the institution itself distinguish possibility from demonstrated capability?

PANDORA becomes more useful when we stop asking it to prove a weapon and start asking it to show us how governments behave when they cannot afford to wait for certainty.

The answer is messy. They investigate multiple hypotheses. They compartment information. They sometimes conceal the real reason for data collection. They make measurement errors. They commission animal studies. They argue about thresholds. They brief senior officials. They worry about press consequences. They compare their own standards with an adversary’s standards. They try to infer intent from a physical stimulus that does not arrive with a label attached.

And eventually somebody has to study the people.

The State Department commissioned an epidemiological investigation led by Johns Hopkins epidemiologist Abraham Lilienfeld and colleagues, comparing personnel associated with the Moscow post with employees from other Eastern European posts. The 1978 study did not report convincing evidence of a broad mortality excess attributable to the exposure and found limited evidence regarding general health status. That result has itself been contested. Later authors have criticized aspects of exposure reconstruction, follow-up, statistical power, and interpretation. Those criticisms matter. So does the original negative evidence.

The temptation is to use uncertainty as acid: pour enough of it on an inconvenient study and eventually no result remains. That is not what this book will do.

A weak epidemiological design does not prove harm. A null result does not prove perfect safety. A later critique does not retroactively manufacture an effect. The job is to reconstruct what the study could actually detect, what exposure variable it possessed, what outcomes were measured, how long subjects were followed, how comparison groups differed, and what claims the data can bear without snapping.

This is where the story stops being a Cold War curiosity and becomes a modern instrument.

In the twenty-first century, anomalous health incidents involving U.S. personnel generated renewed speculation about directed energy. Moscow was immediately available as precedent. Of course it was. Here was a documented case in which a hostile state had directed microwave energy at American diplomats while the government secretly investigated possible bioeffects.

But precedent is not identity.

The fact that a microwave signal existed in Moscow does not identify the cause of a later neurological complaint in Havana, Vienna, Washington, or anywhere else. It establishes something narrower and still important: states have used directed electromagnetic energy against diplomatic facilities for purposes that were not initially clear to the target, and U.S. agencies have considered biological effects among the hypotheses worth testing.

That changes the prior. It does not close the case.

The difference matters because historical analogies are often smuggled across exactly this gap. We begin with a true statement—*something like this has happened before*—and end with a much stronger one—*therefore this is what happened now*. The missing work is everything in between: frequency, waveform, power density, exposure geometry, duration, target coupling, biological endpoint, latency, instrumentation, symptoms, dose-response, and alternative mechanisms.

The physics is not optional because the history is dramatic.

Nor is the history optional because the physics looks weak.

That is the bargain of this book. We will not dismiss a claim because intelligence agencies once found it embarrassing, and we will not promote a claim because intelligence agencies once classified it. We will follow the beam.

Where was it measured? How strong was it? How did that estimate change? What did researchers reproduce in the laboratory? What disappeared when exposure parameters were corrected? What health data were collected, and what was concealed from the people supplying them? What did American officials actually say about Soviet intent at each point in the chronology? Which later claims are supported by the archive, which are testimony, which are allegations, and which are our own synthesis?

There is a reason to be severe about this case. It is one of the few places where the conspiracy-shaped version of history begins with a conspiracy-shaped fact.

A foreign government secretly aimed microwaves at an embassy.

The target government secretly studied what those microwaves might do to living things.

Employees were not initially told the full reason for medical sampling.

Senior officials worried about health and diplomacy.

All of that can be documented without proving a microwave mind weapon, a cancer mechanism, or the cause of any later anomalous-health event.

If we cannot keep those categories separate here, where the archive gives us unusually solid footing, we have almost no chance when the evidence is worse.

So start with the beam.

Do not ask it to carry more than it can.
