# Respiring Metal

The natural cable-bacterium story originally centered on oxygen and nitrate. The laboratory story kept widening.

Electroactive microbes can use electrodes or insoluble minerals as electron acceptors. Cable bacteria, with their extraordinary conductive anatomy, became obvious candidates for similar experiments. But obvious does not mean demonstrated.

Work leading into 2026 increasingly tested whether the filament could interface directly with artificial electrodes and solid-phase acceptors. Skeletonized conductive sheaths were even used as catalytic electrodes, blurring the boundary between living physiology and extracted biomaterial.

The biological question is sharper: can intact cable bacteria grow by sending electrons to an insoluble acceptor when oxygen is absent?

That is not merely an engineering trick. If yes, then the cable’s function is more general than bridging sulfide to oxygen. It is an extracellular electron-transfer platform capable of connecting internal metabolism to external solid redox partners.

This would place cable bacteria in conversation with Geobacter, Shewanella, and other electroactive organisms, but not collapse the categories. Their conductive materials and architectures differ. Similar ecological problems have produced different solutions.

The comparison is valuable because it shows that ‘electrical life’ is not one lineage or one molecular invention. It is a recurring evolutionary response to the inconvenience of insoluble or distant electron acceptors.

The question is no longer whether biology uses electricity. The question is how many architectures for electrical metabolism remain unrecognized.
