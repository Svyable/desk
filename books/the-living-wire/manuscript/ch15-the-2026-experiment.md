# The 2026 Experiment

The useful thing about an electrode is that it does not care about the story you wanted the organism to tell.

For years, cable bacteria had been extraordinary in a very specific way. They oxidized sulfide down in anoxic sediment, carried the electrons centimetres along a multicellular filament, and spent them near the sediment surface where oxygen was available. That architecture was already strange enough to force a revision of ordinary microbial scale. A redox reaction that most textbooks would draw across a membrane had been stretched across thousands of cells and a visible piece of geography.

But the canonical picture still had a tidy terminal end: oxygen.

In 2026 Kartik Aiyer, Leonid Digel and colleagues asked whether that terminal end was actually a requirement. They worked with the freshwater cable bacterium *Electronema aureum* GS and replaced the usual oxic destination with a controlled solid electron acceptor. The organism was kept under anoxic conditions. The acceptor was an electrode. The question was no longer whether electrons could travel inside the filament. The question was whether the bacterium could get them back out.

That distinction is easy to miss because the words **electrical conduction** and **extracellular electron transfer** sound like neighboring descriptions of the same trick. They are not.

Long-distance conduction asks whether charge can move through the conductive periplasmic fiber system from one part of a filament to another.

Extracellular electron transfer asks whether the cell can exchange those electrons across its own envelope with an insoluble object outside it, in a way that conserves enough energy to support metabolism and growth.

A copper wire can conduct beautifully and still have a terrible connector. Biology has the same interface problem.

The 2026 paper attacked that interface with three different experimental geometries rather than one spectacular current trace. Differential pulse voltammetry and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy on intact filaments were used to identify surface redox activity. Sediment-based bioelectrochemical systems tested whether polarized electrodes could function as terminal acceptors while the organism grew without oxygen. A miniaturized trench-slide system put intact filaments against an electrode under live microscopy, making the physical interface visible while the electrochemistry was manipulated.

The result was not one mechanism but at least two.

*E. aureum* GS could transfer electrons directly to an electrode. It could also transfer them through riboflavin, a soluble flavin molecule that accumulated in the bioelectrochemical systems and increased current production. The paper additionally resolved two cell-surface redox components whose behavior was metal-dependent, pH-sensitive and heat-labile, a pattern consistent with redox proteins such as outer-membrane cytochromes without, by itself, proving the complete molecular identity of the pathway.

That last distinction matters. Electrochemistry can tell you that a redox-active component behaves like a protein-bound cofactor. It does not let you skip the genetics, structure and localization required to name the machinery with confidence.

Then came the voltage experiment.

At an electrode poised at **+250 mV versus Ag/AgCl**, the bacteria did more than produce current. The authors estimated doubling times ranging from about **13.5 to 36.8 hours**, depending on how the qPCR-derived starting and ending abundances were bounded. Using mean values across replicates, the estimated doubling time was about **20 hours**. That sits in the same broad range reported for *Electrothrix* growing under oxic conditions.

This is the result behind the phrase in the paper's title: growth rates comparable to aerobic respiration.

It should not be allowed to become a slogan detached from the condition that produced it.

At **+600 mV versus Ag/AgCl**, electron transfer still occurred. The cells could remain metabolically active against an unusually oxidizing electrode potential for an electroactive bacterium. But growth was much poorer. The best-case estimated doubling time was still roughly **39 hours**. Mean-based estimates were around **115.5 hours**, longer than the entire 84-hour experimental window, and some comparisons showed little or no increase in gene-copy abundance at all.

So the important result is not that higher redox potential simply made a better respiratory outlet.

The organism had a window.

That is more biologically interesting than a heroic maximum voltage. A respiratory system is not a wire rated only by the largest potential it survives. It is a coupled energy-conservation apparatus with kinetics, transport limits, enzyme turnover, proton balance, membrane energetics and toxicity constraints. A terminal acceptor can be thermodynamically attractive and still be physiologically awkward.

The experiment therefore gives the book a better question than “can cable bacteria breathe metal?”

What external redox potentials actually maximize useful growth, and why?

An answer requires separating several layers that the electrode conveniently compresses into one number.

First is the surface transfer step: how quickly electrons cross from cellular redox components to the solid acceptor.

Second is the long-distance conductor: how much current the internal nickel-containing fibers can carry without becoming the bottleneck.

Third is the metabolic source of those electrons: sulfide oxidation or another donor must still supply reducing power.

Fourth is energy conservation. The organism must convert some part of the redox drop into a biologically spendable ion gradient or equivalent work rather than merely dumping electrons into a favorable sink.

Fifth is growth. Biomass production integrates all of those steps plus the ordinary costs of building and maintaining a multicellular filament.

Those layers can fail independently.

The paper is strongest because it did not infer growth from current alone. Current proves charge crossed an interface. It does not prove the current funded reproduction. By pairing electrochemistry with abundance estimates, the experiment moved the claim from conductive behavior toward respiratory physiology.

It also complicates the old ecological diagram of a cable bacterium stretched between sulfide and oxygen.

If a filament can use an insoluble extracellular acceptor in the absence of oxygen, then the natural environment contains more possible circuit topologies than the classic two-zone picture suggests. Iron and manganese minerals, conductive mineral surfaces, electrode-like interfaces in sediments, and redox-active neighboring organisms become candidates for interaction.

Candidates are not demonstrations.

A laboratory electrode is almost offensively cooperative. Its potential is controlled. Its surface is accessible. Its current can be recorded continuously. It does not dissolve, become passivated unpredictably, get buried under precipitates, or arrive as a heterogeneous grain whose reactive sites differ every few micrometres. A graphite or other engineered electrode at a known potential is therefore a proof of capability, not a survey of what the bacterium actually respires in a lake bed.

The riboflavin result carries the same boundary. Riboflavin accumulated and could mediate electron transfer in the experimental system. That does not establish that free riboflavin is the dominant shuttle in intact natural sediment, at environmentally relevant concentrations, against the mineral phases cable bacteria usually encounter.

The right next experiment is not another demonstration that current can be made.

It is a matrix.

Give *E. aureum* the same donor supply and the same anoxic conditions, then vary only the terminal acceptor: poised electrodes across a potential series, ferric iron minerals of known surface area and crystallinity, manganese oxides, conductive sulfides, inert particles, and sterilized natural mineral assemblages. Measure current, donor consumption, cell abundance, filament extension, ATP or another energy-state proxy, local pH, mineral transformation and any soluble mediators.

Then interrupt the suspected EET machinery.

Block or genetically perturb candidate surface cytochromes. Remove riboflavin or intercept flavin shuttling. Damage the long-distance conductive fibers without simply killing the cells. If the organism truly couples internal conduction to external respiration, the failure pattern should reveal where the circuit opens.

The most revealing experiment may be spatial rather than biochemical.

Put the electron donor and the insoluble acceptor far enough apart that no single cell can touch both. Let a cable filament bridge the distance. Measure whether growth and current collapse when the filament is severed in the middle, then recover when a conductive path is restored. That would close the loop between the phenomenon discovered in 2012—centimetre-scale internal electron transport—and the extracellular terminal behavior demonstrated in 2026.

A second experiment should ask whether neighboring microbes can enter that loop. The 2023 bacterial-flock work showed other bacteria physically associating with cable bacteria linked electrically to oxygen. Association is not yet an electron ledger. Stable-isotope labeling, electrochemical perturbation and cell-specific redox reporters could test whether a partner cell's electrons actually cross into the cable filament and emerge at a distant acceptor.

If that happens, the object becomes stranger again.

A cable bacterium would no longer be merely a multicellular organism with unusual respiration. It would be infrastructure inside a microbial community.

That claim remains open.

What the 2026 experiment establishes is narrower and already substantial. *Electronema aureum* GS can perform direct and riboflavin-mediated extracellular electron transfer to insoluble electrodes under anoxic conditions. At +250 mV versus Ag/AgCl, that process supported growth on roughly the timescale of aerobic cable-bacterium growth. At +600 mV, electron transfer persisted but growth slowed sharply.

The filament is therefore not simply a biological extension cord with oxygen soldered permanently onto one end.

It has a terminal physiology.

And once the terminal can change, the map of possible respiration changes with it.
