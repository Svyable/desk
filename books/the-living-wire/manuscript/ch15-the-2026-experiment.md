# The 2026 Experiment

In May 2026, a Nature Communications paper pushed cable bacteria into a new physiological category.

Working with the freshwater species Electronema aureum GS, the researchers showed extracellular electron transfer to insoluble acceptors under anoxic conditions. The bacterium could interact with electrodes directly and through riboflavin-mediated transfer. Growth rates were reported as comparable to aerobic respiration under the tested conditions.

The voltage range was striking. The organism transferred electrons to electrodes poised at unusually high redox potential for electroactive bacteria.

This matters because it separates two ideas that had often traveled together. The first is long-distance internal conduction along the filament. The second is extracellular electron transfer across the organism-environment interface. The 2026 work demonstrates that cable bacteria can combine them.

A conductor inside the body is one innovation. A versatile terminal that can hand electrons to external solids is another.

The paper does not prove cable bacteria naturally respire every mineral found in sediment, nor that electrode behavior maps perfectly onto environmental conditions. Electrodes are controlled experimental surfaces. Riboflavin mediation in a reactor is not automatically the dominant pathway in a lake bed.

But the experiment alters the plausible ecological space. A cable bacterium need not be imagined only as a bridge from sulfide to oxygen. It can participate in redox networks where the terminal acceptor is solid and oxygen is absent.

That opens a new way to read sediment. Mineral grains are not passive scenery. In an electrically competent microbiome, they can be nodes.

The 2026 result is valuable not because it completes the story, but because it makes the old definition too small.
