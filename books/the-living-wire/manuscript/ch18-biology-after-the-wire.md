# Biology After the Wire

The cable bacterium is easy to underestimate because its headline sounds like a metaphor: bacteria that conduct electricity.

The literal fact is more interesting.

These organisms physically separate the two halves of respiration across centimetres of sediment. They conduct electrons through specialized protein fibers. Those fibers contain unusual nickel chemistry. The current reorganizes pH, sulfide, iron, phosphorus, sulfur cycling, and sometimes methane production. Other bacteria gather around the filaments and may exploit the connection. In 2026, a freshwater species was shown to transfer electrons to insoluble acceptors under anoxic conditions at growth rates comparable to aerobic respiration in the tested system.

None of this requires a mystical biology. It requires abandoning an overly local one.

The larger consequence is methodological. Biology has spent a century getting better at resolving smaller things: molecules, organelles, genes, single cells. Cable bacteria are a reminder that some mechanisms become visible only when the field of view widens. The important variable can be continuity across distance.

That lesson travels. Neural tissue, fungal networks, plant vasculature, biofilms, microbial consortia, conductive minerals, and redox gradients all raise versions of the same problem: when does transport architecture become physiology rather than context?

Cable bacteria provide a clean case because the answer is measurable. Remove the connection and the separated half-reactions cannot remain coupled in the same way.

The wire is part of the metabolism.

The book began with mud carrying a current. It ends with a narrower claim than a revolution and a larger one than a curiosity. A living system can make geography part of respiration.

That fact was sitting in sediment long before we learned how to measure it.
