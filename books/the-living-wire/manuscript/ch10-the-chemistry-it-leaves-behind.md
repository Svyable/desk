# The Chemistry It Leaves Behind

Cable bacteria are easier to find indirectly than many charismatic organisms are to find directly. They leave gradients.

Their electrogenic metabolism produces a recognizable pH signature: acidification associated with sulfide oxidation deeper in the sediment and alkalinization near cathodic oxygen reduction. The acid can dissolve iron sulfide minerals. Mobilized iron can move and reoxidize elsewhere. Carbonate equilibria shift. Nutrients bind and release in different places.

The organism therefore writes itself into mineral chemistry.

This is one reason cable bacteria matter beyond microbial physiology. Sediment geochemistry is often modeled as a stack of local reactions. Long-distance electron transport introduces a nonlocal term. A reaction in one layer changes mineral stability somewhere else because electrons are physically exported.

The effect can survive longer than the filament. Iron oxides precipitated during one season can influence phosphorus retention later. Mineral dissolution can alter pore structure and reactive surface area. The biological current creates a geological afterimage.

That phrase is more than metaphor for astrobiology. Biosignatures are usually discussed as molecules, isotopic fractionations, textures, or atmospheric disequilibria. A metabolism that systematically reorganizes mineral redox state across space suggests another category: spatially structured geochemical disequilibrium generated by charge transport.

We do not yet have a validated remote biosignature of cable-like life. That would be speculation. But on Earth, the logic is already practical. Researchers can infer electrogenic activity from microsensor profiles and mineral patterns before every filament is individually visualized.

Life can leave a circuit diagram in mud.
