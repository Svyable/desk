# Nickel in the Dark

The next surprise was chemical.

In 2021, spectroscopy and chemical imaging implicated sulfur-ligated nickel in the conductive core of the cable-bacterium fibers. Removing or oxidizing the nickel reduced conductivity. The result suggested a form of biological long-range electron transport distinct from the better-known heme-rich strategies of extracellular electron-transfer bacteria.

Nickel is not exotic to life. It appears in important enzymes, including hydrogenases and ureases. But its role here is unusual: not simply as a catalytic center handling a local reaction, but as part of a conductive protein architecture extending across a multicellular filament.

This is one of those cases where chemistry changes the evolutionary story. If the conductor depended on a familiar cytochrome chain repeated thousands of times, one might imagine cable bacteria as a stretched version of known redox biology. A nickel-dependent conductive core suggests something less derivative.

The mechanism is not fully closed. Identifying a metal cofactor is not the same as deriving the precise electron-transfer physics through the protein. Questions remain about electronic states, hopping versus band-like descriptions, structural organization, and how physiological conditions tune conductivity.

That uncertainty is useful. It stops the book from becoming a triumphal sequence of solved mysteries. Cable bacteria are well enough understood to overturn an old assumption, but poorly enough understood to remain a real frontier.

There is also an astrobiological temptation here. Nickel-rich conductive proteins could, in principle, broaden the materials by which metabolism moves charge in environments unlike surface Earth. That belongs in the speculative class, not the established one. No cable bacterium has been found beyond Earth, and no extraterrestrial biosignature has been tied to this chemistry.

Still, the nickel matters because it expands the catalog of things biology can make into circuitry. Life did not borrow copper wire. It assembled a conductor out of proteins, sulfur coordination, and a metal already available to metabolism.
