# Electric Life Elsewhere

Astrobiology often asks what life requires. Water. Free energy. Carbon or another scaffold. Redox disequilibrium. Some means of maintaining boundaries and reproducing.

Cable bacteria add a quieter question: must the donor and acceptor occupy the same microscopic place?

The answer on Earth is no.

That matters for worlds with steep chemical gradients. Ice-ocean interfaces, hydrothermal sediments, porous rock, mineral-rich brines, and subsurface environments may contain useful redox couples separated by distances too large for ordinary cells but not necessarily too large for conductive biological structures.

This does not make cable bacteria evidence of extraterrestrial life. The distinction is crucial. They are an existence proof for a metabolic architecture, not a detection claim.

The architecture expands habitability models conceptually. A region that looks locally energy-starved may still participate in metabolism if it is electrically connected to another region.

The same principle affects biosignatures. Long-distance electron transport can create spatially separated oxidation and reduction products, unusual mineral fronts, or persistent geochemical gradients. Interpreting such patterns requires models that allow nonlocal biology.

This is speculative territory, but it is disciplined speculation because each step can be stated. We know filamentous bacteria on Earth transport electrons over centimetres. We know this transport can couple separated redox half-reactions. We know the activity changes minerals and porewater chemistry. We do not know whether analogous organisms exist elsewhere or whether their products would be detectable remotely.

The useful move is not to declare alien wires. It is to stop assuming alien metabolism must fit inside one tiny neighborhood.
