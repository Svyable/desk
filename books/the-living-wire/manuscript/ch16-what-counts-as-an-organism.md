# What Counts as an Organism

Cable bacteria make philosophical questions annoyingly experimental.

Where is the organism?

A filament may contain thousands of cells. Different sections perform different half-reactions because they occupy different chemistry. Current continuity links them. Break the conductor and the remote metabolism fails.

One answer is simple: the filament is the organismal unit relevant to electrogenic respiration.

But the bacterial flocks around cable filaments complicate that answer. If neighboring microbes can use the cable as an electron conduit, function extends beyond lineage. The active metabolic object may sometimes be a consortium organized around shared electrical access.

Biology already tolerates such ambiguity. Lichens, biofilms, microbiomes, syntrophic consortia, and host-associated communities all challenge clean boundaries. Cable bacteria add a physical criterion: electrical continuity can create physiological integration without cytoplasmic continuity.

This does not abolish the cell. Cells remain essential units of replication, membrane control, biosynthesis, and gene expression. The mistake would be to treat one scale as universally privileged.

For some questions the cell is the right unit. For long-distance electrogenic respiration, it is not enough.

The practical consequence is methodological. Experiments that homogenize filaments into cell suspensions may destroy the phenomenon they intend to measure. Genomes alone cannot reveal geometry. Single-cell measurements can miss a circuit whose defining property is distance.

A good ontology should survive contact with the instrument. Cable bacteria demand an ontology that includes connections.
