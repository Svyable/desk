# The Centimetre Problem

A centimetre is an ordinary distance until you ask a protein to move electrons across it.

Biological charge transfer is usually constrained by molecular contact, tunneling distances, redox hopping, diffusion of carriers, or short conductive appendages. Cable bacteria forced a different measurement problem. If electrons really traveled along intact filaments, then the conductive path had to survive cell boundaries and remain functional over thousands of cell lengths.

The early geochemical evidence established long-distance coupling, but coupling is not the same thing as a directly characterized wire. Several alternatives had to be considered: diffusible intermediates, ionic conduction, chains of local redox reactions, or some unidentified extracellular mechanism.

The 2019 Nature Communications work on intact filaments made the electrical claim harder to evade. Direct electrode measurements detected nanoampere currents through millimetre-scale filaments. Extracted periplasmic fiber sheaths retained similar conductive behavior. The reported conductivity of the fiber network reached values remarkable for biological material. Conductance collapsed on exposure to air but remained stable under vacuum, supporting electronic rather than simply ionic transport.

That experiment narrowed the mystery. The question was no longer whether a filament-scale conductor existed. It was what the conductor was made of and how charge crossed such an absurdly long biological path without dissipating the advantage of respiration.

The physical problem deserves emphasis. Long conductors accumulate resistance. Biological materials are wet, heterogeneous, and chemically reactive. Cell-cell junctions create discontinuities. Sediment flexes. Filaments bend. Any useful system would need redundancy or repair, and microscopy eventually revealed a network architecture rather than one fragile strand.

This is where the subject begins to look less like a curiosity and more like an evolutionary solution. Evolution did not merely extend an existing electron-transfer chain. It built a conductive infrastructure through multicellular anatomy.

The temptation is to leap immediately to biomaterials. That work may matter, but it can distract from the stranger natural achievement. Before humans isolated the fiber sheath and placed it on electrodes, a bacterium had already solved the problem for a purpose: keeping an energetic reaction alive across a chemical boundary in mud.

The centimetre is the clue. It tells us the metabolism cannot be understood by zooming in forever.
