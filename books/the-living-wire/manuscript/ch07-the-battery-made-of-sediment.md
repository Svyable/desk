# The Battery Made of Sediment

A cable bacterium does not carry a battery. It inhabits one.

Redox-stratified sediment contains separated chemical potentials. Reduced compounds accumulate below. Oxidants arrive from above. Diffusion and microbial consumption sharpen the gradient. The cable filament inserts itself across that gradient and turns spatial separation into an energy source.

The analogy to an electrochemical cell is productive if we stay precise. Sulfide oxidation supplies electrons in deeper sediment. Oxygen or nitrate reduction consumes them nearer the surface. The conductive fibers connect the two half-reactions. Ionic movement through porewater closes the larger circuit.

The result is detectable as an electric field and characteristic pH profile. Deep anodic activity produces acidity. Cathodic oxygen reduction near the surface consumes protons and creates alkalinity. Mineral equilibria respond. The sediment becomes chemically reorganized around the current.

This is important because metabolism is usually measured by gases, substrates, products, biomass, or isotopes. Here electric potential itself becomes ecological evidence.

The system also reveals why ‘energy source’ can be misleading. Sulfide is not useful in isolation. Oxygen is not useful in isolation. Energy becomes available through the difference in redox potential and the organism’s ability to connect the two environments. The conductive filament is therefore part of the energy-harvesting apparatus in the same way that a membrane is part of respiration in ordinary cells.

But unlike a membrane, this apparatus can span centimetres.

That scale lets cable bacteria exploit gradients unavailable to a cell confined to one microenvironment. A deep cell need not wait for oxygen to diffuse downward. A surface cell need not encounter sulfide directly. The organism routes electrons through itself rather than moving bulk reactants through sediment.

Once that possibility exists, the sediment is no longer just substrate. It is circuit geometry.
