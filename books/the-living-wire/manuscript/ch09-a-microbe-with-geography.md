# A Microbe with Geography

Most microbial ecology is cartography at small scale. A few hundred micrometres can separate radically different communities because oxygen, nitrate, iron, sulfate, methane, and sulfide are consumed in sequence.

Cable bacteria refuse to stay inside one province.

Their filaments cross boundaries that usually define microbial niches. In doing so, they complicate the meaning of habitat. Is the habitat of a filament the anoxic layer where sulfide is oxidized, the oxic surface where oxygen is reduced, or the entire corridor between them?

The answer is the corridor.

This changes how abundance should be interpreted. Counting cells without recording their position and continuity can erase the functional organism. A filament is not simply many individuals occupying neighboring layers. The electrical connection is what makes the distribution meaningful.

Freshwater discoveries strengthened this point. Cable bacteria were first characterized in marine sediments, but later work found related lineages and electrogenic signatures in streams and freshwater sediments. That expansion suggested the strategy was not a narrow adaptation to one coastal niche.

Sediment type still matters. Conductivity, sulfate supply, organic matter, oxygen exposure, iron chemistry, and physical disturbance all influence the opportunity for long-distance electrogenic metabolism. But the ecological principle is portable: wherever useful donors and acceptors are spatially separated, conduction can be advantageous.

Geography enters physiology.

This sounds abstract until one remembers that a centimetre-long bacterium filament is living across conditions that would be different worlds to an ordinary cell. One end may experience oxygen. Another may inhabit sulfide. Between them are cells whose role depends on a circuit passing through.

The organism is mapped onto the sediment’s chemistry.
