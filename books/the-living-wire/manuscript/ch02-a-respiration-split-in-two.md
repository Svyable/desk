# A Respiration Split in Two

Respiration is usually taught as a compact bargain. An electron donor gives up charge; an electron acceptor receives it; the organism captures some of the free energy released between them. Different organisms use different donors and acceptors, but the architecture remains intimate.

Cable bacteria break the intimacy.

In their canonical sediment metabolism, sulfide oxidation occurs in cells buried in anoxic sediment while oxygen reduction occurs in cells near the sediment surface. Electrons travel along the filament between those zones. The half-reactions are physically separated.

The language can make this sound more familiar than it is. Engineers separate half-reactions in batteries all the time. Electrochemists expect electrodes to occupy different places. But cells are not supposed to need geography in this way. The deep cells cannot finish respiration by themselves if they lack access to an external acceptor. The surface cells can receive electrons without being the cells that encountered the fuel. The filament turns spatial organization into part of metabolism.

This has consequences for individuality. A cell in the lower portion is metabolically incomplete in a way that is stronger than ordinary ecological dependence. It is not merely relying on another species to produce a vitamin or remove a waste product. It is participating in a redox circuit whose other half may be millimetres or centimetres away.

The distinction matters because multicellularity is usually narrated through differentiation, development, adhesion, communication, or reproductive organization. Cable bacteria offer a different route: electrical interdependence. Thousands of cells can form one conductive unit because the energy transaction itself is distributed along the chain.

There are limits to the analogy. A filament is not a nervous system. It is not a power cable in the industrial sense. We should not use technological words as proof of technological equivalence. Yet the engineering comparison remains useful if handled carefully because it exposes the peculiar topology. The filament is a conductor linking chemically distinct compartments in the environment.

The strongest empirical question is therefore not whether cable bacteria are ‘electric.’ They are. The harder question is what fraction of their physiology must be understood at the scale of the filament rather than the cell. Growth, damage, current limitation, resource access, and ecological competition may all look different depending on which scale is treated as the operative organism.

The old diagram of respiration fits on a page because it removes space. Cable bacteria put space back in.
