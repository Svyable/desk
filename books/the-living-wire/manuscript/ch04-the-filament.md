# The Filament

Cable bacteria look almost disappointingly simple under low magnification: long threads, segmented into cells, crossing sediment like pale scratches. The important architecture appears only when the filament is examined more closely.

Longitudinal ridges run along the cell envelope. Beneath them lies a shared periplasmic organization containing parallel fibers. At cell junctions, the conductive elements remain connected. The morphology is not incidental decoration. It is the physical basis for current continuity.

The filament may contain thousands of cells. This raises an awkward biological question: what happens when one cell is damaged? An ordinary electrical wire fails if its conductor is severed. A living cable needs either extraordinary resilience or redundancy.

Microscopy of the junctions has revealed a cartwheel-like organization that links conductive fibers into a network. That topology matters. Multiple parallel paths can preserve conductivity when one path is interrupted. The biological conductor is therefore not simply long. It is organized for continuity.

The system also creates a division of metabolic labor without requiring obvious permanently specialized cell types. Position along the redox gradient changes what a cell can do. Deep cells encounter sulfide. Surface cells encounter oxygen. The environment imposes a functional map onto a genetically connected filament.

This is multicellularity by landscape.

That phrase should not be pushed beyond the evidence. Cable bacteria are not animals in miniature and there is no reason to import assumptions about developmental programs or centralized coordination. But the filament demonstrates that physical continuity can allow an environmental gradient to produce distributed function. The organism does not have to carry every resource to every cell. It can carry electrons instead.

The distinction is efficient. Moving sulfide or oxygen over centimetres through sediment would require diffusion against rapid consumption and reaction. Moving charge along a conductor bypasses much of that material transport problem.

The filament is therefore both body and circuit. Its morphology is not merely where metabolism happens. Morphology determines which chemical neighborhoods can be joined into one metabolism.

A useful way to think about the organism is not as a chain of identical cells but as a moving boundary-spanning device. It grows through a landscape and makes distant chemistries adjacent.
