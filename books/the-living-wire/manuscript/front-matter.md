# Front Matter

The first mistake is to imagine a bacterium as a bag.

A membrane around chemistry. A little jurisdiction with fuel on one side, waste on the other, and all the important negotiations happening within a few micrometres. That picture is not wrong. It is simply local.

Cable bacteria make locality negotiable.

In waterlogged sediment, where oxygen disappears within millimetres and sulfide rises from below, these organisms can stretch across the boundary. Cells deeper down oxidize sulfide. Cells near the surface reduce oxygen. The two halves of respiration do not have to occur in the same cell, or even the same chemical neighborhood. Electrons are moved through a conductive structure running along the filament.

Centimetres matter here. In a bacterial world, centimetres are geographical distance.

The point of this book is not that microbes are secretly electronic gadgets. The point is more unsettling and more useful: metabolism can be distributed in space. A redox reaction that textbooks draw as a compact arrow can be physically separated into territories connected by living conductor. That fact changes how we think about organisms, sediments, biogeochemical cycles, and perhaps the signatures by which we search for life in places where ordinary assumptions about proximity fail.

There is no need to embellish the story. The documented biology is strange enough. This book will therefore keep four kinds of evidence apart: what has been directly measured and published; what is credible but incomplete; what is claimed but not independently resolved; and what is speculative synthesis. The farther the argument moves from sediment cores and electrodes, the more visible those boundaries should become.

What follows begins in mud because mud is where the old picture broke.
