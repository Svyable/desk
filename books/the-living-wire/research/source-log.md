# Source Log

Primary and serious sources checked for this draft.

1. Pfeffer et al. (2012), “Filamentous bacteria transport electrons over centimetre distances,” *Nature* 491, 218–221. https://doi.org/10.1038/nature11586 — Discovery paper for centimetre-scale long-distance electron transport in cable bacteria. Treats the sediment redox separation problem; later work supplies the direct fiber-level electrical measurements.
2. Meysman et al. (2019), “A highly conductive fibre network enables centimetre-scale electron transport in multicellular cable bacteria,” *Nature Communications* 10, 4120. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-019-12115-7 — Direct measurements on intact filaments/extracted conductive structures; supports high conductivity of the periplasmic fiber network. Ex vivo conductivity is not by itself a full specification of in-vivo current density or respiratory energy conservation.
3. Boschker et al. (2021), “Efficient long-range conduction in cable bacteria through nickel protein wires,” *Nature Communications* 12, 3996. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-021-24312-4 — Identifies sulfur-ligated nickel associated with the conductive fibers and strongly constrains their composition; microscopic charge-transport mechanism remains an active question.
4. Risgaard-Petersen et al. (2015), “Cable Bacteria in Freshwater Sediments,” *Applied and Environmental Microbiology*. https://doi.org/10.1128/AEM.01064-15 — Establishes freshwater occurrence and extends the phenomenon beyond marine sediment systems.
5. Sulu-Gambari et al. (2016), “Cable Bacteria Control Iron–Phosphorus Dynamics in Sediments of a Coastal Hypoxic Basin,” *Environmental Science & Technology*. https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.est.5b04369 — Strong sediment-biogeochemistry case for cable-bacteria-driven iron/phosphorus redistribution; system-specific magnitude should not be promoted into a universal global effect.
6. Schauer et al./related cable-bacteria freshwater and sediment literature, with individual claims to be verified against primary records in expansion.
7. “Cable bacteria with electric connection to oxygen attract flocks of diverse bacteria” (2023), *Nature Communications*. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-023-37272-8 — Demonstrates microbial flocks associated with cable bacteria electrically connected to oxygen. Physical/ecological association is not yet a direct electron ledger proving that partner-cell electrons enter the cable filament.
8. Aiyer, Digel, Linhartová et al. (2026), “Extracellular electron transfer in cable bacteria enables growth rates comparable to aerobic respiration,” *Nature Communications* 17, 6622. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-73282-y — Primary 2026 EET paper on *Electronema aureum* GS. Uses differential pulse voltammetry, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy, sediment bioelectrochemical systems and a trench-slide setup to show both direct and riboflavin-mediated extracellular electron transfer under anoxic conditions. Two cell-surface redox components were metal-dependent, pH-sensitive and heat-labile, consistent with outer-membrane redox proteins/cytochromes but not a final molecular identification. At +250 mV vs Ag/AgCl, estimated doubling times spanned ~13.5–36.8 h depending on qPCR abundance bounds, with a mean-based estimate ~20 h, comparable to reported oxic *Electrothrix* growth. At +600 mV, EET persisted but growth was much slower: best-case estimated doubling time ≥39.4 h; mean-based estimate ~115.5 h, with some comparisons showing negligible/no net abundance increase. Claim boundary: “comparable to aerobic respiration” applies to the favorable +250 mV condition, not all tested electrode potentials. Electrodes demonstrate capability; they do not establish which natural mineral phases are used as terminal acceptors in situ.
9. Digel et al. (2023), “Cable bacteria skeletons as catalytically active electrodes,” *Angewandte Chemie International Edition*. https://doi.org/10.1002/anie.202312647 — Demonstrates useful electrochemical properties of cable-bacteria-derived structures; biomaterial/electrode performance should not be conflated with intact-organism physiology.
10. Wang et al. (2021), freshwater cable bacteria sulfur cycling, *Science of the Total Environment*. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.scitotenv.2020.144541 — Freshwater sulfur-cycling context; quantitative ecological effects should remain system-specific unless replicated across sites.
11. Nielsen et al. (2010), “Electric currents couple spatially separated biogeochemical processes in marine sediment,” *Nature* 463, 1071–1074. https://doi.org/10.1038/nature08790 — Primary discovery of the electrical-coupling phenomenon before the biological conductor was identified. Changing oxygen in overlying water produced a hydrogen-sulfide response in less than one hour more than 12 mm below the oxic zone, too fast for ordinary molecular diffusion; mass balance attributed more than 40% of sediment oxygen consumption to electrons conducted from the anoxic zone. Crucial claim boundary: this paper inferred electrical coupling but did **not** identify cable bacteria as the conductor; it proposed several possible conductive components, including bacterial nanowires, pyrite, soluble shuttles and outer-membrane cytochromes. Pfeffer et al. (2012) supplied the later filament-level identification.

## Expansion rules

Every quantitative claim should be traced to a primary paper, and reviews should be used for orientation rather than as substitutes for experimental records. Keep four electrical claims separate unless a paper explicitly closes them: **internal conduction**, **cell-surface extracellular electron transfer**, **energy conservation/growth**, and **natural mineral/community electron routing**. A current trace does not automatically prove growth; an electrode experiment does not automatically identify an environmental acceptor; microbial association with a cable filament does not automatically prove interspecies electron transfer.
