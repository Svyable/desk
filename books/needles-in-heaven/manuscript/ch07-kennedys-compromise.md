# Kennedy's Compromise

West Ford reached the White House because orbital mechanics had become diplomacy.

The Kennedy administration faced an experiment that was militarily defensible, scientifically controversial, technically uncertain, and internationally visible. The solution was not simply to cancel it or wave it through. The project was constrained by conditions intended to make the second test more acceptable: attention to orbit selection and lifetime, scientific evaluation, and limits on follow-on activity while results were assessed.

The important point is not to romanticize this as modern environmental review. It was narrower and shaped by Cold War priorities. But it shows a government discovering that an engineering program could create obligations outside the agency that wanted the capability.

The compromise also exposes a recurring pattern in frontier technology. When law is immature, governance often arrives first as a bespoke political bargain. A president, scientific advisory bodies, program managers, and critics construct temporary rules around one case. If the rules work, they later look obvious. At the time they are improvised.

West Ford's orbit was part of the argument. If the individual dipoles could be shown to decay on an acceptable timescale, the project could be represented as a temporary intervention rather than permanent occupation. That promise depended on dispersion and on the accuracy of orbital modeling.

The agreement did not erase objections. International astronomers did not suddenly accept that one state had a natural right to populate orbit with experimental matter. Nor did national-security planners stop believing reliable communications justified technical risk.

What changed was the burden of explanation. The project could no longer be discussed solely in terms of whether the radio link would work. It had to answer questions about persistence, interference, review, and future repetition.

That expansion of the design envelope may be West Ford's most durable achievement. The experiment taught institutions that a system operating in a commons has requirements beyond functional performance.

Then the launch date arrived.
