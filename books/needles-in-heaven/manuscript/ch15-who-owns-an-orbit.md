# Who Owns an Orbit

West Ford arrived before the Outer Space Treaty and before a stable international answer to what states were allowed to do above one another.

Orbital space was strange territory for classical sovereignty. A satellite could circle Earth repeatedly, crossing above dozens of countries without asking each government for overflight permission. If ordinary airspace law were simply extended upward, orbital flight itself became difficult to conceptualize.

West Ford added a new twist. A spacecraft passes. A distributed belt occupies.

No single needle claimed territory, but the project intentionally changed an orbital region to serve one nation's communications objective. That made the legal question less like overflight and more like environmental use.

The emerging international framework would eventually emphasize free exploration and use, non-appropriation, responsibility for national activities, and avoidance of harmful contamination or interference in various forms. West Ford should not be credited with writing those principles. It did, however, make the abstract problem tangible.

The project also reveals the limits of ownership language. The central issue was not whether the United States owned the orbit. It was whether freedom to use a commons included freedom to alter its physical conditions for everyone else.

That question has only become harder as orbital systems scale.
