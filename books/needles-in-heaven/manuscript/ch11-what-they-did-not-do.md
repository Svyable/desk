# What They Did Not Do

West Ford attracts exaggeration because the verified story is already improbable.

The project did not create a solid metallic ring visible around Earth. It did not permanently replace the ionosphere. It did not establish that near-Earth space became unusable for astronomy. It did not prove that military engineers were indifferent to orbital lifetime. It did not continue into an operational network of repeated copper-belt deployments.

These negatives matter because spectacular history is vulnerable to narrative inflation. Once a reader accepts that hundreds of millions of copper dipoles were actually launched, almost any additional claim can ride on the same incredulity.

The evidence supports something more interesting. The project was technically competent, politically contested, environmentally imperfect, and rapidly overtaken by active satellite communications. Most properly dispersed individual dipoles behaved approximately as the disposal argument expected. Some aggregated material did not.

The experiment's scientific critics were partly reassured by post-deployment observation. The political criticism remained harder to retire because it concerned authority and precedent rather than only measured interference.

The project also did not 'cause' modern space law in any simple sense. International space governance emerged from multiple pressures: reconnaissance, communications, weapons fears, scientific cooperation, sovereignty questions, rescue, liability, and the general problem of activities beyond national territory. West Ford belongs in that ecology. It should not be promoted into a solitary origin story.

A disciplined account keeps the scale of each claim matched to the evidence. The copper belt was real. The global-governance consequences were indirect. The surviving debris is documented but modest relative to the contemporary orbital population.

There is no need to enlarge the story. Its actual dimensions are sufficient.
