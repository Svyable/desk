# The Clumps That Stayed

The environmental afterlife of West Ford is hidden in one engineering distinction: needle versus clump.

An individual dipole had an extreme surface-area-to-mass relationship. Solar radiation pressure could change its orbit efficiently. The project could plausibly argue that correctly dispersed fibers would not remain forever.

Aggregates were different objects.

Material that failed to separate cleanly behaved more like conventional debris. NASA's Orbital Debris Program would return to West Ford half a century later, asking where the needles had gone. The answer was not simply 'they all burned up.' Cataloged clumps from the experiments remained in orbit, and a population of additional small aggregates was possible.

This does not mean Earth is encircled by a dangerous invisible minefield of copper. The surviving West Ford contribution is small beside the debris environment produced by decades of launches, fragmentations, upper stages, dead spacecraft, collisions, and anti-satellite tests.

Its significance is chronological. West Ford created a debris-management problem before orbital debris management existed as a mature field.

The case also shows how a project's environmental boundary can be narrower than its physical boundary. The communications test effectively ended. The hardware history did not. Objects remained available to radar catalogues and debris researchers long after the operational question had become irrelevant.

Engineers often define completion by mission objectives. Environments define completion by persistence.

The clumps are the part of West Ford that refused to accept the program's schedule.
