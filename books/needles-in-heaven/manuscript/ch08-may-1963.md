# May 1963

On May 9, 1963, the second West Ford experiment went up.

The launch did not place a conventional communications satellite into service. It carried the machinery for releasing a population. The canister's job was to stop being a canister. Its contents had value only after they became an orbital distribution.

The release succeeded far better than the 1961 attempt. Hundreds of millions of copper dipoles entered a near-polar orbit and gradually spread. The belt was not a solid ring. It was a statistical medium: sparse particles distributed through a long orbital volume.

The system's strangeness becomes clearest from the ground. High-gain antennas in Massachusetts and California aimed toward a common region of the belt. Microwave energy left one station, encountered enough resonant copper wires to scatter a tiny useful fraction, and returned toward the receiver. The path existed because a cloud of individually useless objects had been placed in motion around Earth.

Nothing about that required science fiction. The hard parts were ordinary engineering multiplied by severe geometry.

Contemporary descriptions made the project sound both more dramatic and more primitive than it was. A 'ring of needles' suggested visible structure. An 'artificial ionosphere' suggested a replacement atmosphere. The actual system was subtler: an engineered radio-scattering population whose effectiveness depended on density, orbital position, beam overlap, Doppler spread, and receiver design.

The success was real enough to prove the principle. That is crucial. West Ford was not abandoned because the physics was nonsense. It was overtaken by a better technological trajectory.

This distinction changes the moral of the story. Failed grand projects are easy to dismiss. West Ford is harder because the engineers were substantially right about what they could make copper do. The more interesting question is whether technical success gives an institution sufficient authority to modify a shared environment in order to obtain it.

In 1963, that question was still being invented.
