# Communications After the Bomb

A communications system is easiest to admire when nothing is trying to destroy it. West Ford was conceived inside the opposite assumption.

Long-distance military communication in the 1950s depended on channels that were excellent until one imagined war. Undersea cables were physical objects with known routes and vulnerable landing points. High-frequency radio depended on the ionosphere, a naturally occurring plasma that could carry signals beyond the horizon but refused to behave like a guaranteed utility. Solar activity mattered. Geography mattered. Frequency mattered. The upper atmosphere was not a switchboard under military control.

Nuclear weapons made the problem uglier. The same strategic environment that demanded uninterrupted command also threatened to change radio propagation. A system designed only for ordinary atmospheric conditions could fail during the period when failure mattered most.

Walter E. Morrow and colleagues at MIT Lincoln Laboratory approached this as engineers. If the natural ionosphere was variable, perhaps one could create a more predictable reflector. The proposal did not need active electronics distributed around Earth. It needed resonant pieces of conductor occupying the right orbital volume. Ground stations would supply the complicated equipment: high-gain antennas, high transmitter power, precise pointing, and signal processing. Space would contain the passive medium.

The scheme inherited the logic of chaff. Aircraft had already used clouds of metallic strips to confuse radar. West Ford would turn the same physical intuition toward communication rather than deception and stretch the geometry from an aircraft encounter to a planetary belt.

This is the first place where later retellings can flatten the project. Calling it an artificial ionosphere is evocative but technically loose. The copper dipoles were not intended to reproduce the plasma physics of the ionosphere. They were engineered scatterers. Their usefulness came from resonance, number density, orbital distribution, and the intersection of narrow transmitter and receiver beams.

The system therefore had an awkward personality. It was geographically immense but physically sparse. It looked like pollution to a critic and like precision microwave engineering to its designers.

The Cold War supplied the urgency. Emerging satellite communications supplied the competition. West Ford existed in the short interval when it was plausible that the best route to dependable global military radio might be neither cable, nor natural ionosphere, nor sophisticated active relay satellite, but a man-made scattering belt.

That interval did not last long. The experiment did.
