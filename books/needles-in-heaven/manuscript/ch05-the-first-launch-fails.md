# The First Launch Fails

The first West Ford flight in October 1961 did not create the intended belt. The failure is easy to treat as prelude. It is more useful as an environmental experiment the engineers did not intend to run.

The release system was supposed to turn a compact payload into a vast population of independent dipoles. If the transformation failed, the same mass occupied orbit in a different physical state. Clumps had different area-to-mass ratios, different optical and radar properties, and different lifetimes.

The first launch therefore exposed the weakest assumption in the disposal argument: the orbital future of the project depended not only on the material chosen but on successful dispersal.

The hardware problem was attacked. Later descriptions emphasize the use of a matrix intended to release the needles as the carrier material sublimated in vacuum. The revised system succeeded well enough in 1963 to create the communications belt.

But the failed payload did not vanish from history. Decades later, debris analysis still had to ask what became of clustered West Ford material. NASA's orbital-debris literature eventually returned to the needles precisely because some clumps behaved unlike the individual fibers that were expected to leave orbit quickly.

Failure changed the ethical arithmetic. A risk model built around properly functioning hardware must contain a second model for malfunction. That sounds obvious now because launch-failure debris, fragmentation events, passivation, collision cascades, and disposal compliance belong to the ordinary language of space operations. In 1961 the vocabulary was less mature.

The first West Ford launch sits at an awkward historical point: sophisticated enough to engineer an orbital microwave scatterer, early enough that the long-tail debris consequences of a dispersal failure were not governed by the kind of international debris-mitigation norms that would later become familiar.

It also hardened the public dispute. An experiment that had promised temporary material had demonstrated, before its successful test, that not all material would necessarily behave as planned.

The second launch would therefore carry more than copper. It carried a credibility problem.
