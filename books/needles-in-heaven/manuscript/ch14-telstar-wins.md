# Telstar Wins

West Ford was defeated by a better answer arriving at almost the same moment.

Active communications satellites changed the trade. Instead of scattering a weak signal from dumb matter, a spacecraft could receive, process, and retransmit. The orbital asset became expensive and targetable, but the link became far more capable.

Telstar's 1962 demonstrations made the alternative visible to the public. Lincoln Laboratory itself moved deeply into active military satellite communications. West Ford's successful 1963 experiment therefore proved a concept whose strategic window was already closing.

This is a useful antidote to deterministic histories of technology. The copper belt was not obviously foolish when conceived and obviously useless when launched. It occupied a branching point. Had active satellite electronics, launch reliability, or space power developed differently, passive orbital scatter might have retained strategic value longer.

Instead, the architecture lost.

Obsolescence altered the environmental calculus. A temporary intervention is easier to defend when it supports a unique capability. Once another technique offers better performance without requiring intentional dispersal of hundreds of millions of objects, the burden shifts quickly.

The project did not need a treaty ban to end. Competition killed it.

That too is a governance mechanism. Sometimes the cleanest environmental regulation is a superior technology.
