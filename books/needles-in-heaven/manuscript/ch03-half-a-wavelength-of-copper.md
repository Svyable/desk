# Half a Wavelength of Copper

Project West Ford becomes less absurd when reduced to one wire.

The dipoles were cut to about 1.78 centimeters, approximately half the wavelength associated with the project's roughly 8-gigahertz microwave band. Resonance did the conceptual work. A thin conductor at the right length could scatter incoming radio energy efficiently enough that a great number of them, distributed through a shared volume of space, became useful to two carefully pointed ground stations.

The phrase hundreds of millions invites the wrong mental picture. They were not copper nails. They were hair-thin fibers. The 1963 version was thinner than the earlier design. Their total mass was modest enough to ride as an experimental payload; their number was enormous because the communication link depended on probability and geometry. The beams from two ground stations had to intersect a region containing enough dipoles moving in enough orientations to return a measurable signal.

This imposed penalties. The reflecting elements were not stationary mirrors. They occupied independent orbits with slightly different velocities and positions. The received signal spread in frequency and delay. Antenna pointing needed to be severe. Transmitter power needed to be substantial. A passive belt removed one class of vulnerability while demanding a remarkable ground system.

The engineering was not accidental. Lincoln Laboratory had deep experience in radar, long-range communication, and systems design. The project grew out of a culture accustomed to treating atmosphere, electronics, signal geometry, and military requirements as pieces of the same machine.

The disposal problem was also embedded in the physics. Individual needles had enormous area relative to mass. Solar radiation pressure and atmospheric effects would perturb and eventually remove them. This was part of the defense against critics: the belt was supposed to be transient.

That claim later became the hinge between design intention and debris reality. A correctly separated needle behaved differently from a clump. A system whose environmental acceptability depended on individual-object dynamics could fail environmentally without failing as a radio experiment if the dispenser created aggregates.

The lesson is not that the engineers ignored orbital lifetime. They did not. The more interesting lesson is that environmental assurances can be structurally fragile when they depend on every stage of an engineering sequence behaving as modeled.

A half-wavelength wire was easy to understand. Four hundred million of them required a theory of the sky.
