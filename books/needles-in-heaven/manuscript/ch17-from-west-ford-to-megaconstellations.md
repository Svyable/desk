# From West Ford to Megaconstellations

The easiest modern analogy for West Ford is also the most dangerous one.

Large satellite constellations now provoke astronomical concern because thousands of bright moving objects alter optical images, radio environments, and the statistical character of the sky. West Ford involved a vastly different technology, purpose, object size, controllability, economic model, and risk profile.

The connection is not equivalence. It is governance structure.

In both cases, an actor can obtain substantial private or public value by changing an observational environment shared with communities that do not control deployment. Mitigation becomes technical: coatings, orientation, frequency coordination, orbital choices, prediction software, data processing. Critics then face a difficult question. If mitigation works partially, does that legitimate the scale of deployment or merely reduce one externality?

West Ford offers an early answer: measure first, but do not confuse measured low harm in one implementation with a general license.

It also warns against nostalgia. The pre-satellite sky was not a politically pure commons. Military radars, high-altitude nuclear tests, radio transmitters, aircraft, rockets, and other systems already altered scientific environments. The novelty was growing scale and persistence.

The modern sky is far more engineered than the one West Ford entered. That makes the old copper belt look small. It also makes the old argument easier to recognize.

We are still negotiating permission after capability.
