# Chapter 12 debris-state audit

## Core distinction

West Ford did not produce one debris population. The book should preserve at least three physical states:

1. **properly dispersed individual dipoles** — ~1.8 cm long, ~0.00178 cm diameter, too small for routine SSN tracking and designed for rapid orbital evolution through solar-radiation-pressure sensitivity;
2. **incompletely dispersed clumps** — bundles with lower area-to-mass ratio and much longer persistence;
3. **mission/dispenser hardware** — ordinary catalogable spacecraft debris.

Collapsing these into `needles in orbit` obscures both the successful cleanup mechanism and the persistence failure.

## Primary authoritative anchor

NASA Orbital Debris Program Office, `West Ford Needles: Where are They Now?`, *Orbital Debris Quarterly News* 17(4), October 2013:

https://orbitaldebris.jsc.nasa.gov/Quarterly-News/pdfs/ODQNv17i4.pdf

### 1961 mission

- launch: 21 October 1961;
- intended orbit reached: roughly **3500 × 3750 km**, inclination **95.9°**;
- dipoles did not dispense individually because of design characteristics and unanticipated thermal effects;
- seven small debris objects believed associated with the experiment were cataloged;
- NASA reported all seven still in orbit in the 2013 snapshot.

### 1963 mission

- launch: 9 May 1963;
- initial orbit roughly **3600 × 3680 km**, inclination **87.4°**;
- estimated **120–215 million** wires released;
- wire dimensions: **1.8 cm × 0.00178 cm diameter**;
- planned population: roughly **480 million dipoles**;
- post-mission assessment: only **25–45%** dispersed properly;
- properly dispersed individual needles were believed to have reentered within a few years, largely because of solar-radiation-pressure effects;
- four larger 1963 debris were likely dispenser subunits;
- beginning in 1966, SSN cataloged **144 small objects associated with the second experiment**;
- NASA noted two additional objects carrying West Ford labels were likely miscataloged from another mission;
- **46 clumps** were known to remain in orbit in the 2013 snapshot;
- only **9** of those 46 then had perigees below 2000 km.

### Subcatalog population

NASA also reported that mid-1990s Goldstone data suggested a moderate population of small debris above 2500 km and that these objects had been proposed as small clumps from the 1961 failure.

Safe wording: **suggested / proposed association**.

Unsafe wording: **NASA proved all small high-altitude detections were West Ford clumps**.

## Claim boundaries

### Safe

- Properly dispersed individual dipoles and aggregated clumps had materially different orbital lifetimes.
- West Ford left catalogable debris/clumps for decades after the communications experiment ended.
- The 2013 NASA snapshot counted 46 known surviving clumps.
- A failed or partial dispersal mechanism can transform the environmental lifetime of an experiment without changing its material composition.

### Do not claim

- `46 remain today` without a fresh current catalog reconstruction.
- Hundreds of millions of individual West Ford needles remain in orbit.
- Every cataloged West Ford object is a clump of copper dipoles; some are dispenser/mission hardware.
- Every uncataloged small object near the historical orbital band is West Ford material.
- West Ford is a major quantitative contributor to the modern orbital-debris hazard.

## High-value next work

Reconstruct the current 2026 state from public orbital catalogs and, where possible, original ESA/NASA clump-identification analyses. Use object-by-object identifiers, epoch, perigee/apogee, decay status and classification confidence rather than carrying the 2013 `46` snapshot forward as if timeless.
