# The Animal in the Loop

## 1. The Pigeon Has the Camera

The photograph is funny until you understand the altitude.

A pigeon wears a camera on its chest. That sentence arrives preloaded with comedy. It sounds like a prop department lost an argument. But the engineering proposition was serious. A reconnaissance aircraft sees from tens of thousands of feet. A satellite sees from orbit. A pigeon can cross a target a few hundred feet above the roof and attract less attention than a man standing on the sidewalk looking up.

The Central Intelligence Agency now says plainly that its Office of Research and Development made cameras light enough for pigeons and explored their use over denied areas. Parts of the story remain classified. That last fact should make us more careful, not more imaginative.

The interesting thing is the substitution. Intelligence engineers did not ask how to build a tiny aircraft with exquisite navigation, obstacle avoidance, self-stabilization, launch tolerance, covert appearance and a return-to-base function. They found an organism that already had those things and attached the missing sensor.

This is the book in miniature.

The bird was not the payload. The camera was not quite the payload either. The system was bird, camera, harness, release geometry, target, weather, handler, homing behavior, film, recovery and the ugly probability that one of those pieces would refuse to cooperate. A living thing entered the block diagram and the diagram stopped behaving like a diagram.

That is where the history becomes useful now.

We are surrounded by arguments about autonomous systems as though autonomy arrived with software. It did not. Engineers have been trying to command capable agents they did not fully understand for a very long time. Sometimes those agents had feathers.

## 2. A Component With a Pulse

Hardware is attractive because it can be insulted without consequences.

A gyroscope does not get bored. A pressure transducer does not decide that the technician is frightening. A camera does not require fish. Machines fail, but their failures are supposed to be legible. The fantasy of engineering is not that nothing breaks. It is that breakage belongs to a causal chain.

Animals ruin this comfort.

The same biological system that supplies extraordinary capability supplies internal state. Hunger matters. Stress matters. attachment matters. fatigue matters. reinforcement history matters. The environment is not merely an input; it can become a competing world.

This made animals irresistible to military research. Evolution had already paid development costs no procurement office could afford. Odor detection, magnetic orientation, echolocation, hydrodynamic movement, low-light vision, homing and adaptive locomotion came packaged in bodies that repaired themselves and learned.

But evolution had optimized none of these bodies for a contracting officer's definition of reliability.

The question was never simply whether an animal could perform a task. The question was whether the task could be made operational: repeatable enough, transportable enough, interpretable enough, maintainable enough and controllable enough that another institution could depend on it.

That distinction—capability versus operational utility—will recur until it becomes annoying. It should. Institutions routinely confuse demonstrations with systems.

## 3. The View From Three Hundred Feet

Low altitude is a form of intimacy.

The famous strategic reconnaissance systems of the Cold War solved the denied-area problem by climbing. U-2. OXCART. CORONA. Distance was survival. But distance also had optical consequences.

The pigeon program inverted the geometry. Instead of making the camera more powerful, bring the camera closer.

This sounds obvious. It is not. Getting closer usually makes the platform easier to detect. The pigeon offered camouflage not through paint or radar shaping but through category. It belonged there. A bird over a city was environmental clutter.

That is a different kind of stealth.

Modern engineers call versions of this biomimicry, low observability, signature management. The pigeon did something stranger: it borrowed the statistical normality of life. Detection required first deciding that an ordinary object was not ordinary.

The trade was control. A powered aircraft can be redirected. A pigeon has a route because the pigeon has a home.

The mission designer therefore had to exploit behavior rather than merely issue commands. That is a deep distinction. You do not tell a homing pigeon where home is at the moment of release. You arrange the world so that its own objective carries your sensor through the place you care about.

That is mechanism design with feathers.

## 4. Acoustic Kitty

No animal-intelligence story has been damaged more thoroughly by being too good a story.

The phrase Acoustic Kitty survives because it is irresistible. CIA technicians, in the popular version, surgically transformed a cat into a walking listening device; the cat was released near Soviet officials; a taxi immediately killed it; millions of dollars vanished beneath a tire.

Parts of that story have become folklore through repetition. The responsible move is to separate what surviving declassified material establishes from what later retellings embroidered.

The CIA did investigate cats as acoustic platforms. The technical premise was not insane: microphones and transmitters could be concealed in an animal that might approach conversations without producing the social alarm of a human operative. The biological premise was much worse. Cats are extraordinarily capable mobile organisms and famously weak employees.

That mismatch is the point.

A cat can move through human environments with astonishing access. It can balance, hide, hear, orient and improvise. Those are expensive capabilities. But the very agency that makes the cat capable makes it unreliable as a remotely specified route executor.

The project is usually told as a joke about government waste. It is more useful as an experiment in control theory.

The sensor could work. The transmitter could work. The animal could work. The system could still fail.

## 5. The Problem of Coming Home

Homing pigeons solve a navigation problem we still describe more confidently than we understand it.

They use multiple cues. Olfaction matters. Sun compass information matters. Visual landmarks matter. Magnetic information may matter under some conditions. The exact weighting is context dependent. A pigeon is not a GPS receiver with wings. It is a multisensor estimator grown by evolution and trained by experience.

For an intelligence program this is both gift and trap.

The gift is robustness. Remove one cue and the animal may compensate. The trap is epistemic opacity. If you do not know which cues dominate in a particular release, you cannot easily know which environmental change will destroy the mission.

Engineering loves redundancy when redundancy is specified. Biology often provides redundancy whose arbitration rules are hidden inside the organism.

This becomes a recurring feature of animal systems. Their competence is partly produced by internal processes the operator does not observe. The better the animal is at solving its own problem, the more tempting it becomes to pretend that its problem and yours are identical.

They are not.

## 6. The Navy Goes Underwater

Water punishes machinery.

Radio attenuates. Light disappears. Pressure climbs. Corrosion waits. Turbidity erases vision. The seabed offers clutter that can make a manufactured sensor stupid.

The U.S. Navy says its Marine Mammal Program began in 1959 and eventually examined more than a dozen marine-mammal species as well as sharks, rays, sea turtles and marine birds. Today it relies principally on bottlenose dolphins and California sea lions for tasks including detecting, locating, marking and recovering objects and helping protect harbors against unauthorized swimmers and divers.

This is not a rumor. It is an acknowledged military capability.

The program survived because the animals could do useful work in a domain that makes ordinary sensing difficult. Dolphins bring exquisite active biosonar. Sea lions bring vision, maneuverability and trainability. Both bring bodies evolved for the medium.

The Navy did not invent an underwater sensor and then decide to make it cute. It encountered a sensor that was already swimming.

## 7. Echolocation Is Not Sonar

Calling dolphin echolocation “sonar” is useful until it becomes misleading.

The analogy captures emission and echo. It misses the animal.

A dolphin does not merely produce a click, record a waveform and run a fixed classifier. It changes its behavior. It approaches. It samples from new angles. It alters click timing. It integrates touch, vision, memory, expectation and reinforcement. Perception is active.

This matters operationally because a dolphin can detect objects that are difficult acoustic targets for conventional systems. Navy accounts emphasize mine detection and object location, including buried objects.

But the phrase “better sonar” encourages the wrong engineering fantasy: extract the organ, reproduce the waveform, replace the animal.

The capability may live in the loop between sensing and movement rather than in the transmitter alone.

That is inconvenient. It means copying the click is not copying the perception.

## 8. Tuffy

There is a 1969 Navy photograph of a porpoise named Tuffy pushing a buzzer on the side of a boat.

I love this photograph because it is almost aggressively undramatic.

No enemy diver. No explosive harness. No secret harbor. An animal presses a button.

That is what serious training often looks like before mythology gets hold of it: repetitions, cues, reinforcement, transport, veterinary care, handlers learning the animal while the animal learns the handlers.

Tuffy became famous enough to occupy the edge between research animal and public character. But the useful historical question is not whether a particular demonstration looked impressive. It is how an institution converts animal learning into a deployable procedure.

The procedure includes welfare because physiology is performance. It includes handler consistency because ambiguity is error. It includes transportation because a sensor that cannot arrive is not a sensor. It includes recovery because the platform is not disposable in the ordinary sense.

A living system makes logistics intimate.

## 9. The Mine in the Mud

Mines are designed to make the sea uncertain.

The mine need not destroy anything to alter behavior. It needs to make a commander believe that a patch of water might contain something waiting.

That turns detection into a strategic problem.

The Navy's use of dolphins for mine countermeasures is therefore not a circus trick. It sits inside a brutal requirement: find small, dangerous objects in a noisy, cluttered medium without first touching them with a ship.

The animal's value is not mystical. It is ecological. Dolphins evolved to make discriminations underwater because failure to perceive underwater is a bad long-term reproductive strategy.

Yet operational mine hunting exposes the animal-platform paradox in its cleanest form. The animal can outperform a machine on the narrow perceptual task while demanding a larger support ecology than the brochure admits.

Trainer. veterinarian. transport. food. rest. communication. recovery. environmental limits. interpretation.

The sensor has a social system.

## 10. The Howell Torpedo

In March 2013, during a training exercise off San Diego, Navy dolphins indicated an object buried on the seafloor. Divers investigated. What emerged was not a mine but history: sections of Howell Mark I torpedo No. 24, lost from USS Iowa during training in 1899.

Only fifty Howell torpedoes had been ordered. Before this recovery, two were known to survive.

There is something almost too neat about the episode. A nineteenth-century machine weapon, lost by one generation of naval technology, was rediscovered by a twentieth-century biological sensor program in the twenty-first century.

The dolphins did not know they had found an artifact. That distinction matters. Detection and interpretation were distributed across species.

The animal found an acoustic anomaly. Humans supplied history.

This is what “human in the loop” looks like when the machine is alive: neither participant contains the whole solution.

## 11. The Rumor of the Killer Dolphin

Classification is a rumor factory with a security clearance.

For decades, the Navy could not fully discuss the missions of its marine-mammal program. In that vacuum grew stories of dolphins carrying explosives, dolphins killing divers, dolphins trained as underwater assassins.

The Navy now explicitly says that decades of classification helped generate such speculation and that offensive-weapon allegations could not be countered while the missions remained classified. After declassification in the early 1990s, it publicly described detection, marking, recovery and harbor-security roles.

An official denial is evidence of an official position, not a magic solvent for every historical uncertainty. But the episode demonstrates a recurring epistemic hazard: secrecy does not merely hide true information. It changes the market for false information.

When ordinary explanations cannot be confirmed, extraordinary explanations gain room.

The public then mistakes the size of the information vacuum for the size of the secret.

## 12. Classification Makes Monsters

A classified animal program has an unusually high myth yield.

The ingredients are perfect: intelligence agencies, surgery, military handlers, creatures whose sensory capacities already seem uncanny, and records released decades after rumor has hardened into memory.

This produces two symmetrical errors.

Believers treat classification as positive evidence that the wildest claim is true. Skeptics treat absurdity as evidence that no serious program existed.

Both shortcuts fail.

Governments really did spend money on animal platforms. Some projects really were strange. Some strange projects failed. Some animal capabilities became durable operational systems. Some sensational claims remain unsupported.

The only useful response is claim-level accounting.

Was the program documented? Was the specific mission documented? Was a capability demonstrated? Was it deployed? Was it effective? Was the story contemporaneous or retrospective? Does the source have an incentive to exaggerate success, secrecy, cruelty or absurdity?

A good book about secret animals should contain less magic at the end than at the beginning.

## 13. Charlie the Catfish

Eventually the animal becomes a machine again.

CIA's robot fish Charlie was developed as an unmanned underwater vehicle to explore aquatic robotic technology and covert water sampling. The Agency's museum describes a pressure hull, ballast, communications, tail propulsion and radio control.

Charlie is the hinge of the story.

The pigeon solution says: use the organism because it already has locomotion and navigation.

Charlie says: imitate the organism because perhaps we can now manufacture the useful parts without the inconvenient agency.

This is the ancient dream of biomimetics. Keep the hydrodynamics. Lose hunger. Keep maneuverability. Lose fear. Keep environmental normality. Lose the veterinarian.

But imitation is selective. A fish is not a tail attached to a pressure hull. The machine copies visible geometry because geometry is copyable.

Biology's deepest advantage may be integration.

## 14. Copying the Animal

Engineers are forever stealing from animals and discovering that theft is difficult.

Birds become aircraft metaphors. Bats become sonar metaphors. fish become underwater-robot metaphors. insects become swarm metaphors.

The metaphor is productive because evolution is a gigantic search process. It is dangerous because evolved systems are compromises embedded in environments, not catalogs of isolated tricks.

A dolphin's biosonar belongs to a dolphin. Its skull, tissues, musculature, auditory pathways, locomotion, cognition and social training all participate.

The more impressive the biological capability, the more likely we are to underestimate how distributed it is.

This is why animal military programs deserve to sit beside robotics history rather than in its comic appendix. They reveal what engineers wanted before engineers could build it.

Small autonomous reconnaissance. Covert mobility. adaptive navigation. target discrimination. environmental blending. recovery. persistence.

The wishlist looks contemporary because the wishlist barely changed.

## 15. The Handler Is Part of the Machine

Remove the handler from an animal system and you have not automated it. You have amputated it.

Handlers observe changes too small for a specification sheet: hesitation, appetite, arousal, distraction, fatigue, willingness. They also become part of the cue environment. That creates capability and risk.

The history of animal behavior is full of warnings about inadvertent cueing. Clever Hans did not perform arithmetic; the horse read human signals that people did not know they were giving.

A military animal system therefore has a peculiar verification problem. If the animal succeeds, what information did it actually use?

This is not academic. A detector that keys on handler posture rather than target signature can look brilliant in training and collapse in deployment.

Modern machine learning has rediscovered this problem under different names: shortcut learning, leakage, spurious correlation, distribution shift.

The horse was there first.

## 16. Failure Without Malfunction

A machine can function exactly as designed and still fail its mission. Animals make this obvious.

The cat that follows a smell instead of a surveillance route is not broken.

The pigeon that returns home by a path that misses the target is not broken.

The dolphin that responds differently under environmental stress may not be broken.

Mission failure and component malfunction separate.

This is the conceptual gift of the animal programs. They force us to describe objectives at multiple levels. The institution wants intelligence. The handler wants a trained behavior. The animal wants whatever its nervous system currently values. The environment offers competing signals.

Control exists only where those objectives can be aligned for long enough.

Autonomy is not independence. It is the presence of an internal policy the operator does not directly write.

## 17. Autonomy Before Autonomy

We have made autonomy sound digital because software made it scalable.

But the underlying governance problem is older. How do you delegate action to an agent whose local perception exceeds yours? How do you know when its judgment should override your expectation? How do you calibrate trust when the agent cannot explain itself in your language? How do you distinguish a bad instruction from a bad execution?

A dolphin hunting a mine and a machine-learning system classifying an image are not the same thing. Pretending they are would flatten biology into metaphor.

The comparison is narrower and more useful.

Both can be valuable because they transform inputs through internal processes the operator only partly understands. Both can exploit cues the operator did not intend. Both can look robust inside a training ecology and fail when the ecology shifts. Both invite anthropomorphism in opposite directions: we imagine too much mind in the machine and too little in the animal.

The animal programs are not prototypes of artificial intelligence.

They are prototypes of delegated competence.

## 18. The Animal We Keep Reinventing

The future arrived and it wanted to be a pigeon.

Small drones now fly low over targets. Autonomous underwater vehicles search seabeds. robotic fish copy body forms. machine-vision systems classify objects. navigation stacks fuse sensors. Swarms promise cheap redundancy.

We spent decades removing the animal from the loop.

The victory is real. Machines do not need fish. They can be stored. replicated. updated. sacrificed. Their performance can sometimes be characterized more cleanly. They do not possess welfare interests that make the word “platform” morally evasive.

But something was lost in the simplification.

Animals remain astonishingly integrated machines that are not machines. Their sensing is active. Their intelligence is embodied. Their robustness comes partly from adaptation. Their failures expose hidden assumptions in our task design.

The strangest lesson of the spy pigeon and the Navy dolphin is not that Cold War institutions were willing to try bizarre things. Of course they were. Institutions under strategic pressure search.

The stranger lesson is that the bizarre thing sometimes worked.

A pigeon really can carry a camera. A dolphin really can find a buried object. A sea lion really can recover things underwater. Those facts do not vindicate every rumor built around them. They make the epistemology harder.

Ridicule is not a method.

Neither is awe.

The useful question is always the duller one: what, exactly, did the system do?

Then another: what did we have to build around it before we could depend on the answer?

That second question is where the animal was hiding all along.
