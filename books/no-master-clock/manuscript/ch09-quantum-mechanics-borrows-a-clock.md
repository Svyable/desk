# Quantum Mechanics Borrows a Clock

Quantum mechanics has a clock problem before gravity enters the room.

It hides the problem well.

Write the Schrödinger equation and time appears on the outside. A quantum state changes with respect to a parameter t. Position is represented by an operator. Momentum is represented by an operator. Energy is associated with the Hamiltonian. Time, in the standard nonrelativistic formulation, is usually not an observable represented by an operator in the same straightforward way. It is the parameter against which the state evolves.

This asymmetry works spectacularly.

If I prepare an atom in a superposition of energy states, quantum mechanics predicts how the relative phase evolves with time. If I drive a transition with a laser, the theory predicts probabilities as functions of pulse duration and detuning. If I release a wave packet, the equation tells me how it spreads. In every case, t arrives already supplied.

The laboratory rarely complains.

There is a clock on the wall, a pulse generator, an oscillator, a controller, a sequence file. The experimental apparatus contains physical timing devices, but the mathematical model can treat time as an external classical parameter with negligible error. The clocks are large, stable, and effectively independent of the tiny quantum system being modeled.

The borrowed clock is an excellent loan.

Problems begin when you ask where it came from.

Suppose the system under description is not one atom but the entire closed universe. What external clock does the universal wavefunction evolve against? There is no laboratory outside the universe with a pulse generator. If gravity is also quantum, the spacetime geometry that ordinarily helps define temporal relationships is part of the quantum system. The background has entered the experiment.

This is one route into the problem of time in quantum gravity.

The phrase covers several related technical and conceptual difficulties, and specialists divide them more finely than a trade book can without becoming a textbook. The central tension is enough for our purpose. General relativity treats spacetime geometry as dynamical and coordinates as lacking direct physical significance by themselves. Standard quantum theory describes evolution relative to an external time parameter. Quantizing gravity asks the two theories to share a house while they disagree about where the clock lives.

This is not the same problem as the psychological arrow of time.

It is not the same problem as why entropy increases.

It is not the same problem as whether the present moment feels special.

It is not evidence that yesterday did not happen.

The temptation to mix these questions is nearly irresistible because each uses the word *time*. Resist it.

In ordinary quantum mechanics, the time parameter can be tied operationally to an external classical clock. The theory’s predictions have been tested with absurd precision. There is no practical crisis. The conceptual issue appears when the separation between system and external temporal reference can no longer be assumed.

A clock is then one quantum system among others.

That sentence changes the architecture.

Take the simplest possible clock: a two-level quantum system. Prepare a superposition of two energy eigenstates. The relative phase evolves. Measure that phase and you have a periodic temporal reference. An atomic clock is vastly more engineered, but the core idea is related: quantum evolution provides regular phase against which duration can be inferred.

The clock’s ability to tell time comes from changing.

Yet the mathematical description of its change already uses time.

We have a loop.

In the laboratory the loop is harmless because the clock we use to describe the atom is not the atom we are modeling. We can nest references: a microwave oscillator times the pulse that interrogates a transition; the oscillator is compared to another standard; the standard is compared through a metrological chain. Somewhere the theorist stops modeling the reference as quantum and declares t classical.

This is not cheating.

Physics is built from controlled cuts. We do not include the quantum state of the Moon when calculating a transistor. We do not include every air molecule when modeling a clock laser. A theory becomes useful by deciding what can be treated as environment or background.

The question is whether the cut can remain fundamental when the system is everything.

Quantum cosmology does not have an obvious outside.

Canonical approaches to quantum gravity make the issue especially stark. In a Hamiltonian formulation of general relativity, the theory contains constraints reflecting diffeomorphism invariance. When quantized in the Wheeler-DeWitt style, one arrives schematically at an equation often written as H Ψ = 0. There is no external ∂Ψ/∂t term resembling the ordinary Schrödinger equation.

The wavefunction of the universe appears frozen.

Calling it frozen can mislead because “frozen” already assumes an external time in which nothing changes. The formal issue is that the usual time-evolution structure is absent. The physical states satisfy constraints. How, then, do we recover the evident world of changing clocks, expanding cosmological scale factors, decaying particles, rotating galaxies, and people missing trains?

The problem is not that the equations predict a still universe in the ordinary sense.

The problem is explaining change relationally inside a formalism without the usual external time.

This is where clocks stop being passive instruments and become candidate coordinates built from matter.

Suppose some degree of freedom changes monotonically enough to serve as an internal clock. Instead of asking, “What is the value of quantity A at external time t?” we ask, “What is the value of A when quantity B, chosen as the clock, has value b?” Dynamics becomes correlation.

A simple classical analogy comes first. Imagine two hands on a clock face but remove all labels from external time. One hand makes twelve revolutions while another makes one. You can still say where the fast hand is when the slow hand points north. The relationship contains dynamics without consulting another clock outside the pair.

Quantum mechanics makes the analogy technically difficult because the clock itself can be uncertain, entangled, finite, and disturbed by interaction. But the relational instinct is clear.

Change can be described as one subsystem changing relative to another.

This idea has old roots. Leibniz and Mach loom in the philosophical background. In twentieth-century physics, relational observables become important in generally covariant systems. Bryce DeWitt, John Wheeler, Karel Kuchař, Chris Isham, Carlo Rovelli, Don Page, William Wootters, and many others approached facets of the time problem differently. There is no single relational solution that everyone accepted and went home.

That pluralism matters.

A book looking for a dramatic thesis can cherry-pick one proposal and announce that physics has proven time emerges from entanglement or entropy. The literature does not justify that confidence.

What it justifies is more interesting: serious physical frameworks exist in which the time relevant to dynamics is recovered from relationships among physical degrees of freedom rather than postulated as an external universal parameter.

Page and Wootters provided one especially memorable construction in 1983. They considered how a globally stationary quantum system could display dynamics when one part serves as a clock and another as the system. Conditional on a clock reading, the system can appear to evolve according to ordinary quantum mechanics even though the joint state satisfies a stationary constraint.

“Evolution without evolution,” they called it.

The phrase sounds like Zen advertising. The paper is mathematics.

We will spend the next chapter inside it. For now, notice how the proposal completes a circle begun with relativity. Newton put time outside the world. Einstein made local clocks part of spacetime geometry. Quantum gravity asks whether even the temporal parameter used to describe quantum evolution can be reconstructed from within the world.

The clock keeps losing privilege.

There is a danger in seeing a historical march toward one metaphysical conclusion. Physics does not move on rails. Different formulations use different structures. Quantum field theory on curved spacetime can use background spacetimes with suitable time notions. Perturbative quantum gravity around a background can use a time coordinate. Some approaches to quantum gravity identify useful internal or emergent times; others formulate observables without putting the same philosophical weight on emergence. The “problem of time” is not one equation with one missing variable.

The right attitude is architectural rather than triumphant.

Ask what a theory needs in order to make predictions.

Ordinary quantum mechanics needs a temporal parameter in its standard dynamical law. A real experiment needs a physical timing reference. General relativity says physical clocks measure proper time along worldlines and coordinate time is part of a spacetime description. A quantum theory of gravity must reconcile these facts in a regime where clocks and geometry may both have quantum properties.

The external master clock is no longer an innocent assumption.

This has practical echoes even before quantum gravity is tested.

Quantum metrology treats clocks as finite quantum systems. Their precision is limited by quantum projection noise, laser coherence, atom number, decoherence, control errors, and fundamental trade-offs described through quantum Fisher information and related tools. Entanglement and squeezing can improve sensitivity. A clock is not an infinite-resolution parameter oracle. It consumes physical resources.

The clock can also become entangled with what it times.

In proposed quantum-clock interferometry, internal states act as clocks while the center of mass follows superposed paths. If the paths accumulate different proper times, the internal clock states can carry which-path information and reduce interference visibility. The proposal by Pikovski, Zych, Costa, and Brukner made this relationship vivid: proper time can be operationally encoded in a quantum degree of freedom, and the clock’s own time record can affect interference.

Later work has refined, challenged, and generalized aspects of quantum time dilation and clock models. The field is active. The established point is not that one tabletop experiment has quantized gravity. It is that quantum systems can function as clocks and that treating clocks as quantum exposes relationships hidden when time is an external number.

This is the frontier distinction the book will maintain.

Quantum clocks are real laboratory systems.

Quantum gravity remains experimentally underconstrained.

Relational-time frameworks are serious theory, not established ontology.

A credible synthesis must preserve all three statements at once.

The temptation to oversell comes partly from language. “The universe is timeless” is irresistible. It makes an excellent cover line. But if a formal quantum-gravity state satisfies a Hamiltonian constraint, the statement does not automatically mean lived change is an illusion. The physical content depends on how observables, correlations, semiclassical limits, and internal clocks recover empirical predictions.

Timeless equations can encode change.

A photograph does too.

One image of a pendulum at many superposed positions would not itself be a movie, but correlations within a static mathematical object can represent a sequence. A road map is static while encoding many possible journeys. A musical score sits motionless on paper while specifying temporal relationships when performed. These analogies are incomplete because quantum constraints are not pictures or scores. They are useful only to break the reflex that a stationary formal object cannot describe relational change.

Physics has long separated representation from experience.

The block spacetime of relativity is sometimes described as a four-dimensional structure in which past and future events are equally part of the geometry. People then ask why time feels like it flows. That is a legitimate philosophical and cognitive question, but it is not answered by declaring the geometry static. A worldline contains ordered events and proper time. Organisms inside the worldline have memory, entropy production, metabolism, and asymmetric records. The experience of passage may require another explanatory layer.

Quantum gravity adds another representational puzzle, not a license to erase the world.

The most disciplined way forward is to ask what a clock must do.

It must correlate states.

If a clock reads 7, some other subsystem is expected to have one distribution of possible values. When the clock reads 8, the distribution has changed. The predictive content lies in the conditional relation. Whether an external t exists behind both may be unnecessary to the operational statement.

This is the seed of relational time.

The seed is modest enough to be familiar. Every actual time measurement is a comparison. We do not sense seconds directly. We compare a process with a reference: heartbeats to a stopwatch, a chemical reaction to an oscillator, a photon arrival to a detector clock, Earth rotation to atomic time.

The radical move is to ask whether fundamental theory needs more than comparison.

Maybe it does.

Maybe some emergent or semiclassical notion of external time is indispensable in the regimes we inhabit.

Maybe different formulations reveal different layers rather than one final answer.

The book will not decide by adjective.

What we can say now is enough to make Newton’s missing clock look even stranger. In classical mechanics, absolute time was an unobservable background parameter. In ordinary quantum mechanics, an external time parameter remains central. In general relativity, spacetime is dynamical. At the quantum-gravity boundary, the borrowed clock comes due.

We have to account for it.

Page and Wootters tried by putting the clock inside the state.
