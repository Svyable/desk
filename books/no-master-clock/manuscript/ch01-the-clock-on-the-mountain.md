# The Clock on the Mountain

The first useful thing to know about a precision clock is that it is never just telling you what time it is.

It is telling you something about where it has been.

Put two ordinary wristwatches on a table and that distinction is useless. Their errors are so large, and the relativistic differences across the tabletop so small, that talking about worldlines would be a good way to avoid getting any work done. A watch on your wrist is allowed to behave as though the whole room shares one time. A kitchen timer can be forgiven for believing in Newton.

An optical atomic clock is less forgiving.

In February 2022, a JILA team reported resolving gravitational redshift across a cloud of strontium atoms about a millimeter tall. General relativity says clocks deeper in a gravitational potential tick more slowly than otherwise identical clocks higher up. This had been demonstrated over large height differences and then over centimeters. The strontium experiment pushed the comparison inside a single atomic sample. The upper part of the cloud and the lower part did not accumulate exactly the same proper time. The difference was tiny. The clock was good enough to see it.

A millimeter is not much of a mountain. It is enough.

The result is sometimes described as a test of Einstein at small scales, which it was. There is another way to read it. The experiment is a warning label attached to the noun *time*. Once your instruments become sufficiently good, you can no longer say “the time in the lab” without deciding how much lab you mean.

That is not a philosophical complication added after the measurement. It is in the measurement.

The atoms in an optical clock are prepared so that an exquisitely stable electromagnetic field can interrogate a narrow transition between quantum states. The transition frequency is the regular event. The engineering problem is to isolate it from everything that can shift or broaden it: thermal radiation, electromagnetic fields, collisions, motion, imperfect laser light, interactions among atoms. Modern clock papers read like a catalog of ways matter refuses to be an abstraction. The clock becomes better as the experimenters learn which parts of the world are leaking into the tick.

Then gravity itself becomes one of those parts.

The phrase “gravitational redshift” can make this sound as though the atom is doing one thing and gravity later colors the signal on its way to us. In general relativity the structure is deeper. Proper time is what an ideal clock measures along its path through spacetime. Different paths between events can contain different amounts of proper time. A clock held at one gravitational potential and a clock held at another need not agree after they are compared. Neither one is defective. There is no hidden third clock behind them whose reading would settle which was right.

We are accustomed to errors around a true value. Relativity gives us legitimate differences.

That distinction is the hinge for this book.

Suppose I put one clock at the bottom of a tower and another at the top. I synchronize them, let them run, and compare them later using a method whose own delays and relativistic corrections I understand. If the upper clock gains relative to the lower one by the amount general relativity predicts, it would be perverse to say the upper clock ran fast. “Fast” ordinarily means deviation from a reference process that both devices were supposed to match. Here the rate difference is the phenomenon. The reference process is local.

A sufficiently accurate clock becomes a gravity sensor.

This is already useful. Chronometric or relativistic geodesy aims to use clock comparisons to infer differences in gravitational potential. Traditionally, surveyors determine height through networks of leveling measurements, satellite observations, gravimeters, and geoid models. Optical clocks offer another route because frequency can encode gravitational potential directly. In the roughest near-Earth language, raising a clock changes its rate by about one part in 10^18 per centimeter of elevation. The exact relation belongs to the gravitational potential and the reference system, not to a carpenter’s ruler, but the practical implication is startling: the frontier of timekeeping has entered the scale of centimeters.

At that point the old question “How accurate is the clock?” begins to eat its own tail.

Accurate relative to what potential?

If I move the apparatus to a different floor, I have changed the rate I am asking it to realize. If groundwater moves, if tides redistribute mass, if the solid Earth deforms, the gravitational potential changes. Better clocks eventually make the planet underneath the laboratory part of the clock problem. What looks like clock noise may contain geophysics. What looks like geodesy may be performed by comparing oscillators.

A clock this good does not become detached from the world. It becomes a more sensitive participant in it.

That is worth holding onto because popular accounts of precision often use the opposite metaphor. The ideal clock is imagined as a device increasingly insulated from reality, sealed off until nothing can touch its perfect beat. There is truth in the laboratory practice: one suppresses perturbations. But the endpoint is not a tick floating outside physics. The endpoint is a system whose known couplings are controlled well enough that the remaining differences can be interpreted.

The distinction between isolation and interpretation matters even more when clocks are compared over distance.

You cannot place Boulder and Tokyo side by side. You send signals. The signals propagate through fiber, atmosphere, or space. Their paths have delays and asymmetries. The clocks occupy different gravitational potentials and move differently with Earth’s rotation. A useful comparison requires a model of these effects and a transfer technique that makes them tractable. The better the clocks, the more seriously you have to take the transfer.

There is an irony here. The pursuit of a universal time standard has produced instruments that keep revealing the local character of elapsed time.

This does not make international timekeeping impossible. It makes international timekeeping impressive.

The Bureau International des Poids et Mesures does not own a magical oscillator in Sèvres from which world time pours. International Atomic Time, TAI, is computed from an ensemble of atomic clocks maintained by timing laboratories around the world. Laboratories compare clocks, data are combined, frequency standards calibrate the scale interval, and a timescale is produced. Coordinated Universal Time, UTC, is derived within that international machinery and distributed through national realizations and networks.

Common time is therefore not one object. It is a maintained relationship among objects.

The more deeply you look, the less resemblance it bears to the schoolroom picture of a master clock.

There are reasons to be cautious with the rhetoric. Atomic clocks do not prove that “time is an illusion.” A clock experiment measures frequency ratios and elapsed proper times within theories that have passed severe tests. It does not by itself tell us whether time is fundamental in quantum gravity, why we experience a psychological present, why entropy increases, or whether the universe has a beginning. Those questions are different questions. A bad book about time welds them together because they share a noun.

I want to do the opposite.

The JILA result belongs first to general relativity and metrology. Its force comes from not asking it to solve more than that. Two portions of an atomic sample at different gravitational potentials accumulate measurably different proper time. Precision clocks can resolve the effect. This is established physics.

From there a modest conclusion follows. There is no operational need for a single universal rate behind those clocks. Relativity tells you how to relate local measurements. The theory does not repair them back into Newton’s absolute time.

That is already strange enough.

Newton had imagined absolute, true, and mathematical time flowing equably without relation to anything external. It was an extraordinarily productive idealization. It gave the equations a common parameter. It fit the human experience of a world in which travel speeds are tiny compared with light and gravitational potential differences are usually beneath notice. It made simultaneity feel innocent.

Einstein did not discover that clocks were badly synchronized. He discovered that the demand for a universal synchronization had been smuggled into the structure of mechanics.

Modern clocks let us watch the consequences move from blackboard to bench.

Consider what the strontium atoms are doing in that millimeter cloud. The experiment does not require anyone to believe that the top atom feels older. No consciousness is involved. No human observer causes the rate difference. No philosophical interpretation changes the predicted frequency shift. The claim is brutally physical. Put clocks at different gravitational potentials, control the relevant variables, compare them, and the accumulated phases differ in the way the spacetime metric says they should.

This is why “time is subjective” is such a poor summary of relativity.

Subjective suggests preference. Proper time is not preference. If two astronauts follow different trajectories and reunite, their clocks can show different elapsed durations. They can argue about politics on the flight home; they cannot vote the readings into agreement. The paths matter.

Nor is “everything is relative” useful. Relativity is full of invariants. Proper time along a timelike worldline is one. The speed of light in vacuum is another central structure. The theory does not relax physics into opinion. It replaces certain absolute quantities with relationships whose transformation rules are exact.

A clock is powerful because it can carry one of those invariants.

The clock on the mountain therefore gives us two lessons that look contradictory until we stop expecting a master clock.

First, timekeeping can be astonishingly objective. Independent laboratories can build different optical clocks based on different atoms and compare frequency ratios to fantastic precision. They can test general relativity, constrain possible variation of fundamental constants, search for dark matter signatures, improve geodesy, and contribute toward a future redefinition of the SI second. The measurements are not loose metaphors.

Second, objective timekeeping does not require one universal time.

It requires explicit relationships among local clocks.

That second lesson is harder because our institutions hide their achievement. Your phone gives you a timestamp without telling you about satellite ephemerides, atomic standards, cable delays, leap-second tables, gravitational potential conventions, or the hierarchy of clocks that made the timestamp possible. The interface presents time as ambient. The infrastructure knows better.

This is a general feature of successful coordination systems. Once the difficult agreement works, the user experiences it as nature.

A meter seems like length itself, not a maintained realization of a unit. A currency price seems like value itself, not an agreement inside a market and legal system. A street address seems like a property of the building, not a constructed indexing scheme. A second on your phone seems like time itself.

The danger in this book is to take that analogy too far. Nature is not a bureaucracy. Relativistic proper time is not socially constructed because UTC is institutionally constructed. The gravitational redshift would remain if the BIPM were dissolved. I will keep returning to this boundary because the interesting claim sits exactly on it.

The universe gives us local physical processes.

We decide which ones deserve to become references for everybody else.

The distinction can be seen in the coming redefinition of the second. Since 1967 the SI second has been defined using the hyperfine transition frequency of cesium-133. Optical frequency standards based on atoms and ions such as strontium, ytterbium, aluminum, and others have surpassed the best cesium fountain clocks in important measures of accuracy and stability. The international timekeeping community has therefore been working toward a possible optical redefinition. As of the 2026 decision process, the BIPM roadmap anticipates that ratification could occur in 2030 or later, depending on whether technical criteria are met.

Nothing about the universe changed when cesium was chosen. Nothing fundamental will change if an optical transition replaces it.

The second is not becoming truer because a better atom has been discovered. We are choosing a more capable physical anchor for a human unit while maintaining continuity with the old one.

That may sound deflationary. I think it is the opposite. There is something grander in a civilization that can admit the convention and still make it precise.

A childish idea of objectivity says the standard must be independent of us. A mature one asks whether different people can realize it, compare results, expose errors, and maintain a coherent mapping from the standard to the phenomena they care about. We do not need the universe to print “one second” on an atom. We need a reproducible process whose relation to our other measurements can be made exact enough for the work.

At high precision, that work includes the fact that the clock itself sits somewhere.

The clock has to know the mountain.

Or, more precisely, the people operating it have to know enough about the clock’s gravitational environment to interpret what it is doing. The instrument cannot be separated from spacetime and still be a relativistic clock. Its locality is not a flaw we eliminate. It is part of the quantity.

This chapter began with a millimeter because a millimeter makes the abstract problem hard to evade. We can tolerate relativity when it belongs to black holes and spacecraft. Those are special places. A laboratory table feels ordinary enough that a universal present ought to fit on it.

It does, approximately.

Approximation is one of civilization’s greatest tools and one of philosophy’s recurring traps. We use a single time coordinate across a building because the correction is usually irrelevant. Then we mistake the convenience of the coordinate for evidence that the underlying world contains the absolute thing we named.

Better clocks withdraw that permission.

They force the approximation to announce itself.

A century ago the argument over time could be staged with trains, light signals, and thought experiments. Today a cloud of cold atoms can make the same point across the thickness of a pencil tip. The distance has shrunk. The conceptual gap has not.

There is no master clock in the room.

There are clocks, paths, fields, comparisons, and a theory precise enough to relate them.

We will spend the rest of the book learning how much follows from taking that sentence seriously.
