# The Equation That Refuses to Move

The most notorious equation in quantum cosmology looks as though someone forgot time.

That is close enough to begin and wrong enough to be useful.

In canonical general relativity, one can rewrite Einstein’s theory in a form that resembles Hamiltonian mechanics: split spacetime into spatial slices, describe the geometry and its conjugate momentum on each slice, and impose constraints that encode the theory’s gauge symmetry. The lapse and shift describe how one moves from one slicing to another. Coordinates help organize the calculation, but coordinate labels are not themselves observables.

Quantize this structure in the most direct Wheeler-DeWitt style and the Hamiltonian constraint becomes an operator condition on a wave functional of geometry and matter. Schematically:

H Ψ = 0.

No external time derivative appears.

The universe looks stationary.

This is the famous “frozen formalism.” The phrase is so vivid that it can freeze the reader’s thinking too. A stationary physical state in ordinary quantum mechanics is familiar: an energy eigenstate acquires only an overall phase and probabilities for time-independent observables do not change. But the Wheeler-DeWitt setting is not ordinary quantum mechanics with the clock accidentally set to zero. The vanishing Hamiltonian arises from the constraint structure of a generally covariant theory. Coordinate time evolution is tied to gauge.

The problem is not simply that the universe stopped.

The problem is that the usual mathematical distinction between changing state and advancing time has been rearranged.

To understand why, return to general relativity before quantization. A spacetime can be sliced into three-dimensional spatial hypersurfaces in many ways. Different foliations can describe the same underlying spacetime. The coordinate label t attached to the slices is not automatically a physical clock. If we take that arbitrary coordinate and then quantize as though it were Newton’s external time, we risk giving physical meaning to a choice of labeling.

General covariance has sent the bill.

This is sometimes described through the “problem of observables.” In a diffeomorphism-invariant theory, quantities evaluated at a coordinate point are generally not gauge-invariant observables merely because the coordinate has a name. Physical predictions often need relational specification: the value of one field when another field has a particular value, the distance between physical objects, the reading of a clock when an event occurs.

Relational observables are not philosophical garnish.

They are how you avoid measuring a coordinate system.

In classical general relativity, one can often choose convenient coordinates and calculate without losing sleep. The geometry has a clear spacetime interpretation. In canonical quantum gravity, the constraints become operator equations and the construction of physical observables becomes much harder. Different choices of internal time can lead to inequivalent-looking descriptions. A variable that serves as a good clock in one region of configuration space may stop being monotonic elsewhere. Quantum fluctuations can make the clock uncertain. The “multiple-choice problem,” in Kuchař’s terminology, asks in part whether different choices of internal time can lead to different quantum theories.

The master clock does not merely disappear.

It fragments into candidate clocks.

This is the point where a popular book is tempted to declare victory for relationalism. That would be premature. The problem of time has been studied for decades precisely because no universally accepted solution trivializes these difficulties. Loop quantum gravity, canonical quantum cosmology, string-theoretic approaches, causal sets, causal dynamical triangulations, spin foams, group field theory, shape dynamics, holography, and other programs handle temporal structure in different ways. Some do not frame the issue through the Wheeler-DeWitt equation at all.

The equation is a doorway, not the whole building.

Still, it exposes an unavoidable tension. Quantum theory wants to say how states change. General relativity refuses to make arbitrary coordinate change into physical change. A quantum theory of spacetime must define evolution in a way that respects that refusal.

One strategy is deparametrization.

Find a physical variable that can serve as a clock. Solve the constraint in terms of that variable and describe other degrees of freedom as evolving relative to it. Matter fields can sometimes play this role. In cosmological models, a scalar field may be monotonic over a regime and provide an internal time. In loop quantum cosmology, for example, a scalar field is often used as a relational clock to describe the evolution of geometric quantities such as volume.

This can yield perfectly ordinary-looking dynamics in relational form.

When the scalar field reads φ, the probability distribution for volume has such-and-such form. As φ changes, the geometry changes relative to it. No external universal clock is required inside the model.

But the choice carries assumptions.

A useful clock variable must vary in a sufficiently regular way. If it turns around, becomes strongly quantum, or interacts significantly with the system, the interpretation can become complicated. In a full quantum-gravity theory, there may be no single physical degree of freedom that behaves as a good global clock everywhere.

This is not surprising if relativity has taught us anything.

Why should the universe contain one variable obliged to imitate Newtonian time for all other variables?

The insistence that it must may be the residual intuition under examination.

A different strategy is to focus on correlations without trying to recover a preferred clock globally. Carlo Rovelli’s “partial observables” framework, for example, distinguishes quantities that can be measured from complete predictions about correlations among them. One can ask for the probability of one reading given another. The theory concerns relationships among observable quantities rather than evolution against an external parameter.

Again the slogan is easier than the mathematics.

Relational predictions must reproduce the excellent temporal predictions of ordinary quantum mechanics in regimes where an external laboratory clock works. They must produce a semiclassical spacetime with approximately local proper time. They must handle causality, unitarity where appropriate, and the emergence of familiar dynamics. A theory that merely says “everything is correlation” has explained nothing until it tells you which correlations and with what probabilities.

The frozen equation is therefore a quality-control device for metaphysics.

It forces every grand claim to become operational.

If time is emergent, from what variables and in what approximation does it emerge?

If change is relational, what plays the clock and when does it fail?

If the universe is timeless, why do internal observers recover a Schrödinger equation with a time parameter so accurately?

If there is a preferred fundamental time after all, how does that structure coexist with general covariance and relativistic tests?

These are research questions, not rhetorical opportunities.

There is another reason to take the frozen formalism seriously without taking it literally. Physics already contains descriptions in which motion is encoded in a static object.

The classical action principle assigns an action to an entire path and finds the physically realized trajectories through a variational condition. The four-dimensional spacetime of relativity can be treated as a complete geometric structure rather than a three-dimensional world being dynamically generated one present slice at a time. In statistical mechanics, an equilibrium ensemble is a static probability distribution even though individual microstates contain dynamics. A Feynman path integral can sum over histories as a whole.

A representation does not have to move in order to represent motion.

This observation prevents one crude mistake but not the hard problem. The Wheeler-DeWitt equation still has to recover the temporal structure of quantum experiments. It is not enough to say the whole history “is there.” An internal observer sees records, performs interventions, predicts future outcomes, and updates clocks. The theory has to explain why the relational structure supports that behavior.

Semiclassical approximations offer one route.

When gravitational degrees of freedom behave approximately classically, one can sometimes separate “heavy” geometric variables from “light” matter variables and recover an approximate Schrödinger equation in which an emergent time parameter is constructed from the classical gravitational background. The mathematics resembles Born-Oppenheimer reasoning in molecular physics: slow heavy nuclei provide an approximate background for fast electronic degrees of freedom, except the quantum-gravity version is conceptually more delicate.

The familiar external time can reappear as an approximation.

This is a powerful possibility because it explains why ordinary quantum mechanics works so well without making its time parameter fundamental. The laboratory clock would be like pressure in thermodynamics: perfectly real and useful at its level, even if the microscopic theory describes different variables underneath.

But “emergent” does not mean fake.

Temperature is emergent from statistical behavior of microscopic degrees of freedom. Touch a hot stove and the burn is not an illusion. Sound is emergent from collective molecular motion; it can break glass. If time in some quantum-gravity framework is emergent, the everyday elapsed time measured by clocks can remain objectively robust.

This analogy also warns us about domain.

Temperature is not defined the same way for every microscopic nonequilibrium configuration. Hydrodynamic variables break down at scales where continuum approximations fail. An emergent time may likewise have regimes where no single classical temporal parameter exists.

Quantum cosmology could then resemble a material near a phase transition: the variable we use macroscopically ceases to be the right coordinate at the microscopic boundary.

This possibility is one reason “before the Big Bang” can be a malformed question in some models. If classical spacetime and its temporal parameter emerge only in a certain regime, asking what happened at an earlier value of that same parameter may be like asking what lies one kilometer north of the North Pole. In other models, a pre-Big-Bang phase, bounce, or extension does make sense. The theory decides; language alone does not.

No one gets to solve cosmology with grammar.

The frozen formalism also changes the meaning of prediction.

In ordinary physics, we specify an initial state at t0 and evolve it to t1. In a constrained timeless formalism, prediction may take a conditional form: given that clock variable C has value c1 and system variables have distribution P1, what is the probability of finding system value s2 when C reads c2? The temporal ordering is internal to the correlation structure.

This is not as alien as it sounds.

A geologist does something similar with strata. There is no cosmic clock label written in a rock layer. The layer’s age is inferred through relationships: radiometric decay, fossil assemblages, stratigraphic position, magnetic reversals, orbital cycles. The history is reconstructed from correlated records. The analogy is only epistemic; geology still assumes physical time. But it helps us imagine prediction and reconstruction organized around internal records rather than an external timestamp.

A universe can contain clocks without being embedded in a larger clock.

That sentence is the central intuition.

It may turn out incomplete. A final theory could have a fundamental causal order, discrete temporal structure, boundary time, or another entity deserving the name time. The point is not to ban fundamental time. It is to stop assuming Newton’s version before the theory earns it.

One of the best disciplines in frontier physics is to ask which part of ordinary structure is input and which part is output.

In Newtonian mechanics, time is input.

In special relativity, the Minkowski metric and causal structure are input while elapsed proper time depends on paths.

In general relativity, the metric itself is dynamical output from field equations given matter-energy and boundary conditions.

In quantum gravity, perhaps classical spacetime and its temporal structure are output from a deeper quantum description.

The hierarchy is not established fact all the way down. It is the question.

That question has experimental consequences, though they are difficult. Quantum gravity is notoriously hard to probe because the Planck scale is remote from ordinary laboratory energies. But time is one of the places precision may help. Atomic clocks can search for violations of local position invariance, variation of fundamental constants, ultralight dark matter, and other new physics. Matter-wave interferometers can test aspects of quantum mechanics and gravity. Proposed quantum-clock experiments aim to put internal clock states into regimes where proper-time differences become coherent quantum information.

None has yet told us which quantum-gravity solution is right.

They are making the clock more quantum.

That alone is valuable because every theoretical account of relational time eventually has to meet a physical clock.

The equation H Ψ = 0 can remain on the blackboard indefinitely. The world will not grant it meaning until we know how an observer inside Ψ reads 12:03 and predicts 12:04.

This is where Page and Wootters become more than a phrase.

They built a model in which the joint state can be stationary while a subsystem evolves conditionally relative to a clock subsystem. The clock is not outside the universe. It is part of the quantum correlations.

The equation refuses to move.

The correlations do not.
