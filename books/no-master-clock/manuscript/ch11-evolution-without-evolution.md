# Evolution Without Evolution

In 1983 Don Page and William Wootters published a paper with one of those titles physics occasionally permits itself after the algebra has been done: “Evolution without evolution.”

The phrase invites abuse.

It sounds like proof that nothing happens. It sounds like a loophole through which a stationary universe can be smuggled into mysticism. It sounds like one more excuse to tell a reader that time is an illusion and then sell them a meditation app.

The actual idea is narrower and more interesting.

Suppose a closed quantum system is divided conceptually into two parts. One part will serve as a clock. The other is the system whose dynamics we care about. The combined state can satisfy a stationary constraint. Yet when we condition on a particular clock reading, the state of the other subsystem can vary with that reading in exactly the way ordinary quantum evolution requires under suitable assumptions.

Globally stationary.

Relationally changing.

The trick is not that motion is hidden in a secret external time. The clock reading is an internal quantum variable correlated with the system.

A stripped-down analogy helps. Imagine a box containing two synchronized mechanical wheels. The first has numbers painted around its rim. The second carries a pattern. You take one photograph of the entire device after removing every external timestamp. If the photograph somehow contained a superposition of all joint configurations, you could ask a conditional question: when the first wheel reads 3, what configuration is the second wheel in? When it reads 4, what configuration? The sequence of correlations can encode a dynamic relationship even if the mathematical object containing all correlations is not itself evolving relative to a separate clock.

The quantum construction is not a photograph. The analogy only points at conditionality.

In the Page-Wootters mechanism, the clock has states associated with different readings. The joint clock-system state is entangled in a way that correlates each clock reading with the corresponding system state. To an observer who uses the clock, the system evolves. From the perspective of the global constraint, the total state can be stationary.

Entanglement becomes temporal bookkeeping.

This does not mean entanglement “creates time” in the sweeping sense often attached to the proposal. If the clock and system are not appropriately correlated, you do not automatically get the Schrödinger equation. A clock needs suitable states and dynamics. The construction has idealizations. Finite-dimensional clocks, imperfect resolution, periodicity, clock-system interaction, and the treatment of constraints all generate technical questions.

The literature after 1983 is partly the history of making the idea less ideal.

Later work showed how relational quantum dynamics can be formulated more systematically and how Page-Wootters-type constructions connect to quantum reference frames and constrained systems. Experiments have simulated aspects of relational time using entangled quantum systems, but these demonstrations do not establish that the universe fundamentally uses the Page-Wootters mechanism. They show that the conditional mathematics can be physically instantiated in controlled systems.

This distinction is the difference between a model and an ontology.

A model can work without being the final furniture of reality.

What Page and Wootters give us is a proof of concept against one intuition: a globally stationary quantum description need not imply the absence of internal dynamical experience.

That intuition matters because the Wheeler-DeWitt equation had made timelessness look fatal. If Ψ does not depend on an external t, where does motion come from? Page and Wootters answer: look at correlations between parts of Ψ.

The clock does not stand outside the state.

It is a subsystem.

This changes the status of time in a way that resembles what relativity did to simultaneity. Relativity did not destroy time measurement; it localized it to worldlines and frames. Page-Wootters does not destroy dynamics; it relocates the temporal parameter into relations with a physical clock.

The resemblance should not be mistaken for identity. Special relativity is experimentally established. The Page-Wootters mechanism is one framework for relational quantum dynamics. But the philosophical direction is continuous: stop asking the reference structure to live nowhere.

Make the clock pay rent inside the universe.

Once you do, imperfections become unavoidable.

An ideal clock in many theoretical treatments has an unbounded Hamiltonian or an infinite set of perfectly distinguishable time states. Real clocks have finite energy, finite dimension or effective state space, decoherence, noise, and limited resolution. Quantum theory imposes trade-offs on how accurately a system can encode time. The clock can disturb or become entangled with the system. If the total system is constrained, clock-system interaction can modify the effective evolution.

Time becomes resource-dependent.

That phrase needs care. It does not mean a poor person literally receives less relativistic proper time because they cannot afford a better clock. It means the *resolution with which a physical system can serve as a temporal reference* depends on physical resources and noise.

A perfect external t costs nothing in a textbook.

A real clock costs energy, apparatus, isolation, calibration, and entropy production.

This is one reason quantum-information thinking has become useful in foundational time research. A clock carries information about temporal relationships. Quantum Fisher information can quantify sensitivity of a state to time translations generated by a Hamiltonian. Energy variance relates to how quickly a state can become distinguishable from itself. Bounds such as Mandelstam-Tamm and Margolus-Levitin connect energy resources with dynamical speed limits in different ways.

A clock that never changes cannot distinguish moments.

A clock that changes too quickly but repeats ambiguously may not distinguish *which* moment.

Good timekeeping requires structured distinguishability.

This is already familiar in ordinary clocks. A second hand alone repeats every minute. You need minute and hour information to disambiguate longer intervals. An oscillator has phase ambiguity after complete cycles unless a counter records cycles. Atomic clocks therefore involve not just a transition frequency but control systems, phase comparisons, counters, and timescale algorithms.

Quantum clocks inherit the same logic with quantum limitations.

A finite clock can have a finite range before recurrence. Its “ticks” may not be perfectly orthogonal. Measurement can add disturbance. The more we insist on treating the reference as physical, the more the clean classical parameter dissolves into engineering.

This is not a defect of relational time.

It may be its most honest feature.

A fundamental theory should not be required to provide an infinitely precise clock that no physical universe could contain.

The problem becomes what kind of approximate time can emerge from imperfect subsystems and why the approximation is so spectacularly good for us.

Here scale helps. The clocks used in laboratory quantum mechanics are enormous relative to the quantum systems they time. A laser and control electronics contain huge numbers of degrees of freedom. Environmental decoherence stabilizes classical records. The clock’s quantum uncertainty is negligible for most experiments. Treating t as a classical external parameter is not metaphysical laziness; it is an effective theory justified by scale separation.

The relational account becomes interesting at the boundary where scale separation fails.

Quantum gravity is one such boundary by definition. A universe-wide clock cannot be external to the universe. A clock near the Planck regime may gravitationally affect what it times. A clock in quantum superposition may define a reference frame with no single classical trajectory. At that point the approximation of one sharp background time may fail.

Page-Wootters gives us one language for asking what remains.

Correlations remain.

If clock state |t⟩ is correlated with system state |ψ(t)⟩, an internal observer reading the clock sees the system at |ψ(t)⟩. The full entangled state contains the whole correlation structure. The mathematical details vary by formulation, but the essential move is conditional probability.

This has a curious consequence for the notion of a present.

There is no need for one global “now” shared by every subsystem. A clock reading defines a relational slice for the system correlated with it. Another clock may define another description. If the clocks are good and semiclassical, the descriptions agree in their overlap according to familiar physics. If the clocks are genuinely quantum, transformations between their perspectives can become nontrivial.

The next frontier is not merely “what time is it?”

It is “relative to which quantum clock?”

Quantum reference-frame research takes this seriously. A reference frame need not be a classical coordinate grid outside the quantum state. It can be a physical quantum system. Changing reference frames then becomes a transformation between relational descriptions. Position, momentum, superposition, entanglement, and potentially temporal localization can look different relative to different quantum systems while operational predictions remain consistent.

This is relativity with sharper teeth.

Again, do not confuse analogy with theorem. Einstein’s principle of relativity and modern quantum-reference-frame formalisms are not one idea. But they share an intellectual refusal: do not grant the observer’s ruler or clock a classical exemption merely because calculations are easier that way.

Put the reference into physics.

The payoff can be conceptual cleanliness. Consider a massive object in a quantum superposition of positions. If gravity responds quantum mechanically, the spacetime metric may be indefinite in a way that makes the localization and causal relation of events reference-dependent. Work by Magdalena Zych, Fabio Costa, Časlav Brukner, Flaminia Giacomini and collaborators has explored quantum clocks and quantum reference frames in such regimes. Some results show that event localization can be relative to the chosen quantum clock frame while local physics retains familiar unitary form.

These are theoretical constructions at the quantum-gravity interface, not experimental confirmation of indefinite spacetime.

Their value is to force precision into a question that popular language mangles.

If a clock is in quantum superposition, what is “the time shown by the clock” before a measurement or relational conditioning? If two clocks become gravitationally entangled, can one still speak of a single classical temporal relation? If an event is sharply localized relative to one quantum reference system and delocalized relative to another, which description carries operational meaning?

A master clock cannot answer because the premise has removed it.

This is where the human intuition rebels.

We experience clocks as classical objects. A watch has one reading. A satellite follows one trajectory to excellent approximation. A national timescale is a sequence of published values. Our entire cognitive system evolved in a regime where macroscopic temporal references decohere and gravitational quantum effects are negligible.

We should not be surprised that fundamental regimes feel linguistically wrong.

The mistake is to use that discomfort as evidence for a mystical alternative.

A quantum clock is not consciousness. It is a system with states that can serve as a temporal reference. The relational mechanism does not require a mind to look at it. Conditional probabilities can be defined physically. An automated detector can correlate an event with a clock reading without anyone being awake.

Humans arrive later.

We decide which physical clock states to elevate into a public standard.

That is a different kind of relation.

The Page-Wootters idea therefore creates a useful two-level picture. At the foundational level, time may be encoded through correlations among physical subsystems rather than supplied as a universal external parameter. At the civilizational level, shared time is encoded through comparisons among physical clocks rather than supplied as a universal social parameter.

The parallel is seductive.

Keep it bounded.

UTC is not a quantum constraint state. BIPM algorithms are not the Wheeler-DeWitt equation. A fiber link between laboratories does not instantiate the Page-Wootters mechanism in any relevant technical sense. The analogy is structural only: reference by relation rather than by a thing outside the system.

That is enough.

The analogy explains why the book keeps returning to synchronization. If there is no master clock at either the relativistic or quantum boundary, what survives is the ability to map one local reference into another.

The map can be exact in theory and uncertain in practice.

The map can fail.

That is not weakness. It is where empirical content enters.

Suppose two candidate quantum clocks produce different conditional dynamics. The theory must say why and under what transformation their descriptions agree. Suppose a finite clock causes corrections to ideal Schrödinger evolution. Those corrections can in principle be quantified. Suppose gravitational interaction between clock and system modifies time localization. The model should predict an effect.

A relational theory earns reality by producing relationships that can be tested.

This is much healthier than asking whether time “exists.”

Existence questions often dissolve into definitions. Operational questions generate experiments.

Does a clock’s proper-time difference reduce interference visibility under the predicted conditions?

Can a quantum reference-frame transformation recover consistent local physics?

What corrections appear when an internal clock is finite?

Can a semiclassical clock variable reproduce ordinary unitary dynamics over the regime we observe?

These are hard questions with possible wrong answers.

The frontier should contain wrong answers.

Otherwise it is theology.

Page and Wootters offered a strange but disciplined possibility: the whole state need not evolve with respect to an external time in order for subsystems to observe one another evolving relative to internal clocks.

The universe does not need a wristwatch outside itself.

It needs enough correlation inside.
