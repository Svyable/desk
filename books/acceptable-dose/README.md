# Acceptable Dose

## Uranium, Radon, and the Men Asked to Prove the Mine Was Dangerous

**Sven Hardy Benson**

The American uranium boom contains an unusually sharp documentary problem. The danger was not discovered only after the miners were gone. European mining history had already connected certain mines with extraordinary lung-cancer mortality; the U.S. Public Health Service began studying Colorado Plateau uranium miners in 1950 because radon was already suspected; yet the men being measured were not simply given the knowledge in the form we would now consider informed occupational warning. The research program and the labor system occupied the same tunnels.

This book asks a narrower and harder question than “who knew?” It reconstructs what different institutions knew, when they knew it, what they measured, what they told miners, what they withheld, and how a population of Navajo workers became both labor force and epidemiological evidence for the nuclear age.

It does **not** claim that every miner was deliberately used as an experimental subject, that uranium itself explains every later illness, or that a single agency possessed one complete secret. Radon progeny, silica, diesel exhaust, smoking, mine ventilation, federal procurement, private mine ownership, tribal sovereignty, language, epidemiological uncertainty and compensation all have separate histories. The point is to recover those histories before compressing them into accusation.

## Reader promise

Follow the paper trail from nineteenth-century European miner disease through the Atomic Energy Commission procurement boom, the Public Health Service cohort, Navajo mines, ventilation disputes, lung-cancer findings, delayed standards, litigation and the Radiation Exposure Compensation Act. At every stage the book distinguishes hazard knowledge from causal proof, population evidence from individual causation, research participation from informed consent, and federal demand for uranium from day-to-day mine operation.

## Evidence discipline

The working manuscript uses four explicit classes: **Class 1 — verified/declassified/documented facts; Class 2 — credible but incomplete reporting/testimony; Class 3 — disputed or insufficiently corroborated allegations; Class 4 — speculative synthesis/open conjecture.** Claims can move between classes only when the source trail earns it.
