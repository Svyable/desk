# Work After Expertise

Expertise used to arrive attached to a person.

That fact organized entire economies.

If you wanted legal analysis, you hired a lawyer.

If you wanted a diagnosis, you saw a clinician.

If you wanted software, you found a programmer.

If you wanted a financial model, a technical drawing, a translation, a lesson plan, a marketing campaign, a research summary, or a first pass at a contract, you located someone who had spent years learning how to produce it.

The knowledge and the worker came bundled together.

That bundle is beginning to come apart.

Not completely.

Not evenly.

Not safely in every domain.

But enough to change the bargaining structure around expertise.

A machine can now produce useful fragments of work that previously required a trained person to begin.

It can draft.

Explain.

Classify.

Summarize.

Translate.

Search.

Write code.

Propose alternatives.

Transform formats.

Review documents.

Generate hypotheses.

Build first-pass analyses.

None of those verbs proves that an occupation disappears.

They prove something narrower and more important.

Parts of expertise can become callable.

That is an industrial change because occupations are bundles of tasks, and the economic value of a bundle changes when some tasks become cheaper.

The International Labour Organization's 2025 global index estimates that one in four workers is employed in an occupation with some degree of exposure to generative AI.

The same work concludes that transformation is more likely than outright redundancy for most affected jobs because human input remains necessary.

Both halves of that finding matter.

Exposure is broad.

Replacement is not automatic.

The public debate often throws away one half.

Optimists point to complementarity and assume employment will be fine.

Pessimists point to exposure and assume employment will collapse.

The task structure says something more complicated.

A job can survive while becoming a different job.

A title can remain while the valuable part moves.

The number of workers can fall without reaching zero.

The number of workers can rise even as the work becomes more automated if lower costs create more demand.

A profession can become easier to enter while becoming harder to advance within.

Productivity can rise while bargaining power falls.

Average wages can rise while the distribution of wages widens.

Technology does not give one labor-market answer.

It changes the parameters of the negotiation.

A useful piece of evidence comes from customer support.

In a study of 5,179 workers, Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li, and Lindsey Raymond examined the rollout of a generative-AI assistant.

Access to the system increased issues resolved per hour by fourteen percent on average.

The gains were much larger for novice and lower-skilled workers: thirty-four percent.

Experienced, high-skilled workers saw much smaller gains.

The authors found evidence consistent with the system diffusing some of the best practices of more capable workers to less experienced workers.

That is a remarkable pattern.

The machine did not simply replace workers.

It compressed part of the experience curve.

What had once been learned through time, repetition, coaching, observation, and accumulated examples became partially available through the tool.

This is one of the most important labor mechanisms in the Fourth Industrial Revolution.

The machine can carry fragments of institutional memory.

That can be good for the novice.

It can also undermine the economic scarcity of being experienced.

Imagine a workplace where senior employees have spent years accumulating tacit knowledge.

They know which customer complaint signals a larger problem.

They know which clause in a contract is usually negotiable.

They know which software failure looks harmless but becomes expensive later.

They know which supplier promise deserves skepticism.

They know how to phrase the difficult email.

They know what the formal process says and what actually works.

If a model trained or conditioned on the organization's records can surface some of those patterns, the organization may become less dependent on any one person's memory.

That can make the company more resilient.

It can also shift power from the worker to the system owner.

Expertise has always had at least two economic roles.

It produces good decisions.

It also gives the person holding it bargaining power because the knowledge cannot easily be separated from them.

Machine intelligence can weaken the second role before it eliminates the first.

This is why productivity statistics alone will not tell us whether the transition is good for workers.

A worker can become twenty percent more productive and receive none of the gain.

A company can use AI to make every employee more capable and then decide it needs fewer employees.

A professional can use a model to serve more clients and capture higher income.

A firm can use the same model to standardize professional work and compress fees.

The technology is compatible with all four outcomes.

Ownership and competition matter.

So does the scarcity of the human complement.

What remains scarce after the model arrives?

Sometimes it is judgment.

Sometimes trust.

Sometimes a license.

Sometimes legal accountability.

Sometimes access to the customer.

Sometimes physical skill.

Sometimes the ability to persuade.

Sometimes taste.

Sometimes the willingness to take responsibility when the answer is uncertain.

Sometimes deep context that never made it into the data.

Sometimes nothing defensible at all.

That last possibility is uncomfortable but necessary.

Not every professional task contains a hidden reserve of uniquely human value.

Some work is expensive because qualified people have been the only available production method.

If a machine can produce an acceptable version more cheaply, the market may stop paying a premium for the old method.

Industrial revolutions have always done this.

A hand process can contain craftsmanship and still lose economic ground to mechanization.

A local clerk can know the ledger intimately and still be displaced by software.

A telephone operator can be skilled and still become unnecessary when switching changes.

The dignity of the worker does not guarantee the permanence of the task.

The opposite mistake is equally serious.

A task becoming automatable does not make the occupation unnecessary.

Work is bundled because real situations are messy.

A nurse does not only retrieve medical information.

A lawyer does not only generate legal prose.

An engineer does not only perform calculations.

A teacher does not only explain concepts.

A manager does not only produce plans.

A salesperson does not only write messages.

Occupations combine knowledge with responsibility, coordination, relationships, physical presence, institutional authority, and adaptation to situations that do not arrive in standardized form.

This is why task exposure is a more useful starting point than job apocalypse.

The bundle can be rearranged.

The human may perform fewer components while remaining essential to the whole.

But rearrangement creates a second-order problem.

How does a person become an expert if the machine performs the junior work?

Professional ladders are full of tasks that are economically modest but educationally important.

A junior lawyer reads documents before arguing major cases.

A young analyst builds spreadsheets before making capital-allocation decisions.

A medical trainee encounters routine cases before managing rare complications.

A programmer fixes bugs and writes ordinary features before designing critical systems.

A journalist covers routine events before leading investigations.

An architect draws and revises before directing complex projects.

The beginner contributes less partly because the work is training.

Organizations tolerate inefficiency because the novice is becoming the future expert.

If machine assistance removes too much of that work, firms can improve short-term productivity while damaging their own talent pipeline.

This is the apprenticeship paradox.

The tasks easiest to automate may be the tasks through which humans learn the context needed to supervise automation later.

An organization can end up with fewer beginners and eventually too few experts.

That problem does not appear in a benchmark.

A model can produce excellent code today without answering who will understand the system ten years from now.

A drafting tool can make a junior lawyer faster without answering whether the junior learns to recognize the clause the tool got subtly wrong.

An AI assistant can make a clinician more efficient without answering how trainees develop diagnostic intuition if they rarely reason unaided.

Industrial systems have to reproduce their human capability as well as their output.

That is an educational function hidden inside work.

Earlier automation often attacked physical repetition.

This wave can attack cognitive repetition.

Both forms of repetition can be unpleasant.

Both can also be training.

The answer is not to preserve drudgery for sentimental reasons.

It is to redesign apprenticeship deliberately.

Simulation.

Rotations.

Supervised practice.

Mandatory unaided work in some contexts.

Review of machine errors.

Exercises that expose edge cases.

Responsibility that grows with demonstrated competence rather than years served.

If routine production no longer teaches the next generation automatically, institutions will need to teach on purpose.

This is another example of a general rule.

When technology makes an old function cheap, we discover which other functions had been bundled with it for free.

The office task produced output.

It also trained the worker.

The meeting transferred information.

It also built relationships.

The physical workplace coordinated labor.

It also created weak social ties.

The apprenticeship job produced inexpensive junior work.

It also created senior professionals.

Automation can remove the visible cost and accidentally remove the invisible benefit.

The labor transition will therefore be shaped by what organizations notice in time.

There is also a distinction between assistance and delegation.

A worker with an AI assistant remains the operator.

The machine proposes and the worker acts.

As agentic systems gain permissions, the machine can begin doing parts of the workflow itself.

It can send the message.

Update the record.

Schedule the appointment.

Issue the refund within a limit.

Run the test.

Open the pull request.

Generate and distribute the report.

Monitor the queue.

The labor impact changes when assistance crosses into action.

A tool that saves ten minutes per task may increase worker throughput.

A delegated system that absorbs the task end to end may reduce the number of workers required for that function.

This is why current employment evidence cannot be projected mechanically into the future.

The architecture is still changing.

The ILO's transformation conclusion is an evidence-based description of exposure under present capabilities and task structures.

It is not a law guaranteeing that every future agentic system complements labor.

Nor is an impressive agent demonstration proof that mass displacement is imminent.

Industrial adoption sits between those claims.

Cost matters.

Reliability matters.

Liability matters.

Integration matters.

Customer preference matters.

Regulation matters.

The quality of human review matters.

The speed of demand expansion matters.

The availability of complementary workers matters.

The labor market responds to the whole system.

Robotics extends the same argument into physical work.

People often divide jobs into “knowledge work” and “safe physical work,” as though the second category sits outside the revolution.

That boundary is too simple.

Physical work is harder to automate because the world is less standardized than a spreadsheet.

But where environments can be structured, robotics can already remove or transform tasks at scale.

Warehouses, factories, ports, mines, farms, laboratories, and hospitals contain different degrees of structure.

The relevant question is not whether a robot can do every household task.

It is whether enough valuable physical tasks can be standardized to change labor demand in important sectors.

At the same time, the growth of robots creates work.

Installation.

Maintenance.

Fleet management.

Safety engineering.

Integration.

Repair.

Process redesign.

Facilities work.

The revolution removes tasks and creates complements.

The composition is what changes.

Biotechnology creates another labor effect.

As design tools improve, some research work moves from manual search toward candidate evaluation.

Laboratory automation can shift technicians from repetitive handling toward system management and exception resolution.

Clinical workflows may gain machine-generated interpretations while retaining human responsibility for patient care.

Manufacturing advanced therapies can create specialized physical jobs even when the design layer becomes more computational.

The convergence therefore does not push all work in one direction.

It can make cognitive tasks more abundant while increasing demand for physical capacity around them.

This may create one of the stranger labor markets in modern history.

A person with a laptop gains access to capabilities once associated with a team.

At the same time, societies need electricians, construction workers, semiconductor technicians, laboratory operators, mechanics, nurses, grid engineers, and manufacturing specialists to make the machine economy real.

The glamour may be digital.

The bottlenecks may be physical.

This has implications for status.

Modern education and labor markets taught generations to associate advancement with moving away from manual work and toward symbolic work.

The more abstract the work, the higher the status often became.

AI challenges that hierarchy.

Some symbolic tasks are among the easiest for models to enter because they already exist as text, code, images, or structured data.

Many physical tasks remain protected not because they are intellectually simple but because reality is difficult to manipulate safely.

The market may gradually reprize kinds of competence that credential systems undervalued.

Not all of them.

But enough to disturb the old ladder.

A licensed electrician working on constrained physical infrastructure may be harder to substitute than a junior analyst producing standard presentations.

A nurse with legal responsibility, physical presence, and patient trust may have a different complementarity profile from a remote documentation role.

A technician who can keep an automated laboratory running may become more valuable as the laboratory becomes more automated.

This is not a romantic return to the trades.

Robotics will continue advancing.

It is an argument against assuming that “knowledge work” is automatically the safest place to be when knowledge itself becomes cheaper to access.

The deeper labor question is distribution.

Who captures the productivity gain?

Suppose a worker and model together produce twice as much.

One possibility is higher wages.

Another is lower prices.

Another is higher profits.

Another is more output with the same staff.

Another is the same output with fewer staff.

Another is a new market that did not exist because the service was previously too expensive.

All are economically plausible.

Institutions determine the split.

Competitive markets may pass gains to customers.

Scarce workers may capture some through wages.

Owners of proprietary models, data, distribution, or physical infrastructure may capture rents.

Labor law and bargaining institutions can influence the outcome.

Professional licensing can preserve scarcity, for good reasons or bad ones.

Entrepreneurs can use cheap expertise to challenge incumbents.

Governments can tax or subsidize the transition.

The technology expands the surplus.

Politics and market structure decide who receives it.

This is why arguments about “AI and jobs” are often secretly arguments about power.

Two societies can deploy similar technology and produce different labor outcomes.

One may use productivity gains to reduce hours, increase wages, and expand services.

Another may use them mainly to reduce headcount and concentrate ownership.

One company may give every worker a powerful assistant and invest in training.

Another may centralize the tool and deskill the workforce.

One profession may redesign entry-level work.

Another may let its apprenticeship pipeline collapse.

Industrial revolutions do not arrive with a predetermined social contract.

That contract is fought over after the machines appear.

There is a final human distinction the labor statistics cannot capture cleanly.

Expertise is not only a production function.

For many people, it is identity.

A person spends years becoming a lawyer, teacher, physician, designer, programmer, researcher, accountant, translator, or engineer.

The skill does not feel like a detachable unit of output.

It becomes part of the self.

When a machine can produce a competent version of the artifact, the threat can feel personal even if the job remains.

The worker is forced to ask which part of their expertise was valuable because it was scarce and which part remains valuable because judgment, responsibility, and human relationship still matter.

That is a difficult question.

It is also historically normal.

Industrialization has repeatedly separated identity from production method.

The hand weaver did not stop being skilled because the loom changed economics.

The typesetter did not become foolish because desktop publishing arrived.

The navigator did not lose all knowledge because GPS became ordinary.

A technology can reduce the market value of a capability without reducing the human achievement involved in acquiring it.

Societies handle this badly when they confuse economic redundancy with personal worth.

The book will return to that distinction at the end.

For now, the practical conclusion is narrower.

Expertise is not disappearing.

It is being decomposed.

Some parts become cheap and callable.

Some become more valuable because they complement the machine.

Some shift from production toward supervision.

Some remain embodied in licenses, relationships, responsibility, tacit context, or physical action.

Some may genuinely lose market value.

The people and institutions that adapt fastest will be those that identify the new scarce layer without pretending the old bundle will remain intact.

And no institution faces that problem more directly than education.

Work can tolerate a person using a machine to produce the right answer.

School has a harder question.

What if producing the answer was supposed to be the process by which the person learned?
