# The Things We Should Not Optimize

A system becomes dangerous when it gets very good at the wrong thing.

That sentence is easy to agree with because it sounds like a warning about machines.

It is also a warning about us.

A machine does not choose most of the objectives society gives it.

People do.

Companies do.

Governments do.

Markets do.

Institutions do.

Sometimes nobody chooses explicitly.

An objective emerges from the metric that is easiest to count.

Clicks.

Throughput.

Test scores.

Arrests.

Claims closed.

Units produced.

Cost per case.

Time on platform.

Revenue per user.

Probability of default.

Risk score.

Engagement.

Utilization.

The metric becomes a target because the system can act on it.

Then the target starts reshaping the world around the metric.

This problem predates artificial intelligence.

Bureaucracies optimized long before neural networks.

Factories optimized.

Armies optimized.

Financial markets optimized.

Schools optimized.

Hospitals optimized.

Industrial civilization is partly the story of converting vague goals into measurable processes and then improving them.

That has been extraordinarily useful.

Measurement made quality control possible.

Standards made products interoperable.

Accounting made large organizations legible.

Scheduling made complex production reliable.

Statistical medicine helped replace intuition with evidence.

Optimization is not the enemy.

It is one of civilization's great capabilities.

The Fourth Industrial Revolution changes the stakes because the cost of optimization is falling.

A capable model can search a larger decision space.

An agent can execute more of the chosen policy.

A robot can carry optimization into the physical world.

A biological system can become an object of deliberate intervention.

More intelligence means more objectives can be pursued effectively.

That makes objective choice more important.

When a system is weak, a bad objective may fail harmlessly.

When a system is powerful, a bad objective can succeed.

That is the uncomfortable version of technological progress.

Success itself can become the hazard.

Imagine a school that can predict with increasing accuracy which students are likely to score poorly.

That capability can be used to provide help.

It can also be used to lower expectations, route students away from difficult opportunities, or optimize institutional averages by concentrating resources on students closest to a threshold.

The prediction is not the policy.

The policy decides what the prediction means.

Imagine a hospital system that can optimize bed utilization.

High utilization looks efficient.

But a hospital with no spare capacity is brittle during a surge.

The metric rewards removing slack that resilience depends on.

Imagine a city that can optimize traffic flow.

The system may reduce travel time.

It may also route more vehicles through a neighborhood whose residents have little political influence.

The optimum has distributional consequences.

Imagine a company that can optimize worker productivity minute by minute.

The resulting operation may be efficient.

It may also remove every pause, informal conversation, learning moment, and act of mutual aid that makes the workplace humane and adaptive.

The machine can maximize the metric while consuming the institution that produced the metric.

This is why the phrase *human in the loop* is not enough.

A person can remain in the loop while the objective itself is wrong.

A manager can approve every recommendation generated by a system optimized around an inhumane target.

Human approval does not transform a bad objective into a good one.

The deeper requirement is human determination of ends.

UNESCO's Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence makes human dignity and human rights foundational values and states that AI systems should not displace ultimate human responsibility and accountability.

That language matters because rights are not simply additional objectives to place beside efficiency.

A right is often a constraint on optimization.

A person accused of a crime has procedural rights even if skipping them would improve throughput.

A worker has safety protections even if removing them would increase output.

A patient has consent rights even if compulsory participation would improve a dataset.

A citizen has privacy interests even if universal surveillance would improve prediction.

A voter retains equal political standing even if a model predicts that another person's vote is more informed.

Rights say there are things the system may not trade away merely because another outcome scores better.

That is a different architecture.

Optimization says find the best point in the permitted space.

Rights help define the permitted space.

But rights do not eliminate the hard part.

Rights can conflict.

Privacy can conflict with public safety.

Autonomy can conflict with protection from serious harm.

Equal treatment can conflict with efforts to correct unequal starting conditions.

A patient's right to choose can collide with a clinician's duty not to provide a treatment that is unsafe or futile.

A worker's interest in privacy can collide with another worker's interest in a safe workplace.

A community's ability to delay infrastructure can collide with another community's need for power, housing, transport, or jobs.

Calling something a right does not make the tradeoff disappear.

It changes the terms of the tradeoff.

The decision now requires justification strong enough to cross a protected boundary.

That is why rights need institutions around them.

Courts.

Procedures.

Standards of evidence.

Appeals.

Elected lawmaking.

Professional duties.

Public explanation.

The point is not that a human being possesses a moral intuition a machine could never approximate.

Human beings have defended slavery, torture, discrimination, corruption, censorship, and war with great confidence.

Human judgment is not automatically humane because a human supplied it.

Nor is a machine recommendation automatically dehumanizing because a model supplied it.

A statistical system can expose discriminatory patterns a human institution normalized for decades.

A model can make a public benefit easier to access.

Automation can remove arbitrary discretion from a process in which people were treated differently depending on which desk handled the case.

Decision support can reduce fatigue, inconsistency, and clerical error.

Sometimes the machine makes the human institution more humane.

That possibility matters because otherwise this chapter becomes nostalgia disguised as ethics.

The relevant question is not whether the final act contains a human hand.

It is whether the system preserves legitimate authority, contestability, responsibility, and the values the institution is supposed to serve.

A human signature at the end of a bad automated process is weak protection.

A well-designed automated process with clear limits, auditable evidence, meaningful appeal, and accountable human governance can be better than the discretionary system it replaced.

The moral standard has to be comparative.

Compared with what?

The old process may be slow, biased, inaccessible, humiliating, expensive, or corrupt.

The benchmark for a new system cannot be an imaginary human institution that never makes mistakes.

It has to be the real institution we have, including its errors.

This distinction becomes crucial as machine systems get better at finding tradeoffs humans would not have seen.

A powerful optimizer can discover that a small harm to one group produces a large measured benefit elsewhere.

A purely consequential system may take the trade.

A rights-based system may forbid it.

The constraint can look inefficient.

That is its purpose.

Civilization contains many valuable inefficiencies.

A jury is inefficient compared with a single decision-maker.

An appeal is inefficient compared with finality.

A secret ballot is inefficient compared with public voting that can be instantly verified by social pressure.

Academic freedom is inefficient if the only goal is producing research aligned with today's priorities.

Redundant infrastructure is inefficient during normal operations.

A park is inefficient land if the only metric is taxable floor area.

A childhood is inefficient if the only objective is labor-market preparation.

A friendship is inefficient if every interaction must produce measurable value.

A democracy is inefficient if the only objective is rapid coordination.

The word *inefficient* often hides an unstated objective.

The question is inefficient at what?

This is why an age of abundant machine intelligence may require a renewed defense of things that are valuable precisely because they are not optimized for output.

Play.

Rest.

Conversation without agenda.

Art that fails commercially.

Research without immediate application.

Care work whose value cannot be compressed into minutes per task.

Local institutions that are redundant from a national efficiency perspective but socially important to a place.

Human judgment that is slower than a model because it carries responsibility.

These activities are not anti-technology.

They are parts of life whose value is not exhausted by the output they produce.

This brings us to a critical distinction between instrumental and intrinsic goods.

An instrumental good is valuable because it helps produce something else.

A train is useful because it moves people.

A spreadsheet is useful because it organizes information.

A model is useful because it contributes cognition.

An intrinsic good is valuable at least partly in itself.

Love.

Dignity.

Freedom.

Belonging.

Beauty.

Play.

Understanding.

Participation in one's own life.

A society can use technology to support these goods.

It gets into trouble when it replaces them with proxies.

Consider care.

A care system can optimize medication adherence, appointment scheduling, fall detection, transportation, and documentation.

These are real benefits.

But a person receiving care may value being known by another person.

A perfectly efficient system that minimizes human contact could improve every administrative metric and make the lived experience worse.

The mistake would not be automation.

The mistake would be defining care as the completion of care-related tasks.

The same problem appears in education.

If the objective is correct answers, abundant machine tutoring and answer generation can improve performance rapidly.

If the objective includes becoming capable of reasoning independently, then difficulty has value.

The student sometimes needs to struggle.

An optimizer trained on immediate task completion may remove exactly the friction through which skill forms.

The system is helping the wrong temporal version of the student.

Today gets an answer.

Tomorrow inherits less capability.

This is why short-term optimization can consume long-term agency.

The pattern appears in work.

A company can use AI to eliminate the tasks junior employees perform poorly.

That can increase current quality.

Those same tasks may be how juniors become seniors.

The organization can optimize this quarter while eroding its future expert pool.

An apprenticeship contains productive inefficiency.

The novice is slower than the expert.

That is not a reason to prevent novices from working.

It is a reason to understand that capacity is reproduced through participation.

A fully optimized production system can fail to reproduce the people needed to supervise it.

The same is true in democracy.

A citizen deciding an issue is inefficient compared with asking an expert system for the statistically best policy.

Public deliberation is noisy.

People are misinformed.

Meetings take time.

Elections create discontinuity.

Compromise produces policies nobody considers ideal.

But democratic participation is not merely a mechanism for calculating policy.

It is also a mechanism for political equality and consent.

If an AI system could predict a better policy outcome, that would not give it democratic authority.

Accuracy and legitimacy are different dimensions.

This is the theme the machine-speed state made visible.

A state can know more and still owe reasons.

It can predict better and still owe due process.

It can administer faster and still owe an appeal.

It can optimize resource allocation and still confront disagreements about justice.

Machine intelligence can improve the means.

It does not settle the ends.

That sounds obvious until the means become persuasive enough to disguise the end.

A model can present a recommendation with quantitative confidence.

Numbers create authority.

The recommendation can feel like a fact rather than a choice.

This is one way politics gets hidden inside optimization.

A risk model may be statistically accurate.

The threshold at which the state acts is still a political choice.

A hiring model may predict performance.

The definition of performance is still an institutional choice.

A medical model may predict survival.

The decision about how to allocate scarce treatment remains an ethical choice.

A military model may rank targets.

The permissible use of force remains a legal and moral choice.

The system can clarify consequences.

It cannot make value conflict disappear.

More capable AI may actually make value conflict more visible.

When we could not achieve an objective, debating its exact definition had limited practical consequence.

When the objective becomes achievable, definition matters.

Suppose a city could dramatically reduce crime through pervasive biometric monitoring.

The old debate about privacy becomes concrete.

Suppose an employer could predict with high accuracy which workers are likely to quit.

Should it?

Suppose an insurer could infer a person's future health condition from data the person never understood would reveal it.

Should that inference be usable?

Suppose parents could use biological intervention to reduce a child's risk of severe disease.

What happens when the intervention moves from disease to enhancement?

Capability turns philosophy into policy.

This is why the Fourth Industrial Revolution changes humanity even if no machine becomes conscious.

The philosophical pressure comes from power, not personhood.

We have to decide what should be done with new abilities.

Consciousness is not required for a surveillance system to threaten freedom.

Personhood is not required for an optimization system to reshape work.

A robot does not need feelings to alter physical labor.

A gene-editing platform does not need intention to change the biological boundary of medicine.

The moral questions arrive because humans deploy systems that work.

This is also why “alignment” cannot be only a technical problem.

A machine can be aligned with the stated objective and society can still dislike the result.

Whose objective?

Chosen through what process?

Subject to which rights?

Revisable by whom?

With what appeal?

The technical question is whether the system does what we intend.

The political question is whether we should intend it.

The institutional question is who gets to decide.

These layers should not be collapsed.

A perfectly obedient system can amplify a bad institution.

A powerful model inside an abusive organization is not made safe by obedience.

This may be the central governance lesson of the century.

Do not ask only whether the machine is aligned.

Ask what the institution is aligned to.

The answer will often be incentives.

A company responds to revenue, competition, regulation, investor expectations, employee norms, and reputation.

A government responds to law, elections, bureaucratic incentives, budgets, public opinion, courts, and geopolitical pressure.

A hospital responds to clinical standards, reimbursement, capacity, liability, and professional ethics.

A school responds to assessments, parents, funding, admissions systems, and culture.

AI enters these incentive systems.

It rarely replaces them.

If the incentives are distorted, machine capability can help the institution pursue the distortion more effectively.

That makes institutional reform complementary to technical safety.

The temptation will be to solve political problems with smarter systems.

Fraud?

Better detection.

Misinformation?

Better classification.

Poor education?

Better tutoring.

Health costs?

Better prediction.

Crime?

Better surveillance.

Bureaucratic delay?

Better automation.

Each tool may help.

But a technical improvement does not dissolve the underlying structure.

Fraud can reflect incentive design.

Misinformation can reflect distrust.

Education can reflect poverty and family instability.

Health costs can reflect prices and institutions.

Crime can reflect opportunity, social conditions, and enforcement choices.

Bureaucratic delay can reflect law, capacity, or deliberately protective procedure.

The machine can optimize inside the system.

Sometimes the system is what needs changing.

This is where refusal becomes a legitimate design choice.

Not every available optimization should be deployed.

A school may decide not to automate a form of feedback because the teacher-student interaction is itself educational.

A court may refuse predictive tools in a context where individual judgment and explainability outweigh statistical gain.

A company may choose not to monitor workers continuously even if monitoring would raise output.

A family may reject a biological enhancement even if it is technically possible.

A society may ban a class of autonomous weapon.

A person may choose to write, paint, calculate, navigate, remember, or decide without assistance.

Refusal is not necessarily fear.

It can be the expression of a value.

The ability to refuse becomes more important when systems are infrastructural.

If machine assistance is optional, choice is easy.

If every employer, school, bank, insurer, hospital, and government assumes its use, opting out becomes expensive.

The technology becomes a norm before it becomes a law.

That can erode formal freedom through practical dependence.

A meaningful right to human agency may therefore require preserving non-machine paths in selected domains.

Not forever.

Not everywhere.

But where the stakes justify them.

A person should sometimes be able to reach a person.

A student should sometimes be required to work without assistance.

A public decision should sometimes be made slowly.

A worker should sometimes be unmeasured.

A citizen should sometimes remain illegible to the state.

A life should sometimes be inefficient.

There is a cost to preserving these spaces.

A human appeal channel costs money.

A low-surveillance workplace may sacrifice some measurable productivity.

A teacher spending time with one struggling student may reduce average throughput.

A public process with real contestation takes longer than automatic execution.

A non-machine route can be slower, more expensive, and harder to staff.

If the book treats these costs as imaginary, the argument becomes easy to praise and easy to ignore.

The serious claim is not that dignity always wins at zero cost.

It is that some costs are worth paying because the thing purchased is agency, legitimacy, resilience, or participation rather than output.

That choice still has to be defended.

A requirement for human service can become a luxury mandate that raises prices for people who would prefer a cheaper automated option.

A ban on machine assistance can entrench professional scarcity.

A privacy rule can make some forms of fraud detection harder.

A slow process can become a shield for insiders who know how to navigate it.

The point is not to sanctify friction.

It is to know what the friction protects and whether there is a better way to protect it.

The danger in an age of machine intelligence is not that optimization will become evil.

It is that optimization will become ambient.

Every process will present itself as improvable.

Every delay as reducible.

Every uncertainty as predictable.

Every behavior as measurable.

Every person as a vector of features.

Every relationship as a source of data.

Every institution as a function to maximize.

That worldview can produce extraordinary capability.

It can also forget what capability is for.

The falsification test for this chapter is straightforward.

If machine systems remain primarily tools that people use selectively, if optimization stays local and contestable, and if institutions preserve plural forms of judgment without pressure to convert every human activity into measurable objectives, then the concern may be overstated.

That is possible.

Technology can remain bounded by culture.

People ignore features.

Organizations retain rituals.

Laws constrain surveillance.

Markets reward human service.

Communities protect spaces from measurement.

The future is not obligated to maximize what the technology can do.

That is the hopeful point.

The ability to optimize is not a command to optimize.

Power creates responsibility precisely because capability and obligation are different things.

The Fourth Industrial Revolution gives us more ways to act.

It does not give us the purpose of action.

That remains ours.

And once more of the instrumental work can be delegated—calculation, drafting, searching, planning, coordinating, manipulating matter, even editing parts of biology—the final question becomes harder to avoid.

What is the human part for?

Not the economically protected part.

Not the part machines cannot yet do.

The part we would choose to keep even if a machine could do it.

That is where this book ends.
