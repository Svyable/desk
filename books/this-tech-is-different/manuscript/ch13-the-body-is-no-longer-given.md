# The Body Is No Longer Given

Changing the machinery of the person sounds more radical than changing the machinery around the person.

It is.

It is also older than the phrase makes it sound.

Humans have been modifying bodies for as long as medicine has existed.

A broken bone is set.

A cataract is removed.

A missing tooth is replaced.

A pacemaker regulates a heartbeat.

Insulin changes the chemistry of a body that cannot regulate glucose alone.

A prosthetic limb restores part of a lost physical function.

A cochlear implant converts sound into electrical stimulation.

None of these technologies waited for the Fourth Industrial Revolution.

The body has never been entirely given.

It has always been treated, supported, repaired, trained, disciplined, decorated, and altered.

So a book claiming historical difference has to be more precise.

The novelty is not that technology can affect a human body.

The candidate difference is that increasingly powerful information systems can become directly coupled to biological function.

A genome can be read as data and edited at a targeted location.

Neural activity can be measured, decoded, and converted into action outside the body.

Machine learning can help interpret biological signals too complex for simple rules.

Synthetic speech can be generated from activity in a brain whose owner can no longer speak normally.

The interface moves inward.

That is the important change.

In 2025, researchers reported a brain-to-voice neuroprosthesis for a man with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis and severe dysarthria.

The system decoded neural activity from 256 implanted microelectrodes and synthesized speech with closed-loop audio feedback.

It did more than produce text.

The participant could modulate features of the synthesized voice in real time, including intonation, and the system supported short sung melodies.

That is a remarkable result.

It is also easy to misuse rhetorically.

One person in a research setting does not prove that brain-computer interfaces are about to become ordinary consumer devices.

Implantation is invasive.

Clinical systems must survive biological variability, device maintenance, calibration, safety constraints, and long-term use.

A successful demonstration does not erase surgery, cost, regulation, or the difficulty of translating a laboratory system into durable care.

The right conclusion is smaller and, in some ways, more profound.

A signal that once remained trapped inside a person's motor system can be decoded by machinery and returned to the world as expressive speech.

The machine is not merely helping the user move an external tool.

It is helping restore a function that people ordinarily experience as part of the self.

Speech feels intimate because it is.

A voice carries identity, timing, emotion, hesitation, humor, urgency, and social presence.

Text can communicate meaning without reproducing those layers.

A system that restores voice therefore sits close to the border between assistive device and embodied self-expression.

That border matters more than the science-fiction question of whether people will one day install brain implants to answer email faster.

The near-term reality is already enough to force the philosophical issue.

Where does the person end?

A wheelchair can become part of how a person moves through the world.

A hearing aid becomes part of how a person accesses conversation.

A pacemaker becomes part of the machinery sustaining life.

A neuroprosthesis that translates intended speech into synthesized voice becomes part of the pathway between thought and other people.

Calling all of these things “tools” is correct.

It is also incomplete.

Some tools are used occasionally.

Others become incorporated into the user's practical body.

They become infrastructural to the person.

That distinction will matter as devices become more adaptive.

A traditional prosthetic component performs a relatively stable mechanical function.

A software-mediated prosthesis can change through updates.

It can use learned models.

It can be calibrated to an individual.

It can depend on remote services.

It can generate logs.

It can be patched.

It can fail because of software rather than only hardware.

It may be governed by permissions, accounts, warranties, cybersecurity practices, and vendor support.

The industrial system enters the body through the maintenance contract.

This sounds mundane.

It is not.

A person's physical capability can become dependent on a company remaining solvent, a device remaining supported, a battery remaining replaceable, a software stack remaining secure, or a regulator allowing continued operation.

The boundary between medical technology and platform governance begins to blur.

This is familiar in pieces.

Medical devices have long depended on manufacturers.

Implants have long required servicing.

What changes as software and machine intelligence become more central is the frequency with which capability can be modified after deployment.

A model update can alter performance without changing the physical implant.

A new decoder can improve function using the same sensor.

A software error can degrade a bodily interface.

The body becomes attached to a release cycle.

That should make us cautious about easy language like *upgrade the human*.

An upgrade is simple when a device is optional and reversible.

Medicine is rarely so clean.

The relevant questions include surgical risk.

Infection.

Tissue response.

Device longevity.

Calibration drift.

Battery replacement.

Privacy of biological signals.

Cybersecurity.

Clinical evidence.

Insurance coverage.

Access to skilled care.

Long-term vendor support.

The dramatic interface rests on an ordinary health system.

This is the pattern the book keeps finding.

A frontier capability becomes socially meaningful only when an institution can carry it.

Brain-computer interfaces make that visible because the institution has to carry the technology all the way to the nervous system.

Genome editing makes the same point from another direction.

The 2023 approval of Casgevy established that a therapy based on genome editing could cross into regulated medicine.

The 2025 personalized base-editing treatment for an infant with CPS1 deficiency pushed the boundary further by demonstrating a therapy designed around one patient's rare mutation.

Neither event means the genome has become ordinary source code.

Biology remains full of interactions, uncertainty, delivery constraints, and irreversible consequences.

But the events do change what can responsibly be called imaginable.

A genetic condition is not always a fixed sentence written at conception.

In some cases, molecular information can become the basis for a targeted intervention.

That is the sense in which the body becomes less given.

The distinction between treatment and enhancement becomes unavoidable here.

Medicine has long tolerated interventions that restore or preserve ordinary function.

A hearing aid is not usually treated as a moral crisis because it helps a person hear.

Insulin is not considered an unfair competitive advantage.

Surgery that restores mobility is not condemned because the patient becomes more capable than they were the day before.

Restoration has a familiar moral logic.

Enhancement is harder because the reference point changes.

If an intervention allows a person to function beyond an ordinary human range, the question is no longer simply whether medicine can repair loss.

It becomes whether society should engineer advantage.

That transition will not occur at one clean line.

The categories overlap.

A prosthesis designed for an amputee can sometimes perform differently from a biological limb.

A drug developed for disease can be used by healthy people.

A neural interface developed for paralysis could eventually have nonmedical applications.

A genetic intervention that reduces disease risk could also alter traits people value competitively.

The same platform can cross moral categories as its use changes.

This is why the governance question cannot be reduced to the technology itself.

We regulate purposes, populations, risk levels, and contexts.

The same capability can be defensible in one setting and unacceptable in another.

The Fourth Industrial Revolution does not abolish that distinction.

It multiplies the cases in which we have to draw it.

There is another line that matters even more: somatic versus heritable intervention.

A treatment that modifies cells in one patient is ethically different from a change intended to pass through future generations.

The second affects people who cannot consent and can propagate beyond the original decision.

It can alter the inherited human population rather than only the treated individual.

That is a much higher bar.

The existence of powerful somatic medicine does not imply that heritable editing should become normal.

Technological possibility is not a mandate.

This is one of the central mistakes of deterministic futurism.

It treats every frontier as a destination.

Humans routinely discover things and then restrict their use.

We know how to build chemical weapons.

We do not therefore treat civilian chemical warfare as the inevitable endpoint of chemistry.

We know how to split atoms.

That does not make every possible nuclear use acceptable.

Capability expands the decision space.

Politics and ethics decide what portion of that space becomes legitimate.

The same should be true of interventions in the human body.

The more intimate the intervention, the more consequential consent becomes.

Consent is already complicated in medicine because illness changes choices.

A person facing a lethal disease may reasonably accept risks that a healthy person would reject.

A parent making decisions for a sick child operates under different pressure again.

A worker asked to use a wearable monitoring device is not in the same position as a patient choosing a therapeutic implant.

A soldier ordered to use an enhancement is not exercising the same freedom as a consumer buying an optional device.

The label *choice* can hide the institution surrounding it.

This becomes important if human augmentation moves beyond medicine.

Imagine no speculative device at all.

Use technologies that already exist in rougher forms.

Wearable sensors can measure bodies.

Software can score performance.

Employers can optimize schedules.

Athletes can be monitored continuously.

Insurance systems can price risk.

Medical devices can transmit data.

The body becomes legible to institutions.

Enhancement is not the only future pressure.

Measurement itself can redistribute power.

A body that produces machine-readable signals creates a new information surface.

Who owns the data?

Who can require access?

Who can infer something the person did not intend to reveal?

Can a worker refuse a sensor without losing the job?

Can an insurer penalize a person who declines continuous monitoring?

Can a school demand cognitive or biometric measurements from students?

Can a device provider use biological data to improve a commercial model?

These questions are not theoretical decorations around the technology.

They determine whether an interface is empowering or extractive.

The body can become more capable and less private at the same time.

That tradeoff deserves more attention than fantasies about immortality.

There is also a security problem that becomes qualitatively different as digital systems touch bodily function.

A compromised social-media account is harmful.

A compromised medical device can be physically dangerous.

A stolen password can be reset.

A leaked genome cannot be reissued.

Biological information is unusually persistent.

It can contain information about relatives as well as the individual.

Neural signals may reveal more than the narrow control variable a device needs.

The safe design principle should therefore be minimization.

Collect what is needed.

Keep authority narrow.

Make local control possible where practical.

Separate therapeutic function from unnecessary surveillance.

Build graceful failure.

Make updates auditable.

Preserve human override where override is meaningful.

The more a technology becomes part of the person, the less acceptable careless platform design becomes.

This creates an inversion of the software mantra.

Move fast and break things is difficult to defend when the thing is attached to a nervous system.

The body imposes a different innovation tempo.

That does not mean slow forever.

Medical technology can improve quickly.

It means speed has to be earned through evidence.

The more irreversible the intervention, the more expensive error becomes.

The same principle applies to gene editing.

The ability to target a molecular sequence is extraordinary precisely because the consequence can persist.

There is no simple undo button for every biological intervention.

This is where the digital metaphor fails.

The genome is information, but the organism is not a text file.

An edit interacts with development, physiology, environment, other genes, and time.

The body is a system with history.

That history limits the usefulness of treating biology as programmable in the ordinary software sense.

A program can be duplicated into an identical environment.

Patients are not identical environments.

A software patch can be rolled back.

A cellular change may not be.

The metaphor of writability is useful because targeted intervention is real.

It becomes dangerous when writability is confused with complete control.

Neural technology has a similar trap.

Decoding a useful signal from the brain does not mean reading the mind in full.

A model trained to identify attempted speech is not a universal interpreter of thought.

The scientific achievement can be enormous without granting mystical access to a person's interior life.

This distinction should be defended because exaggerated claims create both hype and fear.

The real systems are constrained.

They can still change lives.

That is a recurring lesson of this revolution.

Transformative impact does not require omnipotence.

A narrow interface at the right boundary can be enough.

Restore speech to someone who cannot speak.

Correct a disease-causing sequence in a patient.

Provide a prosthesis that adapts better to the user.

Translate a neural intention into cursor movement.

Improve hearing.

Stabilize a heartbeat.

Each intervention is narrow compared with the fantasy of redesigning humanity.

Each can be civilization-changing for the person receiving it.

Scale enough of those interfaces and the social meaning of the body begins to change.

Disability is part of that change.

Technology can reduce barriers, but only if society does not define the person by the device.

A prosthesis is not an obligation to approximate a norm.

A deaf person is not simply a failed hearing system waiting for correction.

A disabled person can value an assistive technology without accepting a cultural demand to be “fixed.”

The availability of intervention does not erase identity, community, or the right to refuse.

This matters because abundance of technical options can create coercive expectations.

Once an enhancement exists, refusal can become costly.

If most workers in a field use a cognitive aid, declining it may look like underperformance.

If a prenatal intervention reduces a serious disease risk, parents who refuse it may be judged.

If an implant provides a competitive benefit, insurers or employers may eventually price around its use.

The technology can remain formally voluntary while institutions make non-use expensive.

That is how choice becomes infrastructure.

The most difficult ethical questions may therefore arrive not when a technology is rare, but when it becomes ordinary.

A rare device is a medical exception.

An ordinary device can become a social expectation.

Eyeglasses moved in this direction long ago.

Nobody thinks glasses corrupt human nature.

They are cheap, reversible, external, and low-risk.

The moral temperature of future augmentation will depend heavily on similar properties.

How invasive is it?

How reversible?

How expensive?

How unequal?

How necessary for participation?

How much data does it expose?

How much autonomy does the user retain?

Does the benefit accrue to the person or mainly to the institution measuring them?

These variables are more useful than a binary argument over “natural” and “unnatural.”

Industrial civilization has made human life profoundly unnatural in the literal sense.

Vaccines, anesthesia, sanitation, antibiotics, spectacles, air conditioning, contraception, and surgery all alter the conditions under which bodies live.

The moral question was never simply whether technology interfered with nature.

The question was what the interference did to human flourishing, freedom, risk, and justice.

That will remain the right question.

The body is no longer given in another sense too.

Its capabilities can increasingly depend on systems outside itself.

A person using an advanced prosthesis depends on manufacturing.

A patient receiving a personalized genetic treatment depends on sequencing, compute, molecular design, specialized production, clinical expertise, and regulation.

A neuroprosthesis depends on electrodes, models, electronics, power, software, support, and care.

The body becomes more technologically capable by becoming more infrastructurally entangled.

That entanglement can liberate.

It can also create dependency.

This is not a reason to reject the technology.

It is a reason to design the dependency well.

Interoperability matters.

Repairability matters.

Continuity of care matters.

Data portability matters.

Long-term support matters.

Public policy matters when a life-changing device is too important to abandon because a product line is discontinued.

The body creates obligations that ordinary consumer electronics do not.

This may become one of the clearest ways the Fourth Industrial Revolution changes institutions.

Software companies begin confronting duties that resemble medical stewardship.

Healthcare systems begin managing software-like update cycles.

Regulators have to evaluate adaptive systems.

Patients become users without ceasing to be patients.

Clinical evidence and cybersecurity become part of the same safety case.

A biological intervention may be designed with computational tools, manufactured through advanced industrial processes, delivered through medicine, monitored digitally, and revised through new data.

The old sector boundaries begin to fail.

That is convergence again.

The human body is not merely another market vertical inside it.

It is the place where the consequences become personal.

This is why the chapter's title should not be read as a prediction that everyone will become engineered.

The body remains stubbornly given in countless ways.

Aging is not solved.

Complex disease remains complex.

Brains remain poorly understood compared with the fantasies built around them.

Most traits are not controlled by one convenient switch.

Surgery has risk.

Biology has side effects.

Access is unequal.

Regulation moves slowly for reasons that are sometimes wise.

Human preferences differ.

People may reject interventions even when they work.

If enhancement remains peripheral for decades, the book should not pretend otherwise.

The stronger and more defensible claim is already visible.

A growing set of human functions can be treated as interfaces rather than immutable facts.

Signals can cross from brain to machine.

Information can cross from genome to model and back into therapy.

Software can mediate prosthetic function.

Biological measurements can enter continuous feedback systems.

The line between person and tool becomes less geometrically obvious.

That changes more than medicine.

It changes identity.

Because once a person's voice can be synthesized, their likeness generated, their actions delegated, and parts of their bodily function mediated by systems, the old question “is this real?” becomes inadequate.

A synthetic voice can be genuinely yours.

A generated message can be authorized by you without being typed by you.

A prosthetic action can be machine-mediated and still be your action.

A perfect imitation can sound like you while having nothing to do with you.

Authenticity stops being a property of appearance.

It becomes a question of provenance, authorization, and control.

The body is no longer given.

Soon, neither is the evidence that a body was ever there.
