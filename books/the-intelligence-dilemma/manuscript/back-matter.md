# Evidence Note

The research files accompanying this manuscript are intended as an audit trail for concrete claims, not as an exhaustive bibliography of learning science, education, assessment, or artificial intelligence.

The evidence base is uneven by design because the book asks questions that cross several mature fields and one rapidly changing technology.

Some claims rest on longstanding learning-science research: prior knowledge, retrieval, instructional guidance, formative assessment, teacher–student relationships, cognitive apprenticeship, and validity in educational measurement.

Other claims rely on recent generative-AI experiments whose results are necessarily narrower. A study of high-school mathematics does not establish what AI does to writing. A structured physics tutor does not establish what a generic chatbot will do in elementary school. A near-term exit ticket is not the same outcome as durable transfer. A specific interface, model version, prompt design, age group, subject, or institutional setting can materially change the result.

The chapter research trails therefore distinguish several kinds of evidence wherever practical:

- randomized or controlled studies from observational evidence;
- immediate performance from later unaided performance or transfer;
- student and teacher self-report from measured learning outcomes;
- broad instructional mechanisms from claims specific to generative AI;
- policy guidance from evidence that the policy improves learning;
- institutional examples from general recommendations;
- current findings from working papers or prototype systems that remain subject to revision.

Several boundaries are especially important to the argument.

The book does **not** claim that generative AI generally harms learning. Structured tutoring and human-AI augmentation can produce real gains under some conditions.

It does **not** claim that struggle is inherently beneficial. Difficulty helps only when it performs useful cognitive work and is matched to the learner, task, and subsequent support.

It does **not** claim that unaided performance is the only authentic form of competence. Modern professional capability often includes tools, collaboration, retrieval, automation, and delegation.

It does **not** claim that human teachers are always better than artificial systems at explanation, diagnosis, motivation, or judgment. The institutional claim is narrower: consequential educational decisions need accountable ownership, and reciprocal human relationships are themselves part of what schools may reasonably value.

It does **not** claim that AI-text detectors are universally useless. The evidence discussed in Chapter 13 supports treating detector outputs as fallible signals whose validity and consequences require scrutiny, not as direct proof of authorship.

It does **not** claim that oral assessment is AI-proof or inherently fairer than writing. Oral and interactive formats are presented as one form of responsive evidence within a plural assessment system and carry their own constraints around anxiety, disability, language, examiner consistency, staff time, and scale.

It does **not** claim that FERPA, COPPA, or other privacy rules prohibit educational AI generally. Those laws provide partial legal baselines in defined U.S. contexts. The manuscript’s arguments for purpose limitation, data minimization, and selective forgetting are broader educational design principles, not descriptions of universal statutory requirements.

The book’s central claim should weaken if future evidence shows that abundant generative assistance reliably increases durable, transferable student capability in roughly the same proportion that it increases visible performance, with little meaningful effect on independent judgment, source evaluation, assessment validity, or the learner’s ability to perform when assistance is removed.

It should also weaken if the distinction between assisted and independent capability proves largely irrelevant in the professional and civic settings schools prepare students to enter.

The constructive recommendations should weaken if multiple forms of evidence fail to improve learning assurance relative to artifact-centered assessment, if process-visible or apprenticeship-style designs consistently underperform more efficient instruction after comparable support and time, or if simulated relationships provide the same educational goods as continuing reciprocal human relationships without meaningful social costs.

The argument would also require revision in the opposite direction if evidence showed that schools gain little from teaching students to use capable AI systems. Tool-rich competence is real competence in many domains. Some manual procedures should disappear. Some forms of AI assistance may disproportionately benefit learners who previously lacked access to tutoring, translation, feedback, or specialist support.

The manuscript therefore treats its recommendations as conditional rather than ideological.

Where newer evidence changes the balance, the book should change with it.

Chapter-specific sources, limitations, counterevidence, and follow-up reporting questions are recorded in `books/the-intelligence-dilemma/research/`.
