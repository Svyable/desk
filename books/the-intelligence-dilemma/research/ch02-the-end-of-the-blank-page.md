# Research Trail — Chapter 2: The End of the Blank Page

## Purpose

Support the chapter’s distinction between useful cognitive offloading and assistance that removes learner generation before it can reveal or build capability.

## Sources read

### Risko & Gilbert (2016), “Cognitive Offloading”

- Source: *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 20(9), 676–688.
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2016.07.002
- What it supports: “cognitive offloading” is an established research term for using external actions or tools to reduce the internal information-processing demands of a task. The review also emphasizes that offloading decisions are influenced by metacognitive judgments that can be imperfect.
- Use in chapter: conceptual background for treating AI as part of a long history of external cognitive support rather than assuming all delegation is inherently harmful.
- Limitation: this is a pre-generative-AI review across several offloading domains. It does not establish that AI drafting has any particular educational effect.

### Slamecka & Graf (1978), “The Generation Effect: Delineation of a Phenomenon”

- Source: *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Learning and Memory* 4(6), 592–604.
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.4.6.592
- What it supports: across five experiments with verbal materials, self-generating target items produced better later memory than simply reading presented items under the tested conditions.
- Use in chapter: background for the narrower principle that generating before being shown an answer can sometimes create learning advantages.
- Limitation: the tasks were controlled verbal-memory experiments, not essay writing or AI use. The chapter deliberately does not claim a universal “generation is always better” rule.

### Roediger & Karpicke (2006), “Test-Enhanced Learning: Taking Memory Tests Improves Long-Term Retention”

- Source: *Psychological Science* 17(3), 249–255.
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x
- What it supports: in two experiments using prose passages, retrieval practice produced stronger delayed retention than repeated study, even though repeated study could produce greater short-term confidence.
- Use in chapter: supports the broader educational reason to preserve moments where a learner must attempt retrieval or production before receiving assistance.
- Limitation: retrieval practice is not identical to open-ended generation, composition, or AI-assisted work. It is evidence for sequencing attempts and help, not a blanket ban on scaffolding.

## Claims intentionally kept qualitative

- The chapter does not state that AI-first drafting necessarily reduces learning.
- It does not assign a universal “safe” percentage of generated text.
- Accessibility, multilingual support, executive-function support, and writing anxiety are presented as plausible use cases rather than quantified outcome claims in this chapter.

## Follow-up reporting

- Find studies directly comparing student-generated first attempts with AI-provided first drafts.
- Look for writing-process research on anchoring effects from suggested outlines or generated text.
- Interview writing instructors about whether “first you, then AI, then you again” changes the quality of student revision and oral defense.
