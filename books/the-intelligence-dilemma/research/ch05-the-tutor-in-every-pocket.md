# Research Trail — Chapter 5: The Tutor in Every Pocket

## Purpose

Make the strongest evidence-based case for AI tutoring while preserving the distinction between carefully designed educational systems and generic chatbots.

## Sources read

### Kestin et al. (2025), “AI tutoring outperforms in-class active learning: an RCT introducing a novel research-based design in an authentic educational setting”

- Source: *Scientific Reports* 15, 17458.
- Article: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-97652-6
- Published: June 3, 2025.
- Experimental population: 194 students participating in the randomized crossover experiment in an introductory Harvard physics course.
- What it supports: students using the custom AI tutor showed substantially higher immediate post-test learning gains than students receiving an in-class active-learning lesson covering the same material, while the AI group had a median time on task of 49 minutes compared with an assumed 60 minutes of in-class learning time. Students in the AI condition also reported higher engagement and motivation.
- Design details that matter: the tutor was not a generic chatbot. It used expert-crafted, question-specific prompts; structured sequencing; pedagogical guidance intended to promote active learning and manage cognitive load; and supplied step-by-step answers to improve accuracy.
- Limitations stated by the authors: the study covered a specific physics course and a small number of lessons; the authors do not claim structured AI tutoring will outperform active learning in every context, particularly higher-order synthesis tasks; the result may depend on the careful scaffolding and content design.
- Use in chapter: evidence that a deliberately designed AI tutor can produce meaningful measured learning gains, not just student satisfaction or assignment completion.

### Wang et al. / Tutor CoPilot randomized evaluation

- Stanford National Student Support Accelerator summary: https://nssa.stanford.edu/studies/tutor-copilot-human-ai-approach-scaling-real-time-expertise
- J-PAL evaluation summary: https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/human-ai-cooperation-improve-tutoring-united-states?lang=en
- Working paper: https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2410.03017
- Setting: live virtual mathematics tutoring involving hundreds of tutors and more than 1,000 K-12 students in schools serving students from low-income families.
- What it supports: tutors randomly assigned access to Tutor CoPilot produced higher student lesson mastery as measured by exit tickets; the intent-to-treat gain was about four percentage points, with larger gains among lower-rated or less-experienced tutors. Language analysis found more guiding questions and other higher-quality pedagogical strategies and less tendency to give away answers immediately.
- Use in chapter: evidence for a human-AI model in which the system augments tutors rather than replacing the student-teacher relationship.
- Limitations: the main outcome was near-term lesson mastery/exit tickets, not long-run standardized achievement. Results come from a particular tutoring platform, district context, subject, and implementation.

### Bastani et al. (2025), “Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning”

- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122
- Why included here: provides the necessary counterweight. AI tutoring benefits cannot be inferred from generic model access; the design of hints, safeguards, problem-specific grounding, and answer-giving behavior materially changed learning outcomes in the experiment.

## Claims intentionally bounded

- The chapter does not claim AI tutors have reached human expert-tutor quality across subjects.
- It does not claim the Harvard result generalizes to K-12 education or every discipline.
- It treats accessibility, translation, differentiated pacing, and immediate feedback as important capabilities and design possibilities; chapter-specific outcome claims should be added only after stronger subgroup evidence is collected.
- It treats “more teacher” as a design objective, not a measured universal consequence of teacher AI adoption.

## Follow-up reporting

- Seek independent replications of student-facing generative AI tutoring over full semesters.
- Track retention and transfer outcomes rather than only immediate post-tests.
- Find accessibility and multilingual studies with subgroup-specific outcomes.
- Interview teachers using AI to determine whether time saved is actually converted into more student interaction.
- Investigate product incentives: whether educational tools are rewarded for learner independence or for maximizing engagement and usage.
