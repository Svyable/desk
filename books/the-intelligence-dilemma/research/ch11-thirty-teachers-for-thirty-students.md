# Research Trail — Chapter 11: Thirty Teachers for Thirty Students

## Purpose

Support the strongest institutional case for AI as teacher leverage: more responsive instruction, more differentiated support, and more effective use of scarce adult attention, without collapsing augmentation into replacement.

## Sources read

### Gallup / Walton Family Foundation (2025), “Teaching for Tomorrow: Unlocking Six Weeks a Year With AI”

- Gallup article: https://news.gallup.com/poll/691967/three-teachers-weekly-saving-six-weeks-year.aspx
- Report: https://www.gallup.com/file/analytics/691922/Walton-Family-Foundation-Gallup-Teachers-AI-Report.pdf
- Published: June 24, 2025.
- Sample: 2,232 U.S. public K-12 teachers recruited from the RAND American Teacher Panel; fielded March 18–April 11, 2025.
- What it supports: six in ten teachers reported using an AI tool for work during the 2024–25 school year; 32% reported weekly use. Teachers reported using AI for preparation, worksheets/activities, and modifying materials to meet student needs. Weekly users estimated average time savings of 5.9 hours per week.
- Important limitation: the time savings are self-reported estimates, not independently observed time-use measurements. The chapter therefore describes them as reported/estimated savings rather than established causal productivity gains.
- Use in chapter: evidence that teachers are already experiencing AI as a production and adaptation tool, not only as an academic-integrity problem.

### Wang et al., Tutor CoPilot randomized evaluation

- Stanford NSSA summary: https://nssa.stanford.edu/studies/tutor-copilot-human-ai-approach-scaling-real-time-expertise
- J-PAL summary: https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/human-ai-cooperation-improve-tutoring-united-states?lang=en
- Working paper: https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2410.03017
- What it supports: human tutors randomly assigned access to an AI support system produced higher student lesson mastery on exit tickets; effects were larger among lower-rated or less-experienced tutors. Analysis found increased use of guiding questions and reduced tendency to give answers immediately.
- Use in chapter: evidence for an augmentation model in which AI distributes pedagogical expertise to humans rather than replacing the student–tutor relationship.
- Limitations: near-term lesson mastery is not long-run achievement; the results are tied to a particular math tutoring program and implementation.

### Loeb & Robinson (2026), “Tutoring” — AEFP Live Handbook / National Student Support Accelerator

- Source: https://nssa.stanford.edu/studies/tutoring
- What it supports: randomized evidence and meta-analyses consistently identify tutoring as one of the strongest educational interventions; effective programs tend to use frequent sessions, small ratios, curriculum alignment, formative assessment, and sustained tutor–student relationships.
- Important current conclusion: the 2026 handbook notes that the strongest evidence still supports human-delivered tutoring as the most reliable intervention, while new technology may augment capacity and also introduces replacement risks.
- Use in chapter: establishes why responsive individualized attention is educationally valuable and why scaling it has historically been difficult.

### Stanford National Student Support Accelerator, overview of high-impact tutoring research

- Source: https://nssa.stanford.edu/research/to-date
- What it supports: tutoring is an unusually strong intervention; successful programs commonly include frequent dosage, relationships, formative assessment, curricular alignment, and tutor training/support.
- Use in chapter: supports the distinction between “a chatbot that can answer” and an instructional system built around the mechanisms associated with effective tutoring.

## Claims intentionally bounded

- The chapter does not claim that generic chatbots are equivalent to human tutors.
- It does not claim that teacher-reported time savings automatically improve student outcomes.
- It does not claim AI can accurately infer the complete cause of a student’s behavior or academic difficulty from data traces.
- “Thirty teachers for thirty students” is used as a framing device for responsive support, not as a literal claim that present systems provide expert teacher-equivalent instruction.
- The chapter treats the fate of productivity gains as an institutional design and incentive question rather than a measured universal outcome.

## Follow-up reporting

- Interview teachers who use AI weekly and ask exactly where saved time goes.
- Find longitudinal evidence connecting teacher-facing AI tools to student outcomes rather than teacher sentiment alone.
- Compare districts that use AI to reduce workload with districts that use it to expand class size or staffing ratios.
- Track studies of AI-supported special education, multilingual instruction, and differentiated practice with subgroup outcomes.
- Interview tutoring researchers about which components of human tutoring can be safely automated and which appear to depend on relationship and human judgment.
