# Research Trail — Chapter 13: The Assessment Arms Race

## Purpose

Support the chapter’s distinction between detecting prohibited AI use and assuring that a student actually possesses the capability an assessment or credential claims to certify.

## Sources read

### OpenAI (2023), “New AI classifier for indicating AI-written text”

- Source: https://openai.com/index/new-ai-classifier-for-indicating-ai-written-text/
- Published: January 31, 2023; updated to note retirement on July 20, 2023.
- What it supports: OpenAI described its classifier as not fully reliable; on its challenge set it identified 26% of AI-written text as likely AI-written and incorrectly labeled 9% of human-written text as AI-written. OpenAI later removed the tool because of its low rate of accuracy.
- Use in chapter: concrete historical evidence that authorship detection can produce both missed detections and false accusations, even when developed by a frontier model provider.
- Limitation: this is evidence about one early classifier, not proof that all future detection methods must fail.

### TEQSA (2023), “Assessment reform for the age of artificial intelligence”

- Source: https://www.teqsa.gov.au/guides-resources/resources/corporate-publications/assessment-reform-age-artificial-intelligence
- Published: November 23, 2023.
- What it supports: Australia’s higher-education regulator framed generative AI as exacerbating existing assessment challenges and called for assessment design that both uses AI opportunities and manages risks.
- Use in chapter: supports the move from post-hoc detection toward redesigning assessment for credible evidence of learning.

### TEQSA (2024), “The evolving risk to academic integrity posed by generative artificial intelligence: Options for immediate action”

- Source: https://www.teqsa.gov.au/guides-resources/resources/corporate-publications/evolving-risk-academic-integrity-posed-generative-artificial-intelligence-options-immediate-action
- Published: August 8, 2024.
- What it supports: TEQSA explicitly framed generative AI as a risk to award integrity and offered immediate mitigation strategies while institutions work toward longer-term assessment reform.
- Use in chapter: supports the distinction between protecting award integrity and relying on artifact-level detection.

### TEQSA (2025), “Enacting assessment reform in a time of artificial intelligence”

- Source: https://www.teqsa.gov.au/guides-resources/resources/corporate-publications/enacting-assessment-reform-time-artificial-intelligence
- Published: September 24, 2025.
- What it supports: the resource provides examples of institutions putting assessment-reform principles into practice to assure learning while supporting responsible AI use.
- Use in chapter: current evidence that institutional practice is moving toward layered assessment and learning assurance rather than a detector-only response.

### TEQSA Gen AI resource hub (updated July 31, 2026)

- Source: https://www.teqsa.gov.au/guides-resources/higher-education-good-practice-hub/gen-ai-knowledge-hub/gen-ai-teqsa-resources
- What it supports: as of 2026 TEQSA’s current resource set continues to emphasize assessment, learning assurance, adaptive capabilities, and online-degree assurance in an AI-integrated environment.
- Use in chapter: supports describing learning assurance as a live regulatory/institutional concern rather than a temporary 2023 response.

## Claims intentionally bounded

- The chapter does not claim that all AI detectors are useless.
- It treats detector scores as potentially useful signals whose reliability and consequences must be evaluated, not as proof of authorship.
- It does not claim surveillance is never appropriate; high-stakes independent certification may require controlled conditions.
- The discussion of second devices, manufactured version histories, and evasion tools describes foreseeable/adversarial dynamics rather than prevalence estimates.
- The layered-security analogy is conceptual, not a claim that assessment and cybersecurity are technically equivalent domains.

## Follow-up reporting

- Gather independent 2025–2026 benchmark studies of commercial AI-text detectors across model families, editing levels, and multilingual writers.
- Document institutional appeal processes for students accused on the basis of detector scores.
- Interview academic-integrity officers about how much weight detector outputs actually receive in misconduct cases.
- Compare detector-heavy policies with assessment-redesign approaches on cost, faculty workload, false accusations, and student trust.
- Track provenance and watermarking research separately from statistical text detection; they solve different technical problems.
