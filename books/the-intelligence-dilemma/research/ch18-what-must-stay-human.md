# Chapter 18 research — What Must Stay Human

## Core evidence

### Roorda et al. — teacher-student relationships, engagement, and achievement

- **Source:** Debora L. Roorda, Suzanne Jak, Marjolein Zee, Frans J. Oort, and Helma M. Y. Koomen, “Affective Teacher–Student Relationships and Students’ Engagement and Achievement: A Meta-Analytic Update and Test of the Mediating Role of Engagement,” *School Psychology Review* 46(3), 2017, 239–261.
- **Record:** https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1156223
- **Supports:** the chapter's bounded claim that affective teacher-student relationships are associated with student engagement and achievement, with engagement generally showing the stronger relationship. This supports treating relationship as part of the learning environment rather than as a decorative extra.
- **Caution:** much of the evidence synthesized is correlational. The chapter does not claim that relationship quality alone causally produces achievement gains or that all human teacher relationships are beneficial.

### National Academies — belonging, agency, purpose, and emotionally supportive environments

- **Source:** National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, *How People Learn II: Learners, Contexts, and Cultures* (2018).
- **Record:** https://nap.nationalacademies.org/catalog/24783/how-people-learn-ii-learners-contexts-and-cultures
- **Supports:** the chapter's claim that motivation and learning are shaped by belonging, agency, purpose, perceived value, and emotionally supportive/nonthreatening learning environments. The report also emphasizes that learning is culturally and socially situated.
- **Caution:** this is a broad consensus synthesis, not evidence that a particular social classroom format is always superior to individualized technology-supported learning.

### UNESCO — technology benefits, risks, and human contact

- **Source:** UNESCO Global Education Monitoring Report Team, *Technology in Education: A Tool on Whose Terms?* (2023).
- **Record:** https://www.unesco.org/gem-report/en/publication/technology
- **Online summary:** https://gem-report-2023.unesco.org/technology-in-education/
- **Supports:** the chapter's description of the report as explicitly balancing potential benefits in access, inclusion, quality, and system management against harms and implementation risks. The report's summary identifies distraction and lack of human contact among possible negative effects and argues that technology should be selected according to educational objectives rather than technical possibility alone.
- **Caution:** the GEM report ranges across many technologies and countries. It does not establish that generative-AI tutoring necessarily reduces human contact or learning.

### Allen et al. — belonging as a multidimensional human phenomenon

- **Source:** Kelly-Ann Allen, Margaret L. Kern, Christopher S. Rozek, Dennis M. McInerney, and George M. Slavich, “Belonging: A Review of Conceptual Issues, an Integrative Framework, and Directions for Future Research,” *Australian Journal of Psychology* / review available via PMC (2021).
- **Record:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8095671/
- **Supports:** the chapter's use of belonging as more than physical co-presence: a subjective sense of connection with social groups, places, and experiences that is associated with a wide range of outcomes.
- **Caution:** belonging is broad and measured differently across studies. The chapter does not reduce it to teacher approval or claim that school is the only place students can develop it.

## Falsification / counterevidence note

The chapter should weaken if students can receive the same durable benefits from simulated relationships as from continuing human relationships across engagement, trust, ethical development, conflict repair, belonging, and responsibility; or if removing substantial amounts of peer and teacher contact improves these outcomes without meaningful social costs.

Counterevidence is already central to the argument. Humans can be biased, cruel, inattentive, unsafe, and inconsistent; an AI system can sometimes offer more patient, private, accessible, or less humiliating support. The chapter therefore does not defend human presence as intrinsically good or claim that machines will never become emotionally fluent. Its narrower claim is institutional: schools should deliberately preserve repeated experience with accountable adults and independent peers because responsibility, reciprocal obligation, conflict, belonging, and shared consequences are themselves educational goods, not merely inefficient delivery mechanisms for academic content.
