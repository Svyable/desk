# Research Trail — Chapter 8: The Invisible Curriculum

## Purpose

Document the rapid spread of student AI use, the lag in institutional guidance, and the possibility that differences in norms, support, and AI proficiency become part of an unofficial curriculum.

## Sources read

### HEPI / Kortext, Student Generative AI Survey 2026

- Report page: https://www.hepi.ac.uk/reports/student-generative-ai-survey-2026/
- Published: March 12, 2026.
- Population: UK undergraduates.
- What it supports: generative AI use is now near universal in the surveyed undergraduate population; 95% reported using AI in at least one way and 94% reported using generative AI to help with assessed work. The report also documents student concerns about skill erosion, fairness, false accusations, and inconsistent institutional responses.
- Use in chapter: supports the claim that student adoption has moved faster than many institutional policy processes.
- Limitation: higher education, UK-specific, survey self-report, and sponsored by Kortext. Do not generalize exact percentages to K-12 or other countries.

### Jisc, “Student perceptions of AI 2025”

- Report: https://www.jisc.ac.uk/reports/student-perceptions-of-ai-2025
- Published: May 22, 2025.
- Evidence base: discussion groups with 173 further- and higher-education students plus analysis of seven institutional surveys covering 1,274 student responses.
- What it supports: students reported continuing uncertainty about what AI use was allowed even though many institutions had guidance; they wanted clearer course-level rules and practical training. Students also raised concerns about premium-tool inequity, inconsistent rules between classes, privacy, misinformation, and the needs of neurodivergent, disabled, and international learners.
- Use in chapter: turns the chapter’s “invisible curriculum” mechanism into an observable policy problem: formal guidance can exist while students still learn operative norms locally from individual instructors, peers, and product behavior.
- Limitation: qualitative and mixed-source evidence from UK further and higher education, not a representative prevalence estimate and not direct evidence of effects on learning outcomes.

### “The use and usefulness of GenAI in higher education: Student experience and perspectives” (2026)

- Source: *Computers and Education Open* 10, 100347.
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.caeo.2026.100347
- Sample: more than 8,000 students across four Australian universities.
- What it supports: more than 80% of respondents had used GenAI for study-related tasks, with common uses including editing, summarizing, and idea generation; the study documents demographic variation in engagement.
- Use in chapter: evidence that student use is broad and varied rather than reducible to answer generation.
- Limitation: higher education, Australian universities, self-reported behavior.

### Metzgar (2026), “Student Use of Generative AI in Higher Education: Patterns, Gaps, and Institutional Readiness”

- Source: *Acta Educationis Generalis* 16(1).
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.2478/atd-2026-0004
- Sample: 3,839 students across 16 countries.
- What it supports: 86% reported using AI, while only 23% reported feeling prepared; many respondents described inadequate institutional guidance.
- Use in chapter: supports the adoption-versus-guidance gap.
- Limitation: voluntary, self-reported, cross-sectional sample with selection bias; cannot establish prevalence for all higher education or causal effects.

### EDUCAUSE 2025 Students and Technology Survey

- Survey page: https://www.educause.edu/research-and-publications/research/analytics-services/surveys/2025/educause-student-survey
- What it supports: institutions are explicitly tracking diverse student AI uses such as proofreading/editing, explanation without answers, direct answer seeking, instructor permissions, course incorporation, accuracy concerns, and lack of guidance.
- Use in chapter: reinforces that student AI use is not one behavior and that institutional rules vary across instructors and courses.
- Limitation: use the final published findings rather than the survey instrument for numerical prevalence claims; the current source page primarily documents questions and methodology.

### UNESCO, “Guidance for generative AI in education and research”

- URL: https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/guidance-generative-ai-education-and-research
- Published: September 2023; page updated January 2026.
- What it supports: institutions should approach GenAI through human-centered, age-appropriate pedagogical design and policy, with attention to privacy and human capacity rather than treating tool access as sufficient.
- Use in chapter: policy context for the claim that guided use requires more than software distribution.
- Limitation: normative international guidance, not an empirical effectiveness study.

## Claims intentionally bounded

- The chapter does not generalize higher-education usage percentages to all school-age students.
- It treats “invisible curriculum” as an interpretive concept for peer norms, unofficial practices, product defaults, and household guidance, not a measured variable in the cited surveys.
- It does not assume every institutional inconsistency is harmful; different courses can rationally have different AI rules when their learning objectives differ.
- It argues for legible local rules rather than one universal permission standard.
- Jisc student reports are used as evidence of perceived ambiguity and support needs, not as proof that inconsistent AI rules cause weaker academic outcomes.

## Follow-up reporting

- Seek nationally representative K-12 student-use data with age and socioeconomic breakdowns.
- Interview students about where they actually learned their AI habits: teachers, parents, peers, social media, or product interfaces.
- Compare schools that teach explicit AI-use routines with schools that rely mainly on prohibition or generic responsible-use language.
- Investigate whether paid model access, device quality, and adult guidance change the sophistication of student AI use.
