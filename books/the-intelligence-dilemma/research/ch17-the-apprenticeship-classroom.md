# Chapter 17 research — The Apprenticeship Classroom

## Core evidence

### Collins, Brown, and Newman — cognitive apprenticeship

- **Source:** Allan Collins, John Seely Brown, and Susan E. Newman, “Cognitive Apprenticeship: Teaching the Crafts of Reading, Writing, and Mathematics,” in Lauren B. Resnick (ed.), *Knowing, Learning, and Instruction* (1989); accessible summary by Collins, Brown, and Ann Holum in *American Educator* (1991).
- **Record:** https://www.aft.org/ae/winter1991/collins_brown_holum
- **Supports:** the chapter's central instructional mechanism: traditional apprenticeship makes physical practice visible, while many school tasks hide the cognitive processes experts use. Cognitive apprenticeship proposes making thinking visible through modeling, coaching, scaffolding, articulation, reflection, and exploration.
- **Caution:** this is a general instructional framework developed decades before generative AI. The chapter applies the mechanism to AI-rich classrooms; it does not claim the original authors studied current AI tools.

### Chen and Yang — project-based learning outcomes and moderators

- **Source:** Cheng-Huan Chen and Yong-Cih Yang, “Revisiting the effects of project-based learning on students’ academic achievement: A meta-analysis investigating moderators,” *Educational Research Review* 26 (2019), 71–81.
- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2018.11.001
- **Supports:** the bounded claim that project-based learning can support academic achievement under some conditions and that implementation features moderate outcomes. This supports treating authentic projects as a potentially productive instructional form rather than assuming they are inherently superior.
- **Caution:** project-based learning is heterogeneous across subjects, ages, durations, and implementations. The chapter does not transfer a single average effect to AI-mediated projects.

### Kirschner, Sweller, and Clark — counterevidence on minimal guidance

- **Source:** Paul A. Kirschner, John Sweller, and Richard E. Clark, “Why Minimal Guidance During Instruction Does Not Work,” *Educational Psychologist* 41(2), 2006, 75–86.
- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326985ep4102_1
- **Supports:** the chapter's counterargument that authentic or project-based work can become unproductive when novices are left with too little explicit guidance. The chapter therefore retains direct instruction, worked examples, modeling, and staged support rather than equating apprenticeship with open-ended discovery.
- **Caution:** the paper is a broad theoretical/research synthesis and has generated substantial debate; it should not be read as evidence that all inquiry, project work, or experiential learning fails.

### National Academies — learning technology requires alignment and support

- **Source:** National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, *How People Learn II: Learners, Contexts, and Cultures* (2018).
- **Record:** https://nap.nationalacademies.org/catalog/24783/how-people-learn-ii-learners-contexts-and-cultures
- **Supports:** the chapter's broader design claim that technology effectiveness depends on learner characteristics, learning targets, and context, and that effective implementation requires alignment with goals plus support for instructors and learners.
- **Caution:** this consensus report predates widespread generative-AI use.

## Falsification / counterevidence note

The chapter should weaken if process-visible, coached project work consistently produces less durable learning than artifact-centered instruction after comparable time and support, or if generative systems can substitute for expert modeling and human coaching without reducing students' later independent judgment.

The chapter also explicitly rejects the romantic version of apprenticeship. Projects can consume large amounts of time, hide unequal group participation, and ask novices to discover what explicit instruction could teach more efficiently. The narrower claim is that when AI can cheaply produce finished artifacts, schools gain useful evidence by making consequential decisions, explanations, revisions, and gradually independent practice visible inside the work.
