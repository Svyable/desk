# Research Trail — Chapter 10: The New Achievement Gap

## Purpose

Examine whether abundant AI assistance narrows educational inequality or shifts advantage toward prior knowledge, AI proficiency, implementation quality, household support, and paid access.

## Sources read

### Tetzlaff & Simonsmeier (2025), expertise reversal meta-analysis

- Source: *Learning and Instruction* 98, 102142.
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2025.102142
- Evidence base: 176 effect sizes from 60 experimental studies and 5,924 participants.
- What it supports: lower-prior-knowledge learners generally benefit more from high-assistance instruction, while higher-prior-knowledge learners can benefit from lower assistance. The effect varies across domains and educational levels.
- Use in chapter: complicates the simplistic claim that assistance necessarily weakens novices; properly designed support may be especially important for less-prepared learners.
- Limitation: broader instructional-design literature, not direct evidence about generative AI.

### “Mechanisms of enhancing learning with unequal preparation: An experimental study on generative artificial intelligence use and proficiency in programming learning” (2025)

- Source: *Computers and Education: Artificial Intelligence* 9, 100467.
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.caeai.2025.100467
- What it supports: GenAI use reduced perceived learning cost, especially among less-prepared learners, but this did not automatically produce improved learning outcomes or reduce the educational divide. GenAI proficiency significantly moderated learning-related benefits.
- Use in chapter: evidence that access and ease do not necessarily translate into equal learning gains; skill in using the tool can itself become consequential.
- Limitation: one programming-learning experiment and specific population; do not generalize the null gap-closing result to all subjects or students.

### Oreopoulos & Low (2026), “One Click Away: AI Tutoring with Khanmigo in a Two-Year School Experiment”

- Source: NBER Working Paper 35620.
- URL: https://www.nber.org/papers/w35620
- Setting: two-year cluster randomized trial in 18 Tennessee middle schools using Khanmigo during daily remedial mathematics sessions.
- What it supports: assignment to the AI-tutoring condition raised math achievement modestly, but the AI tutor was used less intensively than simple access might imply; most students tried it, yet substantive dialogue was uncommon and students often interacted through bare answers or suggested prompts.
- Use in chapter: supports the claim that procurement/access, actual use, quality of use, and learning outcomes are separate implementation stages.
- Limitation: August 2026 working paper; not yet a peer-reviewed final publication. Effects may reflect the broader Khan Academy environment as well as the AI component.

### Oreopoulos et al. (2026), “Making AI Tutoring Productive: Evidence from a Mastery-Based Math Practice Experiment”

- Source: NBER Working Paper 35621.
- URL: https://www.nber.org/papers/w35621
- Setting: randomized experiment with more than 6,000 middle-school students in Hamilton County Schools.
- What it supports: structured AI assistance after mistakes can improve next-attempt correctness and change learning behavior, showing how implementation design can determine whether help functions as scaffolding rather than simple answer delivery.
- Use in chapter: supports the claim that equity depends on the type of assistance students receive, not merely access to a model.
- Limitation: recent working paper; topic- and platform-specific.

### Chen & Cheung (2025), systematic review and meta-analysis of GenAI and university learning outcomes

- Source: *Educational Research Review*, article 100737.
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2025.100737
- Evidence base: 57 studies and 97 effect estimates.
- What it supports: GenAI interventions showed positive average effects across several university-learning outcomes, with substantial heterogeneity across learners, contexts, tools, roles, rules, and study conditions.
- Use in chapter: evidence against treating GenAI as uniformly harmful or uniformly beneficial and support for contextual heterogeneity.
- Limitation: higher education only; pooled literature includes varied designs and outcomes and should not be converted into one universal effect claim.

### UNESCO guidance on GenAI in education and research

- URL: https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/guidance-generative-ai-education-and-research
- What it supports: equitable and meaningful use requires policy, human capacity, privacy protection, age-appropriate design, and attention to access rather than simple technology deployment.
- Use in chapter: policy framing for the implementation layer of AI equity.
- Limitation: normative guidance rather than causal evidence.

## Claims intentionally bounded

- The chapter does not claim AI will widen inequality overall.
- It gives the strongest equalizing case substantial space: low-cost tutoring, language support, after-hours help, accessibility, and access to explanations can expand opportunity.
- It distinguishes tool access from quality of use and durable learning.
- It does not claim paid tiers are currently a measured major cause of educational achievement gaps; paid access is presented as a plausible implementation variable requiring further reporting.

## Follow-up reporting

- Find subgroup results from K-12 AI-tutoring trials by prior achievement, income, disability, language status, and race/ethnicity where ethically and statistically appropriate.
- Compare school-provided AI systems with paid consumer tools in model capability, limits, privacy, and integrations.
- Interview families across income groups about how adults teach children to use AI at home.
- Seek evidence on whether explicit AI-literacy instruction disproportionately helps students who begin with less outside support.
- Track whether schools measure meaningful learning outcomes rather than license activation and usage minutes.
