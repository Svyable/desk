# Chapter 19 research — The School That Knows Why

## Core evidence

### Biggs — constructive alignment

- **Source:** John Biggs, “Enhancing Teaching through Constructive Alignment,” *Higher Education* 32 (1996), 347–364.
- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00138871
- **Supports:** the chapter's design claim that intended learning outcomes, learning activities, and assessment should be aligned rather than treated as separate administrative layers. The chapter uses this as an older, technology-independent discipline for deciding whether AI assistance supports or substitutes for the capability being taught.
- **Caution:** constructive alignment is an instructional-design framework, not experimental evidence that a particular AI policy improves learning.

### AERA, APA, and NCME — validity tied to interpretations and uses

- **Source:** American Educational Research Association, American Psychological Association, and National Council on Measurement in Education, *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing* (2014 edition).
- **Record:** https://www.aera.net/publications/books/standards-for-educational-psychological-testing-2014-edition
- **Open-access text:** https://www.testingstandards.net/uploads/7/6/6/4/76643089/standards_2014edition.pdf
- **Supports:** the chapter's bounded validity argument: assessment evidence must support the particular interpretation and use being made from a score or performance. The chapter transfers this discipline cautiously to classroom artifacts, arguing that one polished product may support some capability claims and not others.
- **Caution:** the Standards concern professional educational and psychological testing. Daily classroom assignments are not formal tests merely because the chapter borrows the validity logic.

### TEQSA — assessment reform for widespread generative AI

- **Source:** Jason Lodge, Sarah Howard, Margaret Bearman, Phillip Dawson, and associates, *Assessment Reform for the Age of Artificial Intelligence*, Tertiary Education Quality and Standards Agency, Australia (2023), with later implementation resources.
- **Record:** https://www.teqsa.gov.au/guides-resources/resources/corporate-publications/assessment-reform-age-artificial-intelligence
- **Implementation resource:** https://www.teqsa.gov.au/guides-resources/resources/corporate-publications/enacting-assessment-reform-time-artificial-intelligence
- **Supports:** the chapter's current example that an education regulator has moved beyond a simple detection/banning frame. TEQSA describes generative AI as creating both opportunities and assessment challenges and emphasizes preparing students for AI use while maintaining trustworthy judgments of learning through contextualized, multiple assessment approaches.
- **Caution:** TEQSA regulates Australian higher education. Its guidance is not direct evidence for every K–12 context or national system.

### National Academies — alignment of technology with learning goals

- **Source:** National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, *How People Learn II: Learners, Contexts, and Cultures* (2018).
- **Record:** https://nap.nationalacademies.org/catalog/24783/how-people-learn-ii-learners-contexts-and-cultures
- **Supports:** the chapter's claim that learning technology should be evaluated in relation to learning goals, learner characteristics, context, instructor support, equitable access, and ongoing assessment.
- **Caution:** the report predates widespread generative AI.

## Falsification / counterevidence note

The chapter should weaken if a single universal AI permission rule reliably supports learning and trustworthy certification across ages, disciplines, and assessment purposes; if explicit learning-purpose statements add administrative burden without improving student understanding or assessment validity; or if multiple forms of evidence fail to improve institutions' ability to distinguish assisted performance from durable capability.

The strongest counterargument is bureaucratic. A school can turn purpose statements, AI disclosures, verification interviews, process logs, and revised rubrics into compliance theater that consumes teachers' time without improving learning. The chapter therefore does not prescribe maximal documentation. Its narrower claim is that assistance rules and evidence of mastery should follow from the capability an assignment is meant to build or certify, and requirements that cannot answer “why is this here?” should be candidates for removal.
