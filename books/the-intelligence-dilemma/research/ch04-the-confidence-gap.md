# Research Trail — Chapter 4: The Confidence Gap

## Purpose

Ground the chapter’s distinction between improved assisted performance, actual learning, and a learner’s confidence about what has been learned.

## Source read

### Bastani et al. (2025), “Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics”

- Source: *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*.
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122
- Open full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12232635/
- Population: nearly 1,000 high-school mathematics students in a field experiment.
- Conditions: control; “GPT Base,” designed to resemble a standard GPT-4 chat interface for help on practice problems; and “GPT Tutor,” which added safeguards intended to preserve learning, including hints rather than direct answers and teacher-provided problem information.
- What it supports: both AI conditions improved performance during practice. When AI access was removed for a later exam, the GPT Base group performed worse than the no-AI control group; the negative effect was largely mitigated in the guarded GPT Tutor condition. Interaction analysis suggested students more often used the unrestricted interface as a crutch by seeking/copying solutions. The paper also reports evidence that students did not clearly perceive the learning cost themselves.
- Use in chapter: empirical example of the “confidence gap”: assisted task performance can improve while later unaided capability does not improve and may decline.
- Important limitation: this was one subject, age group, country/context, model generation, and specific implementation. The chapter does not generalize the measured effect size to all AI-assisted learning.

## Related conceptual source

### Risko & Gilbert (2016), “Cognitive Offloading”

- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2016.07.002
- Relevance: provides a broader framework for externalizing cognitive work and notes that offloading behavior is partly shaped by metacognitive judgments that may be inaccurate.
- Limitation: not an AI-in-education study.

## Claims intentionally kept bounded

- The chapter does not claim that generative AI generally causes overconfidence.
- It does not claim that fluent language alone causes students to believe false information.
- It treats novice vulnerability as a reason prior knowledge matters for verification, not as a claim that experts are immune to automation bias or model error.
