# Chapter 16 research — Curriculum After Answers

## Core evidence

### National Academies — knowledge, mental models, motivation, and technology alignment

- **Source:** National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, *How People Learn II: Learners, Contexts, and Cultures* (2018).
- **Record:** https://nap.nationalacademies.org/catalog/24783/how-people-learn-ii-learners-contexts-and-cultures
- **Supports:** the chapter's claim that durable knowledge and increasingly complex mental models are important for inference and flexible problem solving; that prior knowledge can help and bias judgment; and that educational technology works differently depending on learner, target, and context. The report's classroom implications also emphasize alignment with learning goals, instructor/learner support, equitable access, and ongoing assessment.
- **Caution:** this is a broad consensus synthesis, not evidence about generative AI specifically.

### UNESCO — AI competency framework for students

- **Source:** Fengchun Miao, Kelly Shiohira, and Natalie Lao, *AI Competency Framework for Students*, UNESCO (2024; UNESCO page updated January 16, 2026).
- **Record:** https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/ai-competency-framework-students
- **Supports:** the chapter's description of the framework as 12 competencies organized across a human-centred mindset, ethics of AI, AI techniques/applications, and AI system design, progressing through understand, apply, and create. UNESCO explicitly frames the goal as integrating AI learning objectives into official curricula and emphasizes critical judgment and responsible participation, not mere tool operation.
- **Caution:** the framework is normative curricular guidance, not an experimental evaluation showing that these exact dimensions improve learning.

### Wineburg and McGrew — lateral reading as expert source evaluation

- **Source:** Sam Wineburg and Sarah McGrew, “Lateral Reading and the Nature of Expertise: Reading Less and Learning More When Evaluating Digital Information,” *Teachers College Record* 121(11), 2019; working-paper version available through Stanford.
- **Record:** https://stacks.stanford.edu/file/druid:yk133ht8603/Wineburg%20McGrew_Lateral%20Reading%20and%20the%20Nature%20of%20Expertise.pdf
- **Supports:** the chapter's contrast between evaluating a website mainly from its own surface cues and fact-checkers' practice of leaving a page to investigate its source, affiliations, and claims elsewhere on the web.
- **Caution:** lateral reading is one source-evaluation strategy. It does not eliminate the need for domain knowledge or careful reading of primary evidence.

### Wineburg, Breakstone, Smith, Ortega, and McGrew — civic online reasoning intervention

- **Source:** Stanford Graduate School of Education summary of randomized evaluation published in *Journal of Educational Psychology*, April 19, 2022.
- **Record:** https://ed.stanford.edu/news/it-doesn-t-take-long-learn-how-spot-misinformation-online-stanford-study-finds
- **Supports:** the chapter's claim that roughly 500 high-school students in an urban Midwestern district were studied, with the intervention group receiving six 50-minute Civic Online Reasoning lessons; the reported results show substantial improvement in identifying questionable websites, while post-intervention performance still left meaningful room for improvement.
- **Caution:** the study concerns online source evaluation, not generative-AI verification directly, and the Stanford news summary should not be used to imply transfer to every discipline without further evidence.

## Falsification / counterevidence note

The chapter should weaken if students can reliably evaluate sophisticated AI-generated claims without substantial domain knowledge, if explicit source-verification instruction shows little transfer or is quickly rendered obsolete by new interfaces, or if keeping more knowledge internally produces no meaningful advantage once external tools are universally available.

The opposite evidence also matters. Prior knowledge can create bias as well as insight, factual recall can be over-taught at the expense of inquiry, and external aids can productively carry lower-level work. The chapter therefore does not argue for memorizing everything or for treating unaided work as morally superior. Its narrower claim is that abundant answers increase the value of curricular clarity about which knowledge, verification habits, and independent capabilities students still need in order to judge and direct external intelligence.
