# Research Trail — Chapter 14: The Oral Return

## Purpose

Ground the case for oral and interactive assessment as one component of a broader evidence system in which students must demonstrate understanding, ownership, and responsiveness rather than merely submit polished artifacts.

## Sources read

### UCL, “Three categories of GenAI use in assessment”

- Source: https://www.ucl.ac.uk/teaching-learning/generative-ai-hub/three-categories-genai-use-assessment
- What it supports: UCL explicitly lists vivas, discussion-based assessments, in-person exams, and presentations with significant Q&A as assessment formats in which generative AI use may be prohibited because the intended capability requires controlled conditions. UCL also describes investigatory vivas when inappropriate AI use is suspected.
- Use in chapter: contemporary institutional evidence that oral questioning is being used both for assessment design and for authorship/understanding verification.

### Smallhorn et al. (2025), “Utilising one-on-one interactive oral assessments as the major final assessment within a bioscience course”

- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1080/02602938.2025.2502577
- Journal: *Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education*, Volume 50, Issue 7.
- Research summary: https://researchnow.flinders.edu.au/en/publications/utilising-one-on-one-interactive-oral-assessments-as-the-major-fi/
- What it supports: the study reports implementation of interactive oral assessment in undergraduate bioscience and identifies themes involving authenticity, academic-integrity benefits, anxiety, and inclusion. Students’ anxiety reportedly became more positive with experience and did not negatively affect performance in that setting.
- Use in chapter: evidence that oral assessment can be implemented as more than a disciplinary interrogation and can be scaffolded as a normal learning/assessment format.
- Limitation: one disciplinary/contextual implementation does not establish universal equity or scalability.

### Smallhorn & Hunter (2026), “Lessons learnt from interactive oral assessments at scale in a first-year molecular biology unit”

- Source: https://researchnow.flinders.edu.au/en/publications/lessons-learnt-from-interactive-oral-assessments-at-scale-in-a-fi/
- Setting: first-year molecular biology unit with more than 600 students; in 2025 an interactive oral assessment replaced a report component.
- What it supports: current evidence that oral formats are being implemented in large-cohort settings, not only seminar-size courses.
- Use in chapter: supports the claim that scale is a design/logistics problem rather than an automatic disqualifier.

### UCL Interaction Centre (2025), sampled viva proposal

- Source: https://www.ucl.ac.uk/uclic/news/2025/mar/times-higher-education-article-explores-ai-and-future-university-assessment
- What it supports: UCL researchers proposed sampled viva voce assessment as a scalable way to test comprehension and maintain engagement where full oral exams for every student may be too resource-intensive.
- Use in chapter: supports the chapter’s discussion of short or sampled oral defenses.
- Limitation: the sampled-viva argument is a proposal, not evidence of a fully validated large-scale system.

### Brandeis Center for Teaching and Learning, “Oral Assessments”

- Source: https://www.brandeis.edu/teaching/online-guides/assignments/oral-assessments.html
- What it supports: oral assessment can authenticate learning and deepen engagement but raises legitimate concerns about scale, anxiety, equity, linguistic background, and accessibility.
- Use in chapter: supports presenting oral assessment as a tradeoff rather than an AI-proof solution.

### Hartmann (2025), “Oral Exams for a Generative AI World”

- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1080/87567555.2025.2558563
- Journal: *College Teaching*.
- What it supports: humanities educators are actively reconsidering oral exams in response to the vulnerability of take-home writing to generative AI, with attention to practical concerns and logistics.
- Use in chapter: evidence that the oral return is emerging across disciplines, not only laboratory sciences.

### 2026 “AI Viva” / coauthorship-integrity research

- Source: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666920X26000718
- What it supports: researchers are exploring conversational systems that ask students questions about their submissions to gather evidence of epistemic ownership and comprehension.
- Use in chapter: supports the closing discussion of AI-assisted oral verification as an emerging area.
- Limitation: prototype/expert-evaluation evidence should not be treated as established validation for high-stakes automated oral assessment.

## Claims intentionally bounded

- Oral assessment is not described as AI-proof.
- The chapter does not claim oral performance is inherently fairer than writing.
- It explicitly notes anxiety, language, disability, examiner consistency, bias, staff time, and scalability as design constraints.
- It recommends oral evidence as one layer among written, supervised, project, observed, and longitudinal evidence.
- Automated or AI-run vivas are presented as an emerging possibility requiring validation and governance, not a recommended high-stakes replacement for human examiners.

## Follow-up reporting

- Interview instructors running oral assessment in cohorts above 500 students.
- Compare marking reliability between oral and written assessments.
- Find accommodation practices for speech disability, neurodivergence, language learners, and severe performance anxiety.
- Study whether short oral defenses improve the validity of AI-inclusive project assessment.
- Track whether sampled-viva policies meaningfully deter outsourcing without creating inequitable uncertainty for students.
