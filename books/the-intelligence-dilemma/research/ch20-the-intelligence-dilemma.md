# Chapter 20 research — The Intelligence Dilemma

## Core evidence carried into the synthesis

### National Academies — knowledge, mental models, motivation, and technology alignment

- **Source:** National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, *How People Learn II: Learners, Contexts, and Cultures* (2018).
- **Record:** https://nap.nationalacademies.org/catalog/24783/how-people-learn-ii-learners-contexts-and-cultures
- **Supports:** the conclusion's repeated distinction between performance and learning, the need for organized knowledge and mental models, the role of motivation/belonging, and the claim that educational technology should be aligned with learner, goal, context, support, and ongoing assessment.
- **Caution:** broad learning-science synthesis; it predates widespread generative AI.

### Bastani et al. — assistance can improve performance while weakening later independent learning

- **Source:** Hamsa Bastani et al., randomized field evidence on generative-AI assistance in high-school mathematics, recorded in the book's Chapter 5/6 research trails.
- **Book-local record:** `research/ch05-the-tutor-in-every-pocket.md` and `research/ch06-productive-struggle.md`
- **Supports:** one empirical version of the book's central dilemma: unrestricted access to a capable assistant can improve immediate task performance while the design of assistance affects what remains when assistance is removed.
- **Caution:** results are specific to the study population, subject, tool, and experimental conditions. They do not establish that generative AI generally harms learning.

### Kestin et al. and Tutor CoPilot — structured AI assistance can improve learning or tutoring

- **Sources:** Kestin et al. on a structured AI tutor in undergraduate physics; Tutor CoPilot randomized evidence on AI augmentation of live tutoring, recorded in Chapters 5 and 11.
- **Book-local records:** `research/ch05-the-tutor-in-every-pocket.md` and `research/ch11-thirty-teachers-for-thirty-students.md`
- **Supports:** the strong optimistic case preserved in the conclusion: AI can improve instruction, feedback, differentiation, and tutor effectiveness under well-designed conditions.
- **Caution:** these results should not be generalized to every model, subject, age group, or unsupervised use pattern.

### AERA, APA, NCME and TEQSA — trustworthy claims about learning require valid evidence

- **Sources:** *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing* (AERA/APA/NCME, 2014) and TEQSA's *Assessment Reform for the Age of Artificial Intelligence* (2023) plus implementation resources.
- **Records:** https://www.aera.net/publications/books/standards-for-educational-psychological-testing-2014-edition and https://www.teqsa.gov.au/guides-resources/resources/corporate-publications/assessment-reform-age-artificial-intelligence
- **Supports:** the conclusion's argument that schools make consequential capability claims and should repair the evidence behind those claims when AI weakens old proxies; also supports plural/contextualized assessment rather than a single detection-centered response.
- **Caution:** formal testing standards and Australian higher-education guidance are not universal K–12 prescriptions.

### Roorda et al. and UNESCO GEM — human relationship and contact remain educational variables

- **Sources:** Roorda et al. (2017) meta-analytic update on teacher-student relationships, engagement, and achievement; UNESCO GEM Report 2023 on technology in education.
- **Records:** https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1156223 and https://www.unesco.org/gem-report/en/publication/technology
- **Supports:** the conclusion's claim that human attention, engagement, belonging, and continued relationships remain part of the educational system even as explanation and routine feedback become cheaper.
- **Caution:** human presence is not intrinsically beneficial, and technology can improve access, privacy, patience, and support in cases where human environments fail.

## Falsification / counterevidence note

The book's central claim should weaken if abundant generative assistance reliably increases durable, transferable student capability in the same proportion that it increases visible output, with no meaningful loss of independent performance, assessment validity, source judgment, or educational relationship. It should also weaken if the distinction between assisted and independent capability turns out to have little practical value in the professions and civic settings schools prepare students to enter.

The conclusion intentionally preserves the opposite evidence. AI tutoring and augmentation can produce real learning gains; some old manual skills should disappear; tool-rich competence is authentic competence in many domains; human teachers can be biased or ineffective; and controlled independent assessment can become oppressive if overused. The narrower settlement is empirical rather than ideological: schools should organize assistance around whether students become more capable, collect evidence appropriate to the capability claim being made, and revise the balance as tools and evidence change.
