# Research Trail — Chapter 7: The Prompted Mind

## Purpose

Examine prompting as a form of task decomposition and cognitive delegation rather than a superficial new literacy. Distinguish active direction and evaluation from passive offloading.

## Sources read

### Risko & Gilbert (2016), “Cognitive Offloading”

- Source: *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 20(9), 676–688.
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2016.07.002
- What it supports: humans routinely use external actions and representations to reduce internal cognitive demand; offloading is a general feature of cognition rather than something created by AI.
- Use in chapter: provides the broader conceptual frame for notes, calculators, calendars, maps, and AI as external cognitive supports.
- Limitation: the review predates generative AI and does not directly establish educational effects of LLM use.

### “Cognitive offloading in student–AI collaboration: A longitudinal analysis of prompting strategies” (2026)

- Source: *Computers in Human Behavior Reports* 22, 101130.
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chbr.2026.101130
- Sample: 281 prompts from 122 submissions across four assignments.
- What it supports: students often accepted LLM output with little modification or critical engagement; stronger submissions showed more cohesive prompting patterns with context and clearer instructions, while lower-quality work tended toward low-effort, minimally contextualized prompts.
- Use in chapter: supports the observation that access to a conversational model does not automatically produce sophisticated direction or critique.
- Limitation: observational analysis of a specific assignment type involving synthetic social-network datasets; it should not be generalized to all students or all disciplines.

### Ding et al. (forthcoming 2027), “More vs. less cognitive offloading from AI assistants: impacts on novices’ collaborative performance and skill development”

- Source: *Information Processing & Management* 64(1), article 105046; published online in 2026 for the January 2027 issue.
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ipm.2026.105046
- Design: controlled experiment with 130 participants assigned to no-AI, high-offloading, moderate-offloading, or low-offloading assistance conditions in a legal-decision task.
- What it supports: high-offloading assistance produced the highest immediate accuracy and fastest decisions but weaker skill development; moderate and low offloading supported stronger skill gains. The authors identify metacognitive miscalibration as an important mechanism.
- Use in chapter: supports the argument that a user can appear highly effective while becoming less calibrated about underlying ability.
- Limitation: high-stakes legal-domain simulation, adult participants, and not a classroom study. Treat as a useful adjacent result rather than direct K-12 evidence.

### Tetzlaff & Simonsmeier (2025), expertise reversal meta-analysis

- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2025.102142
- What it supports: the amount of beneficial assistance depends on prior knowledge; novice and expert learners do not necessarily need the same instructional supports.
- Use in chapter: supports the claim that the same AI delegation pattern can be sensible for an expert and educationally premature for a novice.

## Claims intentionally bounded

- The chapter does not claim that prompting skill is unimportant; it argues that prompt wording is subordinate to problem framing, domain knowledge, evaluation, and verification.
- It does not treat cognitive offloading as inherently negative.
- It does not claim that AI use necessarily reduces independent thinking; the mechanism depends on how cognitive work is distributed.
- It distinguishes command at the interface from epistemic control over the result.

## Follow-up reporting

- Interview students who use AI intensively but differently: one as a production shortcut, another as a thought partner.
- Find experimental work measuring calibration between students’ perceived and actual ability after AI-assisted practice.
- Seek longitudinal evidence on whether repeated AI-assisted drafting changes independent writing or problem-framing ability.
- Investigate classroom designs that require students to document delegation decisions rather than raw prompt logs.
