# Revision pass 01 — Opening and Part I

## Purpose

This pass begins the editorial revision of *The Intelligence Dilemma* after the 20-chapter complete draft.

The first priority is not sentence polish. It is argument economy: reduce places where the opening states the same distinction several times before the reader has had a chance to experience it.

## Highest-impact opening issue

The front matter and Chapter 1 currently spend much of the same conceptual capital:

- the artifact is not the learning;
- generative AI can preserve the appearance of capability without preserving the capability;
- practice with assistance differs from proof of independent capability;
- some friction is educational and some is merely administrative;
- the central design question is which cognitive work the learner should still have to do.

All of these belong in the book. They do not all need to be fully argued twice before Chapter 2.

### Revision rule

Front matter should establish the stakes and the human dilemma, then stop before it becomes a miniature version of Chapters 1–5.

Chapter 1 should own the full “crisis of inference” argument: schools infer invisible capability from visible work, and AI can now manufacture more of the visible evidence without requiring the old path.

## Part I chapter jobs

### Front matter

**Job:** invite the reader into the problem and establish the book’s governing question.

Keep:
- answers have become cheap;
- AI can genuinely help and genuinely substitute;
- the book rejects both blanket prohibition and blanket permission;
- the governing test is whether the student becomes more capable.

Compress or defer:
- long catalogs of assessment, privacy, achievement-gap, and teacher-role consequences;
- extended practice/performance analogies;
- repeated explanations of useful versus useless friction;
- detailed design prescriptions that later chapters can earn.

### Chapter 1 — The Homework Machine

**Job:** prove the crisis-of-inference thesis at the assignment level.

Keep the strongest sequence:
1. old assignment;
2. plausible machine artifact;
3. missing path;
4. weak proxy becomes visible;
5. practice and proof separate;
6. schools need better evidence of capability.

Watch for repetition of the same thought under “artifact,” “thermometer,” “proxy,” “residue,” and “inference.” One or two metaphors should carry the argument; five weaken it.

### Chapter 2 — The End of the Blank Page

**Job:** move from institutional inference to student initiation and agency.

Protect the distinctive idea that AI changes the first commitment by supplying a frame before the learner has chosen one. This chapter should not re-argue the entire assessment problem.

### Chapter 3 — What a Grade Means Now

**Job:** move from the assignment to the credential.

Protect the distinction between open-tool performance and independent capability. Reduce any material that merely repeats Chapter 1’s take-home-assignment case unless it is needed to explain why grades and credentials are public trust mechanisms.

### Chapter 4 — The Confidence Gap

**Job:** move inside the learner.

This chapter earns its place when it focuses on metacognition, novice judgment, fluent error, and the difference between assisted performance and retained capability. Avoid restating general anti-cheating or assessment arguments already established.

### Chapter 5 — The Tutor in Every Pocket

**Job:** make the strongest affirmative case before Part II.

Keep the empirical contrast between unrestricted assistance and designed tutoring, and the teacher-augmentation evidence. The chapter should feel like an expansion of possibility, not a defensive concession after four cautionary chapters.

## Voice audit

Recurring risks across Part I:

- repeated `not X / but Y` antithesis;
- sequences of one-line paragraphs that behave like presentation slides;
- lists that restate the preceding paragraph instead of adding evidence;
- aphoristic summaries before the reader needs them;
- repeated use of the same professional analogies (pilot, athlete, musician, programmer) across adjacent chapters.

The goal is not to eliminate the book’s clipped rhythm. The rhythm is part of the voice. The revision should reserve it for turns that deserve emphasis.

## Evidence audit

Part I already contains chapter-specific research trails. Revision should preserve claim boundaries:

- do not generalize one model or one field experiment into “AI harms learning”;
- distinguish immediate task performance from later unaided performance;
- distinguish carefully engineered tutors from generic chat interfaces;
- keep equity claims framed as access-plus-capability questions, not assumptions that equal tool access equalizes outcomes;
- keep the strong positive case intact.

## Success test for this pass

After revision, a reader finishing Chapter 5 should be able to state the movement in one line per chapter without hearing the same chapter five times:

- **Front matter:** AI can deepen learning or cheaply preserve its appearance.
- **Ch 1:** that breaks the old inference from finished work to student capability.
- **Ch 2:** it also changes who makes the first consequential decisions.
- **Ch 3:** therefore grades must become clearer about what they certify.
- **Ch 4:** fluent assistance can make students less aware of what they do not know.
- **Ch 5:** designed well, the same technology can provide forms of tutoring that were previously scarce.
