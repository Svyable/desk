# 17 — A Treaty for Ambiguity

Arms control usually counts objects. Ambiguity is harder to count.

Yet the Able Archer record suggests that exercises, alert postures, communications changes and intelligence expectations can alter nuclear risk without changing the number of warheads. Risk reduction therefore needs protocols for meaning as well as inventories.

Hotlines, advance exercise notifications, launch notifications, data exchanges and crisis communications are all technologies for reducing semantic error. Their value is easy to underrate because success produces nothing. The message arrives; the exercise remains an exercise; nobody launches.

Future agreements should take machine-mediated warning seriously. States need not reveal sensitive algorithms to establish norms against cyber interference with nuclear command-and-control, discuss dangerous interactions between exercises and alert states, or create protected channels for rapid clarification of ambiguous aerospace events.

Verification is difficult and adversaries can lie. That objection is real. Communication is not trust. It is another independent channel whose information can be weighed against sensors and intelligence.

The goal is not to abolish ambiguity. Adversarial states will never share one reality perfectly. The goal is to prevent ambiguity from being forced, by doctrine and speed, to masquerade as certainty.
