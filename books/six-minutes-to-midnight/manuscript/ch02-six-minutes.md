# 2 — Six Minutes

Six minutes is long enough to discover a mistake and short enough to build a civilization around panic.

The 1979 record is useful because it resists melodrama. The State Department's briefing emphasized that no strategic nuclear units took steps toward advanced alert and that the warning was unambiguously identified as false in under six minutes. That is evidence of resilience, not merely danger. The redundancy worked.

But redundancy is not magic. It works when supposedly independent channels are actually independent, when operators know what disagreement means, when communications survive, and when doctrine permits uncertainty to remain uncertainty. Correlated failure is the enemy of every safety story. Two screens agreeing because they share a poisoned upstream feed are one witness wearing two hats.

The warning problem has a peculiar geometry. A defender wants early detection because incoming ballistic missiles compress decision time. Early detection, however, occurs when evidence is thinnest. Later evidence is better but arrives after options may have narrowed. The system therefore has to assign political meaning to incomplete physical observations. That is not a sensor problem. It is a decision problem wearing a sensor's uniform.

Cold War engineers attacked it with multiple phenomenologies: satellites, radars, communications paths, command centers, procedures. The valuable word is *phenomenologies*. A radar return and an infrared detection can fail differently. Diversity matters more than duplicated confidence.

This is why the 1979 event belongs beside aviation's insistence on independent instruments and medicine's insistence on confirmatory tests, but with one monstrous difference: a nuclear response can alter the evidence environment before anyone learns the original diagnosis was wrong. Once missiles leave silos, there is no laboratory correction.

The six minutes therefore should not be fetishized as a countdown. They are a measurement of epistemic recovery. The system was shown a coherent fiction and recovered contact with reality before irreversible action. Humanity's problem is preserving that recovery interval while military technology keeps trying to sell it for speed.
