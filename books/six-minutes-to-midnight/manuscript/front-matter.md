# Six Minutes to Midnight

## False Warning, Nuclear Command, and the Machinery That Must Never Be Certain

Sven Hardy Benson

### Author's note

This is a book about machines that announce the end of the world and the people required to distrust them.

It is not a book of miracle saves. The phrase *nuclear near miss* has been worn smooth by retelling. Some famous incidents are richly documented. Some are reconstructed from memoir and testimony. Some acquire an extra bomber, an extra minute, an extra trembling hand every time they cross the internet. The distinction matters because the subject is already frightening enough without embroidery.

The stranger fact is institutional. Nuclear warning systems have repeatedly produced false indications while functioning partly as designed. Redundancy caught errors. Independent sensors contradicted computers. Procedures created pauses. People noticed that the world outside the screen did not match the world inside it. These are not embarrassing details around an otherwise perfect machine. They are the machine.

Four evidence classes are used in the research apparatus: documented facts; credible but incomplete testimony/reporting; disputed allegations; and speculative synthesis. Counterfactual statements—*war would have begun if X had done Y*—are treated as counterfactuals, not historical events.

The book's argument is narrower and more urgent than the folklore. Civilization has built systems in which minutes separate observation from decisions that cannot be recalled. Faster sensors and better classifiers do not abolish that political fact. They can make it worse by making a mistaken picture more internally persuasive. The safety problem is therefore not simply accuracy. It is organized doubt.
