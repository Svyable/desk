# 4 — Two Clocks

Nuclear warning runs on two clocks. The first measures incoming weapons. The second measures understanding.

Military technology rewards shrinking the first: faster detection, faster communications, faster decision support. Human safety often requires lengthening the second. A radar track accumulates. A second sensor reports. Someone calls the anomaly by the right name. Someone else notices that an exercise is running. Context arrives late.

Launch-under-attack concepts make the conflict obvious. If survivable forces are believed vulnerable, leaders may feel pressure to decide before incoming warheads arrive. But the earlier the decision, the less mature the evidence. A posture intended to preserve retaliation can therefore purchase military optionality with epistemic debt.

This is not an argument that any one state's current doctrine mechanically launches on warning; public doctrine, operational plans and presidential choices are not identical. It is an argument about incentives. Short flight times make ambiguous warning more politically expensive.

The humane engineering question is consequently strange: how much time can a system deliberately refuse to optimize away? Submarine survivability, mobile forces, hardened communications and other second-strike capabilities matter partly because they can buy cognition. A weapon that survives the first blow can be, paradoxically, a machine for making slower decisions.

Deterrence literature usually prices survivability in warheads. It should also price it in minutes.
