# 10 — False Alarms Are Not All Alike

The phrase *false alarm* hides too much.

A training tape crossing an operational boundary is not a failed electronic component. A satellite optical misclassification is not an adversary's misreading of an exercise. A cyber intrusion would not be any of those. They can all end with the same sentence on a screen.

A useful taxonomy begins with source: simulation contamination; hardware failure; software/data failure; environmental mimic; human reporting error; intelligence misinterpretation; adversarial deception; cyber compromise; and correlated combinations. Then ask which independent evidence can falsify each class.

This matters because redundancy against one class may increase vulnerability to another. Networking sensors improves fusion while creating common software and cyber dependencies. Standardizing displays reduces operator confusion while allowing one semantic error to propagate consistently. Machine learning may reduce nuisance alarms while making residual alerts appear unusually authoritative.

Safety engineering has a bad habit of counting boxes. Three sensors sounds safer than one. The correct question is how many ways of being wrong remain independent.

Nuclear warning is ultimately an exercise in failure diversity. The architecture must assume that the next false world will not resemble the last one closely enough to be caught by its patch.
