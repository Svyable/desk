# 14 — Cyberwar Enters the Warning Room

Cold War false alarms were frightening partly because nobody had to be attacking the warning system.

Cyber conflict adds an adversary who may deliberately manufacture the appearance of failure, attack, or uncertainty. Public officials now describe nuclear command, control and communications as facing cyber and space vulnerabilities alongside aging architecture. Details of current defenses are rightly not public; this chapter stays at the architectural level.

A sophisticated attacker need not forge a perfect missile attack. It may be enough to degrade one channel so leaders distrust real warning, or to create contradictory evidence during a political crisis. Availability and credibility are both targets.

This changes the meaning of redundancy. Multiple sensors sharing software supply chains, timing sources, networks or identity systems may be physically diverse but digitally correlated. Independence has to be audited across dependencies invisible on an organizational chart.

There is also a strategic ambiguity problem. If warning data looks corrupted, leaders may not know whether they are seeing malfunction, espionage, preparation for conventional attack, or preparation for nuclear attack. Cyber operations can make uncertainty itself look hostile.

The design objective is not an unhackable system. Such a claim would be unserious. It is a system whose uncertainty degrades gracefully: compromised components can be isolated; provenance remains legible; alternative channels survive; and political leaders retain retaliatory capability without having to resolve every cyber mystery in minutes.
