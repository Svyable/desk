# 9 — Perroots Does Nothing

Lieutenant General Leonard Perroots left behind one of the most valuable sentences in the nuclear archive because it is a question.

His later end-of-tour account looked back at Soviet military reactions during Able Archer and asked what might have happened if the United States had responded to the Soviet alert with its own precautionary generation of forces. Declassified material describes Soviet preparations that included readiness connected to immediate nuclear use. Perroots did not recommend mirroring the apparent escalation.

Doing nothing is difficult to record as an action. Bureaucracies preserve launches, orders, deployments, procurement. Restraint leaves a thinner fossil.

Yet restraint can be causal. If one side interprets the other's readiness as confirmation of its fears, reciprocal precaution becomes an amplifier. Each step is defensible locally. The sequence is insane globally.

The counterargument deserves weight: military forces routinely alter readiness, and not every alert is a staircase to Armageddon. Overstating Able Archer can teach the wrong lesson by making ordinary preparedness seem inherently reckless. The relevant variable is context—what the adversary is believed to believe about the signal.

Perroots's question should be installed in every automated escalation system: what will the other machine think this defensive action means?
