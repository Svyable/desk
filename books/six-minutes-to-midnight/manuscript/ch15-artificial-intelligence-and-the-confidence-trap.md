# 15 — Artificial Intelligence and the Confidence Trap

Artificial intelligence is attractive in nuclear command for the same reason it is dangerous there: too much data arrives too quickly for unaided humans.

Public U.S. Strategic Command remarks have described AI as useful for processing large volumes of information while insisting humans remain in control. That is an important boundary. It is not sufficient by itself.

A human can remain formally 'in the loop' while becoming functionally subordinate to machine confidence. If the model fuses hundreds of streams into one polished assessment, the human may have neither time nor access to reconstruct the dissenting evidence. Authority remains human on paper; cognition has migrated.

The confidence trap is worse when a model is usually right. Operators learn calibrated trust from ordinary performance, then confront an out-of-distribution crisis exactly when training data is least representative. Nuclear war is, mercifully, a domain without a rich labeled dataset of real strategic exchanges.

AI should therefore be used aggressively for tasks that expand human epistemic bandwidth—retrieval, anomaly surfacing, provenance tracing, alternative-hypothesis generation, simulation—while being prevented from silently collapsing heterogeneous evidence into an irreversible recommendation.

The relevant metric is not merely classification accuracy. Ask whether the system makes a rare wrong answer harder or easier to challenge. A model that is 99.99 percent accurate and rhetorically irresistible may be less safe than a noisier system that keeps its uncertainty inspectable.

Human control is not a hand on a button. It is the continued ability to disagree for reasons.
