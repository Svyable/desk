# 7 — RYaN

By 1981 Soviet intelligence was being ordered to look for preparations for a Western nuclear first strike. The operation usually rendered as RYaN turned fear into collection requirements.

Declassified and former Soviet-bloc records published by the National Security Archive show KGB instructions to watch indicators of attack preparation. A February 1983 directive warned that a strike might be concealed under maneuvers or training. This matters because intelligence requirements do not merely discover reality. They decide which fragments of reality become salient.

If analysts are told that an exercise may be camouflage, an exercise stops being reassuring evidence of routine behavior. If unusual communications may indicate launch preparation, improved communications security can look ominous. The hypothesis begins eating its controls.

This is a classic problem of intelligence under adversarial conditions: the enemy really may hide preparations. Paranoia and prudence share instruments. The question becomes how an organization can search seriously for deception without making every absence of evidence evidence of better deception.

RYaN is documented. Claims about exactly how sincerely every Soviet leader believed imminent war varied across people and time. The records show genuine concern at important levels, not a single Soviet mind.

That nuance makes 1983 more frightening, not less. Catastrophe does not require universal panic. It requires enough frightened nodes, enough ambiguous signals and enough procedures that convert concern into readiness. A civilization can be endangered by a minority interpretation if that interpretation controls the right switches.
