## Working chronology

- **1978** — Carter administration officials raise concerns about U.S. strategic C3I after NORAD review and crisis-management exercises.
- **9 Nov 1979** — NORAD test scenario enters operational warning distribution; false attack identified in under six minutes.
- **13 Nov 1979** — Soviet Ambassador Dobrynin conveys Brezhnev's concern over the false alert.
- **3 Jun 1980** — U.S. computer-system problems generate erroneous missile-attack indications; direct sensors contradict them.
- **1981** — Soviet intelligence emphasis on detecting Western preparations for nuclear attack intensifies under the program known as RYaN.
- **17 Feb 1983** — KGB directive describes maneuvers/training as possible cover for attack preparation.
- **26 Sep 1983** — Reported Soviet Oko early-warning false alarm associated with Stanislav Petrov; details require continued primary-source work.
- **2–11 Nov 1983** — Able Archer 83 practices NATO nuclear-release procedures amid elevated Soviet concern.
- **Jan 1989** — Retiring DIA director Leonard Perroots circulates his end-of-tour addendum on the danger of the 1983 war scare.
- **15 Feb 1990** — PFIAB review assesses the Soviet war scare and the possibility U.S.-Soviet relations had been placed on a dangerous trigger.
- **2024** — U.S. Strategic Command publicly discusses NC3 modernization, cyber/space vulnerabilities and AI decision support while insisting on human control.
- **2025** — National Security Archive republishes Able Archer records removed from a republished State Department FRUS volume, making the politics of historical secrecy part of the evidence story.

## Research appendix: the experiment humanity cannot run

Nuclear command safety has a validation problem. We cannot create thousands of realistic strategic crises and observe which architectures avoid accidental war. Historical near-misses are sparse, selected and politically distorted. Simulations cannot fully reproduce fear, deception or irreversible consequence.

The research program therefore has to triangulate: incident reconstruction; human-factors experiments; red-team exercises; formal reliability analysis; dependency mapping; cyber testing; decision-science work on automation bias; and comparison with other safety-critical domains. None is enough alone.

The most valuable future public scholarship would reconstruct false-warning incidents as causal graphs rather than anecdotes: initial physical event, sensor transformations, software paths, human interpretations, communications, doctrine, available independent checks, actions taken, actions considered, and the exact mechanism of recovery. The question is not who was heroic. It is which barrier kept uncertainty alive.
