# 5 — The Satellite Sees the Sun

The Soviet early-warning episode associated with Stanislav Petrov has become a secular saint story: machine says missiles, man says no, world survives. The bones are important; the varnish needs scraping.

Retrospective accounts describe the Oko satellite system reporting a small number of U.S. missile launches on 26 September 1983. Petrov, duty officer at Serpukhov-15, judged the warning false. Later technical explanations implicated sunlight reflected from high clouds viewed in a geometry that confused the satellite algorithm. Ground radar did not confirm an attack.

Compared with the American 1979 and 1980 incidents, the public primary record is thinner and much of the familiar narrative comes through later interviews and reporting. That places details in the credible-but-incomplete class unless independently documented. It does not make the episode useless. It makes its epistemology part of the story.

The striking feature is the reported attack shape. A tiny salvo made little strategic sense as an American first strike. Petrov appears to have reasoned about adversary behavior as well as machine output. That is both reassuring and terrifying. Safety depended not only on sensing physics but on a human model of what the enemy would rationally do.

Models of enemies can be wrong. So can models of clouds. The point is not that intuition beats algorithms. It is that heterogeneous doubt—physical, strategic, procedural—can prevent one elegant error from becoming the whole world.
