# Six Minutes to Midnight

## False Warning, Nuclear Command, and the Machinery That Must Never Be Certain

**Sven Hardy Benson**

### Thesis

Nuclear command systems are built around an impossible asymmetry: they must detect a real attack quickly enough to preserve options while refusing to turn ambiguous machine output into irreversible catastrophe. The neglected history is not merely a catalogue of lucky escapes. The 1979 NORAD training-tape incident, the 1980 computer-chip failures, Soviet warning failures and the Able Archer 83 war scare show several different ways a technically sophisticated system can manufacture conviction. As early warning absorbs machine learning, cyber defense, proliferated satellites and faster delivery systems, the central problem is not whether automation becomes more accurate. It is whether institutions preserve enough friction, independent sensing, dissent and time for a human being to say: the machine is coherent, and the machine may still be wrong.

### Evidence posture

This manuscript distinguishes four classes throughout: **documented/verified facts**, **credible but incomplete reporting or testimony**, **disputed or unconfirmed claims**, and **speculative synthesis/open conjecture**. It does not treat popular near-miss stories as interchangeable. The 1979 and 1980 U.S. false alerts have strong official documentary records. Able Archer is unusually rich in declassified U.S., British and former Soviet-bloc material, but counterfactual claims that nuclear war was literally minutes away remain interpretations. Accounts of Stanislav Petrov are historically important but depend more heavily on retrospective testimony and later reporting than the NORAD cases. Claims about present classified NC3 architecture are bounded to public official material.
