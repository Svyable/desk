Why the World's Most Liquid Markets Keep Disappearing When You Need Them Most.

The working thesis is that liquidity is not a property of an asset but a behavior of the people and machines currently willing to trade it — a standing offer, renewed voluntarily every few seconds, that can be withdrawn the moment continuing to offer it stops being profitable. Modern markets have never displayed more apparent liquidity: narrower spreads, deeper-looking order books, instant execution on an ordinary day. They have also, again and again, discovered that the very mechanisms producing that ordinary-day liquidity — algorithmic market making, ETF share-creation arbitrage, leveraged hedging programs, correlated quantitative models — are conditional, self-interested, and capable of withdrawing in near-unison exactly when a market needs them most.

The book traces that pattern from the portfolio-insurance spiral of Black Monday 1987 through the 2010 Flash Crash, the 2007 "quant quake," the March 2020 dash for cash that broke the U.S. Treasury market, the UK's 2022 pension gilt crisis, the GameStop squeeze, and the collapse of Terra/Luna and FTX, before turning to the open question of what happens to this fragility as trading decisions increasingly pass to autonomous, correlated AI agents. The aim is not to predict the next crisis. It is to make the underlying mechanism — apparent abundance built on conditional, correlated, technologically mediated promises — visible enough that a reader can recognize it before the bid disappears again.
