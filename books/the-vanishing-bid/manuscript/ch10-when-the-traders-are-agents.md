# When the Traders Are Agents

Knight Capital Group had, by the standards of August 2012, one of the more sophisticated automated trading operations on Wall Street, and it took the firm about forty-five minutes to destroy itself with a single software deployment error. A technician rolling out new order-routing code for a New York Stock Exchange program failed to copy the update to one of eight production servers. On that server, a piece of old, dormant code from a discontinued internal function reactivated, repurposing a flag nobody had intended to still be live, and the moment markets opened at 9:30 that morning, the server began firing an escalating stream of erroneous orders into the market. By the time engineers traced the problem and shut the system down, Knight's software had executed more than four million trades across a hundred and fifty-four stocks, moving roughly $7.65 billion in notional value through the market and leaving the firm holding positions it could not unwind without enormous loss. Closing them out cost approximately $440 million, several times the firm's quarterly earnings, and Knight's stock fell by three-quarters in the days that followed. Only an emergency $400 million rescue financing, arranged within the week and diluting existing shareholders down to a minority stake, kept the firm alive long enough to be acquired a few months later by a rival trading firm. Nobody had programmed Knight's system to lose $440 million in forty-five minutes. The system had simply done, extremely fast and without judgment, exactly what a small, unnoticed error told it to do, at a scale and speed no human supervisor could interrupt before the damage was already done.

Knight's collapse belongs in this book's final chapter because it is the clearest available preview of a question the rest of the chapters have circled without quite being able to answer directly: what happens when the trading decisions supplying a market's liquidity are made not by humans applying judgment, nor even by the relatively narrow, purpose-built algorithms that drove the Flash Crash or the quant quake, but by more general, more autonomous systems capable of a wider range of independent action. That question, speculative even a few years ago, has stopped being hypothetical. In July 2025, Bloomberg reported that Man Group, the world's largest publicly listed hedge fund, had built an internal system called AlphaGPT that generates, codes, and backtests trading ideas largely on its own, and that several dozen of the signals it produced had cleared the firm's investment committee and were slated for live deployment with real capital. JPMorgan, separately, built an internal generative-AI platform called LLM Suite, running on models from outside providers and connected to the bank's internal data, and filed a patent application for a system that would generate analyst-style stock ratings directly from company fundamentals, market data, and sentiment. Neither of these is evidence that autonomous AI systems have quietly taken over trading floors; both are, more precisely, evidence that large, sophisticated financial firms are actively building toward exactly that, treating the technology as worth real institutional investment rather than a novelty.

The financial regulators who spend their careers thinking about systemic risk have noticed, and their public statements so far strike a notably careful tone, closer to structured concern than alarm. The Financial Stability Board, the international body that coordinates banking and market regulators across the G20, published a report in November 2024 concluding that rapid AI adoption could amplify existing vulnerabilities in the financial system — concentration among a small number of technology vendors, correlated behavior across market participants, and the model risk that comes from many firms relying on similar underlying tools — while stopping well short of predicting a specific crisis. A follow-up report the FSB released in October 2025 was, if anything, more modest in its claims, noting candidly that most national financial authorities are still at an early stage of even monitoring how AI is being used in their markets, and that regulators do not yet share an agreed definition of what counts as "AI" for the purpose of that monitoring — an admission that the tracking apparatus lags meaningfully behind the technology it is meant to watch. The International Monetary Fund's October 2024 Global Financial Stability Report devoted a full chapter to the question and, in outreach to market participants themselves, found that professional traders and risk managers most frequently named herding and market concentration as the AI-related risks that worried them most, warning that AI-enhanced strategies could deepen the feedback loop this book has traced repeatedly: falling prices triggering more selling, triggering more deleveraging, triggering further price declines.

The Bank of England has gone further than most of its peers in making the concern concrete. An April 2025 financial stability note laid out several distinct channels through which AI could threaten market functioning, explicitly identifying AI-driven herding as a systemic risk capable of producing rapid, correlated selling across multiple, seemingly unrelated asset classes during a period of stress — language that reads, more or less, as a direct restatement of what happened to quantitative hedge funds in August 2007, except aimed forward at a technology still being built out rather than backward at one already tested by a crisis. In a speech at the European Central Bank's Sintra forum on June 30, 2026, the Bank's deputy governor Sarah Breeden pushed the warning further still, arguing that autonomous, "agentic" AI trading systems — agents capable of taking independent action rather than simply generating a recommendation for a human to approve — could amplify volatility sharply in a period of stress and, in a severe enough scenario, contribute to what she described as a market meltdown if enough of those agents behaved identically at the same moment. She cited a Cambridge survey finding that more than half of finance firms already used some form of agentic AI in their operations, and floated the possibility of market-wide circuit breakers or "kill switch" mechanisms specifically designed to interrupt trading if autonomous systems began reinforcing each other's behavior faster than human supervisors could intervene — the same basic idea, translated into a newer technology, that exchanges had already built into their infrastructure after 2010, when the Flash Crash first demonstrated how fast an automated cascade could move.

It would be a disservice to the argument this book has been building, and to the actual state of the evidence, to present that warning as a settled conclusion rather than a genuinely open and contested question. A 2025 Federal Reserve working paper, testing how large language model-based trading agents behaved in laboratory replications of classic economic herding experiments, found something that complicates the simpler version of the AI-herding narrative: under baseline conditions, the AI agents relied more heavily on their own private information and herded less than human subjects typically do in the same experiments, a genuinely counterintuitive result given how much popular commentary assumes AI systems will simply copy one another. The same agents could, however, be induced to herd more readily when explicitly instructed to maximize profit rather than accuracy, a finding that points toward incentive design, not the underlying technology itself, as the more decisive variable. Europe's securities regulator, ESMA, reached a similarly measured conclusion in its own research through 2024 and into early 2026, finding that AI adoption in EU securities markets had not yet produced anything resembling the disruptive overhaul some commentary predicted, and identifying concentration among a small number of AI model and infrastructure providers, rather than trading-floor herding specifically, as its primary concern. None of this means the Bank of England's warning is overstated. It means the honest state of the research, as this book goes to press, is that regulators can see the shape of a new correlated-behavior risk clearly enough to start building safeguards against it, without yet having the kind of hard evidence — a documented incident, a Flash Crash of AI agents — that would let anyone say with confidence how it will actually manifest, or when.

What can be said with more confidence is the structural argument this entire book has been assembling piece by piece, and agentic AI trading, whatever form it ultimately takes, does not obviously break that argument so much as extend it into a new and faster medium. Every liquidity crisis this book has examined shared a common shape: apparent depth built by many participants each pursuing what looked, from the inside, like an independent, well-reasoned strategy, which turned out under stress to be far more correlated than anyone had modeled, because the participants were drawing on the same underlying data, the same academic factors, the same regulatory constraints, or the same crowded trade. Portfolio insurers in 1987 were, in effect, running the same formula. The quant funds of August 2007 were leaning on overlapping signals derived from the same decades of academic finance literature. If a meaningful share of future trading decisions comes to be made by AI systems trained on similar architectures, similar training data, and similar objective functions, there is no obvious reason to assume those systems will turn out to be more genuinely independent of one another than the human-designed quantitative strategies that have already, more than once, discovered they were not. The optimistic case, and it deserves to be stated fairly, is that AI systems capable of processing more information faster than human traders might actually reduce herding, exactly as the Fed's own working paper found under some conditions — by relying on genuine private analysis rather than following the herd out of the same fear and incentive pressure that drives human panic. The caution this book leaves the reader with is narrower and, by now, familiar: whichever way it turns out, the market will look perfectly liquid, with tight spreads and deep-looking order books, right up until the moment a large enough share of the agents supplying that liquidity discover, simultaneously, that continuing to offer it is no longer what their models tell them to do.
