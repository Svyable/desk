# Liquidity Theater

TerraUSD was supposed to be boring. That was the point. A "stablecoin" is meant to trade at exactly one dollar, forever, so that people can move money around a cryptocurrency ecosystem without taking on the price swings that make bitcoin unsuitable as a unit of account. Most stablecoins hold that peg the way a bank holds a deposit: with actual dollars, or short-term government debt, sitting in a reserve account somewhere, redeemable on demand. TerraUSD, known by its ticker UST, held its peg with an argument instead. Burn one dollar of UST and the protocol would mint one dollar's worth of a sister token called LUNA; burn a dollar of LUNA and it would mint a dollar of UST. If UST ever traded below a dollar, the theory went, arbitrageurs would buy the cheap UST, burn it for a dollar of LUNA, and pocket the difference — buying pressure that would push UST back to peg as a side effect of ordinary greed. The mechanism required no reserve because it manufactured its own collateral out of a second token whose only backing was confidence in the first.

Confidence was the entire balance sheet, and for a while it was an extraordinarily generous one. Terra's dominant application, a lending platform called Anchor Protocol, paid depositors who parked their UST there an advertised yield of roughly 20 percent a year, a rate that had nothing to do with what borrowers on the platform were actually paying and everything to do with a subsidy Terraform Labs kept topping up out of its own reserves. Twenty percent on a "stable" dollar-pegged asset is not a market rate; it is a marketing budget. It worked. By the spring of 2022, Anchor held roughly $12.7 billion in deposits, making it one of the largest lending platforms anywhere in decentralized finance, and UST's promise of stability, subsidized so heavily that it stopped being a promise about markets and became a promise about Terraform Labs' willingness to keep paying, had made it the third-largest stablecoin in the world.

Then, in the first week of May 2022, Terraform Labs did something that looks, in hindsight, like the founder unplugging his own life-support system to test it. The team withdrew roughly $150 million of UST from its trading pool on Curve, the largest decentralized exchange for stablecoins, intending to seed a new and deeper pool elsewhere. Pulling that liquidity out first, rather than adding the new pool alongside it, left the existing Curve pool thinner than it had been in months — a market that had absorbed large trades comfortably a week earlier was now considerably easier to knock off balance. Within days, a handful of large wallets sold UST into that thinned-out pool, in trades cumulatively worth well over a hundred million dollars, and the peg cracked: UST slipped to around 98.5 cents. Blockchain forensics firms who later reconstructed the trades found no evidence of a single coordinated attacker pulling the trigger; a few independent, large holders appear to have simply noticed the same weakness and traded on it, which is arguably a worse indictment of the system than a conspiracy would have been. A conspiracy implies the mechanism could withstand ordinary market behavior. This did not.

Once the peg cracked, the theory that was supposed to fix it became the thing that destroyed it. Holders raced to redeem their de-pegging UST for a full dollar's worth of freshly minted LUNA before the exit got any more crowded, and the protocol, doing exactly what it had been built to do, minted LUNA to meet every redemption. LUNA's supply, which had sat around 350 million tokens on May 10, passed 6.5 trillion tokens by May 13 — an inflation of roughly eighteen thousand-fold in three days — and a token whose supply is expanding at that rate has, for practical purposes, no floor. LUNA's price collapsed toward zero, dragging the value of every dollar of UST redeemed for it down with it, which made holding UST even more dangerous, which produced more redemptions, which minted more LUNA. Terra's Luna Foundation Guard spent through a multi-billion-dollar reserve of bitcoin and other crypto trying to buy back UST and hold the line; the reserve did not survive contact with the spiral it was meant to stop. Within about a week, the Terra ecosystem had destroyed on the order of forty billion dollars of value that had, a few weeks earlier, looked like one of the more solid footholds in a volatile asset class. Do Kwon, the Terraform Labs founder who had built and evangelized the mechanism, fled the collapse rather than face it; he was arrested at an airport in Montenegro in March 2023 carrying a forged passport, extradited to the United States at the end of 2024, and in December 2025 was sentenced to fifteen years in prison after pleading guilty to fraud, in a case a federal judge described as an epic scheme built on a yield the company knew it could not sustain on its own.

What Terra offered was liquidity theater: a dollar peg that behaved like a genuinely liquid, redeemable asset for as long as nobody tested it, backed not by a reserve but by a promise that other people would keep believing in the promise. The distance between that and the rest of this book's examples is smaller than it first appears. A bond ETF's share price also depends on the assumption that authorized participants will keep doing the arbitrage that ties it to the underlying bonds; a leveraged pension hedge also depends on the assumption that a margin call can always be met with an orderly sale. Terra simply made the dependency explicit, wrote it into a smart contract, and ran it without a central bank, a Bank of England, or a Federal Reserve standing by to intervene when the arithmetic stopped cooperating. There was no lender of last resort for TerraUSD. There was only Curve, and Curve was not deep enough.

Terra's collapse did not stay contained to Terra. A crypto hedge fund called Three Arrows Capital had put a large bet on LUNA and, by its own account, lost roughly $200 million when the token collapsed — enough, combined with the fund's other leveraged positions, to push it into default on a loan from the crypto lender Voyager Digital in late June 2022. Voyager filed for bankruptcy within days. Another lender, Genesis, had exposure to Three Arrows large enough that its parent company had to inject fresh capital to keep it standing, and a third, BlockFi, was hit hard enough by the same contagion that it drew down an emergency $400 million credit line from a crypto exchange that was, for the moment, still standing and still willing to play lender of last resort to an industry that had no other one: FTX.

That backstop lasted six months. On November 2, 2022, a reporter at the crypto news outlet CoinDesk published the contents of a leaked balance sheet belonging to Alameda Research, the trading firm founded by FTX's chief executive, Sam Bankman-Fried. Of Alameda's roughly $14.6 billion in listed assets, close to $5.8 billion turned out to be FTT, the exchange token FTX itself had created and could, in principle, print more of at will. A firm whose supposed net worth was substantially built from a token its own sister company issued was not holding capital in any sense a bank examiner would recognize; it was holding an IOU from itself. Four days later, the chief executive of rival exchange Binance, which held a large stock of FTT from an earlier investment in FTX, tweeted that Binance would liquidate its position, citing recent revelations and, pointedly, the lessons of Terra's collapse a few months earlier. The tweet did what tweets do to confidence-based systems: it collapsed one. Customers who had never read Alameda's balance sheet and did not need to now had a single, legible signal that something was wrong, and they withdrew roughly six billion dollars from FTX in the following three days. FTX halted withdrawals on November 8. A hastily arranged rescue acquisition by Binance fell apart within twenty-four hours once Binance's own team looked at FTX's books. On November 11, 2022, FTX and more than a hundred affiliated entities filed for bankruptcy, revealing a shortfall of roughly eight billion dollars between what the exchange owed its customers and what it actually had — the difference having been quietly funneled to Alameda for trading, investment, and, prosecutors would later show, outright misappropriation. Bankman-Fried was convicted on all seven counts he faced and sentenced, in March 2024, to twenty-five years in prison.

Regulators and reporters have spent the years since sorting fraud from mechanism in both stories, and the distinction matters for assigning blame, but it matters less for the argument of this book. Whether the operator behind a liquidity promise is lying about the collateral, as prosecutors showed Bankman-Fried was, or has simply built a mechanism that cannot survive its own success, as Terra's design ultimately could not, the promise looks identical from the outside right up until the moment it breaks. Both episodes advertised something markets have offered, in one form or another, for centuries: instant, dollar-for-dollar liquidity on demand. Both delivered it reliably for as long as almost nobody asked to redeem in size at the same time. And both are a preview, at a smaller and faster scale, of a question this book keeps returning to in older and more regulated markets: what a "safe," "liquid," dollar-equivalent asset is actually worth once everyone tries to treat it as one on the same afternoon.
