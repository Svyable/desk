# The Machines Take the Book

For most of the two decades after the 1987 crash, the transformation of American stock trading proceeded the way most infrastructure changes do: gradually enough that almost nobody outside the industry noticed it happening. Stock prices, quoted for two centuries in fractions of a dollar, converted to decimals between 2000 and 2001, immediately shrinking the minimum spread a market maker could charge from a sixteenth of a dollar to a single cent and squeezing out much of the easy money that had made floor-based market making a comfortable business. Electronic exchanges and alternative trading venues multiplied. A new category of firm emerged, staffed less by traders in the traditional sense than by physicists, mathematicians, and engineers who wrote code that could quote, requote, and cancel prices thousands of times a second across thousands of securities simultaneously. By the time the New York Stock Exchange formally phased out most of its remaining human specialists in favor of electronic designated market makers, the transition had already happened in substance if not in name: the majority of the liquidity in American equities was being supplied by algorithms competing against other algorithms, at speeds no human trader could follow, let alone match.

The efficiency gains were real and are worth stating plainly before the rest of this chapter complicates them. Spreads on liquid stocks fell for years. The cost of executing an ordinary trade dropped for essentially everyone, retail investors included, and high-frequency market makers deserve real credit for that: firms racing each other to quote tighter prices produced tighter prices, exactly as competitive theory predicts. What changed less visibly was the nature of the obligation behind those quotes. The old NYSE specialist system, whatever its flaws, had rested on a formal rule: a designated market maker was required to maintain a fair and orderly market in an assigned stock, using its own capital, even when doing so was uncomfortable. The new system rested on incentives instead. An electronic market maker quotes because quoting is profitable, continuously re-evaluates that calculation many times a second, and has no rule compelling it to keep quoting once the calculation turns against it. Regulators had traded an obligation for an equilibrium, betting that competition among many fast, self-interested firms would produce liquidity at least as reliably as a single obligated specialist had. On an ordinary day, it did. May 6, 2010, was not an ordinary day.

The morning had already been tense — European markets were absorbing worry over Greece's mounting debt crisis — when, at 2:32 in the afternoon Eastern time, a mutual fund complex called Waddell & Reed began executing a large hedge, selling seventy-five thousand E-mini S&P 500 futures contracts, worth roughly $4.1 billion, through an automated execution algorithm. The algorithm had one instruction: sell at a pace equal to nine percent of the trading volume measured over the previous minute, without regard to price or time. It was not, by the standards of institutional trading, an unusually reckless instruction. It was the kind of participation-rate algorithm that large funds used routinely to avoid dumping an order all at once and moving the market against themselves. But nine percent of trailing volume adjusts upward as volume itself rises, and as the algorithm's own selling began pushing prices down and drawing in more trading activity from everyone reacting to the falling market, the algorithm read that activity as license to sell faster still, completing the entire seventy-five-thousand-contract order in about twenty minutes — the largest net position change by any single trader in that futures contract all year, compressed into a window where the market's capacity to absorb it was already deteriorating.

What happened next is where the new, algorithm-supplied liquidity revealed a weakness the old specialist system had never had to face at this speed. High-frequency trading firms, holding no obligation to provide a backstop and pursuing strategies built to avoid accumulating risk, responded to the falling futures market exactly as their code had been designed to: by rapidly buying and reselling the same contracts to each other, passing the exposure along rather than absorbing it, in a pattern regulators later dubbed the hot-potato effect. In a fourteen-second window beginning at 2:45:13 p.m., these firms traded more than twenty-seven thousand contracts among themselves — nearly half of all trading in the entire market during that stretch — while their combined net inventory grew by only about two hundred contracts. Almost all of that furious trading activity added no actual buying pressure to the market; it was liquidity that looked present on a screen full of rapidly updating quotes but evaporated the instant anyone tried to rely on it, because every firm holding it was simultaneously trying to pass it to someone else. The Dow Jones Industrial Average fell nearly a thousand points in minutes, a decline of roughly nine percent, before recovering most of the loss within about half an hour. In the chaos, individual stocks briefly detached entirely from reality: shares of Accenture, a consulting firm with no particular reason to be in trouble that afternoon, traded down to a single penny. Some blue-chip names printed trades as high as one hundred thousand dollars a share, prices generated not by any buyer actually willing to pay that much but by automated stub quotes — placeholder orders market makers leave sitting far from the real price, never expecting them to be hit, that became the only bid or offer left standing once every genuine participant had stepped back from the market entirely. Exchanges later canceled roughly twenty thousand trades across some three hundred securities as too disconnected from reality to stand.

The joint report the SEC and the CFTC released that September identified the Waddell & Reed algorithm and the hot-potato dynamic among high-frequency firms as the operative mechanism, a conclusion built on the same underlying insight the Brady Commission had reached about 1987: a market's apparent depth can be a kind of accounting fiction, present in the data because the same unit of risk gets counted every time it changes hands in a chain of instantaneous trades, even though no single participant is actually willing to hold it. Five years later, in 2015, the CFTC and the Department of Justice charged a London-based trader named Navinder Singh Sarao, working from a modified trading program at his parents' house, with a form of manipulation called spoofing — placing large orders he never intended to execute, then canceling them, to create a false impression of supply and nudge prices in a direction that benefited a separate, genuine position — and framed his conduct as a contributing cause of the Flash Crash. Sarao pleaded guilty and was ultimately sentenced to time served, a modest outcome that reflected, among other things, his cooperation with investigators and a diagnosed condition affecting his ability to understand the trial's proceedings. What is worth noting, and what popular accounts of the case often skip, is that peer-reviewed research published after his prosecution — including a detailed reconstruction by finance academics using the same audit-trail data regulators had access to — found his individual actions did not appear central to the crash's severity or timing, whatever their independent illegality. The original 2010 government report, written years before anyone had identified Sarao, never named him or anything resembling his conduct as a cause. The spoofing was real and prosecutable. Whether it was actually load-bearing to the event that made him famous is a separate question, and the evidence for it is considerably thinner than the headlines from his arrest suggested.

The distinction matters for reasons beyond fairness to one London trader. It is more comfortable, as a story, to locate a crash's cause in a single identifiable villain deliberately gaming the system than in the ordinary, legal behavior of a mutual fund executing a hedge and a swarm of market makers doing exactly what their business model rewarded them for doing. But the more comfortable story is largely the wrong one. Nobody in the Flash Crash broke a rule that existed to prevent what happened; Waddell & Reed's algorithm followed a standard institutional playbook, and the high-frequency firms trading around it were, for the most part, managing their own risk exactly as their models instructed. The liquidity that vanished in those minutes had never been a reserve of capital standing ready to buy; it had been a live, continuously re-evaluated bet by thousands of independent algorithms that trading was still profitable, and enough of them concluded, within the same few seconds, that it no longer was. That is not a story about one bad actor. It is a story about what the new liquidity actually was, running exactly as designed, under conditions its designers had not tested for.
