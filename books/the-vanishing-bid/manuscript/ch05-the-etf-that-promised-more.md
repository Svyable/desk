# The ETF That Promised More Than Its Parts

An exchange-traded fund is, at its core, a wrapper around a promise: buy one share of it, and you own a proportional slice of everything inside, redeemable in principle for the underlying assets themselves. What makes the promise work in practice is a mechanism most shareholders never think about, run by firms called authorized participants — usually large banks — who are permitted to create new ETF shares by delivering a basket of the underlying securities to the fund, or to redeem ETF shares by doing the reverse. If the ETF's market price drifts even slightly above the value of what it holds, an authorized participant can profit by assembling the underlying basket, delivering it for new shares, and selling those shares at the premium; if the price drifts below, the same trade runs in reverse. This arbitrage is what keeps an ETF's price tethered to its net asset value, and for stocks, where the underlying securities trade constantly on public exchanges, it works so smoothly that most investors never notice it happening at all.

Bond ETFs asked that same mechanism to do something considerably harder. A fund like LQD, tracking a broad index of investment-grade corporate bonds, or HYG, tracking high-yield corporate debt, trades on an exchange continuously throughout the day, exactly like a stock, with quotes updating every second. The bonds inside it do not. Corporate bonds mostly trade over the counter, in a market where a dealer has to be found, a price negotiated, and a trade settled bond by bond, often with no trade in a given bond occurring for days at a stretch. A bond ETF is, in other words, a continuously liquid instrument built on top of a fundamentally illiquid one, and the arbitrage that keeps the two connected depends entirely on authorized participants being willing and able to trade the underlying bonds fast enough to keep up with trading in the ETF shares. For most of the years since bond ETFs became popular, that dependency was invisible, because bond markets, while never as liquid as stocks, were liquid enough on an ordinary day that nobody had reason to test the seam.

March 2020 tested the seam directly. As the scale of the coronavirus pandemic became clear and investors scrambled to raise cash across every asset class simultaneously, the corporate bond market seized in a way few market participants then working had ever witnessed: dealers, already holding more inventory than they wanted amid post-2008 regulatory limits on how much bond risk banks are allowed to warehouse, pulled back from making markets, and bonds that would ordinarily trade within minutes of a phone call sat for hours or days without a workable price. Bond ETF shares, meanwhile, kept trading exactly as designed, every second the stock market was open, because equity trading infrastructure does not care whether the assets underneath a fund are illiquid. The result was a visible, sustained gap between what LQD and HYG were trading for on the exchange and what their underlying bonds were nominally worth. Industry data from the worst week of the crisis, the week of March 15, showed investment-grade bond ETFs trading at an average discount to net asset value of roughly three and a third percent, with intraday discounts on individual days running past seven percent — a gap that, for a fund supposedly interchangeable with a basket of investment-grade corporate debt, was not supposed to be possible to sustain for more than a few minutes, let alone days.

There are two ways to read that gap, and the more interesting one is not the one most retail coverage settled on at the time. The popular framing treated the ETF discount as a symptom of ETFs being flawed or fragile products, a warning that packaging illiquid assets into a liquid wrapper had been exposed as a con. A more accurate reading is closer to the opposite: the ETF's market price, updating continuously as real buyers and sellers traded it, was very likely a better, more current estimate of what the underlying bonds were actually worth in a stressed market than the bonds' own stale, thinly traded quotes were. The official net asset value of a bond ETF is typically calculated from the last available trade or dealer estimate for each bond it holds, and when a bond hasn't traded in days, that last available price is not a reflection of what anyone would actually pay for it today. The ETF wasn't malfunctioning. It was doing exactly what it is built to do — provide a continuously updated price for a large basket of assets — and the discovery of the gap was less a story about ETFs breaking than about the underlying bond market's true liquidity finally being priced honestly by an instrument fast enough to reflect it, while a slower, staler pricing convention for the bonds themselves lagged behind.

That distinction mattered enormously for what happened next, because it shaped how policymakers chose to intervene. On March 23, 2020, the Federal Reserve announced something it had never done in its history: it would buy corporate bonds directly, and, in an even more unusual step, it would buy corporate bond exchange-traded funds. The facility, backstopped by an initial ten billion dollars from the Treasury's Exchange Stabilization Fund and eventually supported by a much larger capital commitment, was explicitly designed to demonstrate that a buyer with essentially unlimited resources stood ready to step into the corporate bond market if private liquidity did not return on its own. What happened over the following hours illustrates something this book keeps circling back to about the mechanics of confidence in modern markets: LQD rose more than seven percent on the day of the announcement alone, before the Fed had purchased a single bond. Actual purchases under the facility did not begin until May 12, nearly two months later, and when the program finally wound down in 2021, its total holdings had peaked at only around fourteen billion dollars — a modest sum relative to the trillions of dollars in corporate debt outstanding, and a small fraction of the facility's own headline capacity. The announcement, not the buying, closed the gap. Once market participants believed a sufficiently large, sufficiently committed buyer existed, the authorized participants and dealers who had pulled back from bond trading a week earlier found their own confidence restored, and normal arbitrage activity resumed on its own, doing the actual work the Fed's balance sheet had merely promised to do if needed.

The lesson generalizes past bond ETFs specifically. What March 2020 demonstrated is that a liquid-looking wrapper around an illiquid asset does not create liquidity in the underlying asset out of nothing; it reveals, faster and more honestly than the underlying market's own pricing conventions would, exactly how illiquid that asset actually is under stress. For years, investors had treated bond ETFs as offering something close to stock-like liquidity on top of bond-like assets, a genuine improvement over trying to trade individual corporate bonds directly, and in an ordinary market that improvement is real. In a crisis, the ETF wrapper cannot manufacture buyers for bonds nobody wants to buy; it can only make the absence of those buyers visible sooner, in a continuously updating price, rather than hidden inside a bond market's stale quotes. That transparency briefly looked like fragility, because a shrinking discount is uncomfortable to watch happen in real time on a screen, in a way that an illiquid bond quietly not trading for a week is not. But an instrument that tells you the truth quickly is not the same thing as an instrument that has failed, and distinguishing between the two — between a liquidity mechanism breaking and a liquidity mechanism accurately reporting bad news — turns out to be one of the harder judgment calls this book's history keeps demanding of investors and regulators alike.
