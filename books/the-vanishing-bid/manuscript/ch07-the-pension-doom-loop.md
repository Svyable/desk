# The Pension Doom Loop

British defined-benefit pension funds are supposed to be the calmest money in the country. A pension fund knows, to a reasonable approximation, what it will owe retirees decades from now, and its entire job is to hold assets that will still be there when those payments come due. For years, the industry's answer to that problem was a family of strategies called Liability-Driven Investment, or LDI: rather than simply buying long government bonds outright and holding them, a fund would use a smaller pool of gilts, repo financing, and interest-rate derivatives to replicate the interest-rate exposure of a much larger bond portfolio, freeing up the rest of its capital to chase higher returns elsewhere. By 2022, roughly £1.5 trillion of UK pension liabilities — equivalent to something like two-thirds of the country's annual economic output — were hedged this way. The strategy had a design assumption baked into it that almost nobody outside the plumbing of pension finance thought much about: that gilt yields, the interest rate on UK government debt, would move gradually enough that margin calls on the derivatives could always be met by selling a modest slice of other assets, in an orderly way, without disturbing the gilt market itself.

On September 23, 2022, the new Chancellor of the Exchequer, Kwasi Kwarteng, stood up in the House of Commons and tested that assumption directly. His "mini-budget" announced roughly £45 billion of unfunded tax cuts, including scrapping the top rate of income tax, without the customary independent forecast from the Office for Budget Responsibility that normally accompanies a fiscal statement of that size. Investors read the absence of that forecast as a decision not to have the plan checked, and gilt markets, already unsettled by the Bank of England's own plans to start selling bonds, reacted the way markets react to a fiscal authority that appears to be flying without instruments. Over the following days, the yield on 30-year UK government debt rose from around 3.6 percent to a peak above 5 percent — a jump so fast that market historians would later describe it as several times larger than the comparable moves during the 2008 financial crisis or the initial Covid panic of March 2020. The pound fell to its lowest level against the dollar in history.

For an ordinary bond investor, a yield spike of that size is painful but survivable: the market value of your bonds falls, and you wait. For a leveraged LDI strategy, it is closer to an ambush. As gilt yields rose, the value of the interest-rate derivatives pension funds had used to hedge their liabilities moved sharply against them, triggering the kind of margin and collateral calls that any leveraged position generates when it goes the wrong way. To meet those calls, pension funds and the LDI managers running money on their behalf needed cash immediately, and the fastest way to raise cash was to sell the same asset whose price was already collapsing: gilts. Selling gilts pushed gilt prices down and yields up further, which enlarged the very margin calls the selling was meant to satisfy, which forced more selling. A Bank of England staff paper published the following March, examining the episode in granular detail, found that the funds carrying the largest pre-crisis derivative exposures were the ones forced to sell disproportionately more into the crash, while a different set of players — hedge funds willing to take the other side of the trade — stepped in as buyers and were compensated handsomely for the risk. The dispersion in prices different dealers were quoting for the same bond more than doubled during the worst days, a sign of a market where nobody trusted anyone else's price enough to commit real size to it.

The mechanism had a name well before 2022 — a "doom loop," in the language market participants and regulators had already used to describe similar feedback dynamics elsewhere — and the pension industry's version of it carried a particular irony. LDI had been built, marketed, and adopted specifically as a way to reduce risk: a tool for making sure a fund's assets moved in the same direction as its liabilities, so that a sudden change in interest rates would not leave retirees underfunded. In the moment that mattered, the hedge did what a hedge is supposed to do in the sense that it accurately tracked the liability's sensitivity to rates; what it had not been built to survive was the possibility that meeting its own margin requirements would require selling into a market with too few buyers left standing, at a speed the underlying gilt market could not absorb without breaking. Risk management and liquidity are not the same problem, and LDI had been engineered almost entirely around the first one.

The Bank of England had no formal obligation to rescue a pension-fund derivatives strategy, and its own long-term policy, quantitative tightening, was pointed in the opposite direction: selling bonds, not buying them. On September 28, 2022, five days after the mini-budget and with its own Financial Policy Committee warning that continued dysfunction in the gilt market posed a material risk to the stability of the UK financial system as a whole, the Bank announced an emergency, explicitly temporary program to buy long-dated gilts, initially capped at five billion pounds a day. It framed the purchases narrowly, as a financial-stability operation rather than a change in monetary policy, a distinction that mattered to the Bank's credibility on inflation but meant little to the mechanics of what the intervention actually did: it put a large, price-insensitive buyer back into a market that had briefly lost enough of them to function. The cap was raised to ten billion pounds a day on October 10 and widened four days later to include index-linked gilts, before the whole program wound down on schedule on October 14, having purchased a total of £19.3 billion in bonds. The gilt market did not need the Bank to buy every bond in the country. It needed the Bank to demonstrate, credibly, that a buyer of last resort existed at all, which turned out to be most of what was required to break the loop.

The political fallout arrived almost as fast as the market moved. Kwarteng was removed as Chancellor on October 14, 2022, thirty-eight days after his budget, and his successor reversed nearly all of it within hours of taking the job. Liz Truss resigned as Prime Minister on October 20, having lasted forty-four days in office, the shortest tenure of any British prime minister in history, a record that had stood, until that week, for almost two hundred years. It is tempting to read the episode purely as a story about one government's fiscal misjudgment, and the misjudgment was real. But the mini-budget was the spark, not the fuel. The fuel was a trillion and a half pounds of pension money, managed prudently by any conventional definition of prudence, sitting on a hedge that had never been stress-tested against the specific, narrow scenario of gilt yields moving further and faster than anyone building the strategy had thought plausible. The instrument built to protect British retirement savings from interest-rate risk came within days of forcing a fire sale large enough to threaten the market it depended on, and the only thing standing between that outcome and a genuinely disorderly collapse in UK government debt was a central bank willing to step in as the buyer nobody else could afford to be.
