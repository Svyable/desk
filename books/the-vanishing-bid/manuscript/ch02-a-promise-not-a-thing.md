# A Promise, Not a Thing

Ask a trader what liquidity is and the answer usually comes out as a description of weather rather than a definition. Deep. Thin. Dried up. Frozen. The vocabulary borrows from water and ice because liquidity behaves the way people expect a physical property to behave: present in some quantity, filling the available space, occasionally scarce. That instinct is almost exactly backward. A market is not a reservoir that happens to be full or empty. It is a standing offer, renewed voluntarily every few seconds by people and machines who can withdraw it the moment continuing to offer it stops being worth their while.

The textbook version of liquidity has three parts, and it is worth being precise about them because the precision is where the trouble later hides. A liquid market lets you trade quickly, at a price close to the last one, without your own trade moving that price very much. Economists sometimes split this into tightness (how far apart the buy and sell prices sit), depth (how much you can trade at those prices before they move), and resiliency (how fast the market recovers after a big trade knocks it around). A share of Apple stock and a rare eighteenth-century violin are both assets with value, but only one of them can be sold in the time it takes to read this sentence, at a price within a fraction of a percent of the last trade, without the seller doing anything unusual to find a buyer. That gap is what liquidity means in practice, and modern financial markets are built almost entirely around manufacturing more of it, faster, for more things, than any exchange in history.

The manufacturing used to be a person. For most of the twentieth century, a share of stock on the New York Stock Exchange traded through a specialist — later called a designated market maker — a human being assigned to a particular stock who was obligated, by exchange rule, to maintain a "fair and orderly market" in it. The obligation was not abstract. Specialists were required to use their own capital to buy when the public wanted to sell and to sell when the public wanted to buy, smoothing out temporary imbalances between the two. In exchange, they earned the difference between what they paid and what they charged — the spread — and they got a privileged view of the order book that outsiders did not. It was a trade: guaranteed continuous liquidity, provided by someone with an obligation to keep providing it even when providing it was uncomfortable, in exchange for a durable informational and economic advantage. The arrangement worked, more or less, for decades, and it is worth noticing what made it work. The specialist's obligation to trade did not evaporate under stress. It was the whole point of the job.

That arrangement has been almost entirely replaced. Electronic trading, decimalization of stock prices in the early 2000s, and the rise of high-frequency trading firms dismantled the old specialist system stock by stock over roughly fifteen years, and what replaced it looks similar on a screen — a bid price, an ask price, a stack of orders at each — but works on a different foundation. Today, the great majority of the liquidity quoted in U.S. equities comes from firms like Citadel Securities and Virtu Financial, high-speed market makers running automated strategies across thousands of securities at once. They are extraordinarily good at what they do: spreads on liquid stocks have never been tighter, and the cost of trading a hundred shares of a large company has fallen for decades. But the obligation that anchored the old system is largely gone. A modern electronic market maker is not required by law to keep quoting a falling stock. It is a business decision, re-evaluated many times per second, about whether continuing to offer a two-sided market is still profitable given what the firm's models currently believe about where the price is headed.

This is the sleight of hand worth sitting with before the rest of the book gets into specific crises. The liquidity visible on a modern trading screen — the narrow spread, the deep-looking order book — is not a stock of something sitting in a warehouse, waiting to be drawn down. It is closer to a live auction that thousands of independent bidders are choosing, moment to moment, whether to keep participating in. Under ordinary conditions, participating is profitable, so the bidders show up in force and liquidity looks abundant, in some ways more abundant than it has ever been. Under stress, the same automated logic that makes market makers efficient and cheap in calm conditions gives them an equally efficient, equally fast way to stop quoting altogether. Nothing forces them to stay. The absence of that obligation is not a flaw introduced by any particular firm behaving badly; it is a structural fact about how liquidity provision has been rebuilt, and it holds regardless of how sophisticated, well-capitalized, or well-intentioned the market makers of a given decade happen to be.

Economists have a formal name for the piece of this that shows up most starkly in a crisis: adverse selection. In 1985, the economist Albert Kyle published a model, still taught in every market microstructure course, describing a market maker who does not know whether the next trader wants to buy or sell because they possess real information the market maker lacks, or because they simply need cash and are trading for reasons that have nothing to do with what the asset is worth. The market maker's problem is that they cannot always tell the two apart in the moment, and the informed trader, by definition, tends to be right more often than chance would predict. Widening the spread is the market maker's defense — charging everyone a little more to cover the risk that some unknown fraction of the order flow knows something they don't. Kyle's insight, formalized with more mathematics than a general reader needs, is that this defense scales with fear. The more uncertain a market maker becomes about why people are suddenly trading, the wider the spread has to get to keep the business viable, and at some point the honest answer to "how wide should the spread be" is that there should not be a two-sided quote at all. Stepping back from the market entirely is not a failure of the model. It is the model, taken to its logical end.

None of this means liquidity is fake, or that the improvements of the last three decades are an illusion. On an ordinary Tuesday, a retail investor can buy or sell nearly any major stock or bond fund instantly, at a price within pennies of the last one, at a cost that would have astonished a trader in 1985. That is a genuine achievement, built by genuine competition among firms racing to offer tighter spreads and faster execution. The argument of this book is narrower and, in its way, more useful: the price you see quoted on an ordinary Tuesday is not a reliable guide to what will happen on the Tuesday that isn't ordinary. Liquidity, understood correctly, is not a property of an asset. It is a behavior of the people and machines currently willing to trade it, and behavior can change in the time it takes an algorithm to reprice its own risk. The chapters that follow are a history of the moments when it did — beginning with the day the machines of an earlier decade tried to insure a falling market and instead helped guarantee that it fell faster.
