# Back Matter

## About the Book

*The Vanishing Bid* is a book about a single, recurring discovery: that the depth and tightness investors see on an ordinary trading day is not a property of the assets being traded, but a behavior of the people and machines currently willing to trade them, renewed voluntarily and withdrawn the moment continuing to offer it stops being profitable.

The book traces that discovery across nine historical episodes — the 1987 portfolio-insurance crash, the 1998 collapse of Long-Term Capital Management, the August 2007 quant quake, the 2010 Flash Crash, the March 2020 Treasury and bond-ETF seizure, the UK's 2022 gilt crisis, the GameStop squeeze, and the collapse of TerraUSD/LUNA and FTX — before turning, in its closing chapters, to the open question of what happens to this pattern as an increasing share of trading decisions passes to autonomous AI agents. It closes by assembling the book's own mechanisms into a single composite scenario, explicitly not a forecast, meant to make the underlying structure recognizable before the next version of it arrives wearing a new name.

## Notes and Sources

Figures, dates, and quotations throughout the manuscript are drawn from primary and official sources wherever a claim could be checked against one directly: the Brady Commission's 1988 report on the 1987 crash, SEC and CFTC findings on the 2010 Flash Crash, Federal Reserve and Federal Reserve Bank of New York research and public statements, Bank of England financial stability publications and speeches, Financial Stability Board and International Monetary Fund reports on AI and market structure, court records and regulatory filings from the Terra/LUNA and FTX prosecutions, and contemporaneous financial reporting on each episode.

The sourcing discipline is deliberately conservative. A market-structure statistic is dated to the year it was measured, not extrapolated forward. A regulator's warning is reported as a warning, not treated as a confirmed outcome. A single firm's internal AI trading system is described as evidence of institutional investment in the technology, not as proof that autonomous agents already set prices market-wide. Where the research literature is genuinely unsettled, as it is on whether AI trading agents will increase or reduce herding behavior, the manuscript says so rather than resolving the disagreement for effect.

Several figures cited in the later chapters — market-maker share, basis-trade size, 0DTE options volume, and the state of AI adoption in trading — move quickly and should be rechecked against current data before any publication treats them as current rather than as a snapshot of the period this manuscript was drafted in.
