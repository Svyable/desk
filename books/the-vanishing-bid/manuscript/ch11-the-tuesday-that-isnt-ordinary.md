# The Tuesday That Isn't Ordinary

Pick an unremarkable Tuesday and look at what holds the modern market up. By 2025, Citadel Securities alone was routing more than a fifth of all U.S. equity trading volume through its automated systems, and more than a third of the orders retail investors placed through their brokerage apps — up from roughly a fifth just five years earlier — while also standing behind close to a third of all volume in U.S. listed options. Layered on top of that equity flow, zero-days-to-expiry options, contracts that are opened and closed within the same trading session, had grown from a novelty into the largest single share of S&P 500 index options activity, accounting for more than half of all SPX options volume by 2025 and roughly a quarter of the entire U.S. listed options market, a few million contracts of pure same-day directional betting churning through the market before the closing bell. Underneath the equity market, in the market this book has already called the least risky asset most investors believe they own, hedge funds had built a basis trade betting on tiny gaps between Treasury cash bonds and Treasury futures to a scale regulators found difficult to ignore: roughly $830 billion in the trade itself by September 2025, and a broader gross Treasury exposure across large hedge funds that had doubled in two years to some $4 trillion, financed, as this book's account of March 2020 already showed happens to trades like this, with substantial borrowed money. On November 20, 2025, Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook stood up and said, in language a central banker does not use lightly, that those positions had made the $30 trillion Treasury market "more vulnerable to stress."

None of that stopped an ordinary Tuesday from looking, on any given ordinary Tuesday, like the most liquid moment in the history of finance. Spreads on the largest stocks sit at fractions of a cent. A market order for a thousand shares of nearly anything executes in milliseconds, at a price so close to the last quote that the difference is not worth mentioning. A bond ETF trades all day at a price that tracks its underlying basket to the decimal. An options desk can price and hedge a request in the time it takes to read this sentence. Every mechanism this book has examined — portfolio insurance's algorithmic descendants, the market-making firms that replaced human specialists, the ETF creation-and-redemption arbitrage, the leveraged basis trades, the pension hedges, the retail brokerage apps, the stablecoin-style promises now proliferating under names like tokenized money-market funds, and, increasingly, AI systems generating and executing trading decisions with less human review at each stage — is, on an ordinary Tuesday, doing exactly what it was built to do, and doing it so well that the depth on the screen looks less like a promise than a physical fact of the world.

It is worth being precise, before going further, about what this chapter is and is not. It is not a forecast of a specific coming crash, and readers should not treat it as one; nobody, including the authors of the reports and speeches cited throughout this book, can say with any reliability when the next liquidity crisis will land or which of these mechanisms will trigger it. What follows instead is a composite, assembled deliberately out of nothing but the mechanisms this book has already documented happening, separately, in 1987, 1998, 2007, 2010, 2012, 2014, 2020, 2022, and 2022 again. The question worth asking is not whether any one of these things will happen again — most of them, individually, already have, more than once. It is what an ordinary Tuesday looks like if a meaningful number of them happen inside the same week, feeding each other the way this book's chapters keep showing they can.

It could begin almost anywhere. A large, well-known technology company misses an earnings estimate badly enough to remind investors, all at once, that a handful of firms have carried an outsized share of index returns for years. Index funds and volatility-targeting strategies, running variations on the same risk models this book's fourth chapter traced back to a common academic ancestry, mechanically reduce exposure as measured volatility rises — not because any manager decided the world had changed, but because the formula requires it, the same way Leland and Rubinstein's portfolio insurance required selling into a falling market in 1987 without asking whether a buyer existed. The selling pushes prices down further, which raises measured volatility further, which triggers more selling from funds that have never spoken to each other and believe themselves to be running independent strategies.

Options market makers, absorbing the other side of a wave of same-day put buying from an options market that by 2025 had come to expect roughly half its S&P 500 volume to expire before the market closed, hedge their growing exposure by selling stock index futures into the same falling market, exactly the mechanical amplifier that turned a hedge fund's short squeeze into a gamma squeeze in this book's eighth chapter, run now in reverse and at far larger scale. An authorized participant on a large bond ETF, watching the gap between the fund's price and its underlying portfolio widen the way this book's fifth chapter described happening to LQD in March 2020, quietly reduces how aggressively it is willing to arbitrage that gap, because the underlying corporate bonds it would need to sell to do the trade have themselves stopped trading at any price a dealer wants to commit capital to. A hedge fund running the Treasury basis trade at leverage ratios regulators had already flagged as high as eighteen to one receives a margin call it cannot meet without selling the underlying Treasuries it was long, into a market whose primary dealers, this book's sixth chapter already showed, cannot always absorb that selling without yields moving in ways that are supposed to be impossible for the world's safest asset. A newer instrument, built on the same confidence-not-collateral logic that undid TerraUSD, discovers that its own peg depends on an assumption nobody stress-tested against a week when everything else is falling at once.

And somewhere inside all of it, a growing share of the actual trading decisions are no longer being made, in the first instance, by a person watching a screen. They are being made by systems trained on similar architectures, fed similar market data, optimized toward similar objectives, exactly the condition this book's tenth chapter found regulators at the Bank of England and the Financial Stability Board worried about without yet being able to prove. Each individual system may be behaving rationally, by its own lights, exactly as each individual portfolio insurer was behaving rationally in 1987, each individual quant fund was behaving rationally in August 2007, and each individual LDI manager was behaving rationally in September 2022. None of them has to be wrong for the aggregate to become something none of them intended. A market built from many correlated rational actors can produce an outcome no single actor would have chosen and no single actor can be blamed for causing.

What stops this, historically, has never been the market itself. It has been Alan Greenspan's one sentence on the morning of October 20, 1987, promising the Federal Reserve stood ready as a source of liquidity. It has been the Federal Reserve's emergency repo operations and its unlimited Treasury and mortgage-backed securities purchases in March 2020. It has been the Bank of England's unscheduled gilt-buying program in September 2022, adopted within days of a mechanism nearly two-thirds of the country's pension liabilities depended on. Central banks have proven, across every episode this book has examined, that they can supply the liquidity private markets withdraw, when they choose to and when the crisis is large enough and legible enough for them to recognize in time. What none of those interventions changed was the underlying structure that made the intervention necessary in the first place. The mechanisms this book has traced were not repaired after each rescue. They were, in nearly every case, replaced by a newer mechanism carrying the same vulnerability forward in a different wrapper: portfolio insurance gave way to risk parity and volatility targeting; specialist market makers gave way to high-frequency algorithms; a handful of large banks holding Treasury inventory gave way to leveraged hedge funds running the basis trade at a scale several multiples larger than before the last crisis it contributed to.

That is the pattern this book leaves the reader with, more than any single date or dollar figure. Liquidity, on an ordinary Tuesday, is not something a market has. It is something a very large number of independent-looking participants are each, individually, choosing to keep offering, second by second, for reasons that make sense to each of them until, for reasons that also make sense to each of them, they stop. The screen cannot tell the difference between the two states until it is too late to matter to whoever is trying to trade at that moment. Central banks can, and repeatedly have, arrived afterward to supply what the market briefly could not. What they have not been able to do, and what no institution examined in this book has yet managed to do, is make an ordinary Tuesday a reliable guide to what will happen on the Tuesday that isn't. The bid on the screen is real for as long as someone is willing to honor it. The only honest answer to how long that will be is: for exactly as long as it remains in their interest to keep offering it, and not one trading day more.
