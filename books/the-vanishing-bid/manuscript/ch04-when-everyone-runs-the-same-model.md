# When Everyone Runs the Same Model

John Meriwether had already lived through one version of this story before he tried to build a firm designed to avoid repeating it. As the former head of bond arbitrage at Salomon Brothers, he had watched leveraged trading desks blow up before, and when he founded Long-Term Capital Management in 1994, he assembled what looked, on paper, like the strongest possible defense against ever being surprised by a market again: veteran arbitrage traders, a former vice chairman of the Federal Reserve, and two academic economists, Myron Scholes and Robert Merton, who would share the 1997 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for the option-pricing formula that much of modern finance, including LTCM's own strategies, was built on top of. The fund's approach was convergence arbitrage: find two securities that should, by any reasonable financial logic, trade at nearly the same price, and bet that a small, temporary gap between them would close. The gaps LTCM specialized in finding were often tiny, a handful of basis points, which meant the strategy only became worth doing at scale if it was run with enormous leverage. By the end of August 1998, LTCM's balance sheet held on the order of $125 billion in assets against a capital base that had shrunk to roughly $2.3 billion, a leverage ratio above fifty to one before even counting a derivatives book with more than a trillion dollars in notional exposure sitting off that balance sheet.

The strategy's underlying premise — that markets occasionally misprice closely related securities and that a sufficiently well-capitalized, sufficiently patient trader can profit from the gap closing — was not wrong. What the models could not price was what would happen if LTCM itself needed to exit its positions all at once, because the models had been built on historical data from a world in which LTCM's own trading was too small to move the markets it operated in. By 1998, that was no longer true; LTCM's positions were large enough, in some markets, to be a meaningful fraction of the total market itself, and a fund that large trying to unwind a position was no longer merely observing a market's price — it was setting one, in the worst possible direction, exactly when it could least afford to.

The trigger, when it came, arrived from a market LTCM believed it had hedged. On August 17, 1998, Russia devalued the ruble and declared a unilateral moratorium on tens of billions of dollars of its own short-term treasury debt. LTCM held Russian exposure, but the fund's models had assumed that a Russian default would come paired with a ruble devaluation profitable enough, through separate currency positions, to offset the bond losses — a hedge that depended on the counterparties on the other side of those currency trades remaining solvent and the Russian government continuing to allow currency trading at all. Neither held. The banks LTCM had hedged through were themselves reeling, and Russia froze currency markets alongside the debt moratorium, leaving LTCM's supposed hedge worthless at precisely the moment it was needed. What followed was not confined to Russian exposure. A broad flight to quality moved through global markets as investors everywhere, spooked by Russia, pulled back from anything resembling risk, and because LTCM's convergence trades spanned dozens of ostensibly unrelated markets — mortgage bonds, European interest rate swaps, merger arbitrage, emerging market debt — nearly all of them moved against the fund at once. The gaps LTCM was betting would narrow instead widened, because the same flight to quality that was crushing Russian bonds was simultaneously making every other slightly-risky, slightly-illiquid asset LTCM held look unattractive next to the safest, most liquid instruments available. Diversification, the fund's own risk models had assumed, would cushion any single market's losses. In a genuine liquidity crisis, diversification across models that all rewarded the same underlying appetite for taking on illiquidity turned out to offer no protection at all; everything the fund owned was correlated by a single hidden factor none of its models had isolated, which was simply LTCM's own bet that being patient with illiquid, complex positions would eventually pay off.

By September 1, 1998, LTCM's capital had fallen to roughly $2.3 billion, and the fund faced a problem that had nothing to do with whether its underlying trades were, in the abstract, still good bets. It had a problem of size: a book too large to sell into a market that had stopped wanting to buy, held by a firm whose own selling would move prices further against itself with every attempt to reduce risk. William McDonough, president of the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, recognized that LTCM's failure would not stay contained to LTCM. The fund's derivatives book ran through nearly every major bank on Wall Street, and a disorderly collapse threatened to leave those banks holding one-sided positions against a counterparty that had simply vanished, with nobody able to say in advance how far the damage would spread. On September 23, 1998, McDonough convened representatives from fourteen major banks and brokerages at the New York Fed's offices and brokered a private recapitalization: the consortium put in $3.6 billion in exchange for roughly ninety percent ownership of the fund, taking control away from Meriwether's team and winding the positions down in an orderly fashion over the following year. It is worth stating plainly what the Fed did and did not do, because the distinction gets blurred in popular retellings. The central bank put none of its own money into the rescue. It organized the room, applied the pressure, and let the private sector foot the bill — a model of intervention distinct from, and in some ways more elegant than, simply printing money to make a failing fund whole, though it depended on having fourteen banks in the room who all understood that letting LTCM collapse disorderly would cost each of them more than writing a check to prevent it.

Nine years later, a different, less dramatic version of the same underlying failure played out across a handful of days in early August 2007, without any single firm's name attached to it the way LTCM's collapse had been. In the first two weeks of that month, against the backdrop of the widening subprime mortgage crisis, a wide range of quantitative equity hedge funds — market-neutral strategies that had little in common with mortgage debt and, in many cases, had never held a subprime-related security — began losing money simultaneously, at a scale their own risk models treated as close to statistically impossible. Goldman Sachs' flagship quantitative fund, Global Alpha, lost more than twenty percent in August alone; a related fund, Global Equity Opportunities, fell close to thirty percent in a matter of days, prompting Goldman and a group of outside investors to inject three billion dollars of emergency capital and slash the fund's leverage. Renaissance Technologies' multi-billion-dollar equity fund fell nearly nine percent over the same stretch. Goldman's chief financial officer, David Viniar, gave reporters a line that became a permanent piece of finance folklore: the firm was seeing, he said, twenty-five standard deviation moves, several days in a row — an event so far outside what a normal statistical model would predict that, taken literally, it should not occur even once across many multiples of the age of the universe. The line was memorable specifically because it captured the actual problem precisely: the models were not wrong about ordinary conditions. They were blind to a specific correlation nobody had built into them, because the correlation did not come from the companies these funds' models were valuing. It came from the funds themselves.

Researchers who examined the episode afterward, most notably the MIT economists Amir Khandani and Andrew Lo, found a telling pattern in the timing: the losses peaked mid-week and then, on Friday, largely reversed — a shape that pointed away from any change in the underlying value of the stocks involved and toward a technical, mechanical cause. Their hypothesis was that one or more large quantitative portfolios, likely facing a margin call or a mandated reduction in risk unrelated to any of the individual stocks in question, had been forcibly and rapidly liquidated. Because so many quantitative equity strategies across different firms leaned on a similar, relatively small set of underlying signals — value, momentum, and a handful of other well-known factors that decades of academic finance research had made standard tools of the trade — that single fund's forced selling pushed prices in ways that inflicted losses on every other fund running a similar model, even though those funds had no direct exposure to whatever had originally forced the first fund to sell. The losses, in other words, were correlated not because the underlying businesses were connected, but because the traders betting on those businesses were all, without coordinating or even necessarily realizing it, running variations of the same idea.

LTCM and the quant quake are, on their surface, different stories — one a single storied fund undone by leverage and a sovereign default, the other a diffuse, multi-firm event with no single villain and no headline-grabbing bailout. What connects them is the same lesson the Flash Crash would later teach in a different market, at a different speed: diversification and independence are not the same thing, and a portfolio, or an industry, can look diversified across assets while remaining dangerously undiversified across strategies. When enough participants build their models on the same academic ideas, backtest them on the same historical data, and respond to stress with the same instinct to reduce risk, the market they are all trading stops behaving like a collection of independent judgments and starts behaving like a single, oversized position — one that, when it needs to unwind, discovers there is no genuinely separate buyer standing on the other side, only more of itself.
