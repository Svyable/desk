# The Day the Bids Disappeared

Hayne Leland and Mark Rubinstein were finance professors at Berkeley, not traders, and the product they built in the early 1980s was sold to institutional money managers as something close to a miracle: a way to hold stocks and still sleep at night. The idea, formalized with a partner named John O'Brien in a firm called LOR, borrowed the mathematics of the Black-Scholes options-pricing model and turned it into an instruction manual. A pension fund or insurance company that wanted downside protection on its stock portfolio, without paying for an actual option, could instead replicate one synthetically: as the market fell, sell a calculated amount of stock-index futures; as it rose, buy some back. Do this continuously and precisely enough, the theory went, and a portfolio would behave as though it owned a put option nobody had ever written, cushioning losses in a crash while preserving most of the upside in a rally. LOR called it portfolio insurance, and by October 1987 something on the order of sixty to ninety billion dollars in institutional assets were being managed this way — an amount roughly comparable to the entire market capitalization of a large industrial economy's stock market a generation earlier, all following variations of the same mechanical formula, all recalculating their required trades from the same underlying logic, every trading day.

The theory had one assumption buried inside it that almost nobody outside a handful of quantitative specialists thought to interrogate: that the market being hedged would remain liquid enough, at every moment, to absorb whatever volume the formula demanded. Portfolio insurance did not ask whether other buyers existed. It issued an instruction — sell this many futures contracts, at whatever price is available — because the formula's entire purpose was to protect the fund, not to worry about the market's capacity to take the other side. For as long as only a modest fraction of the market ran this strategy, that assumption held well enough. October 19, 1987, arrived at a moment when it no longer did.

The market had already been sliding for a week and a half before that Monday, unsettled by rising interest rates and a weakening dollar, and by the time trading opened the mood on Wall Street was tense rather than calm. What followed was not, at first, a single dramatic event so much as an accelerating mechanical loop. As prices fell through the morning, portfolio insurance programs across dozens of institutions independently recalculated their hedges and independently arrived at the same conclusion: sell more futures. The selling pushed futures prices down faster than the underlying stocks that the futures were supposed to be tracking, opening a gap between the two markets that specialized traders, running a strategy called index arbitrage, tried to close by simultaneously selling stocks and buying the now-cheaper futures — which meant even more selling landed in the stock market itself, on top of the portfolio insurers' own direct sales. The New York Stock Exchange, still built around human specialists working the floor with paper tickets and telephone lines, could not process the volume. The Chicago futures pits, faster and more automated, kept transmitting sell signals into a stock market that was falling behind in real time, unable to open some names for trading at all for stretches of the morning because no specialist could find a price at which buyers and sellers both existed.

By the close, the Dow Jones Industrial Average had fallen 508 points, 22.6 percent, still the single worst one-day percentage decline in the index's history — worse than the opening crash of the Great Depression in 1929. Roughly half a trillion dollars of American equity value had evaporated in a single session, and estimates of the global toll ran past a trillion and a half. Volume on the New York Stock Exchange hit a record 604 million shares, nearly triple a typical day, a figure that sounds almost quaint by the standards of modern electronic markets but represented, at the time, more trading than the exchange's own infrastructure had been designed to handle.

The federal commission convened to investigate the crash, led by the financier Nicholas Brady and known afterward as the Brady Commission, published its findings in January 1988, and its central conclusion deserves to be stated precisely, because it is easy to compress into something either too dramatic or too tame. Portfolio insurance and the associated index-arbitrage trading did not, in the Commission's analysis, start the decline; ordinary macroeconomic worry did that. But the Commission calculated that sell programs run by just three large portfolio insurers accounted for close to two billion dollars of the roughly twenty-one billion dollars in total stock sold on the floor of the New York Stock Exchange that day, and that portfolio-insurance-related selling in the futures market amounted to some four billion dollars more, more than a third of that market's entire volume — concentrated, mechanical, price-insensitive selling, arriving from a strategy explicitly designed not to care what price it received. What the Commission described was not a market being fooled by bad information. It was a market being overwhelmed by good information, repeated by too many participants at once, running the same formula, faster than the humans on the other side of their trades could reasonably be expected to absorb it.

The following morning, October 20, the newly installed chairman of the Federal Reserve, Alan Greenspan, barely two months into the job, issued a single sentence to the press: the Federal Reserve, he said, affirmed its readiness to serve as a source of liquidity to support the economic and financial system. It was a promise from an institution that could, in the most literal sense, manufacture the liquidity a panicked market had just discovered it did not actually have, and the Fed backed the statement by pumping reserves into the banking system and leaning on major banks to keep extending credit to securities firms that suddenly needed it. The stock market did not need the Fed to buy a single share. It needed someone, credibly, to promise that a buyer of last resort existed, and the promise itself did much of the work — a pattern this book will return to more than once, because central banks would use exactly this move again, at larger scale and higher stakes, in nearly every liquidity crisis that followed.

What makes 1987 worth beginning with, rather than treating as a historical curiosity from an era of paper stock certificates and floor traders shouting across a pit, is how little of its underlying mechanism has actually gone away. Portfolio insurance as a specific product mostly disappeared after the crash, discredited by its own performance in the moment it was supposed to prove its worth. But the structure it exposed — a market whose apparent depth depends on the assumption that participants will behave independently of one another, tested by a moment when a large enough share of them turn out to be running the same calculation at the same time — did not need portfolio insurance to survive. It only needed a new mechanism to carry it forward. Index funds that must rebalance on the same schedule, risk models built on the same historical correlations, volatility-targeting strategies that mechanically de-risk in the same direction at the same moment, and, this book will eventually argue, trading agents built on similar AI architectures reacting to similar signals, are all variations on the same underlying vulnerability Leland, Rubinstein, and O'Brien stumbled into without meaning to: a market can look perfectly liquid, right up until enough of its participants discover, simultaneously, that they were never as independent from one another as the depth on the screen implied.
