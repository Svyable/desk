# RF localization counterevidence audit

## Why this matters

Chapter 1 currently treats radiofrequency disruption as a potentially powerful mechanistic fingerprint: perturb the candidate spin physics and watch the compass fail. That is a good experimental ambition, but the inference becomes too clean if the book does not confront a result in which apparently similar RF exposure behaves differently depending on how it is delivered.

## Primary research checked

### Kobylkov et al. (2020), *Scientific Reports*

Kobylkov and colleagues tested long-distance migratory garden warblers with a miniature coil system attached to the head so an oscillating magnetic field could be applied locally to the eyes. At about 5 nT, the eye-local oscillating field did **not** disorient the birds: they retained seasonally appropriate migratory orientation. The same birds were clearly disoriented when exposed to a homogeneous oscillating field of the same reported amplitude generated by large stationary coils.

Source: Kobylkov et al., “Magnetic compass of garden warblers is not affected by oscillating magnetic fields applied to their eyes,” *Scientific Reports* 10 (2020), 3473. https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-020-60383-x

The authors explicitly argued that their result challenged the inference that RF disruption is caused by a photochemical magnetoreceptor in the eye. That conclusion is stronger than the experiment alone can establish, but the experimental contrast itself is exactly the kind of counterevidence this book needs.

### Muheim & Phillips (2023), *Scientific Reports*

A later zebra-finch study found that low-level RF fields can have qualitatively different behavioral effects depending on frequency, intensity, complexity, and prior exposure. Roughly 10 nT Larmor-frequency fields could alter the magnetic input while retaining learnable directional information; broadband fields could eliminate usable directional information even when birds were trained in their presence. The authors emphasize that ambient RF differences between laboratories may therefore contribute to variability.

Source: Muheim & Phillips, “Effects of low-level RF fields reveal complex pattern of magnetic input to the avian magnetic compass,” *Scientific Reports* 13 (2023), 19970. https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-46547-5

This strengthens the need to treat `RF disruption` as a family of interventions rather than one binary fact.

## What the manuscript may safely say

RF disruption remains important evidence because multiple experiments report systematic effects at weak field strengths and because frequency/intensity dependence can constrain mechanism. But **RF disruption by itself is not yet a localization result**. A field applied around the whole animal can differ from an eye-local field in spatial uniformity, polarization, orientation relative to the static field, coil geometry, induced artifacts, effective exposure at candidate receptors, or effects on tissues outside the eye.

The book should therefore separate three questions:

1. Does an RF intervention alter magnetic-compass behavior?
2. Are the relevant RF parameters quantitatively consistent with a proposed spin-chemical mechanism?
3. Does spatially targeted intervention identify the anatomical receptor?

Evidence for the first does not automatically answer the third.

## Strongest counterargument to the counterevidence

The 2020 eye-local result does **not** prove that the avian compass is outside the eye or that radical-pair magnetoreception is wrong. The miniature and large-coil exposures may not have been physically equivalent at the receptor despite sharing a nominal amplitude. Field geometry and orientation matter to radical-pair predictions, and a head-mounted apparatus introduces its own boundary conditions. The result therefore creates an experimental burden rather than a clean falsification.

Conversely, those caveats cannot simply be used to discard the negative localization result. If the mechanism predicts that a 5 nT eye-local perturbation should disrupt orientation, then the actual field at the candidate retinal receptor, polarization/orientation, spectral purity, static-field geometry, and behavioral denominator must be reconstructed. A mechanistic fingerprint that survives only when unsuccessful interventions are declared incomparable after the fact is not yet a fingerprint.

## Revision target

When Chapter 1 is next edited, revise the `interference fingerprint` section so that the 2020 local-versus-homogeneous contrast appears before the book promotes RF disruption toward “spectroscopy performed on a living animal.” The useful narrative turn is that the fingerprint itself has structure: whole-body and local perturbations do not automatically agree, and that disagreement is evidence rather than nuisance.

A concise eventual formulation could make the distinction without overexplaining it: the radio experiment is valuable not because `radio breaks compass` uniquely proves cryptochrome, but because a sufficiently characterized perturbation matrix—frequency, amplitude, orientation, bandwidth, spatial targeting, light condition and species—can progressively exclude mechanisms.

## Publication holds

Before attaching a quantitative mechanistic conclusion to the Kobylkov contrast, recover from the paper/supplement:

- exact RF frequency and bandwidth;
- peak/RMS convention for the reported ~5 nT amplitude;
- local field vector and gradient across both eyes;
- static-field magnitude and orientation;
- orientation of RF relative to static field;
- stationary-coil field uniformity and calibration;
- bird counts, exclusions and repeated-measures structure;
- blinding/randomization details;
- light spectrum and intensity;
- whether the miniature apparatus itself altered baseline orientation;
- any later direct replication of the eye-local protocol.

Until those are extracted, do not convert `same reported amplitude` into `physically identical exposure`.
