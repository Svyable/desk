# Contradictions and incentive audit

## Canada

Incentives included protecting public safety, asserting sovereignty, recovering costs, reassuring communities, extracting technical information, and demonstrating competent response. Those incentives can pull in different directions. Public reassurance can compress uncertainty; compensation claims can encourage broad cost accounting; intelligence value can limit disclosure.

## Soviet Union

Incentives included protecting military-space capabilities, limiting legal and diplomatic exposure, controlling technical disclosure, and avoiding a precedent that made every future space failure expensive. These incentives do not by themselves prove deception in any specific statement.

## United States

Incentives included allied assistance, nuclear-emergency preparedness, Soviet technical exploitation, tracking validation, and broader arguments about nuclear satellites. U.S. technical assistance should not be interpreted as neutral to intelligence interest merely because it also served public safety.

## Search organizations

Operational teams had incentives to avoid missed hazardous debris while conserving aircraft time and personnel exposure. Threshold choices may therefore produce conservative false-positive burdens.

## Later narrators

Space-law accounts often maximize doctrinal significance. Nuclear-safety accounts maximize emergency significance. Intelligence histories maximize the Soviet military angle. Canadian institutional histories may emphasize successful improvisation. A complete manuscript should use each source for what it can establish rather than allowing any one institutional narrative to own the event.
