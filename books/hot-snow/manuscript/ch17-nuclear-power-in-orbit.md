# Nuclear Power in Orbit

A bad history of Cosmos 954 ends by declaring nuclear power in space too dangerous. A better history asks where nuclear power is uniquely useful and what design obligations follow from that usefulness.

Deep-space missions can move far enough from the Sun that solar power becomes difficult. Shadowed environments and long-duration planetary work can also favor nuclear sources. Radioisotope systems and fission reactors are different technologies with different inventories and failure modes, though public debate often collapses them into one radioactive category.

Cosmos 954 belonged to another context: a reactor-powered military surveillance spacecraft in Earth orbit. Its accident helped drive international attention toward the safety of nuclear power sources and emergency notification.

The IAEA now describes global arrangements for accidents involving satellites or other space objects carrying nuclear or radioactive sources. That institutional maturity is one of Morning Light's afterlives.

There is a deeper design lesson. Safety cannot rely only on a nominal disposal maneuver. It has to include credible failure states: propulsion failure, attitude loss, collision, premature re-entry, breakup and uncertainty in where material will land. Modern debris concerns make that systems view even more important.

Nuclear power is not uniquely subject to this logic. Every high-consequence technology accumulates layers of contingency until a rare coupled failure bypasses several at once.

The reactor that fell on Canada is therefore not an argument against ambitious machinery. It is an argument against defining the machine too narrowly.

The machine includes what happens when it stops obeying.
