# What Was Never Found

Recovery is not the same thing as accounting.

Canadian authorities later estimated that roughly 0.1 percent of Cosmos 954's power source was recovered. The number is famous because it is small. It is also easy to misuse.

The unrecovered remainder was not necessarily sitting intact under snow waiting for a better search team. Re-entry is violent. Reactor materials can ablate, fragment and disperse. Some debris may land where it is difficult to detect or recover. Some may enter water. Some may be reduced to forms whose radiological significance differs sharply from a compact hot fragment.

A serious reconstruction has to distinguish at least four inventories: what was aboard before breakup, what survived re-entry as material, what reached the Canadian surface or near-surface environment in detectable form, and what teams actually recovered.

Those denominators are not interchangeable.

This is also where intelligence history can distort accident history. A recovered component may have technical value disproportionate to its mass. From an intelligence perspective, one mechanically revealing fragment can matter more than kilograms of less interesting debris. From a public-health perspective, activity and accessibility matter more than espionage value.

The same object therefore lives inside different accounting systems.

The missing material also created a long tail of uncertainty. Search operations end because marginal effort ceases to justify expected benefit, not because the universe certifies that nothing remains. October 1978 was an operational endpoint, not metaphysical closure.

This distinction is worth keeping. Institutions routinely translate 'we stopped searching' into 'the problem ended.' Sometimes that is reasonable. It should still be recognized as a decision.
