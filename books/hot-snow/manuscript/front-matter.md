# Hot Snow

## Cosmos 954, Operation Morning Light, and the Reactor That Fell on Canada

Sven Hardy Benson

A satellite is an argument that the sky is infrastructure. Cosmos 954 made the opposite argument on January 24, 1978. It fell.

Not all at once and not as one recoverable machine. A Soviet radar-ocean-reconnaissance satellite carrying a nuclear reactor disintegrated during re-entry and spread radioactive material across northern Canada. The event became a strange inversion of the Cold War. Hardware built to watch naval movement from above became an object of search below. A remote territory became a debris field. Geological instruments designed to read naturally occurring uranium became detectors for fragments of a foreign reactor. Lawyers who had spent years turning abstract space treaties into language suddenly had an invoice.

This book is about what happens after a system escapes the category that made it manageable. In orbit, Cosmos 954 was a military spacecraft. During re-entry it was a trajectory problem. On the ground it became a radiological emergency, a sovereignty problem, a search-and-recovery operation, an environmental question, an intelligence opportunity, and eventually a precedent in international law.

That sequence matters more than the drama. It is easy to retell Cosmos 954 as a nuclear satellite crashing into Canada. The interesting part begins when everyone has to decide what that sentence means operationally.
