# Geologists Hunt a Satellite

The Geological Survey of Canada had spent more than a decade learning how to see radiation from aircraft. The intended quarry was geology.

Airborne gamma-ray spectrometry is useful because different radioactive elements produce characteristic energy signatures. Geological surveys can map variations associated with potassium, uranium and thorium. By the late 1970s, Canadian scientists had instruments, aircraft experience, calibration habits and northern field knowledge built for mineral exploration and mapping.

Then a Soviet reactor fell out of orbit.

The Survey's own institutional history says its airborne spectrometer made the first identification of radioactive Cosmos 954 debris on the frozen surface of Great Slave Lake, about forty-three kilometres northwest of Fort Reliance. Full-spectrum recording helped distinguish gamma rays from manufactured isotopes from natural radioactivity.

This was not a lucky gadget repurposing. It was accumulated capability moving sideways. The scientists knew what ordinary terrain looked like in gamma spectra because they had spent years measuring it. Anomaly detection works best when the observer understands the normal background.

That is an important lesson for frontier sensing in general. We often imagine special threats requiring special sensors. Sometimes they require mature boring sensors operated by people who have learned the nuisance signals.

The satellite hunt also inverted the politics of uranium. The same measurement tradition associated with finding uranium resources was used to find uranium-bearing debris that nobody wanted. Resource became contaminant by context.

Tools do not know why they were built. Institutions do. That can make institutional imagination more limiting than hardware.

Cosmos 954 gave Canadian geophysics a task its designers had not optimized for, and the instrument still became useful because the physical question was adjacent enough: what kind of gamma radiation is coming from the ground, and is it supposed to be there?
