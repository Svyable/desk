# Sending the Bill

Canada eventually did something wonderfully bureaucratic to a fallen Soviet reactor. It itemized the consequences.

The Canadian claim sought compensation for costs associated with locating, recovering, removing, testing and cleaning up radioactive debris. Later United Nations legal analysis records Canadian expenditures of roughly C$13.97 million and describes Canada's argument that reasonable, proximate, calculable response costs should count as compensable damage.

This was not a trivial move. International liability law often becomes clearest after somebody has to price an unprecedented event.

What is the damage from a radioactive satellite if nobody can point to a destroyed house? Canada argued, in effect, that preventing and limiting harm costs money and that those costs are part of the harm relationship. Search aircraft, laboratory work and cleanup are not voluntary luxuries simply because catastrophe was avoided.

That principle matters far beyond space law. Emergency response frequently succeeds by creating an absence: no one picks up the hot fragment, no contaminated object remains in a community, no later exposure occurs. A narrow legal system can mistake successful prevention for evidence that little happened.

Canada's claim pushed against that paradox.

The invoice also forced an epistemic discipline. Costs had to be connected to the intrusion with enough certainty to defend. Not every government expense during Morning Light automatically belonged on Moscow's bill.

This is where law can improve historical thinking. Causation, proximity and reasonable mitigation are not merely courtroom vocabulary. They are tools for separating the consequences of the satellite from everything governments chose to do around it.

A bill is a theory of causation with numbers attached.
