# Law after Impact

Space law is often written while everyone is still optimistic about space.

The Outer Space Treaty, Rescue Agreement, Liability Convention and Registration Convention emerged from an era trying to keep expanding national capability from becoming unbounded terrestrial conflict. Cosmos 954 tested that architecture with a distinctly unromantic object: debris.

Debris creates legal clarity because it lands somewhere. The launching state is no longer a philosophical category. The damaged state's territory is not an orbital abstraction. Search teams cost money. Hazard has coordinates.

The Liability Convention imposed absolute liability for damage caused by a space object on the surface of Earth. Cosmos 954 became the canonical case because Canada actually advanced a state claim connected to the convention.

Yet the event also exposed limits. What counts as damage when the principal cost is precaution? How should environmental contamination be valued when population exposure is low? How much recovery effort is reasonable? What information must the launching state provide about a hazardous object before impact?

Later nuclear-power-source principles addressed parts of this terrain, and the IAEA now treats space nuclear-source accidents as part of emergency preparedness. That evolution matters because technology can outrun institutional ownership more quickly than treaty negotiation.

The accident did not produce a ban on nuclear power in space. Nor should the history be simplified into that result. Nuclear systems remain valuable for missions where sunlight is weak or intermittent. The governance problem is not 'nuclear equals forbidden.' It is how to design, launch, operate, dispose of and, when necessary, respond to systems whose failure crosses borders.

Good law begins where engineering promises stop.
