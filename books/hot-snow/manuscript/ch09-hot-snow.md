# Hot Snow

Snow made the accident legible and treacherous at the same time.

A dark fragment on white ground can look conspicuous, but the dangerous property was not color. Radioactive debris did not need to resemble machinery. Some pieces were tiny. Some locations were remote enough that the probability of casual contact was low, which reduced immediate human risk while making systematic search harder.

Cold also helped preserve the strangeness of the operation. Aircraft and field teams were not searching an industrial site with fences and known piping. They were searching lakes, forest, snow and tundra for pieces whose spatial distribution had been created by orbital breakup and atmospheric transport.

The phrase radioactive contamination can encourage a mental picture of a smooth stain. Cosmos 954 produced something more granular: scattered objects and dispersed material across a huge region. Risk depended on proximity, fragment activity, exposure pathway and time.

This matters when reading later claims about the affected area. Health Canada gives at least 124,000 square kilometres. The IAEA describes a footprint on the order of hundreds of kilometres and more than 100,000 square kilometres. Those are operational geographies, not statements that every point inside received the same contamination.

The search therefore had two scales that did not fit comfortably together. Strategically, the footprint was enormous. Tactically, a detector might be hunting a specific hot piece.

That mismatch is why public comparisons are hard. 'Only' a small amount was recovered can sound comforting or alarming depending on what one thinks the unrecovered fraction did. Without a materials-and-dispersion model, recovery percentage is not a complete risk metric.

Hot snow is a useful phrase precisely because it is wrong to the senses. The hazard was real. The landscape looked normal. Instruments had to teach responders where ordinary winter had become evidence.
