# Radar from Orbit

Cosmos 954 was not a science satellite that happened to contain a reactor. Its power source existed because the mission wanted something difficult: persistent radar surveillance from orbit.

Radar is hungry. Optical sensors can wait for sunlight. Passive radio receivers listen. Active radar sends energy out and waits for a return, which means useful performance competes with power, aperture, geometry, orbit and mass. The Soviet Radar Ocean Reconnaissance Satellite system solved part of that problem with nuclear power. A compact reactor could provide electricity independent of sunlight while the spacecraft searched oceans for targets whose military importance was obvious even when the specific intelligence products remained classified.

This matters because an accident begins in design. Cosmos 954 carried risk on purpose, not carelessly in the colloquial sense, but because the mission accepted a technology whose failure modes included a radioactive object eventually coming back down. The intended safety architecture was to dispose of the reactor core into a higher orbit at the end of useful life. Most comparable Soviet spacecraft did this. Cosmos 954 did not.

It is tempting to narrate the failure backward from impact and treat nuclear power in low Earth orbit as self-evidently reckless. The harder question is what the designers believed they had contained. A reactor that can be boosted to a long-lived disposal orbit is not the same risk as one expected to re-enter at mission end. Safety engineering lives in those distinctions. So does accident history.

The later legal and political argument often made the reactor itself sound like the event. The reactor was only one layer. There was also the surveillance mission, the orbital mechanics, the failed disposal sequence, tracking by other states, uncertainty about re-entry, and a recovery effort shaped by the fact that the object was both hazardous material and foreign military technology.

That dual status complicated everything. A fragment could be dangerous because it was radioactive, valuable because it was intelligence, diplomatically sensitive because it was Soviet property, and scientifically useful because it offered direct evidence about a reactor designed for space.

There are cleaner categories after the fact. There were fewer on the snow.
