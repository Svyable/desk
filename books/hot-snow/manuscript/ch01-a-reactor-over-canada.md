# A Reactor Over Canada

On January 24, 1978, northern Canada acquired a nuclear reactor it had not ordered. It arrived without a container, without a manifest that Canadian officials controlled, and without any reliable promise that all of it would arrive in one place.

Cosmos 954 was a Soviet reconnaissance satellite powered by a small nuclear reactor. Its orbit had decayed. Re-entry broke the spacecraft apart. Radioactive debris spread across a long northern footprint that Canadian public-health authorities now describe as covering at least 124,000 square kilometres, running from the Great Slave Lake region toward northern Alberta and Saskatchewan. The scale is awkward to imagine because the debris was not a plume with a clean edge. It was a search problem laid over winter geography.

The accident has sometimes been compressed into one reassuring number: only a tiny fraction of the reactor fuel was recovered. Health Canada, citing the former Atomic Energy Control Board, gives an estimate of about 0.1 percent of the power source. That number sounds like failure until the denominator is understood. Nobody knew where every fragment had gone, what had burned, what had aerosolized, what had fallen into snow or water, or which signals on an instrument were natural geology rather than Soviet machinery.

The operation that followed was called Morning Light. Hundreds of Canadian military and government personnel took part, joined by a 120-person American Nuclear Emergency Search Team. The name has the false calm of many government codenames. What those teams were doing was closer to forensic ecology. They were trying to distinguish ordinary radiation from debris made extraordinary by origin, isotope, geometry and location.

One of the first useful clues came from an instrument that had not been designed for this accident. The Geological Survey of Canada had spent years developing airborne gamma-ray spectrometry for geological mapping, mineral exploration and environmental radiation monitoring. The same expertise could now be pointed at the ice. A tool for finding useful uranium deposits became a tool for finding unwanted uranium debris.

That conversion is the spine of this story. States prepare by categories: military, geological, medical, diplomatic, legal. Emergencies arrive cross-indexed.

Cosmos 954 did not merely fall through the atmosphere. It fell through organizational boundaries. The people who eventually made sense of it were often useful precisely because they had been trained for something else.
