# Another Reactor Falls

Cosmos 954 was not the last Soviet nuclear-powered satellite to force the world to watch a reactor come down.

In 1983, Cosmos 1402 suffered its own failure. Its components re-entered separately, and the reactor core came down over the South Atlantic rather than populated land. No Canadian-scale cleanup followed.

The comparison is useful because outcome can disguise design risk. Cosmos 1402 could be remembered as less serious because geography spared governments the spectacle of radioactive fragments on land. But an ocean impact does not retroactively make an uncontrolled nuclear re-entry a well-controlled disposal method.

Accident learning therefore needs near misses and lucky outcomes, not only damage totals.

After Cosmos 954, international bodies studied nuclear power sources in outer space more systematically. Engineering practices also evolved. The historical record of Soviet radar ocean-reconnaissance satellites includes other reactor disposal outcomes, allowing a finished book to compare successful high-orbit ejection, failed ejection, partial breakup and later re-entry behavior.

That comparison is essential before making claims about systemic recklessness. One failure tells us a system can fail. A fleet history tells us how the system actually performed.

The Cold War liked hardware that could be judged by mission success. Safety asks for a different denominator: launches, anomalies, successful disposal events, uncontrolled re-entries and consequences.

Cosmos 954 became famous because Canada was underneath it. Cosmos 1402 reminds us that the sky does not care whether a failure becomes a headline.
