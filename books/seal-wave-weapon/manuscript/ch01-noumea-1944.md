# 1 — Nouméa, 1944

The first thing to remove is the tsunami.

Not from the story. From the first few minutes of it.

Put a charge underwater and detonate it. Water moves. This is not controversial. Put the charge in a different depth and the water moves differently. Change the bottom, the distance between charges, the timing, the geometry of the bay, and you have changed the experiment again. Somewhere inside that parameter space is the seed of Project SEAL, a wartime investigation that would later acquire one of those names history cannot resist: the tsunami bomb.

The name is almost too good. It arrives already filmed.

A weapon goes off beyond the horizon. The sea stands up. A city disappears.

That is not where the evidence begins.

It begins in the Pacific war, with amphibious operations and an engineering question. Could explosions in water generate a useful inundation effect? The question was not occult, and it was not ridiculous. Navies already understood underwater blast as a physical fact. Coastal attack was a military problem. The Pacific supplied more coast than anyone wanted. What remained was the treacherous word *useful*.

Useful is where weapons leave demonstrations behind.

A physicist can be satisfied that an effect exists. An engineer has to make it happen on purpose. A weapons planner inherits worse requirements: the effect must happen at a useful scale, at a useful distance, against a useful target, with equipment that can be delivered, under conditions the enemy is unlikely to arrange for your convenience. It should preferably work twice.

This book lives in those extra requirements.

Thomas D. J. Leech, an engineer associated with Auckland University College and wartime New Zealand research, became the central technical figure in the work later called Project SEAL. A New Zealand Department of Scientific and Industrial Research report bearing his name, *Project SEAL: The Generation of Waves by Means of Explosives*, was published in Wellington in 1950. That bibliographic fact is wonderfully stubborn. Whatever folklore later accumulated, there really was a Project SEAL, and its subject really was the generation of waves by explosives.

That sentence proves less than it feels like it proves.

This is the recurring hazard of secret-history books. The discovery of a genuine document produces a little narcotic rush. The program existed. The code name is real. The stamp was real. The people were real. Therefore the rumored capability must have been real too.

No.

Existence is the first rung.

Project SEAL deserves something better than debunking because it deserves something better than credulity. The interesting question is not whether military institutions have investigated frightening ideas. Of course they have. Their job description practically requires a tolerance for frightening ideas. The interesting question is how an idea travels from effect to weapon, and where, exactly, it stops.

Released New Zealand records became the basis of a burst of reporting in 1999. Contemporary accounts described wartime experiments, later U.S. and British interest, and postwar contact involving Karl Compton. They also helped fix the phrase “tsunami bomb” in public memory. Those reports are useful maps to an archive. They are not substitutes for it.

That distinction matters because the famous details are precisely the details most likely to outrun their paperwork.

One number says thousands of experimental explosions. Another says millions of kilograms of explosive arranged along kilometers of coastline could produce a destructive wave. Another retelling promotes the concept until it stands beside the atomic bomb. Each may have an archival ancestor. But a number without its denominator is costume jewelry. Thousands of what? A calculated explosive requirement under what depth profile? A destructive wave at what range? Comparable to an atomic weapon according to whom, and in which dimension—strategic promise, energy, novelty, or enthusiasm?

The archive has to answer before the adjective does.

There is also a physical reason to be suspicious of clean retellings. Tsunamis are not merely very large ordinary waves. Their destructive behavior depends on wavelength, water depth, propagation, seafloor displacement, coastal geometry and shoaling. The ocean is not a billiard table on which energy placed at one point arrives obediently at another. It spreads, refracts, reflects, disperses, breaks, and wastes your beautiful calculation on geography.

That is why scale is not a footnote to Project SEAL. Scale is the plot.

Suppose a small underwater detonation produces a measurable wave. Good. We have established an effect. Suppose a pattern of charges produces a larger one. Better. We have learned something about geometry. Neither result, by itself, tells us that a practical weapon can generate a city-destroying coastal inundation from an operationally useful position.

Between those statements sits almost the entire ocean.

And yet secrecy changes how that gap feels from the outside.

When an ordinary engineering program fails, its failure can become part of the public record: the prototype missed its requirement; the cost exploded; the test article broke; the contract died. A secret program can leave a stranger fossil. The frightening objective escapes. The tedious limiting conditions remain in a report few people read. Decades later the objective is remembered as the capability.

This is one reason classified technological history is so vulnerable to what might be called noun inflation.

Remote viewing becomes psychic spying.

Radar deception becomes phantom aircraft.

Wave-generation research becomes a tsunami bomb.

The nouns are not necessarily false. They are compressed. Compression deletes the part an engineer would most want to know.

Did it work?

Not: did something happen.

Did the system do the military job assigned to it?

SEAL gives us an unusually good place to enforce that distinction because the physics refuses to flatter the story. Water keeps the books. Energy has to go somewhere. A charge that produces an impressive local disturbance has not negotiated a treaty with bathymetry. A wave that exists has not promised to survive the continental shelf. A model that says a geometry could work has not placed the explosives. A classified file that says officials were interested has not supplied an operational unit.

This does not make the project trivial. Quite the opposite.

The serious version is stranger than the cartoon. During a world war, officials and engineers were willing to test whether the ocean itself could be recruited into amphibious warfare. They performed enough work that a formal technical report followed. Interest survived long enough to touch the early nuclear-test era. Then the project receded, while the phrase that best described its ambition survived better than the engineering distinctions that constrained it.

That is a pattern worth learning.

Modern claims about hidden technology often arrive in the same grammatical form: a program existed, therefore a capability existed; a capability was discussed, therefore it was demonstrated; a demonstration occurred, therefore it scaled; a system scaled, therefore it was deployed. Four separate bridges are crossed in one sentence.

Project SEAL lets us walk back across them.

The job ahead is deliberately unglamorous. Recover the test logs. Recover Leech’s report. Identify what the celebrated numbers actually measured. Reconstruct who believed what, and when. Separate wartime urgency from postwar assessment. Compare the project’s internal hydrodynamics with later technical work on explosion-generated waves. Find the point where the weapon stops being an experiment and becomes a calculation—or, if the evidence says otherwise, find the point where the calculation became a demonstrated system.

No outcome gets immunity.

If the primary record shows a weapon much closer to practicality than later skeptics assume, that belongs in the book. If it shows an ingenious experiment inflated by secrecy and retrospective language into a capability it never possessed, that belongs here too.

For now we have a real program, a real physical effect, a real archive, and a famous weapon whose demonstrated performance remains to be reconstructed.

That is enough.

It is also much more interesting than starting with a wall of water.
