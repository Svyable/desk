# Project SEAL — explosion-wave scaling audit

## Scope

This is one bounded stress test of the book's central distinction between **an explosion making a wave** and **a practical weapon producing destructive coastal inundation at useful range**. It does not settle Project SEAL's wartime performance before Leech's 1950 report and test records are recovered.

## Later U.S. Navy-sponsored research gives the manuscript a hard physical comparator

In October 1968 the U.S. Office of Naval Research sponsored *Handbook of Explosion-Generated Water Waves, Volume I — State of the Art*, prepared by William G. Van Dorn, Bernard Le Méhauté, and Li-San Hwang. A stable public copy is hosted by the University of California's eScholarship repository:

https://escholarship.org/uc/item/13h5j9k9

The report matters because it is not a retrospective newspaper judgment about whether the wartime New Zealand project was a "tsunami bomb." It is a technical review of explosion-generated waves as a class: generation, propagation, scaling, and coastal effects. That makes it a useful independent control on any Project SEAL claim that jumps from a measured local wave to useful distant inundation.

A later peer-reviewed reanalysis, Korycansky and Lynett, “Offshore breaking of impact tsunami: The Van Dorn effect revisited,” *Geophysical Research Letters* 32 (2005), explicitly traces the offshore-breaking problem back to Van Dorn et al. while also showing that later modeling complicated the original picture. The authors note that bottom damping can reduce wave amplitude enough to delay breaking until much nearer shore, rather than producing the enormous offshore surf zone implied by a simple breaking criterion.

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1029/2004GL021918

That correction is important. The manuscript should not replace Project SEAL folklore with a second neat story in which "the continental shelf kills the wave." The stronger physical claim is narrower: **source efficiency, propagation, bathymetry, bottom interaction, breaking, and run-up all sit between explosive energy and useful inundation, and later technical work has revised how some of those losses should be modeled.**

## Strongest counterargument

A reader could reasonably object that a 1968 handbook and a 2005 impact-tsunami paper cannot tell us what Leech's particular charge arrays achieved in 1944–45. Correct. Generic hydrodynamics cannot substitute for the Project SEAL test geometry, measured wave records, bathymetry, charge depth, spacing, timing, and scaling assumptions. The later literature is a comparator and falsification tool, not a verdict on archival facts not yet recovered.

The reverse objection also matters. Demonstrating that explosive waves suffer severe scaling and propagation constraints does not establish that every militarily useful inundation concept is impossible. A near-shore target, favorable shelf, deliberately phased array, or different delivery concept can change the problem. "Inefficient" and "useless" are not synonyms.

## Manuscript consequence

Chapter 1 currently has the right instinct when it says scale is the plot, but later chapters should make that claim quantitative rather than rhetorical. Once Leech's report is obtained, reconstruct at least one proposed full-scale configuration from its actual assumptions and carry it through a transparent ledger:

- explosive mass and energy;
- charge depth, spacing, and timing;
- fraction of explosive energy transferred into the relevant wave field;
- initial wavelength and amplitude;
- water depth and source-to-shore distance;
- propagation and geometric spreading;
- bottom interaction and damping;
- breaking criterion;
- shoaling and run-up at the proposed target coast;
- operational delivery burden.

The famous `2,000,000 kg over 8 km` figure should not enter narrative as a weapon specification until this reconstruction identifies its archival source and shows what outcome the calculation actually predicted.

## Better book-level distinction

The eventual manuscript should keep at least four propositions separate:

1. an underwater explosion generated a measurable wave;
2. an arrangement of charges increased or shaped that wave;
3. a scaling model predicted destructive coastal inundation under stated conditions;
4. an operational system demonstrated that result at militarily relevant scale and range.

Project SEAL can be historically important even if the record reaches only the first three. A successful experiment is not diminished by refusing to promote it into a deployed capability.

## Research queue narrowed

1. Acquire and page-index Leech's 1950 *Project SEAL: The Generation of Waves by Means of Explosives*.
2. Identify the provenance of the 3,700-test and 2,000,000-kg/8-km claims inside primary records rather than later press summaries.
3. Extract test geometry, bathymetry, charge depth, spacing, timing, measured amplitudes, ranges, and scaling law from Leech.
4. Page-index the 1968 ONR handbook sections directly relevant to generation efficiency, propagation, breaking, and coastal effects.
5. Compare the handbook with the 2005 Korycansky–Lynett correction so the book does not fossilize a superseded shelf-breaking model.
6. Only then revise the manuscript's physics passage and Chapters 7–8 with a worked calculation whose assumptions are visible to the reader.

## Evidence boundary

The 1968 handbook establishes a later U.S. Navy-sponsored technical literature on explosion-generated water waves. The 2005 paper establishes that an influential part of that literature was subsequently re-examined. Neither source establishes what Project SEAL demonstrated. Their value is methodological: they make the manuscript prove each bridge from explosive effect to military capability instead of letting the phrase “tsunami bomb” cross those bridges for it.
