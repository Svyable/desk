# Back Matter

## About the Book

*Moravec's Revenge* is a book about an assumption, not a prophecy: the decades-old idea that abstract reasoning is the hard problem for a machine and physical common sense is the easy one, stated backward from how the two actually rank in computational difficulty.

The book does not argue that general-purpose robots are about to replace physical labor on a fixed schedule, and it does not argue that the last few years of humanoid-robot news are mostly hype with nothing underneath. It argues that a specific, datable, and still incomplete technical convergence — cheap simulation, teleoperated data collection, and video-pretrained foundation models, arriving alongside a decade of electric-vehicle-driven component cost declines — has made a fifty-year-old bottleneck newly tractable, and that the people most exposed to what happens next are rarely the people writing about it.

## Notes and Sources

The manuscript was researched chapter by chapter using the source ledger maintained alongside this book.

The sourcing hierarchy is deliberate. Government and standards-body documents, company filings and primary announcements, peer-reviewed or preprint academic research, and first-party technical publications are preferred for the claims they can answer directly. Contemporaneous reporting from reputable outlets is used where the event itself — a funding round, a pilot announcement, a regulatory action — is the subject. Vendor-supplied figures on fleet size, unit cost, or deployment scale are treated as claims requiring a date and, where possible, independent corroboration, not as settled facts; where no independent corroboration exists, the text says so.

Figures describing funding rounds, unit prices, and deployment scale are snapshots. Robotics companies revise cost targets and production timelines often, and a number accurate on the day it was reported can be stale within a year. The source ledger records access and publication dates so a reader can tell an observed result from a stated ambition.

Where the evidence cuts against the book's own argument — warehouse employment that grew rather than shrank through a decade of automation investment, bank-teller employment that rose for years after the ATM, task-based critiques of headline automation-risk estimates — the manuscript keeps that evidence rather than arguing around it. A thesis is only as useful as its ability to survive the facts that complicate it.

The research files accompanying this manuscript contain the chapter-level source ledger and chapter briefs, including source titles, dates, URLs, and use notes. Those materials are the canonical source record for this draft.

## Acknowledgments

This book depends on institutions and people willing to publish uncomfortable, checkable specifics: standards bodies that write down exactly what a safety requirement covers, statistical agencies that keep a wage table current, regulators who publish disengagement reports and incident investigations instead of press releases alone, academic economists who post their data and methods alongside their conclusions, and engineers and companies willing to say precisely what a robot did on a given day rather than what it might someday do.

The historical record here is a useful corrective. Robotics has produced confident predictions before, and a good number of them aged badly in public. The honest way to write about the current wave is to keep that history in view rather than assume this decade is exempt from it.

Any remaining errors of interpretation belong to the author.
