How machines learned to move, and who pays when they do.

For forty years, the safest labor-market advice in the world was to bet on your hands. Chess, tax law, radiology, and translation looked like the jobs a machine would eventually eat; loading a truck, changing a bedpan, or bussing a table did not, because folding a towel turns out to draw on hundreds of millions of years of evolved sensorimotor tuning that a large language model does not have. This asymmetry has a name — Moravec's paradox, after the roboticist who stated it plainly in 1988 — and it quietly shaped what counted as a resilient career, which industries got called "automation-proof," and which fifty years of robotics funding treated as a slow, unglamorous backwater while software ate the world instead.

The working thesis of this book is that the paradox did not get repealed so much as it got a fifty-year data problem solved out from under it. Between roughly 2022 and 2027, cheap simulation, teleoperated data-collection fleets, and video-pretrained foundation models did for grasping and walking roughly what internet text did for language — and actuators, batteries, and motors borrowed from a decade of electric-vehicle manufacturing got cheap at the same time the software got good enough to use them. The result is not a finished robot revolution. It is a still-uneven, still-overhyped, still economically unresolved one, playing out right now in specific warehouses, specific factories, specific nursing homes, and a small number of actual homes — and the book tries to describe that uneven present honestly rather than promise a tidy future.

It is also a book about who is exposed first. The occupations most vulnerable to a general-purpose, ambulatory, dexterous machine are disproportionately low-wage, physically demanding, and held by workers who were told, correctly for decades, that this exact kind of job was the safe one. The book takes seriously both the real economic history that complicates simple automation-doom stories — ATM machines did not shrink the number of bank tellers for years after their introduction — and the real asymmetry in who bears the risk while that history plays out again.
