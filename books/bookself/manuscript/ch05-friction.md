# Friction

A door can be unlocked and still be hard to open.

The hinge catches. The frame has swollen. Your hands are full. The handle is cold. There is a box in the way. None of these obstacles is dramatic enough to explain a life. Together they can explain why a room remains unused.

Beginnings often fail this way.

We look for motives large enough to match the importance of the project: fear of failure, fear of success, lack of discipline, childhood wounds, perfectionism, self-sabotage. Any of these may be real. But a surprising amount of behavior is controlled by smaller architecture. The form is buried on a website. The guitar is in a case in a closet. The gym requires a drive in the wrong direction. The software opens with twelve settings. The person you need to call is in another time zone. The first task is not defined. The document is named “new final draft 7” and lives somewhere you cannot remember.

Friction is morally boring.

That is why it is underrated.

When a meaningful project stalls, we prefer a meaningful explanation. It feels insulting to discover that the difference between doing and not doing was whether the shoes were by the door.

But systems do not care whether their causes flatter us.

Behavioral economics made one version of this point unusually visible through research on defaults. Brigitte Madrian and Dennis Shea studied 401(k) participation at a large U.S. company after a change to automatic enrollment. Participation rose sharply when the default changed from requiring employees to opt in to enrolling them unless they opted out. The workers had not all undergone a simultaneous philosophical conversion about retirement. The decision architecture changed.

That result belongs to a specific institutional setting, and default effects vary by context. But the broader implication is hard to ignore: sometimes behavior that looks like preference is partly the residue of path design.

The same is true at the scale of one person.

If every beginning requires a fresh act of will, the environment collects a tax.

Consider writing. The romantic image is a person confronting the blank page. The practical reality begins earlier. Where is the file? Is the computer charged? Are notifications on? Is the idea captured anywhere? Is the desk covered in other work? Does the person need forty minutes to rediscover what they meant yesterday? Does opening the project expose five unfinished administrative tasks first?

Each question is trivial.

The total is not.

Friction compounds at thresholds because thresholds are where motivation is least supported by momentum. Once a runner is three miles into a run, tying shoes is irrelevant. Once a writer is absorbed in a scene, file naming is irrelevant. Once a meeting is underway, the effort of finding the address has disappeared. The danger is concentrated in the minutes before the activity can begin generating its own reasons to continue.

This suggests a practical asymmetry: remove friction most aggressively from the first five minutes.

Not because the first five minutes are sacred. Because later minutes have already been subsidized by engagement.

A person who wants to read more can place the book where the phone normally goes. Someone who wants to practice an instrument can leave it accessible rather than ceremonially protected. A team that wants honest incident reports can make the report form short enough to complete while memory is fresh. A company that wants customer interviews can put scheduling authority and a list of names in the same place rather than requiring a new approval chain for every conversation.

Good beginning design is often embarrassingly concrete.

That is its strength.

The opposite mistake is to build elaborate motivational machinery while leaving the physical and procedural threshold untouched. We create goals, reward systems, inspirational reminders, public commitments, and tracking dashboards around an action that still takes eleven annoying steps to initiate.

Then, when the action does not happen, we blame desire.

A better diagnosis starts with the path.

Walk it slowly.

Suppose you want to cook dinner at home three nights a week. Do not ask first whether you value health or savings. Assume you do. Start at six o’clock on a tired Wednesday. What has to be true?

There must be food in the house. The food must not already have spoiled. You must know what you are making. The kitchen must be usable. The pan must not be buried under yesterday’s dishes. The recipe must fit the time available. If a child needs attention, the meal must survive interruption. If your workday ends unpredictably, ingredients with a narrow window create risk. If the only plan requires chopping eight vegetables, motivation has been assigned the job of solving logistics.

The statement “I don’t cook enough” hides an operating system.

The same is true for beginning a career transition. “Apply for jobs” sounds like one action. In reality it may require deciding what roles count, finding openings, tailoring materials, revisiting old experience, contacting references, tolerating the emotional exposure of rejection, and scheduling interviews around current employment. If the first session is simply labeled “job search,” the person begins by having to design the process while already inside it.

Undefined work is friction disguised as freedom.

This explains why an overly broad first step can feel heavy even when it is technically easy. “Work on the book” contains dozens of possible actions. “Open chapter six and rewrite the paragraph beginning with the train station” has already spent some of the decision cost.

Peter Gollwitzer’s work on implementation intentions is relevant here because specifying the cue and the response can reduce the need for in-the-moment deliberation. A plan such as “after I put the children to bed on Tuesday, I will sit at the kitchen table and revise for twenty minutes” is not powerful because Tuesday has mystical properties. It is powerful because the start condition has been made legible.

Ambiguity creates friction.

So does abundance.

Sheena Iyengar and Mark Lepper’s well-known experiments on choice examined conditions in which larger assortments attracted attention yet smaller assortments could produce more purchasing or better motivation. The research has generated a large literature, including debates about when and how choice overload appears; it should not be turned into the slogan “more choice is bad.” The useful point for beginnings is narrower: selecting among options is itself work, and the cost of selection matters most when action has not yet acquired momentum.

The person who wants to exercise and owns a hundred possible workouts may be less operationally ready than the person whose Tuesday plan is always the same walk.

The writer with eight note-taking apps may have more capability and less entry.

The team with a dozen project priorities has not created freedom. It has moved prioritization into every morning.

Friction can be cognitive as well as physical.

There is emotional friction too.

The first action may require asking permission, risking visible incompetence, encountering a person you envy, reopening an old failure, or admitting that an identity has changed. These costs cannot always be engineered away, and pretending they can would cheapen them. But they can sometimes be separated from unnecessary procedural costs.

If the emotional threshold is high, the logistical threshold should be low.

A difficult conversation does not become more courageous because you also forgot to schedule it.

A first therapy appointment does not become more meaningful because the insurance directory is unusable.

A person returning to school after twenty years does not need the application website to add a second test of belonging.

Institutions that care about participation should understand this. Every unnecessary form field is a theory of who has spare patience. Every office hour is a theory of who can arrive at that time. Every unexplained acronym is a theory of who already belongs. Every required document is a theory of who can retrieve it.

Friction is political when it is distributed unequally.

A wealthy person can pay to remove many thresholds. Childcare, transportation, tutoring, administrative help, legal advice, subscriptions, private space, reliable hardware, and flexible scheduling all convert money into lower friction. Social capital does similar work. Knowing one person who can explain the process may replace hours of searching. Having a family member who attended the institution can turn mystery into instructions.

“Just start” ignores this infrastructure.

A serious theory of beginnings has to include it.

The answer is not that individual design no longer matters. It matters differently. Someone facing high external friction may need to spend more of their beginning energy on building access rather than performing the visible activity itself. The first trace might be securing childcare, finding a mentor, gathering documents, or negotiating one protected evening.

Those are not fake starts if they directly alter the path.

The distinction again is diagnostic. Does the preparation reduce a real constraint, or does it merely make the imagined future feel organized?

A folder can be avoidance. A bus pass can be infrastructure.

A course can be delay. A required certification can be infrastructure.

A savings target can be fear. It can also be the margin that makes a dangerous transition survivable.

Context decides.

There is another form of friction that successful people often encounter: reputation friction.

The novice has little status to lose. The expert does.

A lawyer who begins painting, a senior engineer who begins managing, a celebrated founder who returns to school, a professor who learns a new field, an athlete who becomes a broadcaster—each may experience the unfamiliar activity under the gaze of an established identity. Competence in one domain makes beginnerhood in another feel more expensive.

This is not merely ego. Reputation has economic value. Colleagues form expectations. Audiences prefer continuity. A public figure who experiments visibly can be punished for inconsistency. A professional who admits ignorance can encounter a culture that confuses rank with universality.

The threshold is partly social.

One response is to begin where the old reputation has less reach.

Take the class under conditions where you do not have to lead it. Join the workshop where nobody reports to you. Make the first object without attaching the company logo. Study the new field before producing a grand theory about it. Let the beginning be small enough that competence can grow before brand management takes over.

This is not secrecy as cowardice. It is friction reduction for identity.

We will return to private starts later because privacy has its own dangers. Isolation can protect delusion as easily as experimentation. But there is a legitimate period in which a new self benefits from not having to negotiate with the audience of the old one.

The shelf can have a back row.

Friction also explains why “motivation” is such a poor single variable. A person can want something deeply and fail to begin because the start sequence is badly designed. Another person can want it only moderately and begin because the environment makes action ordinary.

We recognize this easily with commercial products. A company studies drop-off in a checkout flow. It counts clicks. It watches where customers abandon a form. It does not accuse them of insufficient commitment to purchasing. It redesigns the funnel.

People deserve some of the same curiosity.

Where do you drop off?

Not abstractly. Literally.

At what minute does the intended action become another activity?

You sit down to write and open email first.

You decide to exercise and cannot find clean clothes.

You plan to practice and realize the instrument needs setup.

You mean to apply and get stuck choosing which role.

You intend to call and start drafting the perfect opening sentence.

The exact point of departure contains better information than the global judgment “I procrastinated again.”

Friction can then be removed, accepted, or used.

Used?

Yes. Not all friction is bad.

Some thresholds protect what matters. A cooling-off period before a major purchase creates useful delay. A review step before sending money reduces error. A password manager that requires confirmation before exposing credentials adds friction for safety. A person trying to stop compulsive checking may log out of an app, moving the action from automatic to deliberate.

Friction is a design variable, not an enemy.

We want less of it in front of valuable beginnings and more of it in front of impulsive alternatives that steal those beginnings.

This creates a practical principle more powerful than “make good habits easy and bad habits hard,” because it can be applied moment by moment: protect the threshold.

The twenty minutes before an important start are often more valuable than the twenty minutes after it.

If your best writing happens once you are inside the document, then email is most dangerous before the document opens. If exercise is enjoyable after ten minutes, the critical design problem is getting outside. If the meeting goes well once it begins, scheduling may be the real bottleneck. If you love studying once the materials are arranged, the room setup deserves more attention than another speech about discipline.

The threshold is where intention remains exposed.

Remove what does not need to be there.

Name what cannot be removed.

Price what must be paid.

And notice something surprising: when friction falls, identity can change without a dramatic internal conversion.

You do the action more often. Because you do it more often, there is more evidence. Because there is more evidence, the description of yourself updates.

One day “I am trying to write” becomes “I write in the mornings.”

Friction is often discussed as though all resistance were a defect. That is too simple.

Some friction is protection. A medication bottle that resists a child's hand, a review before money moves, a checklist before a dangerous procedure, a cooling-off period before an irreversible decision, or a second person required to authorize a consequential change can make action slower because speed is not the only value. The purpose of these barriers is to force attention, consent, redundancy, or competence into the path.

Other friction is inheritance. The form asks for information the institution already has. The meeting exists because nobody trusts the database. A new employee needs permission from a person who left six months ago. A citizen must learn which office owns a problem that the institution itself cannot route cleanly. A beginning fails not because the desire was weak but because the path contains accumulated administrative sediment.

The distinction matters. “Reduce friction” is bad advice if it removes safeguards and timid advice if it leaves pointless burden untouched.

A better question is what the friction is buying.

If a delay buys safety, informed consent, error detection, fairness, or a necessary transfer of knowledge, the design problem is to preserve the benefit while making the cost legible. If the delay buys nothing except organizational habit, status protection, or the convenience of the system, the burden should not be moralized as a test of commitment.

Friction is also unevenly distributed. Ten minutes is not ten minutes in every life. A form that requires a printer is trivial to someone with an office and expensive to someone using a phone on a bus. A class at six in the evening is convenient to one person and impossible to another because care work begins at five-thirty. A $200 refundable deposit is a nuisance to one household and a hard exclusion to another. A networking event that assumes comfort with a dominant language, dress code, or social script makes some beginners spend attention merely decoding the room.

The visible procedure can therefore be identical while the effective threshold is radically different.

This is one reason motivation is such a poor universal explanation for who begins. Systems frequently observe the people who crossed their threshold and infer desire from the survivors. The people who could not afford the threshold become invisible, and the institution congratulates itself on selecting the most committed.

Personal beginnings contain the same mistake. A person builds a plan around the best version of Tuesday rather than the ordinary one. The shoes are stored where they require three extra decisions. The writing file lives behind a forgotten password. The healthy meal assumes ingredients that are never in the house. The project depends on an uninterrupted two-hour block in a life that reliably produces twenty-minute pieces. Each obstacle looks small in isolation. In sequence, they become a tax collected at the exact moment motivation is weakest.

Friction compounds because every threshold is another opportunity for deferral. Locate the file. Remember the password. Find the notes. Clear the desk. Decide where to begin. Recover the context. Notice a message. Answer it. The work has not become harder; access to the work has become a miniature obstacle course.

Designing the threshold means moving some decisions upstream. Leave the instrument ready. End a session with the next action named. Put the recurring block on the calendar before the week becomes crowded. Precommit modest resources. Make the needed material visible. Create a restart note. Ask one person to expect an artifact rather than a promise. The goal is not to remove discipline. It is to spend discipline on the work instead of repeatedly spending it on admission to the work.

Institutions can do the same. Good onboarding does not merely explain the organization; it shortens the distance between arrival and a legitimate first contribution. Good public service design does not require citizens to understand the bureaucracy's internal chart. Good apprenticeship makes the first safe task obvious. Good software makes the next meaningful action more visible than the machinery behind it.

There is a temptation to interpret smoothness as seriousness. Important work should feel hard, so perhaps the difficult entrance is appropriate. But difficulty belongs in the substance. A violin should be difficult because music is difficult, not because the case requires six keys and the practice room can only be booked by fax. Medicine should be difficult because diagnosis and treatment demand expertise, not because the learner cannot discover which supervisor is permitted to sign the form.

Protect useful friction. Remove ceremonial friction. Compensate for friction that cannot be removed. And notice who is paying it.

The threshold is part of the opportunity. If a system repeatedly tells certain people that they lacked the will to begin, inspect the door before inspecting their character.

The sentence changes after the path does.
