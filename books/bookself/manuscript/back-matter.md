# Back Matter

**Bookself: The Story of Getting Started** was written by Sven Hardy Benson.

The book’s central metaphor — a **bookself** as the shelf of selves made legible by what a person has actually attempted — is original framing. It should not be read as the name of a validated psychological construct, clinical category, or scientific instrument.

The manuscript draws on research in psychology, organizational behavior, consumer behavior, decision making, and management to test narrower mechanisms around beginnings: self-efficacy, self-perception, identity-based motivation, implementation intentions, planning fallacy, defaults, temporal landmarks, deadlines, progress, habit formation, attention residue, provisional professional identities, goal disengagement and reengagement, psychological safety, and exploration versus exploitation.

A source ledger, chapter-source map, research brief, and falsification notes are maintained with the working edition in Sven Hardy Benson’s Desk. They are part of the book’s editorial record rather than substitutes for the original research.

No finding cited in the manuscript should be read as proving that beginning automatically improves a life. Starts can be badly chosen, harmful, structurally blocked, too expensive, or imposed by loss. Some commitments should be continued through difficulty. Some should be stopped. Some actions require licensing, informed consent, legal authority, supervision, or safeguards before a beginner has any right to attempt them at consequential scale.

The argument is narrower and, I hope, more useful.

When a question about a possible self can be tested ethically and at tolerable cost, action can create information that intention alone cannot. That information can be read badly. It can be overgeneralized. It can be ignored. It can also make the next decision more honest.

The first page is not a promise about the ending.

It is evidence that the story has entered the world.
