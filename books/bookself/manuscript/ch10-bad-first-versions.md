# Bad First Versions

The first version has two jobs that are easy to confuse.

It has to exist.

And it has to reveal what the second version needs.

It does not have to be admirable.

This should be obvious. It is not.

People routinely ask first attempts to carry reputational weight they have not earned the skill to carry. The first essay should sound like the mature writer. The first prototype should look fundable. The first presentation should feel natural. The first song should survive comparison with music produced by people who have spent decades learning rooms, instruments, software, rhythm, editing, and restraint.

The first version is made to answer an impossible question: “Am I already good enough to deserve continuing?”

No beginner can answer that honestly because continuation is one of the processes by which “good enough” is built.

This creates the oldest trap in making things. The person has enough taste to see the gap and not enough experience to know that the gap is ordinary.

Taste arrives through exposure. Skill arrives through production. In a world of near-infinite exposure, taste can get very far ahead.

A teenager with a phone can listen to the best recordings in history, watch championship athletes, study celebrated design, see museum collections, read award-winning essays, and observe professional kitchens. The standard enters the mind at high resolution.

Then the beginner makes something.

It is terrible by comparison.

The comparison is accurate and useless.

Of course the first cake does not resemble the pastry chef’s work. Of course the first short film does not look like the feature made by hundreds of professionals. Of course the first piece of software breaks. The error is not noticing the difference. The error is treating the difference as unexpected evidence.

Bad first versions are not good because badness builds character.

They are good when they reveal the structure of improvement.

A page tells you which idea cannot yet be explained. A prototype reveals the joint that fails. A rehearsal exposes the transition nobody has learned. A sales call reveals the question that makes the pitch collapse. A lesson plan reveals that students interpret the assignment differently from the teacher. A recipe reveals that “until done” is not useful instruction to someone who has never seen done.

The first version converts vague inadequacy into specific problems.

Specific problems are merciful.

“I am not a writer” is hard to act on.

“This section has no scene and repeats the argument from page six” is workable.

“I am bad at business” is metaphysical.

“Customers understand the product but will not pay enough to cover delivery” is an economic problem.

“I am terrible at dating” is a verdict.

“I avoid saying what I want until the other person has already made plans around my silence” is a behavior that can be examined.

A bad first version shrinks shame by increasing resolution.

Not automatically. Shame often does the opposite. It takes a specific weak result and expands it into a global identity. That is why review matters. The useful question after a poor attempt is not “What does this prove about me?” It is “What is actually wrong with the object or action?”

This shift is one of the quiet disciplines of craft.

Professionals criticize work more locally because they know how many things can be wrong independently. A novice says, “The photograph is bad.” A photographer can see exposure, composition, timing, lens choice, subject distance, color, editing, and the fact that sometimes none of these errors matter because the moment itself carries the image.

Expertise decomposes failure.

That is one reason a good teacher is so valuable at the beginning. They can convert “bad” into a next move.

A bad teacher can make the opposite error. By protecting confidence too aggressively, they deprive the beginner of contact with standards. Every attempt is praised. Every weakness is reframed as style. The student is kept emotionally safe and informationally poor.

Kindness is not the removal of truth.

It is the delivery of truth at a scale a person can use.

The first version therefore needs an appropriate audience.

Too large an audience creates reputational pressure before the work has learned to stand. Too small an audience can preserve delusion. The right early audience knows the difference between a draft and a declaration.

This is why workshops, studios, laboratories, rehearsals, scrimmages, prototypes, sandboxes, and test environments exist across very different domains. They create spaces where reality is invited in under conditions that do not yet carry the full consequence of release.

A rehearsal is not fake performance.

A prototype is not fake product.

A draft is not fake writing.

These are institutions for bad first versions.

They make imperfection operational.

The modern tendency to publish process can weaken these spaces. Social platforms reward “building in public,” daily updates, progress threads, public learning, and visible accountability. This can create community and opportunity. It can also collapse rehearsal into performance.

When every first attempt can be displayed, the beginner starts optimizing the evidence of learning for an audience.

The public draft becomes polished enough to protect reputation.

The private wrong turn disappears.

This matters because some mistakes are valuable only if they are allowed to be embarrassing without becoming searchable.

A scientist needs failed experiments recorded but not necessarily celebrated as content. A writer needs pages that can be killed without a postmortem thread. A leader may need to practice a difficult conversation before having it. A person learning a language needs sentences that no algorithm archives as an identity artifact.

Privacy preserves experimental range.

We will examine private starts directly in the next chapter. Here the point is simpler: the quality of a first version depends partly on whether it is allowed to perform the function of a first version.

That function is discovery.

Research on what Michael Norton, Daniel Mochon, and Dan Ariely called the IKEA effect offers an interesting complication. In experiments involving assembled boxes, origami, and Lego constructions, people sometimes valued objects they had made more highly than comparable objects made by others. The effect depended on successful completion; labor alone did not guarantee increased valuation when the creation was destroyed or left incomplete.

The paper is about valuation of self-made objects, not creative identity. Still, the finding points toward a familiar problem: making something can increase attachment to it.

This is emotionally useful and editorially dangerous.

The first version needs enough ownership that you will work on it again and enough distance that you can see what it needs.

Too little ownership and the object dies at the first criticism.

Too much and criticism becomes an attack on the maker.

Craft requires a strange split: this is mine, and this is not me.

The sentence is mine. Cut it.

The product is mine. Customers do not owe it demand.

The theory is mine. The data can kill it.

The plan is mine. Reality has veto power.

This is one reason versioning is psychologically powerful. Version one does not need to defend eternity. It is explicitly temporary. Naming a file “draft” or “prototype” can sound cosmetic, but language creates permission structures. The object is allowed to be judged by what it teaches, not only by whether it deserves permanence.

There is a danger here too. People can hide forever inside draft status. If every version remains preliminary, no audience ever gets authority to reject it.

A first version needs a scheduled death.

Not necessarily deletion. A point at which it either becomes version two, gets released into a more consequential environment, or is stopped.

The purpose is to prevent provisionality from becoming immunity.

This is similar to the problem of readiness. “Draft” can become another name for “not yet accountable.” The writer revises the opening for three years because as long as the manuscript is private, nobody can decide it does not work. The founder keeps refining the prototype because customers are less controllable than pixels. The student takes another preparatory course because the exam would produce a score.

A good first version is unfinished in a direction.

It points toward a test.

The page will be read by someone who can say where attention dropped.

The prototype will be put in a user’s hand.

The talk will be given to a small room.

The routine will run through a week containing normal disruption.

The new household rule will be tried long enough for everyone affected to respond.

The first version earns meaning by meeting a second environment.

This is what revision is: a conversation between an object and a consequence.

The most common misunderstanding is to treat revision as beautification. Make it cleaner, smoother, more professional. Sometimes that is the work. More often, reality has shown that the first model was wrong in a more fundamental way.

The reader cares about a different question.

The customer uses the feature backward.

The child does not understand the instruction.

The schedule collapses on Wednesday.

The material cannot tolerate the temperature.

The collaborator needs authority you did not expect to share.

Version two is not simply version one with better manners.

It may have a different architecture.

This is why attachment to the original idea can become costly. We want the first version to be secretly right because we want beginning to confirm foresight. But the honor of the first version lies elsewhere. It was wrong in a way that let us become less wrong.

That is a serious achievement.

Some fields institutionalize this better than others. Software assumes updates. Science assumes replication and revision, even if incentives do not always reward correction. Design assumes iteration. Manufacturing prototypes before production. Theatre rehearses. Musicians practice. Athletes train before competition.

Other domains treat first public versions as character evidence. A bad policy proposal can define a politician. A weak first article can feel dangerous in a competitive field. A novice manager’s mistake can affect employees. A parent’s first attempt at discipline occurs on a real child, not a sandbox.

Here the cost of bad versions is borne by others.

That changes the ethics.

“Move fast and break things” is tolerable only when the broken things are yours to break.

Beginners with power need stronger review environments because their learning can become another person’s harm. A new executive does not get to experiment with layoffs as casually as a novice cook experiments with salt. A new doctor learns under supervision because patients are not drafts. A public institution should pilot carefully because citizens cannot always opt out.

Bad first versions are a privilege when consequences are externalized.

The right to begin must be paired with responsibility for who absorbs the beginner’s errors.

This is why apprenticeship matters. It creates a structure in which a novice can encounter real work while responsibility is distributed according to competence. A good apprenticeship does not keep the beginner away from consequence; it calibrates consequence.

Modern economies sometimes remove that middle. Entry-level workers are asked for prior experience. Automated systems reduce the simple tasks from which novices once learned. Professionals are expected to arrive “job ready.” The institutional message is contradictory: do not make beginner mistakes here, but somehow acquire the experience elsewhere.

A society that hates bad first versions eventually runs out of second versions.

This is not only a labor-market problem. It is cultural. We need spaces where people can be visibly new without being treated as unserious.

The dance class for adults.

The community college.

The maker space.

The amateur league.

The open-mic night.

The junior role with actual teaching.

The local board where someone can learn governance before running a nation.

The writing group that knows a draft is not a referendum on intelligence.

These spaces are infrastructure for identity revision.

They let people add a volume to the shelf without pretending it was professionally edited before page one.

There is a tenderness in this that productivity culture often misses.

A bad first version can feel like grief.

You may be meeting the distance between the imagined self and the current self for the first time. The dream was painless because you had never measured the gap. Now the gap has a sound, a number, a failed seam, a page you dislike.

Do not rush to call the discomfort growth.

Sometimes it is simply disappointment.

Let the disappointment be specific.

Then decide whether the work deserves another version.

The question is not whether the first attempt was good enough.

The question is whether it taught you enough to make a second attempt worth the cost.

That is a far more generous standard.

It is also harder to fake.
