# Falsification Notes

## What would make the central thesis weaker?

The manuscript’s central claim is intentionally conditional: real attempts can produce identity-relevant evidence that intention alone cannot. The claim should be weakened if the evidence shows that the mechanisms invoked are narrow, unstable, or better explained by other processes.

The thesis would need substantial revision if research established that, across representative settings, people’s observed behavior rarely contributes useful information to beliefs about capability, preference, or identity once prior beliefs and context are accounted for. It would also need revision if identity experimentation proved generally less informative than deliberate reflection or social assignment, or if early actions systematically created more misleading self-beliefs than useful updates.

No source in the current ledger establishes the Bookself thesis in that broad form. The book therefore treats the thesis as authorial synthesis built from narrower findings rather than as a result already proved by psychology.

## Chapter-level counterclaims to preserve

### Identity evidence

- Behavior can be caused by pressure, incentives, coercion, role expectations, or circumstance and therefore need not reveal a stable preference.
- Self-perception theory explicitly concerns conditions in which internal cues are weak or ambiguous; it should not be generalized to all self-knowledge.
- Self-efficacy beliefs can be inaccurate. A mastery experience may increase perceived capability without establishing competence in a different task or context.
- Identities can be context-sensitive without being infinitely malleable.

### Starting mechanisms

- Implementation intentions can improve initiation without improving goal selection.
- Lowering friction can increase bad behavior as well as good behavior.
- Defaults can support participation while also exploiting inertia or creating paternalistic outcomes.
- More preparation is appropriate when errors are dangerous, irreversible, legally constrained, or costly to other people.
- A small experiment can be too small to produce meaningful information.

### Choice and progress

- Choice-overload effects are heterogeneous across settings; the book should not state that more choice generally reduces action.
- Endowed-progress findings come from a bounded consumer setting. A loyalty-card effect is not a direct model of identity formation.
- Small wins can build momentum toward harmful or badly chosen goals.
- Activity metrics can create false progress.

### Habits and interruption

- Habit formation varies substantially by behavior and person; no universal number of days belongs in the manuscript.
- Missing one opportunity in Lally et al.’s study did not materially derail the modeled habit process, but this does not imply that long interruptions carry no cost.
- Attention-residue experiments concern task switching; they do not prove that every unfinished life project remains cognitively active.

### Quitting and restarting

- Goal disengagement can be adaptive without making quitting generally superior to persistence.
- Some commitments create duties that survive changed preference.
- Reengagement with alternative goals may be constrained by health, law, money, care obligations, discrimination, or lack of alternatives.
- Sunk-cost logic cannot by itself settle intimate moral decisions.

### Success and exploration

- Exploitation of known capabilities can be rational, especially when other people depend on reliable output.
- Exploration consumes resources and can become novelty-seeking rather than learning.
- A successful person’s established identity may contain legitimate duties to employees, family, patients, citizens, partners, or investors.

### Loss

- Posttraumatic growth is reported by some people and should not be universalized.
- Positive change after trauma can coexist with distress and does not make the trauma good.
- The manuscript must not imply that grief has a correct timetable or that a new beginning is the appropriate immediate response to loss.

### Teams and institutions

- Psychological safety is not an absence of standards, accountability, hierarchy, or consequence.
- Pilots can externalize learning costs onto people with less power.
- Institutional memory can preserve useful warning as well as stale classification.
- Some permanent records are necessary for public safety, professional accountability, or informed consent.
- “Let new evidence count” is not equivalent to erasing the past.

## Alternative explanations the prose should tolerate

A person may persist because of social support rather than because identity changed. A new identity may arise because other people named it first. Apparent confidence may reflect reduced anxiety rather than increased skill. A restart may succeed because circumstances improved, not because the person became wiser. A project may succeed through luck despite a poor beginning process. A project may fail despite excellent judgment because the environment changed.

The manuscript should therefore prefer mechanism-specific language — *can*, *may*, *in this setting*, *one interpretation is* — when the evidence does not justify a stronger causal statement.

## Publication audit

Before a later edition is released, re-check the source ledger for:

1. major replications, meta-analyses, or failures to replicate findings the prose treats as important;
2. changes in the choice-overload literature that would make the current treatment misleading;
3. updated work on habit formation and lapse/restart dynamics;
4. critiques or extensions of posttraumatic-growth measurement;
5. newer evidence on adult professional identity transitions and psychological safety;
6. any places where the Bookself metaphor has accidentally been described as an established scientific construct.

A later edition should become less attached to this edition if the evidence changes. That is part of the book’s own argument.
