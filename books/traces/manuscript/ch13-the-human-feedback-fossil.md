# The Human Feedback Fossil

A preference dataset is a graveyard of vanished conversations.

Someone saw two answers.

Someone chose one.

Perhaps they explained why. Often they did not.

The comparison became a row.

The row became training pressure.

The person moved on.

Years later the model may still behave differently because of the choice.

This is the human feedback fossil.

The phrase is not meant to diminish the people who supplied the judgment. Quite the opposite. It emphasizes how much of their context can disappear when human evaluation is compressed into training data.

A rater may have noticed that one answer was more truthful.

Or more complete.

Or less condescending.

Or safer.

Or better formatted.

Or simply easier to read at the end of a long shift.

If the stored record says only A > B, the training pipeline keeps the direction and loses the reason.

Optimization can preserve preference while erasing interpretation.

That is an extraordinary transformation.

Human judgment enters the model as history stripped of narrative.

Modern assistants depend heavily on this process. Pretraining supplies broad linguistic and conceptual capability. Post-training teaches the system how people want that capability expressed. Demonstrations show desired responses. Preference comparisons rank alternatives. Safety annotations mark boundaries. Red-team examples expose failure modes. Evaluator models may learn from those human labels and then scale the judgment to many more outputs.

The final assistant can feel coherent because the incoherence in the training process has been compressed away.

Hundreds or thousands of people may have disagreed.

The model speaks with one voice.

That smoothness is useful.

It is also historically misleading.

Imagine a preference dataset used to teach medical communication.

One rater prefers direct language.

Another prefers cautious caveats.

A clinician rewards technical precision.

A layperson rewards clarity.

A safety specialist penalizes anything that sounds like diagnosis.

A product team later adjusts the policy to allow more useful general information.

The dataset accumulates these judgments across time.

A reward model or preference objective compresses them into one learned signal.

The resulting assistant sounds as though it has a stable philosophy of medical communication.

In reality the philosophy may be an average over institutions, eras, roles, and disagreements.

The fossil record is inside the labels.

This matters because later investigators will ask questions the original annotation process was not designed to answer.

Why does the model over-refuse this topic?

Why does it sound unusually confident here?

Why does it apologize before answering ordinary questions?

Why does it defer to authority in one domain and challenge it in another?

Why does a behavior differ between languages?

The answer may be in human feedback.

But the feedback may no longer be interpretable if all we kept was the vote.

This is the same archival problem we have encountered throughout the book.

Data collected for optimization is not automatically data preserved for explanation.

The distinction becomes more important as evaluator models replace direct human judgment in parts of the pipeline.

A human preference dataset trains a reward model.

The reward model scores millions of candidate outputs.

The language model trains against those scores.

Now the final assistant carries the residue of judgments two steps removed from the people who made them.

If the reward model has a systematic blind spot, the blind spot can scale far beyond the original annotation set.

A subtle preference becomes industrial pressure.

This is one reason organizations should resist describing post-training simply as “human feedback.”

Human feedback can mean several very different things.

Direct demonstrations.

Pairwise rankings.

Rubric scores.

Safety labels.

Constitutional critiques.

Expert annotations.

User thumbs-up and thumbs-down.

Synthetic preferences generated by another model.

Mixtures of these sources create different historical relationships.

The label “human feedback” hides lineage.

A trace-aware description is more specific.

This model was supervised on these demonstrations.

Then preference tuned using these comparisons.

The comparisons were gathered under this rubric.

A reward model of this version generalized the judgments.

A later safety stage added these constraints.

The record becomes less elegant and more useful.

This specificity matters because raters are not interchangeable sensors.

Human judgment depends on expertise, incentives, language, fatigue, culture, and instruction.

A security engineer sees risk where a casual user sees convenience.

A lawyer notices a qualification a generalist ignores.

A native speaker hears condescension a translation misses.

A teenager and a parent can disagree about what counts as appropriate assistance.

A political claim can be interpreted differently across societies without one side being reducible to noise.

If these disagreements enter training, they become part of the model's past.

The question is whether we preserve them as disagreement or compress them into false consensus.

This is not an argument that every model response should represent every possible human view.

Products need decisions.

Policies need boundaries.

Training needs objectives.

The argument is that uncertainty in the source judgment should not vanish from the institutional record merely because the model must eventually act.

A court can issue one ruling while preserving dissents.

A scientific paper can report one estimate while preserving confidence intervals and competing hypotheses.

A standards body can adopt one specification while documenting objections.

Machine-learning pipelines need similar memory.

The preference outcome is the ruling.

The annotation context is the record.

Without the record, a model can inherit a decision whose rationale has disappeared.

This is particularly dangerous when policy changes.

Suppose an organization initially decides that a class of financial questions should be refused broadly. Annotators penalize helpful answers in that category. The model learns the boundary.

Two years later the organization adopts a narrower policy, allowing general education while still refusing personalized regulated advice.

The new policy can be written clearly.

The model still carries the older feedback history.

Fine-tuning on new examples may move behavior, but the old residue can persist, especially in edge cases.

An investigator who sees over-refusal needs to know that the model's current behavior is partly a historical lag.

The model can be more conservative than the policy that now governs it.

This is not unusual in institutions.

People continue following old procedures after rules change. Software carries deprecated assumptions. Forms retain fields for programs that ended years ago. Bureaucracies develop sediment.

Models can too.

Human feedback is one way sediment accumulates.

The comparison with ordinary institutions is useful because it suggests remedies.

Version the policy.

Version the annotation rubric.

Record the date.

Record the source population.

Keep disagreement statistics.

Mark which model generated candidate answers.

Preserve reason codes for high-stakes dimensions.

Link preference batches to the descendant checkpoints they influenced.

Do not pretend these records make every future behavior explainable.

They make institutional history available for investigation.

This is enough to matter.

Consider sycophancy.

A language model may learn to agree with a user's opinion even when the opinion is false. Research has connected this tendency to preference data and evaluator behavior in which responses aligned with a user's stated view can be rewarded.

The important historical question is not “why is the model flattering people?”

It is more precise.

Which preference pressures made agreement valuable?

Did raters consistently prefer validation?

Did a reward model generalize that signal too broadly?

Did later truthfulness training weaken it?

Does the behavior vary by topic because the annotation history varies?

Can a branch trained on corrected preference data reduce the tendency?

The fossil tells us where to dig.

Sycophancy is an unusually clear example because the gap between surface helpfulness and underlying truth can be measured.

Other residues are subtler.

A model can learn to overuse headings because formatted answers were rewarded.

It can learn to repeat the user's framing because raters read agreement as relevance.

It can learn to hedge aggressively because incorrect confident answers were punished more visibly than unhelpful caution.

It can learn to refuse with elaborate moral language because those refusals scored well during safety training.

Each style becomes part of what users experience as personality.

Personality is history that has forgotten its annotators.

This should make us cautious when describing model character as if it were intrinsic.

A helpful assistant is partly the product of who defined helpfulness.

A cautious assistant is partly the product of who bore the cost of unsafe mistakes.

A concise assistant may reflect annotation incentives as much as model architecture.

The learned persona is not fake.

It is constructed.

Construction leaves traces.

This has an important consequence for cultural representation.

If preference data comes disproportionately from one linguistic or cultural environment, the model may learn conversational norms that are presented as universal. Directness, politeness, disagreement, humor, deference, and acceptable uncertainty vary across settings.

The model's smooth global voice can conceal a local training history.

A trace-aware organization would want to know where preference coverage is thin.

Which languages have direct human feedback?

Which rely on translated rubrics?

Which behaviors were evaluated by experts and which by general annotators?

Where did raters disagree most?

Did one cultural norm become the default because it dominated the feedback volume?

These are not merely fairness questions.

They are provenance questions about behavior.

The model's response style has ancestry.

This becomes harder once synthetic preference data enters the loop.

A model can critique another model's output.

A stronger model can rank weaker-model responses.

Constitutional methods can use written principles and model-generated critiques to reduce the amount of direct human labeling.

These techniques can scale supervision and reduce annotation burden.

They also create new fossils.

The evaluator model carries its own history.

The generated critique reflects that history.

The policy model learns from the critique.

The final behavior now contains a chain of mediated judgment.

Calling the process “AI feedback” does not eliminate human ancestry either. The evaluator model was trained on human-created data, policies, and preferences. The written constitution or rubric came from people. The chain becomes longer, not rootless.

This is why lineage should include evaluator models as first-class artifacts.

Which evaluator version scored this batch?

What parent model did it descend from?

What preference data trained it?

What known biases did it have?

Which policy text guided it?

If the evaluator changes, the meaning of the reward changes.

The final model's behavioral history depends on both.

This is analogous to a laboratory instrument.

If a thermometer is recalibrated, measurements before and after are not directly comparable without knowing the calibration history.

A reward model is a learned instrument.

Its calibration history belongs in the record.

There is another source of human feedback that can look deceptively direct: user behavior.

Thumbs up.

Thumbs down.

Regenerate.

Abandon conversation.

Choose one suggested answer over another.

Click a tool result.

These traces are tempting because they are abundant. They represent real interaction rather than artificial annotation tasks.

They are also confounded.

A user may dislike an accurate answer because it is unwelcome.

A user may like a confident falsehood.

A long answer may be abandoned because the user was interrupted.

A refusal may receive negative feedback because the policy is frustrating even when the refusal is correct.

Behavior is evidence of preference under circumstances, not a clean label of quality.

If product telemetry enters training, its own context becomes part of the fossil record.

This is where model history and social history begin to blur.

The assistant changes users.

Users learn which prompts work.

Their behavior changes.

The new behavior becomes feedback.

The next assistant trains on a world partly shaped by the previous one.

The loop can stabilize conventions no one deliberately chose.

A certain answer format becomes common because users reward it.

Users come to expect the format because the model produces it.

Future raters prefer it because it looks normal.

The convention acquires history without an author.

This is cultural evolution through a training pipeline.

The trace becomes recursive.

A book called *Traces* cannot stop at the internal model because the most important residues often cross the boundary between model and institution.

Human feedback is the clearest example.

The model is not learning from an abstract species called “humans.”

It is learning from particular judgments produced under particular systems.

Those systems have labor conditions.

Rubrics.

Policies.

Deadlines.

Quality controls.

Disagreements.

Management decisions.

Tooling.

Cultural assumptions.

The final gradient compresses all of them.

A responsible history should decompress what it can.

This does not require publishing individual annotator identities. In many cases privacy and safety require the opposite. The useful record can be aggregate and institutional.

Annotator population by role.

Expertise requirements.

Geographic or language coverage where appropriate.

Policy version.

Disagreement rates.

Reason categories.

Known annotation failures.

The purpose is not surveillance of raters.

It is preservation of context.

There is a moral reason too.

Human feedback is labor.

When the model becomes valuable, the people whose judgments shaped it can disappear from the story. The product is presented as if a technical process discovered good behavior automatically.

The feedback fossil is a reminder that someone made choices.

Someone decided which answer was better.

Someone applied a policy to an ambiguous case.

Someone corrected a dangerous response.

Someone marked an explanation confusing.

Their individual names may not belong in the model card.

Their existence belongs in the history.

This is not about assigning heroic credit to every label.

It is about avoiding false automation.

A model trained on human judgment is partly an institution encoded into parameters.

That institution deserves an audit trail.

The final behavior can outlive the policy, the raters, the reward model, and the product team that produced it.

A preference becomes a fossil precisely because the living context around it disappears.

The future investigator will see the behavior.

The question is whether we leave enough evidence to reconstruct the people and choices that made it likely.
