# Who Owns the Training History?

A model can have a history without anybody owning the history as one object.

The training data may belong to millions of people and organizations under different rights.

The code belongs to someone else.

The model weights may be controlled by the developer.

The preference labels may have been produced by contractors.

The synthetic data may have been generated by another model.

The logs sit in a cloud account.

The user whose record appears in the corpus may have privacy rights that do not resemble copyright rights at all.

The author may care about copying.

The patient may care about confidentiality.

The developer may care about trade secrets.

The regulator may care about transparency.

The researcher may care about reproducibility.

The same training history can be evidence in several legal and moral systems at once.

This is why the question in the title has no simple answer.

Who owns the training history?

Usually, the interesting answer is that ownership is the wrong single verb.

Different parties have different claims over different parts of the record.

Machine learning makes this difficult because training compresses the parts together.

A dataset is assembled.

The model changes.

The raw records may later be deleted, licensed, disputed, or forgotten.

The weights remain.

A behavior appears.

Someone asks where it came from.

The technical trace can help answer the factual question.

It cannot decide the rights question by itself.

This distinction is essential.

An influence method can estimate that a document mattered to a prediction.

That does not establish copyright infringement.

An extraction attack can show that a model reproduces a passage.

That is relevant evidence, but the legal analysis depends on jurisdiction, facts, licenses, exceptions, market effects, and other doctrine.

A membership-inference attack can suggest that a person's data participated in training.

That does not, by itself, tell us whether the processing was lawful.

Technical ancestry and legal responsibility overlap without being identical.

The pressure to confuse them will grow because technical scores look quantitative.

A court, regulator, creator, or user will naturally want a number.

How much did my work influence the model?

How much compensation does that imply?

How certain are we the data was used?

Machine learning can supply partial measurements.

Rights systems have to decide what those measurements mean.

That is not a failure of science.

Physics can measure how fast a car was traveling. Law decides whether the driver was negligent.

Accounting can show a transfer. Contract law decides whether it was authorized.

A trace can establish facts without supplying the norm.

The useful role of *Traces* is to make the facts less metaphysical.

For years, training-data debates were haunted by the phrase “the model contains the work.”

Contains is a database word.

Sometimes it is directionally useful because a model can reproduce memorized material. Often it obscures more than it clarifies.

A model can be influenced by a work without storing a recoverable copy.

A work can be present in the corpus and have negligible unique marginal influence because substitutes abound.

A model can know facts from many overlapping sources.

A later model can inherit patterns through synthetic data without ever seeing the original record.

The history is more complicated than possession.

This is why provenance records matter even when technical attribution improves.

The best way to know whether a dataset was used is to keep an authoritative record of training inputs.

Model inference should be a check, not the primary accounting system.

This principle is beginning to enter regulation.

The European Union's AI Act requires providers of general-purpose AI models to publish a sufficiently detailed summary of the content used for training according to a template provided by the AI Office. The obligation began applying to new models on 2 August 2025, with the Commission's template asking for categories of training content, major data sources, information about web crawling, private and user data, synthetic data, and relevant processing practices. For earlier models, transitional timing differs.

The requirement is not a demand to publish every training record.

That distinction is important.

The European approach tries to create a public layer of training provenance while preserving room for trade secrets and confidential business information. It is a concrete example of the layered transparency this book has been arguing for.

Public summary.

More detailed internal documentation.

Regulatory access.

Different resolutions for different audiences.

The rule also recognizes model lineage. When a downstream provider modifies or fine-tunes a general-purpose model in a way that makes the downstream entity a provider of the resulting model, the training-content summary can focus on the data used for that modification while referring back to the original model.

That is historical thinking embedded in compliance.

Parent.

Modification.

New data.

New descendant.

The law is beginning to encounter model genealogy because products already have it.

Copyright presents a different set of questions.

In the United States, the Copyright Office's 2025 report on generative AI training surveyed disputes over copying, fair use, licensing markets, and the effects of training practices. The report did not turn data attribution into a legal formula, nor could it. Copyright analysis is not reducible to whether one training example receives a high influence score.

The technical facts can still matter.

Was a work copied into a training corpus?

Was it licensed?

Was access lawful?

Was the work reproduced in outputs?

How much material was used?

What markets exist or may develop?

How transformative is the use under applicable doctrine?

These are legal questions informed by factual history.

A missing training record makes every one harder.

This creates a perverse incentive problem.

A company may fear that preserving detailed provenance creates discoverable evidence in litigation.

The same company may need that provenance to prove that a disputed work was not used, was licensed, was removed, or appeared only in a later fine-tune.

Good records can create risk and defense simultaneously.

This is true in every regulated industry.

The answer is not deliberate amnesia.

It is records governance.

Keep what is necessary.

Protect it.

Define retention.

Make the history trustworthy enough to support both accountability and exoneration.

A system with no records does not become innocent.

It becomes unverifiable.

The question of ownership becomes even stranger for synthetic data.

Suppose Model A is trained on a mixture of human-authored material. Model A generates a large synthetic curriculum. Model B trains on the synthetic curriculum.

Who owns the history relevant to Model B's behavior?

The immediate data files may have been generated by the developer.

The patterns inside them descend partly from Model A's training history.

A human author's work may have influenced Model A and thereby influenced synthetic examples without appearing verbatim in them.

Technical ancestry has become indirect.

Current rights frameworks were not designed around recursive model genealogy.

That does not mean they are powerless. It means factual questions about lineage will become more important.

Which model generated the data?

Which version?

Under which prompt or policy?

Was the synthetic material filtered for memorized content?

Did the teacher model have known licensing restrictions?

Was the student trained in a jurisdiction with different obligations?

History crosses models and borders.

The same problem appears with model merges and distillation.

A descendant can inherit from several parents.

Weights are combined.

A smaller model imitates a larger one.

A domain adapter is attached to a base.

The final product can be the result of several ownership regimes layered together.

The model card needs a family tree more than a single author field.

This is familiar in software.

A product can contain open-source libraries under several licenses, proprietary code, third-party APIs, and generated assets. Software composition analysis exists because the legal status of the product depends partly on its dependencies.

AI will need model composition analysis.

What are the ancestors?

What data obligations attach to each stage?

Which licenses restrict downstream use?

Which modifications create new provider obligations?

Which privacy commitments survive into descendants?

Which open-weight releases permit commercial fine-tuning?

The answers are not all stored in the weights.

They belong to the history around the weights.

This is why chain-of-custody infrastructure can become a rights-management system.

A model lineage record can point to the rights profile of each parent.

A dataset manifest can record license categories.

A fine-tuning stage can record whether private data was used and under what authority.

A synthetic-data stage can record the generating model and policy.

An unlearning record can document what was removed and what tests supported the removal claim.

The technical history becomes the substrate on which legal compliance operates.

This sounds heavy for experimental research.

It is heavy.

The burden should be proportional to consequence.

A graduate student training a small model on a public benchmark does not need the governance machinery of a frontier model provider operating internationally.

A high-stakes model trained on private records should not be allowed to hide behind the norms of a classroom experiment.

Scale changes duty because scale changes externality.

There is another party whose claim is easy to overlook: the annotator.

Preference data and safety labels are often produced by workers whose judgment materially shapes the model. What rights do they have over the resulting history?

Employment and contract law will answer much of this in ordinary ways. The deeper point is moral and historical.

The model's behavioral values can descend from labor that disappears from public accounts of the system.

A provenance record can acknowledge human contribution without exposing individual workers.

This matters because “trained with human feedback” is not a description of a method alone.

It is a statement that people performed work.

The same is true for users whose interactions become future training data.

A user asks a question.

The conversation is logged under a product policy.

A thumbs-up or correction becomes feedback.

The interaction enters a later dataset.

The next model changes.

The user has become an ancestor of a descendant system without necessarily thinking of themselves as a data contributor.

Consent and policy become part of model genealogy.

This is why product terms, privacy settings, enterprise agreements, and retention policies are not peripheral to AI history.

They determine which experiences can legally become training traces.

A model's past is shaped by contracts before gradients ever run.

Ownership language becomes especially awkward around public data.

Publicly accessible does not mean unowned.

A webpage can be readable by anyone and protected by copyright.

A public forum post can contain personal data.

An open dataset can have license terms.

A government record may be public under one regime and restricted under another.

Web crawling collapses these distinctions operationally unless the pipeline restores them through metadata.

A URL is not a rights profile.

This is another argument for provenance at ingestion.

Record where data came from while the information is available.

License or terms where known.

Date collected.

Crawler identity.

Robots or rights reservations where relevant.

Dataset transformations.

Deduplication.

Removal requests.

The record can later answer questions the raw text cannot.

Once the material has been normalized into token shards and the source metadata discarded, rights reconstruction becomes archaeology in the worst sense: expensive, uncertain, and avoidable.

There is a practical business reason to do this well.

Licensing markets need accounting.

If publishers, archives, code hosts, photographers, data providers, or other rightsholders negotiate training licenses, developers need to know which models and descendants used which licensed material.

A license that cannot be traced through the model lineage is difficult to enforce or value.

A future market may require usage ledgers more sophisticated than “dataset purchased.”

Was the dataset used in pretraining?

Which model family inherited from that checkpoint?

Was it used again in fine-tuning?

Did synthetic data generated by the licensed model train another product?

Does the contract permit distillation?

Model genealogy becomes commercial infrastructure.

This is another reason the notion of ownership is too small.

The relevant relations include ownership, license, consent, confidentiality, statutory rights, trade secrecy, attribution, contractual permission, and regulatory duty.

A training history is a junction where those relations meet.

The model itself cannot adjudicate them.

The trace can tell us which junction we are at.

There is a final right worth naming: the right to history itself.

Not a formal universal legal right, but an institutional principle.

When a consequential model makes a decision, the affected person should often be able to know which version operated, under which policy, with which major training or update lineage, and what process exists to challenge the result.

This is less about owning the model's past than refusing to let the past become inaccessible when it matters.

An appeal requires a record.

An audit requires a record.

A deletion claim requires a record.

A licensing dispute requires a record.

A safety investigation requires a record.

Without history, every rights question becomes an argument about an opaque present.

Who owns the training history?

No one answer will survive contact with the real system.

The better question is which claims attach to which trace—and whether we preserved enough of the trace to find out.
