# FIFTY MICROTESLA — RF disruption mechanism audit

## Why this note exists

Chapter 1 currently gives the 2004 7 MHz disruption result substantial mechanistic weight: weak oscillating fields disrupt orientation, and in the radical-pair picture electron spins provide a route by which RF fields can perturb reaction dynamics. That remains an important historical argument, but it now needs a harder boundary. A 2026 experiment reports a radio-frequency disruption pattern that the authors argue is inconsistent with direct cryptochrome electron-spin decoherence and instead may involve a separate detector of magnetic perturbations.

The useful editorial consequence is not `radical pairs disproved`. It is narrower and more interesting: **RF disruption is evidence about the bird-plus-field system before it is evidence about which molecular component the RF field perturbs.**

## Primary / authoritative anchors

### Ritz et al. 2004 — the original disruptive-field result

Ritz et al., *Nature* 429, 177–180 (2004), DOI 10.1038/nature02534.

The European-robin experiments reported disruption under broadband 0.1–10 MHz noise and under a 7 MHz oscillating field in the tested geometry. This became influential because weak RF sensitivity was qualitatively compatible with a spin-chemical compass and difficult to reconcile with a simple permanent-magnet picture.

Publication boundary: the behavioral perturbation does not itself identify the perturbed receptor, radical pair, molecule, tissue, or neural pathway.

### Engels et al. 2014 — the effect broadened beyond one elegant frequency

Engels et al., *Nature* 509, 353–356 (2014), DOI 10.1038/nature13290.

In fully double-blinded experiments, European robins at the University of Oldenburg failed to orient magnetically in the local anthropogenic electromagnetic-noise environment, recovered magnetic orientation inside grounded aluminium-screened huts, lost it again when grounding was removed or broadband noise was deliberately added, and oriented without screening at a rural site. The screens attenuated noise from 50 kHz to 5 MHz by roughly two orders of magnitude. The authors explicitly reported that the disruptive effect was not confined to one narrow frequency band.

This strengthens the behavioral reality of an RF/noise perturbation under those conditions while making a simplistic `one resonant frequency → one radical pair` story less attractive.

Source: https://doi.org/10.1038/nature13290

### Kavokin et al. 2026 — a direct challenge to the usual mechanistic inference

Kirill Kavokin, Julia Bojarinova, Dmitriy Sannikov, Roman Cherbunin, Alexander Pakhomov, Alexandra Fedorishcheva & Nikita Chernetsov, “Disruption of magnetic orientation in migratory songbirds by radiofrequency magnetic fields is mediated by a specialized sensory system,” *Journal of the Royal Society Interface* (2026), DOI 10.1098/rsif.2026.0129.

The authors exposed pied flycatchers to RF magnetic fields with carrier frequencies of 1.41 and 1.5 MHz, comparing unmodulated fields with square-wave amplitude modulation at 500 Hz. They report that the modulated field, despite lower mean power, caused disorientation at a lower carrier amplitude than the unmodulated field. They argue that this ordering is opposite to the prediction they assign to direct RF decoherence of cryptochrome electron spins.

Their proposed alternative is a **separate sensory system for magnetic perturbations**, potentially based on electromagnetic induction and possibly involving vestibular structures. That proposed detector is not established by this experiment. The important result for this manuscript is the claimed mismatch between the observed modulation dependence and the direct-spin-decoherence interpretation.

Primary DOI: https://doi.org/10.1098/rsif.2026.0129

Contemporaneous author-reporting summary checked 8 August 2026: https://phys.org/news/2026-08-pulsed-radiofrequency-magnetic-fields-disorient.html

## What changes in the book's causal ladder

The current chapter tends to use RF perturbation as a bridge from behavior toward radical-pair physics. Preserve the experiment, but split the inference:

1. **Magnetic orientation exists under controlled conditions.**
2. **Some RF / electromagnetic-noise conditions disrupt that orientation.**
3. **The disruption constrains the complete sensory system.**
4. **Which component the RF field perturbs is a separate mechanistic question.**
5. **Cryptochrome radical-pair chemistry has independent molecular and theoretical evidence and should stand or fall partly on that evidence, not inherit receptor identity from RF disruption alone.**

This makes the book's own rule—behavior is not receptor—apply to one of its favorite experiments.

## Strongest counterargument / disconfirming evidence

The 2026 result could materially weaken the chapter's claim that RF disruption “reaches beneath the behavior toward the physics” in the specific sense of electron-spin chemistry. If the modulation result replicates and a separate induction-sensitive system is identified, historical RF-disorientation experiments may have been perturbing a second magnetic sensory channel rather than directly knocking a cryptochrome compass out of operation.

That would not erase light dependence, inclination-compass behavior, CRY4 in-vitro magnetosensitivity, fly work, or radical-pair theory. It would remove one of the cleanest-looking behavioral-to-quantum bridges. The manuscript should allow that loss.

The counterargument to the counterargument is equally important. Kavokin et al. 2026 is one experimental line in pied flycatchers, not a completed receptor identification. Species, waveform, modulation, carrier frequency, amplitude, behavioral protocol, background noise and sensory context differ from earlier robin work. Their induction/vestibular mechanism is a hypothesis. The correct update is therefore **mechanistic uncertainty**, not a new settled story.

## Concrete prose implication for a later manuscript pass

The chapter can become more engaging by letting the RF result change meaning in front of the reader. In 2004, 7 MHz looks like a clue pointing down toward electron spins. By 2014, ordinary urban electromagnetic noise shows that the perturbation is broader and experimentally environmental. In 2026, modulation behavior raises the possibility that the RF field is talking to a different sensory system altogether.

That sequence is better frontier science than a straight march toward CRY4. The clue survives; its address changes.

A later edit should specifically reconsider these current lines:

- `The experiment mattered because ... It was interfering with something. Something small. Possibly two electrons.`
- `This is why the 7-megahertz result mattered. In the radical-pair picture, electron spins create a route ... The interference experiment therefore reaches beneath the behavior toward the physics.`

Do not simply delete them. Preserve the historical attraction, then make the newer challenge arrive before the book converts perturbation into receptor evidence.

## Publication holds

- Do not write that the 2026 paper disproves cryptochrome magnetoreception.
- Do not write that birds have a demonstrated vestibular RF sensor; that is a proposed mechanism.
- Do not treat all RF waveforms, frequencies, amplitudes, species or behavioral paradigms as equivalent.
- Do not infer human RF-health effects from avian compass disruption.
- Do not let the phrase `quantum compass` collapse molecular spin chemistry, receptor identity, RF perturbation, neural coding and navigation into one finding.

## Why this is high-value

The manuscript's central promise is unusually well suited to this correction: a frontier sense becomes credible by accumulating restrictive evidence while refusing to promote one rung into the next. The 2026 paper forces the book to obey that standard where the story is most tempting. It also improves narrative momentum. Instead of presenting RF disruption as a solved clue on a one-way road to cryptochrome, the evidence becomes what research often is: a clue that survives while its interpretation gets less comfortable.
