## Premise

Rupert Sheldrake's hypothesis of morphic resonance proposes that similar self-organizing systems can be influenced by previous similar systems across space and time, as though nature carries habits that are not fully stored in genes, brains, or ordinary physical traces. The proposal is controversial and is not established science.

This book refuses the usual choice between credulity and ridicule. It asks the question underneath the controversy: **where does a pattern go after it has happened once?** A pattern can survive in DNA, epigenetic state, a modified environment, architecture, ritual, language, institutions, standards, markets, algorithms, archives, and trained models. In each case, the world becomes slightly more prepared to produce something it has produced before.

The book calls those preparations **resonance infrastructure**: the material, social, informational, and institutional pathways that lower the activation energy for an old pattern to recur. Many events that feel like memory without a location turn out to have locations distributed across systems. The harder question begins only after those channels are mapped and controlled.

The result is a book about morphic resonance that neither quietly converts a disputed hypothesis into fact nor dismisses the intuition that made the hypothesis compelling. It uses the controversy to build a more rigorous account of repetition, inheritance, path dependence, cultural accumulation, algorithmic recurrence, and the practical problem of making something genuinely new in a world thick with memory.
