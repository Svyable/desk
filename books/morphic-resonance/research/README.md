# Research Brief

## Thesis under test

*Morphic Resonance* begins with Rupert Sheldrake's hypothesis that previous similar self-organizing systems can influence later similar systems through a nonlocal process he calls morphic resonance. Sheldrake describes the hypothesis as a kind of memory in nature in which repeated patterns become increasingly habitual.

The manuscript does **not** assume that hypothesis is established. Its working thesis is narrower and deliberately falsifiable at several levels:

> Many phenomena that feel like memory without a location can be explained by distributed traces of the past — genetic, epigenetic, neurological, ecological, cultural, architectural, institutional, economic, computational, and informational — that alter the probability of later recurrence. The book calls the collection of those traces **resonance infrastructure**. A specifically nonlocal morphic-resonance effect becomes scientifically interesting only after these ordinary channels are mapped, controlled, and shown insufficient under preregistered independent replication.

The book should survive either outcome. If no robust nonlocal residual survives improved controls, the central contribution remains an account of distributed memory and path dependence. If a reproducible residual does survive, the manuscript should describe the anomaly precisely without prematurely treating Sheldrake's entire proposed mechanism as proven.

## Claims that must remain separate

1. **Sheldrake's claim:** morphic resonance is a nonlocal similarity-based influence from past systems to later similar systems and contributes to a collective memory in nature.
2. **Established history dependence:** many physical, biological, cognitive, ecological, social, and computational systems respond differently because their state was altered by prior events.
3. **Distributed inheritance:** genes are one inheritance channel among several relevant to development and behavior; ecological, social, cultural, institutional, and technological channels can also preserve consequences across time.
4. **Resonance infrastructure:** the manuscript's own descriptive concept for the material and informational structures that reduce the cost of recurrence. It is not presented as a new physical force.
5. **Open residual:** failure to identify an ordinary channel is not itself evidence for morphic resonance. A residual must be reproducible, quantitatively characterized, and resistant to increasingly strong controls.

## Core empirical tests

1. **Definition of the hypothesis:** Does the manuscript state Sheldrake's own formulation accurately, including the role of similarity, temporal direction, repetition, and the claim of nonlocal influence?
2. **Similarity:** Can any claimed effect specify the relevant similarity metric before outcomes are observed? Does manipulating similarity change the effect in a predicted direction?
3. **Prior repetition:** If cumulative repetition is causal, does experimentally varying the number or intensity of prior instances produce a dose relationship?
4. **Temporal order:** Do later systems show an effect only when the proposed source instances actually precede them?
5. **Transmission control:** Does an effect survive plausible genetic, social, environmental, experimental, digital, and materials-transmission channels?
6. **Blinding and preregistration:** Does the effect persist when target assignment, scoring, exclusions, primary outcomes, and analysis are fixed before data collection and hidden where possible from experimenters?
7. **Independent replication:** Can laboratories with different prior beliefs reproduce the effect using the same protocol?
8. **Ordinary distributed memory:** In apparently collective phenomena, how much recurrence disappears when environmental traces, defaults, network visibility, institutional rules, feedback loops, or trained models are altered?
9. **Feedback:** When an old pattern becomes more common, is prevalence functioning as an input that increases later exposure or adoption?
10. **Decay:** What mechanisms allow a historical influence to weaken, reset, or be overridden? A theory of memory must also explain forgetting and interference.

## Falsification standard for the strong morphic-resonance hypothesis

Confidence in a nonlocal morphic-resonance account should fall when preregistered, adequately powered tests repeatedly fail to show the predicted direction; when effects shrink materially under blinding or stronger isolation; when target/control roles can be swapped without the predicted reversal; when claimed cumulative effects do not track prior repetition; when similarity manipulations fail; or when ordinary information, environmental, material, genetic, social, or statistical channels account for the result.

Conversely, the manuscript should update toward a genuine anomaly if a quantitatively stable effect survives physical and digital isolation, randomized target assignment, blinded scoring, preregistration, dose manipulation, predeclared similarity metrics, and independent multi-site replication. Such a result would establish an unexplained effect first. It would not by itself validate every claim associated with morphic fields.

## Evidence lanes

### I. Sheldrake on his own terms

Use Sheldrake's own books, essays, and official research pages to state what he claims. Do not rely on hostile summaries for the definition of morphic resonance. Keep his proposal distinct from the manuscript's term *resonance infrastructure*.

Key starting sources:

- Rupert Sheldrake, “Morphic Resonance and Morphic Fields — an Introduction,” official site.
- Rupert Sheldrake, *A New Science of Life* / later editions titled *Morphic Resonance*.
- Rupert Sheldrake, *The Presence of the Past: Morphic Resonance and the Habits of Nature*.
- Sheldrake's FAQ and research pages for his current articulation of nonlocal influence across time.

### II. Development, inheritance, and biological memory

The manuscript must not use “genes are not the whole story” as a gap argument. Developmental biology, gene regulation, cellular organization, neural plasticity, immune memory, and epigenetic mechanisms provide known forms of history dependence. Transgenerational epigenetic inheritance in humans and other vertebrates requires careful qualification because intergenerational exposure, germline resetting, genetics, prenatal environment, parental behavior, and social transmission are difficult to separate.

Research priorities:

- developmental gene-regulatory dynamics and morphogenesis;
- cell-state maintenance and feedback;
- adaptive immune memory;
- mammalian epigenetic reprogramming and well-demonstrated exceptions;
- explicit separation of intergenerational from transgenerational claims.

### III. Ecological inheritance and niche construction

Use niche-construction literature for the narrower empirical proposition that organisms alter environments and that descendants may inherit those altered conditions. Do not imply that niche-construction theory is evidence for a nonlocal field. The useful connection is structural: earlier organisms can change the starting environment of later organisms.

Core sources include Odling-Smee, Erwin, Palkovacs, Feldman, and Laland's 2013 practical guide and Laland, Matthews, and Feldman's 2016 introduction to niche-construction theory.

### IV. Embodied and distributed memory

Use neuroscience and cognitive-science literature to support the claim that prior experience can change later performance without requiring conscious source recollection. Keep the book's broad operational use of “memory” labeled as such; do not imply that every history-dependent physical system has biological memory in the same sense.

The extended-mind literature can be used as conceptual background for externally scaffolded cognition, but the manuscript should not treat the philosophical thesis as experimentally settled merely because notebooks and tools participate causally in tasks.

### V. Cumulative culture

Use cumulative-cultural-evolution research to support the claim that socially transmitted practices can accumulate modifications across generations and that the mechanisms, definitions, and degree of uniqueness to humans remain active research questions. Preserve the narrower meaning of the “ratchet effect”: retention and further modification, not an assumption that culture always improves.

Useful sources include Tomasello's work, Caldwell and Millen's laboratory paradigms, and Mesoudi and Thornton's review defining criteria for cumulative cultural evolution.

### VI. Stigmergy and environmental coordination

Trace stigmergy to Pierre-Paul Grassé's work on social insects and later swarm-intelligence literature. Use the concept narrowly: action leaves an environmental trace that influences later action. Avoid using stigmergy as a universal explanation for all collective behavior.

### VII. Path dependence and institutional memory

Use Paul Pierson and related historical-institutionalist literature for increasing returns, timing, sequence, coordination effects, and the way early decisions can alter the returns to later choices. Distinguish origin from persistence: a practice may begin for one reason and survive for another.

### VIII. Markets, feedback, and increasing returns

Treat price, installed capital, contracts, and expectations as state variables that can carry history. Avoid calling every market cycle a recurrence of a famous bubble. Historical analogy requires a specified mechanism, not merely similar charts.

Where network effects or increasing returns are invoked, use the narrower economic literature and specify which complementarity or coordination mechanism is present.

### IX. Recommendation systems

Use peer-reviewed recommender-systems research for popularity bias and feedback loops. The strongest manuscript claim should remain conditional: some recommendation systems and objectives can amplify popularity and reduce diversity over repeated interaction. Do not universalize one simulation result to all deployed recommenders.

Key sources:

- Mansoury et al. (CIKM 2020), “Feedback Loop and Bias Amplification in Recommender Systems.”
- Klimashevskaia et al. (2024), survey on popularity bias.
- Zoralioglu and Yalcin (2026), dynamic feedback-loop analysis across user groups.

### X. Generative models and memorization

The manuscript distinguishes statistical generalization from exact memorization. Use Carlini and colleagues for demonstrated extractable memorization and Lee et al. for effects of deduplication. Do not describe a language model as a database or claim every output can be traced to a single training example.

Core sources:

- Carlini et al. (2021), “Extracting Training Data from Large Language Models.”
- Carlini et al. (2022), “Quantifying Memorization Across Neural Language Models.”
- Lee et al. (2022), “Deduplicating Training Data Makes Language Models Better.”
- Nasr et al. (2023), scalable extraction work, with care about the distinction between extractable memorization and ordinary generation.

### XI. Reproducibility and adversarial testing

Use the National Academies' 2019 *Reproducibility and Replicability in Science* as a general reference for replication and reproducibility. For the proposed morphic-resonance research program, prefer preregistration, fixed primary outcomes, multi-site replication, open protocols, auditable data provenance, and adversarial collaboration where researchers with opposing priors agree on discriminating tests.

The manuscript's proposed experiments are proposals, not reported findings.

## High-risk claims requiring explicit source checks

- Any statement that morphic resonance has been “proved,” “disproved,” or accepted/rejected by science as a single decisive event.
- Historical claims about the editorial reaction to *A New Science of Life*. Quote or characterize only from verifiable sources.
- Famous rat-learning or crystallization examples associated with morphic resonance. Do not repeat folklore versions without reconstructing the original experiments and controls.
- Claims about human transgenerational trauma inheritance through epigenetic mechanisms. Separate evidence in model organisms from human observational inference.
- Statements that algorithms inevitably homogenize culture. Evidence supports particular feedback mechanisms under particular systems and objectives, not a universal law.
- Statements that language models merely copy training data. Memorization is real, but generation also reflects learned statistical structure.
- Statements that cities, institutions, markets, or rooms literally possess minds or consciousness. The book uses “memory” operationally for persistent history-dependent state.

## What would make the book's own thesis false?

The concept of resonance infrastructure would be too broad to be useful if it could not be operationalized into identifiable state variables, carriers, or mechanisms in each domain. The manuscript should lose or narrow the term wherever it merely renames “history matters.” It earns its place only when it helps ask a discriminating question: **what changed because the pattern happened before, and how does that change lower or raise the cost of recurrence?**

If the term cannot guide a manipulation, comparison, or causal map in a chapter, remove it from that chapter.

## Source discipline

- Prefer primary papers, official datasets, original books, and first-party statements for definitions.
- Use Sheldrake's own sources to define morphic resonance and independent scientific literature to assess adjacent mechanisms and standards of evidence.
- Never manufacture quotations or quote a secondary paraphrase as though it were Sheldrake's wording.
- Separate established mechanism, plausible analogy, speculative extension, and proposed experiment in the prose.
- For every major anomalous claim, retain a note titled “What would make this ordinary?”
- For every strong conventional dismissal, retain a note titled “What evidence would force an update?”
- Record publication date, source type, claim used, and limitation in `source-ledger.csv`.
- Treat 2026 algorithmic and AI evidence as time-sensitive; recheck before release.
