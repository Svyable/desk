# The Inheritance of Environment

A beaver does not give its offspring a dam in the way it gives them genes.

The dam may nevertheless be waiting.

Water has slowed. Sediment has accumulated. Vegetation has changed. Other species have arrived or disappeared. The local environment encountered by the next generation is partly a consequence of what previous organisms did.

This is ecological inheritance in its most intuitive form: descendants inherit not only traits but altered conditions.

Humans do this so aggressively that we forget it is inheritance.

A child is born into roads, laws, property boundaries, languages, electrical grids, schools, debt contracts, religious buildings, sewage systems, supply chains, digital networks, soil contamination, parks, bureaucratic categories, and atmospheric concentrations produced before the child's birth. None of these are encoded in the child's DNA. All can influence development, opportunity, behavior, and health.

The environment arrives pre-used.

That single fact explains a large class of phenomena that otherwise look like history exerting influence at a distance.

The past does not need to reach forward through an invisible field if the present is made of leftovers.

Biologists use the idea of niche construction to describe ways organisms modify environments in ways that can affect selection and future generations. Earthworms change soil. Plants alter light, moisture, and chemistry. Corals build reefs. Microorganisms transform local conditions. Animals create nests, burrows, trails, and territories. The organism is not merely fitted to a preexisting environment. Organisms also participate in producing the environments to which they and others must respond.

Evolution therefore occurs in a world partly engineered by previous life.

This complicates the image of natural selection as an organism standing before a fixed test. The test changes. Sometimes the organism helped write it.

The distinction between environmental modification and ecological inheritance is worth keeping clear because the words can otherwise expand until they mean everything organisms do. In the niche-construction literature, the first step is that an organism alters features of its environment. The second is that the alteration persists long enough, or recurs reliably enough, to become part of the selective or developmental environment encountered later. The legacy is what makes the history consequential.

A footprint in wet soil is an environmental modification.

A network of trails that repeatedly redirects movement is closer to inheritance.

A bird moves a twig and changes a location for a moment.

A nest changes the microenvironment in which offspring develop.

An organism consumes food and leaves less behind.

A population repeatedly changes resource distributions, soil chemistry, vegetation, or habitat structure in ways that affect later organisms.

The boundary is not always clean, which is why the mechanism has to be specified instead of merely named.

John Odling-Smee, Douglas Erwin, Eric Palkovacs, Marcus Feldman, and Kevin Laland emphasized this legacy dimension in their practical guide to niche construction. They used *ecological inheritance* for the inherited environmental consequences of prior niche construction and argued that these legacies can create ecological and evolutionary feedbacks with detectable signatures.

That is a much narrower claim than “organisms direct their own evolution.”

An organism can change a condition without anticipating the evolutionary consequences. A beaver need not understand hydrology. An earthworm need not possess a theory of soil. A tree that changes shade and litter chemistry is not choosing the species that will later thrive nearby.

Intent is unnecessary.

Consequences are enough.

This matters because human intuition tends to equate construction with a constructor holding a plan. In niche construction, the causal work can be entirely local. A behavior changes a physical condition. The physical condition changes survival, development, or interaction. Later generations encounter the modified state.

The environment becomes an intermediary between past and future.

That is exactly the kind of intermediary a theory of morphic resonance must rule out before treating recurrence as nonlocal.

Suppose a behavior appears in descendants even when direct teaching is absent. If the relevant habitat was modified by previous generations, the descendants may be responding independently to a shared inherited environment. Their similar behavior does not require them to retrieve a collective behavioral memory. The world presents similar cues because earlier organisms helped preserve those cues.

This is a form of causal relay.

Past organism.

Changed environment.

Later organism.

The middle term can be overlooked because the environment is treated as background rather than as part of the inheritance system.

The same error appears in laboratory work. A cage, apparatus, substrate, microbial community, odor profile, handling routine, or local material can carry state forward between experimental generations. An experiment designed to test inheritance is only as clean as its reset.

Resetting the animal while preserving the room is not a full reset.

Resetting the room while preserving the experimenter's learned technique is not a full reset either.

The problem grows as systems become more complex because environmental inheritance can be redundant. Several traces may support the same behavior. Remove one and another recreates the cue. The recurrence then looks robust enough to invite an invisible explanation when the visible explanation was simply distributed.

This is why niche construction belongs in this book without being evidence for Sheldrake's hypothesis. It teaches a discipline of looking between generations.

What did the first generation leave in the world?

How long did it last?

Who encountered it?

Did the legacy change development, behavior, or selection?

If the legacy is experimentally removed, does the recurrence weaken?

These questions turn “the environment matters” into a causal program.

They also reveal the difference between inheritance and copying. A later organism does not need to imitate a predecessor. It can independently respond to a condition the predecessor created. Similarity of outcome can therefore arise without social learning, genetic change, or direct contact.

That is a more subtle ordinary channel than most debates allow.

The idea matters for morphic resonance because repetition can become easier through environmental continuity alone. An animal behavior may recur because predecessors created a structure that elicits or supports it. A migration route may persist because physical landmarks, learned social routes, resource patterns, and landscape changes reinforce it. A plant community may reproduce conditions favorable to similar plants. A human occupation may run in families because tools, networks, expectations, and local opportunities are inherited alongside any biological predisposition.

The next generation starts closer to the old answer.

This is resonance infrastructure in literal soil and stone.

The word *inheritance* often makes us imagine a package crossing a generational boundary. Ecological inheritance can work differently. The descendants move into an environment whose state already contains consequences of ancestral activity. Nothing has to be carried through the body because the offspring arrive where the trace remains.

The distinction is powerful.

Suppose a community repeatedly develops houses with similar forms. One explanation is cultural preference. Another is building codes. Another is local materials. Another is climate. Another is lot geometry established by an earlier land division. Another is financing rules that make certain designs easier to insure or appraise. Each generation of builders encounters a design landscape already shaped by prior construction.

The houses “remember” without any house remembering.

The memory is in constraints.

This is especially visible in cities because cities accumulate slowly and resist resetting.

A street laid down for horses can constrain autonomous vehicles centuries later. A rail line can shape industrial parcels that shape later redevelopment after the tracks are gone. A river buried underground can still influence flooding. A zoning decision can change property values, which change political constituencies, which preserve the zoning. Utility infrastructure can make one development pattern cheaper than another for decades.

Urban history is full of causes that outlive their reasons.

People born later experience the results as the natural shape of the place.

This is what inherited environment does to perception: it converts history into background.

A child does not encounter a road as a political decision from 1956. The road is simply there. A worker does not experience a credential requirement as the residue of an old professionalization campaign. It appears as a fact of the labor market. A company does not experience a file format as a standards battle from decades earlier. It appears as compatibility.

The more completely a historical choice is embedded in environment, the less historical it feels.

That is one reason people underestimate path dependence. We see the present arrangement and imagine it was selected from today's alternatives. Often it was selected from yesterday's alternatives and then made expensive to change.

Biological environments work with similar asymmetry. An organism does not choose the starting ecology. It inherits a state produced by prior climate, geology, organisms, disturbances, and chance. Its traits are tested inside that history.

The evolutionary importance of niche construction has been debated in how broadly it should reshape evolutionary theory, but the empirical phenomenon that organisms modify environments is not controversial. The important point here is modest: some inherited similarity travels through the world rather than through the germ line.

Once that is admitted, the map of memory expands again.

Parents can affect offspring without transmitting a new DNA sequence. Maternal condition can influence prenatal development. Nest construction can change temperature and predation risk. Food provisioning changes growth. Social rank changes exposure. Microbial communities can be acquired through contact. Teaching changes behavior. Habitat choice changes what cues offspring encounter.

The family passes a world.

Human families make this obvious because wealth is environmental memory.

Money itself is a claim on future resources created through institutions. Property transfers access to neighborhoods, schools, safety, social networks, and time. Books, devices, expectations, tutoring, nutrition, stress, and space can all differ systematically across households. To call an outcome “inherited” without specifying the channel can collapse biological and social transmission into one foggy word.

The fog becomes politically useful because different channels imply different interventions.

A genetic constraint is not the same as a polluted environment. A cultural practice is not the same as prenatal exposure. A school boundary is not the same as individual ability. An inherited asset is not the same as inherited sequence. If similar outcomes recur across generations, mechanism matters ethically as well as scientifically.

Morphic resonance enters these debates badly when used as a universal solvent. If a family pattern repeats, one could say the family field carries it. But that language may distract from mechanisms that can be changed: housing, trauma exposure, role modeling, financial constraints, expectations, networks, or access to care and education.

Mystery can become a form of political anesthesia.

The same caution applies to positive outcomes. A family tradition of craftsmanship may be described as talent running in the blood. Genes may contribute to relevant traits, but the children also grow up near tools, vocabulary, examples, customers, and adults whose corrections accelerate learning. The environment contains a curriculum before formal teaching begins.

A workshop is inherited memory.

So is a kitchen.

So is a farm.

So is a laboratory.

The physical arrangement of a place can preserve expertise in ways the occupants do not articulate.

Imagine entering a well-designed commercial kitchen. Stations, surfaces, storage, tools, heat sources, drains, ingredient placement, and movement patterns reflect countless prior decisions about what work needs to happen. A novice placed in that room has not acquired a chef's skill, but the environment narrows the space of plausible action. Certain sequences become easy. Others become awkward. The room teaches by making successful behavior cheap.

This is exactly what resonance infrastructure does.

It lowers activation energy.

The first person to solve a recurring physical problem may need imagination. Later people inherit a jig.

A jig is memory converted into geometry.

A checklist is memory converted into sequence.

A road is memory converted into low-friction movement.

A standard is memory converted into compatibility.

A law is memory converted into permitted and forbidden action.

The environment can therefore hold not only traces of what happened but biases toward what should happen again.

That distinction moves us from passive memory to active recurrence.

A worn path records foot traffic and channels future feet. A successful marketplace attracts sellers, which attracts buyers, which attracts more sellers. A technical ecosystem accumulates libraries and trained workers, making the same technology easier to choose again. A neighborhood of specialists attracts suppliers and apprentices, deepening specialization.

Feedback turns environmental memory into self-reinforcement.

This can be adaptive. Reusing established infrastructure saves enormous effort. Civilization depends on not reopening every solved coordination problem. Roads should connect. Measurements should agree. Electrical systems should fit. Medical teams should not improvise every emergency procedure from scratch.

But inherited environment also creates lock-in.

A bad standard can survive because changing it requires everyone to coordinate. An inefficient road network can persist because buildings were placed around it. A harmful land-use pattern can continue because tax systems, mortgages, and political power adapted to it. A workplace practice can remain because software assumes it. Once enough complementary structures accumulate, the environment argues for repetition without saying a word.

The old pattern gains allies.

This is path dependence in material form.

There is a useful thought experiment for any recurring behavior: reset the environment while preserving the organism.

Would the pattern remain?

Then reverse it: preserve the environment while replacing the individual.

Would the pattern return?

The questions are not always ethically or practically testable, but they clarify causal structure. If new occupants reproduce an old workflow because the room, software, incentives, and forms channel them into it, much of the memory is environmental. If the behavior disappears when the environment changes, the environment was doing more work than participants realized.

This logic can be applied to laboratories testing extraordinary inheritance.

A laboratory itself learns.

Technicians become more skilled. Apparatus is adjusted. Protocols are refined. Suppliers change. Temperature control improves. Scoring practices stabilize. Data pipelines become cleaner. The later experiment is not the same environment as the earlier experiment even if the written protocol is unchanged.

If animals or materials appear to perform better over years, laboratory learning is a candidate explanation.

The experimenters may sincerely believe the subjects are learning across distance when the laboratory is learning locally.

This is not a criticism of competence. It is a recognition that competence leaves environmental traces.

Scientific replication tries to manage this by making procedures explicit enough that different laboratories can reproduce results. Yet tacit knowledge remains. Some protocols work only when someone experienced shows a newcomer the details that never made it into the paper. The transfer can look mysterious until the hidden craft is surfaced.

The phenomenon is common enough that science itself offers a model of resonance infrastructure.

Once a method has been performed successfully, future success becomes easier because papers, protocols, equipment, vendors, software, conferences, and trained personnel accumulate around it. A field can move rapidly after a demonstration even without any change in the laws of nature. The first success reorganizes the environment.

This is one ordinary explanation for claims that inventions or crystallization processes become easier after initial discovery. Once a result is known to be possible, researchers change behavior. They persist longer. They choose different parameters. They recognize intermediate states. They publish methods. Suppliers respond. Contaminants or seed material can spread in some domains. The social and physical world is not reset after discovery.

The impossible becomes routine because infrastructure grows around possibility.

This is an important psychological transition. Before a result exists, failure can mean the goal is impossible. After the result exists, failure means the method is wrong. Knowing that someone succeeded changes search behavior even if no technical detail is transmitted.

Possibility is itself environmental information.

That effect is powerful in athletics, engineering, entrepreneurship, and science. A record falls and competitors reorganize training around the new benchmark. A company proves a market and capital floods adjacent attempts. An artist breaks a convention and others now know the audience will tolerate the form. A social movement wins a legal change and activists elsewhere update their expectations.

The pattern can spread through belief about possibility before it spreads through detailed imitation.

This is still ordinary transmission. It travels through news, observation, reputation, and institutions. But it resembles the language of morphic resonance closely enough to create confusion: once something happens, similar things become easier elsewhere.

Yes.

The first question is *why*.

Sometimes because the universe remembers.

More often, based on what we can currently demonstrate, because the environment changed.

The environment includes information.

It includes expectations.

It includes physical traces.

It includes other organisms.

It includes the built world.

It includes markets and standards and stories.

Once the environment is defined broadly enough to include the actual conditions of action, “independent” recurrence becomes rarer than it appears.

This does not make everything environmental. The organism brings inherited capacities. Individuals differ. Randomness matters. Novelty appears. The point is interaction: inheritance is not a parcel delivered to a passive recipient. A developing system enters a historical world.

The world participates.

That may be the most important non-mystical version of the intuition behind morphic resonance.

The past does not disappear after producing us.

We live inside what it changed.
