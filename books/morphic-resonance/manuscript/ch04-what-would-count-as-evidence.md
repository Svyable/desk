# What Would Count as Evidence?

The most dangerous word in a controversial field is *evidence*.

Everyone wants it. Nobody means exactly the same thing by it.

A believer may call an uncanny experience evidence because it changed what they think is possible. A scientist may reserve the word for a controlled observation whose probability under competing explanations can be estimated. A historian may treat a letter as evidence that a person held a belief. A court uses rules about admissibility and burden. An engineer calls a failure log evidence that a component behaves differently under load. In ordinary life, a pattern repeated three times can feel persuasive enough to reorganize a week.

The dispute around morphic resonance becomes hopeless if these standards are allowed to drift in and out of the conversation.

So before examining strange results, the book needs a contract with itself.

What would count?

The answer begins with the kind of claim being made.

If the claim is modest—past systems influence present systems through genetic inheritance, environmental modification, learning, records, tools, norms, or other identifiable channels—then familiar evidence can establish the pathway. Trace the transmission. Manipulate the state. Remove the channel. Observe the change.

If the claim is stronger—past similar systems influence present similar systems even when ordinary channels are absent—then the experiment must do more than produce a surprising pattern. It must survive the ordinary world.

This is harder than it sounds because the ordinary world is inventive.

Information leaks. People cue one another. Samples are contaminated. Randomization fails in small ways. Researchers make decisions after seeing data. Participants guess what an experiment is about. Statistics are tried in several forms and the interesting result is remembered. Studies with null outcomes remain in drawers. Equipment drifts. Shared protocols create shared artifacts. Novel materials turn out not to be novel. A replication follows the published method but not the tacit laboratory practice that mattered.

None of these possibilities proves that a reported anomaly is false. They explain why a scientifically persuasive anomaly requires more engineering than an anecdote.

The first rule is that the effect must be specified before it is hunted.

Suppose a morphic-resonance hypothesis predicts that a target pattern becomes easier to learn after many independent people have learned it. “Easier” cannot mean whatever measure looks best afterward. Is it faster completion? Fewer errors? Better recall? Higher probability of solving? A change in reaction time? The primary outcome should be chosen in advance.

Likewise, “many people” must have a defined dose. If ten prior learners are enough, say so. If the theory predicts a gradual effect, specify the expected relation. If no quantitative theory exists, preregister a small set of directional predictions rather than leaving every result interpretable.

A theory does not become less profound when it becomes more specific. It becomes more vulnerable.

That is the point.

The second rule is that similarity must be defined independently of the result.

Morphic resonance depends centrally on similarity. This creates enormous room for retrospective explanation. A failed experiment can always be rescued by saying the systems were not similar in the right way. A positive result can be emphasized by choosing the dimension on which the groups happened to match.

To avoid that, an experiment should define the relevant features in advance. If genetic similarity matters, measure it. If shape matters, quantify shape. If task structure matters, describe the structure. If temporal pattern matters, fix the timing. Ideally, vary similarity systematically so the hypothesis predicts a gradient.

A cause that operates through similarity should respond when similarity is manipulated.

The third rule is blinding.

This is not because researchers are dishonest. It is because expectation travels through behavior. In animal studies, subtle differences in handling can matter. In human studies, facial expression, timing, wording, tone, and interpretation can matter. In laboratory work, knowing which sample is expected to behave differently can influence judgment about borderline outcomes or decisions about rerunning a trial.

Whenever possible, the people collecting and scoring data should not know which condition is supposed to show the effect. Automation can help where automation does not introduce its own bias. Predefined scoring rules can reduce judgment. Raw data can be preserved for independent analysis.

The fourth rule is separation.

If the proposed phenomenon operates without ordinary transfer, the experiment must model ordinary transfer aggressively. Physical isolation is only one part. Shared digital systems, shared staff, shared suppliers, shared environmental conditions, and shared preparation procedures can carry traces. If a phenomenon involves learning, media exposure may matter. If it involves biological materials, ancestry and contamination matter. If it involves crystallization, microscopic seed material can matter. If it involves language, prior familiarity with the vocabulary can matter.

The phrase “no known contact” is weaker than it sounds.

The fifth rule is a credible null.

An experiment needs a condition in which morphic resonance, as defined, should not operate or should operate less strongly. Without that, an unexpected improvement could reflect generic practice, drift, changes in equipment, learning by the experimenters, or time trends.

Controls should resemble the experimental condition except for the feature the theory says matters. This is basic experimental design, but controversial hypotheses are especially vulnerable to dramatic controls that differ in several ways at once.

The sixth rule is power.

Small studies create unstable stories. A large effect can sometimes be visible in a small sample, but most human and biological effects are noisy. Underpowered studies produce exaggerated estimates when they happen to cross significance thresholds and fail to distinguish modest effects from chance reliably. If a hypothesis has survived for decades partly on intriguing but contested findings, the next generation of tests should be designed to settle more than they stir.

That means enough observations, a justified analysis plan, and a sample size chosen before outcomes are known.

The seventh rule is replication by people who are not socially invested in the result.

Independent replication is difficult in fields that become tribal. Researchers willing to test an unpopular hypothesis may already be sympathetic. Researchers strongly skeptical of it may design experiments differently or interpret ambiguous procedures with less generosity. The ideal collaboration includes people with opposing priors who agree on the protocol before data collection.

Adversarial collaboration is one of the best technologies available for controversial science. It turns disagreement from a source of rhetorical heat into a design constraint.

The eighth rule is that null results must be allowed to reduce confidence.

This seems obvious. It is not.

When a cherished theory fails, the mind produces explanations faster than a laboratory produces data. The participants were wrong. The timing was wrong. The emotional state was wrong. The field was disturbed. The experiment was too artificial. The effect requires more repetitions. The skeptics inhibited it. The measurement collapsed the phenomenon. Some of these explanations could be true. If each is invented only after failure, however, the theory becomes harder to kill with every test.

A useful research program writes down in advance what would count against the claim.

This book will do the same at the chapter level.

Every explanatory lane has a falsification question.

If a pattern is attributed to genetic transmission, what observation would show that genetics is insufficient?

If it is attributed to cultural learning, what happens when cultural contact is absent?

If it is attributed to environmental inheritance, does the effect disappear when environments are reset?

If it is attributed to algorithmic feedback, does the pattern weaken when feedback is interrupted?

If it is attributed to institutional path dependence, can a policy discontinuity break it?

If an unexplained residual is attributed to morphic resonance, what manipulation of similarity, repetition, or temporal ordering should change that residual?

The point is not to create a contest in which one theory wins everything. Complex outcomes can have several causes. The goal is to stop explanations from becoming unfalsifiable atmospheres.

There is another problem with evidence that becomes severe in the age of the internet: anecdote abundance.

Before global communication, a person with a strange experience might know of three comparable stories. Now a search can return thousands. Social networks gather people around rare events. Recommendation systems learn interest and deliver more examples. A person can experience a dramatic rise in apparent frequency without any change in the underlying rate.

This creates a cognitive trap. We confuse searchable with common.

Imagine an event with a one-in-a-million probability that can happen in ten different ways to billions of people over years. There will be many stories. If the event is emotionally vivid, people will share it. If a community is organized around the event, the stories will be concentrated. The resulting feed can feel like a law of nature.

The denominator disappears.

Morphic-resonance claims are especially vulnerable to this because the theory is about similarity and recurrence. The internet is a machine for collecting similarity. It can produce the subjective experience of resonance even when events are independent.

That does not make anecdotes worthless. Anecdotes can generate hypotheses. They can reveal categories researchers did not know to measure. They can expose failures of official data. They can document rare events that are difficult to study experimentally.

But anecdotes are strongest at the beginning of inquiry and weakest at the end.

A repeated story says, *look here.* It does not say, *case closed.*

The same discipline applies to famous examples of simultaneous discovery. Science and technology contain many cases in which similar ideas appeared close together in time. The usual lesson is that ideas can be “in the air.” That phrase is evocative and causally empty.

What was in the air?

Often, shared enabling conditions were. Instruments became available. Previous results narrowed the search. Industrial needs aligned. Mathematical notation matured. Funding expanded. Communication accelerated. Several groups confronted the same bottleneck. Once those conditions are present, multiple discovery is not mysterious. It is a prediction of a shared landscape.

If morphic resonance adds something, the evidence must exceed what a common landscape predicts.

This creates a hierarchy of explanation.

Start with direct transmission.

Then shared environment.

Then independent response to shared constraints.

Then selection and survivorship.

Then measurement and analysis artifacts.

Then other established mechanisms relevant to the domain.

Only after those are modeled does an unknown channel become a serious candidate.

The hierarchy is not a rule that extraordinary explanations must always come last because they are socially disreputable. It is a rule of explanatory economy. Causes already known to exist should be quantified before new causes are added.

Yet explanatory economy can also become lazy. Saying “there is probably some conventional mechanism” is not a completed explanation. A skeptic who refuses to specify the mechanism is leaning on consensus in the same way a believer may lean on mystery.

The standard should be symmetrical.

Claims need mechanisms or at least discriminating predictions.

Alternatives do too.

There is a useful attitude in engineering after an intermittent failure. Do not worship the anomaly, and do not dismiss it because the system usually works. Instrument it. Reproduce it. Narrow the conditions. Change one variable. Preserve logs. Assume the failure is telling the truth about something even if the first interpretation is wrong.

Controversial science needs more of this engineering temperament.

Suppose an experiment repeatedly finds a learning advantage that seems correlated with prior remote learning. The first task is not to decide whether morphic resonance exists. The task is to characterize the effect.

How large is it?

How stable is it over time?

Does it appear with different materials?

Does it depend on participant age, language, or prior exposure?

Does it survive blinding?

Does it survive preregistration?

Does it appear in independent labs?

Does it scale with the number of prior learners?

Does it respond to similarity in the predicted direction?

Can an information-leakage model reproduce it?

Does the effect remain when obvious transmission paths are blocked?

A mysterious phenomenon becomes scientifically interesting when its mystery shrinks into regularity.

The irony is that many people attracted to mystery resist that shrinkage because explanation feels like disenchantment. But a robust unexplained regularity is far more astonishing than a thousand flexible anecdotes. If a nonlocal influence exists, careful controls will not make it less real. They will make it harder to deny.

The same is true if the effect is ordinary. Discovering a hidden channel can be more useful than preserving a supernatural interpretation. A study that appears to show collective learning might uncover subtle cultural transmission. A supposed field effect in an organization might turn out to be encoded in software defaults. A paranormal-seeming convergence in design might trace back to a shared optimization algorithm.

The world does not become boring when a mechanism is found. It becomes buildable.

There is also a moral reason to care about evidence standards.

Ideas about unseen fields, inherited memory, and collective influence can migrate into health, education, finance, and personal decision-making. Once a claim affects treatment choices, money, or vulnerable people, the cost of being wrong changes. A speculative metaphysics tolerated as philosophical exploration may require a much higher evidentiary threshold when sold as intervention.

Curiosity should be generous. Claims of efficacy should be strict.

Morphic resonance can be explored without pretending that it currently offers a validated medical, psychological, or technical treatment. This boundary protects both inquiry and people.

Good evidence also needs provenance.

A result becomes more trustworthy when others can inspect how it was produced. Raw data, protocols, analysis code, exclusion rules, and deviations from plan matter. Provenance is a form of memory: it preserves the causal history of a claim. Without it, science has to trust a polished endpoint.

That is almost poetic in the context of this book. A theory about the memory of nature should be tested by a scientific process that remembers itself.

No invisible revisions.

No selective forgetting of failed runs.

No outcome without lineage.

The requirement sounds bureaucratic until one sees what it protects: the possibility that a surprising result remains surprising after everyone has looked for the ordinary mistake.

A clean anomaly is expensive.

It should be.

If morphic resonance is false, rigorous methods will help explain why people repeatedly thought they saw it. That explanation will teach us about distributed memory, bias, transmission, and the difficulty of isolating complex systems.

If some version of it is true, rigorous methods are the only path by which the result can escape the cultural quarantine of the extraordinary.

Either outcome is valuable.

The worst outcome is permanent ambiguity maintained by experiments too loose to persuade skeptics and stories too compelling to release believers.

What would count as evidence?

A result specified before it appears. A similarity metric chosen before the fit. A control capable of failing for the right reason. A method that blocks ordinary transmission rather than merely assuming it absent. A sample large enough to matter. A replication performed by people who do not share the same hopes. A null result permitted to injure the theory. A positive result that becomes more precise under pressure instead of more evasive.

And after all that, a pattern that still comes back.
