# How to Make Something New

The first half of creativity is inheritance.

This is not the romantic version.

The romantic version begins with escape. The creator steps outside convention, rejects precedent, listens inward, and produces what nobody else could have produced. The image survives because it flatters both creator and audience. It gives novelty a clean origin.

Real novelty is dirtier.

A new sentence uses old words.

A new machine uses old materials, tools, standards, and physical laws.

A new scientific theory uses observations gathered with inherited instruments and concepts.

A new business uses legal forms, payment systems, networks, roads, software, and trained workers built by other people.

A new song arrives through ears trained by old songs.

The new is born inside resonance infrastructure.

The question is not how to escape history.

It is how to keep history from making every decision for you.

That distinction is practical.

A person who tries to become original by refusing influence usually becomes less capable, not more original. Expertise is accumulated compression. It tells you what has already failed, which distinctions matter, what tools exist, and where the interesting edges are. Ignorance can produce strange results, but most are strange because they repeat mistakes experts learned to avoid.

The past is valuable.

The danger begins when the inherited answer becomes the search procedure.

Ask a mature system to solve a problem and it tends to retrieve what its infrastructure can already support.

A company asks for innovation and requests a business case formatted like its current businesses.

A grant program asks for breakthrough research and scores proposals by evidence available before the breakthrough exists.

A recommendation system asks what a user might like and finds more of what the user already consumed.

A generative model is asked for something unprecedented in vocabulary built from precedent and evaluated by a person whose taste was trained by precedent.

A city asks for different transportation while preserving the geometry that makes the current transportation rational.

The request for novelty enters an old machine.

The machine returns a variation.

Variation is not worthless. Most progress is variation. Small improvements compound. Existing systems deserve optimization. A chair that is five percent lighter does not need to reinvent sitting.

The problem is category confusion.

We ask for departure and reward resemblance.

If you want a new pattern, you have to change the conditions under which patterns are selected.

This is the final consequence of the book.

Originality is partly an infrastructure design problem.

The first tool is separation.

If every search begins by looking at successful precedent, precedent becomes the boundary of the search. Sometimes this is desirable. A surgeon should know current evidence before improvising. A bridge engineer should not discover load-bearing principles from scratch.

But in exploratory work, there can be value in delaying exposure to the canonical solution.

Give a team the problem before showing the standard answer.

Ask independent groups to work separately before they compare notes.

Collect sketches before the market benchmark appears on the screen.

Generate hypotheses before searching the literature, then search aggressively enough to discover whether they are actually new or already disproven.

The order matters.

This is not an argument for staying ignorant. It is an argument for protecting a brief interval in which the existing answer is not yet the strongest attractor.

The second tool is altered representation.

A problem often remains trapped because everyone describes it with the same categories. Change the unit. Change the timescale. Change the medium. Change who is considered the user. Change what counts as the output.

A transportation problem described as “how do we move cars faster?” produces a different search space from “how do people reach what they need with less time and risk?”

A publishing problem described as “how do we sell more books?” differs from “how do ideas find readers?”

A software problem described as “how do we automate this workflow?” differs from “why does this workflow exist?”

A medical problem described by one diagnostic category may look different when organized by mechanism.

Representation changes similarity.

Change what counts as similar and different precedents become available.

This is especially important in machine-assisted work. Models are powerful association engines. If the prompt frames a problem conventionally, the model can produce an impressive tour through the conventional neighborhood. To use the system exploratorily, change the neighborhood.

Ask for analogies from distant domains.

Ask which assumptions the prompt smuggles in.

Ask for mechanisms rather than examples.

Ask for counterexamples.

Ask what would make the apparent category disappear.

Then verify the outputs because a model can generate false novelty as fluently as real connection.

AI is useful for escaping one local attractor partly because it has access to many other attractors.

That is not the same as escaping history.

It is cross-breeding histories.

The third tool is protected failure.

Novelty is expensive when every attempt must justify itself immediately. Mature systems have metrics calibrated to mature performance. Apply those metrics to early exploration and the incumbent wins.

A prototype should not be judged as a finished product.

A new research direction should not be judged only by citation density.

A new employee behavior should not be judged only by efficiency inside the old workflow.

A new artistic form should not be judged only by whether existing audiences instantly know where to place it.

Exploration needs a temporary jurisdiction with different rules.

This is why laboratories, studios, skunkworks, sandboxes, pilot programs, residencies, and research grants matter. They create spaces where the activation energy of deviation is subsidized.

The best of these spaces are not free from discipline.

They change the discipline.

A prototype is allowed to fail commercially but should teach something. A scientific pilot can be underpowered for a definitive claim but should improve the design of the next experiment. An artistic experiment can confuse an audience and still reveal a new grammar.

Failure earns its protection by producing information.

Otherwise “experimentation” becomes a flattering word for lack of consequence.

The fourth tool is deliberate diversity of starting points.

If ten teams share the same training, software, examples, incentives, and model suggestions, asking them to work independently may not produce independent search. They can converge because their priors are correlated.

This is one reason diversity matters beyond representation as a moral abstraction. Different histories create different accessible paths.

A person trained in another discipline notices different variables. A person from another market sees assumptions local practitioners stopped seeing. A person with different tools imagines different affordances. A person excluded by the old design encounters frictions its beneficiaries never notice.

Different experience is a search asset.

It should not be romanticized either. Difference does not guarantee insight. Outsiders can be confidently naive. Experts can be creatively heterodox. The point is portfolio structure: a system exploring uncertain territory benefits when not every explorer begins in the same valley.

The fifth tool is decay.

This may be the least fashionable recommendation in a civilization obsessed with retention.

Let some things forget.

Expire stale defaults.

Review rules whose original conditions have disappeared.

Reduce the weight of old behavioral data when a person's preferences may have changed.

Allow reputations to update.

Archive metrics that became targets rather than measures.

Retire interfaces whose categories no longer fit.

Delete model features that operationalize historical injustice without sufficient current justification.

Build sunset clauses where permanence is not necessary.

Forgetting is not the opposite of learning.

It can be part of learning.

An intelligent system needs a way to distinguish durable knowledge from historical residue.

Humans do this imperfectly. Organizations do it worse because externalized memory often has owners, budgets, dependencies, and compliance value. Digital systems can do it worst of all because storage is cheap and historical data is treated as an asset by default.

The past accumulates faster than our ability to decide what should keep voting.

A creative system therefore needs memory governance.

What gets retained?

At what fidelity?

For how long?

For which decisions?

With what possibility of appeal?

Those questions sound administrative because creativity is not only a psychological trait once tools and institutions shape what can be made.

The sixth tool is contact with reality that has not already been summarized.

This may become increasingly valuable in an age of generative systems.

Models are trained on records. Search indexes records. Institutions create reports. Markets compress beliefs into prices. Expertise compresses experience into rules. All of these are useful forms of memory.

They are also filters.

Go where the record is weak.

Talk to the person doing the work rather than only reading the strategy deck.

Measure the physical system rather than only reading descriptions of it.

Watch customers use the product without asking them to repeat the company's categories.

Collect new data.

Run the experiment.

Build the thing.

Travel to the place.

Touch the material.

Reality generates information that inheritance has not yet compressed.

The world is one of the few sources guaranteed not to have been trained only on yesterday's text.

This is not anti-intellectualism. It is an argument for renewing the input stream.

A culture that only recombines archives eventually becomes a commentary on itself.

The seventh tool is provenance.

At first this seems opposed to novelty. Why burden creation with more history?

Because knowing where an idea came from helps distinguish inherited constraint from independent discovery. Provenance allows credit. It reveals repeated assumptions. It prevents a model-generated echo from being mistaken for multiple independent sources. It lets a scientist trace a result to methods. It lets an organization discover that three current rules descend from one old incident.

Provenance makes memory legible enough to edit.

Without it, the past becomes atmosphere.

The eighth tool is counter-resonance.

If the old pattern is self-reinforcing, build structure that makes alternatives easier.

A city wants fewer car trips? It cannot merely ask drivers to prefer something else. It needs safe routes, useful transit, compatible land use, and pricing that changes relative friction.

A company wants more experimentation? It needs budgets, promotion criteria, review processes, and technical environments where experiments can occur without threatening core operations.

A recommender wants more discovery? It needs an objective that values exploration rather than only immediate engagement.

A scientific field wants anomalies tested? It needs replication funding, null-result publication, shared protocols, and reputational rewards for careful contradiction.

A creator wants a new style? They may need tools and collaborators that do not constantly pull toward the familiar one.

You do not defeat infrastructure with intention alone.

You build competing infrastructure.

This is where the book's title turns back on itself.

Morphic resonance asks whether patterns become easier because similar patterns happened before.

In human systems, the answer is often yes for reasons we can identify.

Then the practical question is not whether resonance exists.

It is which resonance we want.

We want aircraft systems to resonate with lessons from prior crashes.

We want medicine to resonate with evidence from prior patients without treating a new patient as a statistical clone.

We want law to resonate with precedent without making injustice immortal.

We want culture to resonate with inheritance without becoming imitation.

We want models to resonate with human knowledge without collapsing possibility into average precedent.

We want people to carry skills without being trapped by habits.

The goal is not originality without memory.

It is memory with a release mechanism.

This changes how I hear Sheldrake's phrase *habits of nature*.

If nature literally accumulates habits through an unknown nonlocal process, that would be an extraordinary discovery and would deserve evidence stronger than the hypothesis currently possesses.

But habit is already a powerful description of the world we inhabit because systems at many levels become history-dependent through known mechanisms.

They acquire state.

They inherit environments.

They train.

They standardize.

They build complements.

They remember failure.

They copy success.

They turn behavior into data and data into future behavior.

The world becomes increasingly prepared to become itself.

That may be the deeper danger hidden inside the romantic idea of resonance.

A universe with memory sounds comforting until memory becomes inertia.

A culture with perfect recall sounds wise until every old pattern remains callable, searchable, trainable, rankable, and cheap to reproduce.

A person with perfect habits sounds disciplined until the environment changes.

Life requires continuity.

Life also requires the capacity to surprise its own history.

This is where novelty actually begins.

Not in emptiness.

Not outside influence.

Not by pretending the past is absent.

Novelty begins when a system can feel the pull of inherited patterns and still create a path that has not yet earned infrastructure.

The path may begin weakly.

It may look inefficient.

It may require explanation.

It may need protection from metrics designed for the old world.

It may fail.

Most new patterns do.

But failure is not evidence that only old patterns are possible.

It is part of the cost of discovering which departures can survive reality.

The first chapter asked where a pattern goes after it has happened once.

We have found many answers.

Into bodies.

Into genes and regulatory systems.

Into environments.

Into trails.

Into rooms.

Into cities.

Into institutions.

Into prices.

Into data.

Into trained models.

Into expectations about what is possible.

The past does not need one vault.

It changes the world.

And because the world is changed, the next pattern does not begin where the first one did.

That is enough to explain a great deal of resonance.

Not all, perhaps.

The remaining question belongs to experiment.

The human question belongs to us.

What should we make easier to happen again?

What should we make harder?

What deserves a long memory?

What needs permission to fade?

And where, in a world full of inherited gradients, are we willing to pay the activation energy for something nobody has taught the world how to want yet?

That is how something new begins.
