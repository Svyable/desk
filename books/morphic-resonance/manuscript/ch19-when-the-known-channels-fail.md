# When the Known Channels Fail

A skeptical book has an obligation skeptics sometimes avoid.

It has to say what would change its mind.

It is easy to become sophisticated at explaining anomalies away. Humans leak information. Laboratories drift. Statistics mislead. Cultures transmit. Environments carry traces. Algorithms correlate people who think they are independent. Bodies remember prior exposure. Institutions remember through process. Markets remember through price and capital.

After seventeen chapters of ordinary mechanisms, almost any recurrence can be met with a plausible alternative.

That can become its own form of dogma.

A theory protected from positive evidence by unlimited alternative explanations is no more scientific than a theory protected from negative evidence by unlimited excuses.

So the door has to be real.

What would it look like if the known channels failed?

Not if researchers failed to identify one on a first pass. Not if an anecdote remained uncanny. Not if a result was statistically significant once. The known channels would have to fail under conditions deliberately constructed to make their failure visible.

The distinction matters.

An unexplained result is a temporary description.

An anomalous result is an unexplained result that survives serious attempts at explanation.

A new phenomenon begins only after the anomaly becomes regular enough to characterize.

This is where a responsible research program on morphic resonance could begin again.

Start with the strongest part of Sheldrake's claim: previous similar systems influence later similar systems through a nonlocal process he calls morphic resonance. On his own account, morphic resonance transfers information from past similar systems and contributes to a collective memory. That claim is much more specific than saying “everything is connected.” It has direction in time, depends on similarity, and proposes cumulative influence from repetition.

Those features can be turned into experimental obligations.

If the phenomenon depends on prior repetition, manipulate prior repetition.

If it depends on similarity, manipulate similarity.

If it operates from past to present, protect temporal ordering.

If it is nonlocal in the ordinary sense, separate systems from conventional exchange.

If it is cumulative, look for a dose relationship rather than a one-time miracle.

If it is real, independent laboratories should eventually be able to produce it without sharing allegiance to the theory.

The word *eventually* matters because not every real phenomenon is easy to reproduce on demand. Biology is noisy. Human behavior is noisy. Environmental sensitivity can be genuine. But difficulty does not eliminate the need for regularity. A phenomenon that never becomes more predictable as methods improve remains hard to distinguish from noise.

The best test would begin with something boring.

Not telepathy.

Not a dramatic personal experience.

Not a task embedded in ordinary culture.

Not a famous image participants might have seen.

Choose a synthetic system whose relevant features can be controlled.

Imagine a large set of newly generated visual patterns created after the experiment is registered. Some patterns are designated high-exposure targets. Others are matched low-exposure controls. Independent populations interact intensively with the target patterns under one protocol. A later population, isolated from those participants and from the digital records of the target exposure, receives a preregistered task comparing target and control patterns.

The outcome cannot simply be “people seemed to recognize the targets.”

The study needs a quantitative prediction.

For example: prior exposure by other people will reduce learning trials required for target patterns relative to matched controls by a specified minimum effect direction, with the difference increasing across predetermined exposure doses.

That would still be vulnerable to ordinary explanations.

The targets and controls might differ accidentally in complexity or memorability.

So the assignment must be randomized after the stimuli are generated, or the roles should be crossed over across replications. A pattern that is a target in one run can be a control in another. Analysts should be blind to assignment. The similarity metrics used to match stimuli should be specified before outcomes.

The later participants must be recruited after the target exposure if the theory requires temporal precedence.

The stimuli must not be released publicly.

Digital systems used to create and present them must be audited so that target status does not leak through filenames, caches, logs, model prompts, recommendation systems, or shared cloud assets.

If generative models are used to create the stimuli, the exact generation process should be recorded. Better yet, use procedures capable of producing large randomized sets without querying systems that might later be available to participants.

Modern information environments make isolation a technical discipline.

The experiment would need more than one site.

One laboratory should not own the finding. The protocol should be portable enough that skeptical and sympathetic teams can run it. Ideally, teams should agree on the design before data exists and commit to publishing outcomes regardless of direction.

This is adversarial collaboration in the best sense: disagreement used to remove ambiguity.

The goal is not politeness.

It is mutual distrust converted into method.

A skeptic asks where information could leak.

A supporter asks whether the control accidentally removes the proposed phenomenon.

Together they write conditions both sides agree would be informative.

Then the data is allowed to be rude to everyone.

A strong result would not immediately prove morphic resonance.

It would establish a reproducible anomaly.

That distinction should be celebrated rather than treated as weakness.

Science often discovers effects before mechanisms. The first achievement is showing that the world behaves differently from the best current null model. Only then does mechanism become urgent.

Suppose such a residual effect appeared.

The next tests should attack its shape.

Does it scale with the number of prior exposures?

Does the effect saturate?

Does physical distance matter?

Does time delay matter?

What dimensions of similarity predict it?

Do similar structures matter more than identical surface features?

Does the effect transfer across sensory modalities?

Does prior exposure to one pattern interfere with a competing pattern?

Can the effect be blocked?

Can it be amplified predictably?

A real phenomenon should become less mysterious as experiments accumulate.

This is where theories earn specificity.

If morphic resonance depends on similarity, similarity must eventually become measurable enough to make competing predictions. Sheldrake's broad descriptions allow many levels of organization—molecules, organisms, behaviors, social systems. That breadth may be philosophically attractive and experimentally dangerous.

A research program should narrow before it expands.

Pick one domain.

Find one robust effect.

Characterize it completely.

Do not use an effect in human behavior to support claims about crystallization unless a shared mechanism has been demonstrated. Do not use organismal development to imply telepathy. Do not allow the word *field* to do the work of a mechanism across unrelated levels.

An unknown effect becomes credible by becoming local in concept even if it is nonlocal in transmission.

This is how science handles new forces and interactions generally: specify where the effect appears, how large it is, what it depends on, and what it does not affect.

Breadth should be earned by successful transfer.

There is another test that would matter greatly: automated replication.

Human experimenters carry expectations. If a proposed effect can be studied in a system with automated stimulus generation, randomized assignment, robotic handling where relevant, machine-scored outcomes, sealed protocols, and audit logs, some ordinary channels can be reduced.

Automation does not create purity. Software has bugs. Hardware drifts. Data pipelines leak. Automation can reproduce a mistake perfectly.

But a well-audited automated system can eliminate many subtle interpersonal cues and discretionary choices.

The more controversial the claim, the more attractive it is to make the apparatus indifferent to belief.

For biological experiments, this could mean independent animal facilities receiving coded protocols and materials, with breeding, housing, diet, handling, apparatus, and scoring standardized and logged. It would mean treating supplier history and ancestry as data rather than background. It would mean randomizing the temporal order of control and target conditions where theory permits and separating cohorts so that local social transmission cannot accumulate.

For crystallization claims, it would mean aggressive contamination control and materials science expertise. Crystals are especially treacherous evidence for mysterious cumulative effects because nucleation can depend on impurities, surfaces, seed crystals, preparation history, temperature, solvent, and handling. A “new form became easier after first discovery” story is not persuasive until physical seeding and procedural diffusion are excluded at a level appropriate to the chemistry.

The romance of an example is inversely related to its usefulness if the experiment cannot control the domain.

This is why expertise from mainstream fields is not an obstacle to anomalous research.

It is the price of admission.

A parapsychologist testing a biological claim needs biologists.

A biologist testing crystallization needs crystallographers and chemists.

A psychologist testing collective learning needs statisticians, experimentalists, and people who understand modern digital exposure.

A physicist evaluating a field claim needs a mathematically specified interaction, not a metaphor imported from physics.

Serious interdisciplinarity is not assembling people who share curiosity.

It is assembling people capable of killing one another's favorite confounds.

That sounds adversarial because it is.

The experiment is the place where generosity toward an idea takes the form of hostility toward ambiguity.

What would failure look like?

Suppose several large preregistered multi-site studies find no effect at the predicted scale. Suppose effects reported in exploratory studies shrink toward zero under blinding and isolation. Suppose apparent cumulative learning is traced to laboratory practice, digital leakage, participant familiarity, or selective analysis. Suppose similarity manipulations do not behave as predicted.

Confidence in the morphic-resonance hypothesis should fall.

Not to absolute zero. Scientific conclusions are rarely metaphysical proofs of impossibility. But the theory should lose probability.

A scientific hypothesis has to become less believed when its risky predictions fail.

This sentence is easy to endorse abstractly and difficult to live by when the hypothesis organizes a worldview.

The same rule applies to skeptics.

Suppose well-powered, preregistered, independently replicated studies show a persistent remote-learning effect. Suppose the magnitude tracks prior repetition. Suppose swapping target and control stimuli reverses the effect. Suppose the effect survives physical and digital isolation. Suppose independent labs with hostile priors reproduce it. Suppose experimenter expectations do not predict outcomes. Suppose conventional exposure models cannot account for it.

Skeptics should update.

They would not be required to accept Sheldrake's entire metaphysics.

They would be required to accept that an unexplained reproducible effect exists.

That would already be a major scientific event.

The next question would be whether the effect reflects a new physical interaction, an unknown biological mechanism, an overlooked information channel, a statistical dependence nobody modeled, or something stranger.

Names should come late.

Effects first.

Mechanisms second.

Worldviews last.

This order protects discovery from branding.

The history of science contains phenomena that were real before their mechanisms were known and claims that disappeared under better measurement. The lesson is not that orthodoxy is always right or always wrong. The lesson is that instruments and methods have to be allowed to arbitrate against identity.

That arbitration requires good null models.

A null model is not “nothing happens.” It is a quantitative account of what should happen under known causes and chance. For human pattern-recognition tasks, that may require modeling baseline learnability, participant heterogeneity, practice effects, multiple comparisons, and stimulus complexity. For cultural convergence, it may require network models and shared exposure. For algorithmic systems, it may require accounting for training-data overlap and recommendation feedback.

A mysterious residual is meaningful only relative to a strong ordinary model.

Weak nulls manufacture miracles.

This is why the research program suggested by this book begins with resonance infrastructure rather than against it. Every mapped channel improves the null. Every confound removed makes a surviving effect more valuable.

The skeptic's work prepares the believer's best possible result.

That relationship is often misunderstood.

A loose experiment can produce an exciting positive result that convinces nobody outside the community already persuaded. A brutal experiment can produce a smaller effect that changes science because alternative explanations have been engineered out.

If morphic resonance is real, it deserves brutal experiments.

If it is false, brutal experiments can still discover how ordinary distributed memory produces extraordinary experiences.

Both outcomes justify the effort.

There is a larger lesson here about epistemology in an era saturated with algorithms and stories.

The human information environment now produces coincidences on demand. Search retrieves obscure parallels. Recommendation concentrates similar experiences. Generative models create plausible echoes. Social media gathers rare events into communities. Statistical tools can find patterns across huge datasets. The number of opportunities to discover an uncanny resemblance has exploded.

Our evidence standards have to rise with our coincidence capacity.

What felt impossible to encounter in 1981 may be routine to surface in a world where billions of people and machines publish continuously.

At the same time, our measurement capabilities have improved. We can preregister studies, timestamp materials, audit code, share data, automate randomization, coordinate multi-site replications, and analyze larger datasets.

The same technologies that make anecdotes more abundant can make experiments more disciplined.

That is the opportunity.

The question “what if the known channels fail?” should not be asked in a dark room after the ordinary explanations have become emotionally unsatisfying.

It should be designed in advance.

Build the room so the channels can fail cleanly.

Then watch.

Maybe nothing happens.

That is an answer.

Maybe a familiar artifact appears and teaches us where memory was hiding.

That is an answer.

Maybe a stubborn residual survives and becomes more regular every time the experiment gets better.

That is the answer worth waiting for.

The goal is not to protect the universe from strangeness.

It is to make strangeness unmistakable when it arrives.
