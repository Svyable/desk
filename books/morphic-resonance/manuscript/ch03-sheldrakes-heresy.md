# Sheldrake's Heresy

A controversial idea changes shape in public long before it is settled in evidence.

Supporters simplify it into a revelation. Critics simplify it into a joke. Journalists simplify it into a conflict. Institutions simplify it into a category. Decades later, many people know the temperature surrounding the idea better than the idea itself.

Morphic resonance has lived in that atmosphere for most of its existence.

Rupert Sheldrake proposed the hypothesis of formative causation in the early 1980s. The broad claim was that the forms and behaviors of self-organizing systems are influenced by previous similar systems. He called the process morphic resonance and described morphic fields as organizing structures associated with systems at multiple levels. In this view, an organism is shaped not only by present material conditions and inherited genes but also by a cumulative influence from similar organisms in the past. Repetition builds habit into nature.

The proposal did not merely add a new molecular mechanism. It challenged a deeper assumption about regularity. Modern science usually treats the laws of nature as stable descriptions or principles that do not become stronger because they have been obeyed more often. Sheldrake asked whether at least some apparent laws might instead resemble habits—patterns stabilized through repetition.

That is a large claim.

It is easy to hear the romance in it. Nature remembers. Species draw on a collective past. The universe learns its own forms. A difficult behavior, once mastered often enough, could become easier elsewhere. A new crystal form, once created, could become easier to produce in laboratories that never encountered the first sample. Memory might not be stored only in brains. Similarity itself might provide access to prior organization.

It is equally easy to hear why scientists resist it. The proposal invokes an influence that is not part of standard physical theory. It risks explaining a huge range of observations with a mechanism whose boundaries are difficult to specify. It raises immediate questions about what counts as sufficient similarity, how influence strength is calculated, why familiar biological and physical mechanisms are not enough, and how to distinguish the hypothesis from unnoticed transmission, selection effects, experimenter bias, publication bias, or ordinary historical accumulation.

The right response to a claim this large is neither awe nor insult. It is compression.

Reduce the worldview to predictions.

What should happen that would otherwise be surprising?

What should not happen if the theory is wrong?

What observation would force revision?

The strongest version of morphic resonance is attractive precisely because it appears to make risky predictions. If rats learn a genuinely novel task under controlled conditions, later comparable rats elsewhere should learn it more readily even without genetic descent, communication, shared training, or other conventional transmission. If a novel pattern is established repeatedly, subsequent formation of similar patterns could become easier. If memory depends partly on resonance with one's own past states rather than only on physical storage in the brain, some familiar assumptions about learning and recall would require reinterpretation.

Those are not small adjustments. They would alter several sciences at once.

That scale creates a problem for both believers and skeptics.

Believers can become too willing to accept ambiguous results because the implications are exciting. Skeptics can become too willing to reject experiments in advance because the implications are disruptive. In both cases the status of the theory becomes social before it becomes empirical.

Science is full of ideas that were initially ridiculed and later accepted. It is also full of ideas that were initially exciting and later abandoned. The existence of the first category does not improve the odds of every current heresy. “They laughed at X” is not evidence. People laughed at correct ideas and incorrect ideas. Likewise, institutional rejection does not prove suppression. Most rejected hypotheses are rejected because evidence does not support them, methods fail, predictions are vague, or better explanations exist.

The romance of the persecuted truth is one of the most durable human stories because it allows lack of acceptance to be counted as evidence of importance.

A rigorous treatment of morphic resonance must refuse that move.

It must also refuse its mirror image: the belief that respectable consensus ends inquiry by itself.

Consensus is evidence about evidence. It is not a substitute for reading the evidence.

The historical reaction to Sheldrake included unusually intense rhetoric. That rhetoric became part of the story and can distract from the scientific work. A theory can be badly received and still be wrong. It can be mocked and still be interesting. It can be interesting and still fail. The social drama has no authority over the experiment.

What makes morphic resonance worth examining today is not that it has secretly conquered biology. It has not. The value lies elsewhere.

First, the hypothesis isolates a real conceptual discomfort: form is not explained simply by listing ingredients.

A developing organism begins from inherited molecular machinery and a structured cell, not from an empty bag of genes. Cells interact. Signals vary across space and time. Mechanical forces matter. Gene regulation changes. Development proceeds through feedback, constraints, and reconstruction. Modern developmental biology has sophisticated ways to study these processes, and none of that requires morphic fields. But the intuition that form arises from organized dynamics rather than from a static blueprint was not foolish.

The mistake is to move from a correct criticism of a crude metaphor to an unsupported mechanism.

Genes are not literal architectural blueprints. That does not mean a nonlocal field must contain the building plan.

Second, Sheldrake's emphasis on habit points toward an underappreciated property of complex systems: repeated states often change future state transitions. Neural learning does this. Epigenetic regulation can do this. Ecological modification does this. Cultural transmission does this. Institutions do this. Algorithms do this. The mechanisms differ, but the family resemblance is real.

A radical hypothesis can therefore be useful even where its proposed mechanism is unproven. It can point at a question other disciplines answer in pieces.

Third, the theory exposes how much depends on the definition of similarity.

“Similar things influence similar things” sounds intuitive until measurement begins. Similar in what dimensions? Genetically? Structurally? Functionally? Behaviorally? Temporally? Contextually? A rat trained on one maze resembles another rat in countless ways and differs in countless others. A crystal resembles prior crystals by lattice structure but also differs in impurities, temperature, solvent, preparation, and laboratory technique. A person recalling an event resembles their past self more than other people in some respects and less in others.

If similarity is defined after seeing the result, almost any outcome can be made to fit.

This is not a special problem for morphic resonance. It appears throughout science and machine learning. A model can find patterns because researchers chose representations in which those patterns become visible. A medical study can generate subgroups after the fact that look meaningful. A historical analogy can be made persuasive by emphasizing matched features and ignoring mismatches. The more flexible the similarity metric, the easier it is to manufacture resonance.

A serious morphic-resonance experiment would need to define similarity before outcomes are known.

Fourth, the theory forces attention onto hidden transmission.

If an effect is supposed to occur without ordinary communication, the experiment must be designed by people who are almost paranoid about ordinary communication. Animals can be affected by handling, scent, housing, breeding, diet, circadian conditions, stress, experimenter expectations, and many other variables. Human participants can acquire information through media, language, cultural exposure, and subtle cues. Laboratories share suppliers, equipment, protocols, software, and publication networks. Crystallization outcomes can be altered by contamination and seed crystals at scales that are not obvious to casual observation.

An experiment testing a nonlocal influence must first become an experiment in how difficult locality is to guarantee.

This is one reason extraordinary claims are methodologically useful. They demand better maps of the ordinary world.

Suppose a study reports that participants recognize newly created material more easily after many other people have learned it. Before invoking morphic resonance, investigators would need to rule out differences in the material, exposure, expectation, scoring, participant selection, statistical analysis, and information leakage. If the effect survives, independent researchers should reproduce it. If the effect declines under tighter controls, that decline is informative. If it vanishes entirely, the null result must be allowed to matter.

A theory that treats every failure as evidence that conditions were insufficiently resonant becomes difficult to distinguish from a belief system.

This is not an insult. Belief systems can be meaningful. Scientific theories have a narrower obligation: they must expose themselves to losing.

Sheldrake has repeatedly described morphic resonance as testable. That commitment is the most scientifically important feature of the proposal. It gives critics and supporters a shared standard in principle, even when they disagree about experiments in practice.

The challenge is to specify the losing conditions.

Imagine three possible worlds.

In the first, all apparent cases of morphic resonance eventually resolve into ordinary transmission, statistical artifacts, selective reporting, developmental mechanisms, environmental inheritance, cultural learning, or other known processes. The intuition that “the past makes repetition easier” remains true, but the proposed nonlocal channel is unnecessary.

In the second world, most cases resolve conventionally, but a small, robust residual effect remains across carefully controlled experiments. Independent laboratories reproduce it. The effect has a stable relationship to predeclared measures of similarity. It scales predictably. Information leakage and conventional mechanisms fail to explain it. That would be scientifically important even before anyone knew the physical mechanism.

In the third world, morphic resonance turns out to be a broad organizing principle of nature. Repetition itself alters future probabilities across distance and time in ways that force revision of existing theory.

The evidence currently available does not justify writing this book as though we live in the third world.

The intellectually interesting task is to build methods capable of distinguishing the first from the second.

That task has consequences far beyond Sheldrake.

We live in an age of pattern claims. People see synchronicities in markets, politics, health, media, and personal life. Online communities can assemble thousands of anecdotes in hours. Algorithms surface similar stories to people already interested in them. A rare event, once searchable, appears common because every instance is visible. Correlation can acquire emotional weight before anyone knows the denominator.

The old problem of morphic resonance has become a modern literacy problem.

When does repeated similarity imply a mechanism?

When is it selection?

When is it shared environment?

When is it contagion?

When is it a consequence of the way information is collected?

When is the apparent recurrence simply the number of opportunities for coincidence?

And when does a residual pattern remain after all of that?

The scientific culture around controversial ideas often fails because each side becomes trained in only half of these questions. Skeptics learn to generate alternative explanations. Enthusiasts learn to notice anomalies. Good inquiry requires both abilities in the same person.

An alternative explanation is not a refutation until it is tested.

An anomaly is not a discovery until it survives attempts to remove it.

Morphic resonance deserves this harder treatment because the theory makes a promise about the structure of causation. It says similarity across time is not merely descriptive. Similarity can transmit influence.

That promise can be sharpened.

If similarity matters causally, then changing similarity while holding other conditions constant should change the effect. If accumulated past instances matter, then controlled increases in those instances should produce predictable changes later. If spatial separation does not weaken the influence in the ordinary way, distance manipulations should not behave like known signaling mechanisms. If the influence comes only from the past, temporal ordering matters. If self-resonance contributes to individual memory or form, carefully designed disruptions should reveal patterns that differ from purely local storage models.

These statements still need mathematical and experimental precision, but they point in the right direction. They make the theory pay rent.

The more flexible the theory remains, the less informative a positive result becomes. If any amount of prior repetition can count, if any dimension of similarity can be invoked, if any delay is acceptable, and if negative results can always be attributed to insufficient conditions, then the hypothesis can absorb the world without predicting it.

A good theory does not merely fit after the fact. It excludes.

This is where Sheldrake's heresy meets the broader argument of the book.

Most of the world already contains mechanisms by which repetition lowers the cost of repetition. Once a standard exists, manufacturers coordinate around it. Once a path exists, traffic reinforces it. Once a cultural practice exists, learners imitate it. Once a market category exists, capital and regulation organize around it. Once a model has been trained on a corpus, statistical patterns become easier to regenerate. Once a bureaucracy creates a form, future decisions are sorted into the fields on the form.

These are ordinary forms of resonance in the broad sense: prior structure increases the probability of similar future structure.

They are also confounds for any extraordinary form.

The better we understand them, the smaller and cleaner the unexplained remainder becomes.

Some readers may worry that this approach domesticates the mystery. If every strange recurrence is redescribed as infrastructure, perhaps the book has decided its conclusion in advance.

It has not.

The method contains a door that remains open. It simply refuses to call the door evidence that someone has walked through it.

If controlled effects survive after genetic, environmental, cultural, informational, behavioral, and experimental channels are removed, then something interesting is happening. The response should not be to protect the existing worldview at any cost. Science progresses by discovering where its maps fail.

But map failure has to be demonstrated.

This is the dignity of a real heresy. It does not ask for gentler standards. It asks for standards strong enough that, if the impossible happens, nobody can explain it away with a loose screw in the apparatus.

Sheldrake's most durable contribution may ultimately be his specific hypothesis. It may not. The evidence will decide over time, not the drama around the name.

The deeper contribution is the provocation that nature's regularities deserve causal questions. Why does a form recur? Why does a habit stabilize? Where is the relevant history carried? What kind of memory does a system need in order to behave as though the past remains present?

Those questions survive any verdict on morphic resonance.

A heresy is most valuable when it forces orthodoxy to become more precise.

This one forces us to ask where the world's memory actually is.
