# Institutions Have Ghosts

An institution can obey a person who is no longer there.

The instruction may not survive as a quotation. There may be no portrait on the wall, no memo anyone can find, no employee who remembers the original meeting. The person made a decision, the decision became a procedure, the procedure became a role, the role acquired a budget, the budget acquired reporting requirements, and thirty years later someone new is still clicking the box.

This is how a ghost becomes administration.

Institutions are built to outlast individual memory. That is one reason they exist. A hospital cannot relearn infection control every time staff turns over. A court cannot reconstruct legal procedure from first principles for each case. A bank cannot improvise settlement. A university cannot renegotiate every degree requirement with every student. Organizations preserve decisions because continuity is valuable.

The preservation is rarely clean.

Some rules carry explicit reasons. Others carry only form.

The rule remains while the story evaporates.

This produces a familiar experience inside large organizations: a procedure everybody follows and nobody can explain. Ask why and the answer arrives in passive voice. It has always been done that way. Compliance requires it. Legal wants it. Finance needs it. The system won't allow anything else.

Sometimes one of those answers is correct. Sometimes the current rule is a fossil whose original environment disappeared years ago.

The institution still behaves as if the old world exists.

That is institutional memory in a stronger sense than archives. An archive preserves a record of the past. A procedure preserves the effect of the past on present action.

The distinction is the same one that has followed us from bodies to cities.

Representation remembers what happened.

Structure remembers by changing what happens next.

Institutionalists have long studied path dependence: sequences in which early choices alter the returns to later choices, making some arrangements increasingly difficult to reverse. Paul Pierson's work on increasing returns in politics emphasizes that timing and sequence can matter, that small contingencies can have large consequences, and that a course of action can become hard to reverse once institutions and expectations grow around it.

That description could almost be mistaken for a social version of morphic resonance.

A pattern happens.

The system becomes more likely to produce the pattern again.

The difference is that institutional analysis points to mechanisms: coordination effects, learning, adaptation, sunk costs, power, expectations, legal commitments, and complementary structures. The old choice does not reach through time by resemblance alone. It leaves descendants.

A rule has children.

A reporting requirement creates software fields. The software fields create data. The data creates performance metrics. The metrics create incentives. The incentives shape hiring. Hiring shapes professional identity. Professional identity supplies defenders of the process.

By the time someone asks whether the original rule is still necessary, an ecosystem depends on it.

The ghost has a constituency.

This is why institutional reform so often disappoints people who imagine rules as text. Delete the sentence and the behavior may remain. The practice may now be encoded in contracts, habits, expertise, software, budgets, physical layouts, risk models, and expectations.

Changing the formal rule can remove the trunk after the branches have rooted themselves.

The same pattern can work in reverse. A rule may remain on paper while practice quietly routes around it. Institutions contain multiple memories that do not always agree. Formal law says one thing. Organizational habit says another. The database makes a third possible. Professional norms add a fourth.

This is a useful correction to any theory that gives an institution one field or one culture.

Institutions are internally resonant and internally contradictory.

Different layers preserve different pasts.

A university can contain a nineteenth-century calendar, a twentieth-century departmental structure, a twenty-first-century software system, and professional norms inherited from several disciplines. A hospital can combine old building geometry with new clinical evidence and reimbursement rules written for another generation of medicine. A government agency can carry statutory duties, court decisions, emergency workarounds, procurement rules, and technology platforms that arrived decades apart.

The present is a coalition of historical layers.

That is why institutional behavior often looks irrational from the outside. The observer sees one organization and expects one objective. Inside, several historical objectives have sedimented into the same machinery.

A procurement rule designed to prevent favoritism may slow experimentation. A documentation requirement created after scandal may increase accountability and administrative burden at once. A credential requirement intended to protect quality may later become a barrier to entry. A risk control introduced after one crisis may leave an institution less prepared for a different crisis.

Memory solves the problem it remembers.

The future is allowed to be different.

This is one of the deepest tensions in any learning system. To learn from failure, you encode a response. To remain adaptable, you must avoid mistaking the last failure for the only failure possible.

Institutions struggle because they learn asymmetrically.

A visible catastrophe can produce permanent controls. A catastrophe successfully prevented is harder to see. A process that quietly wastes thousands of hours may attract less attention than one dramatic incident. Rules accumulate around what became legible enough to frighten the organization.

Institutional memory therefore has salience bias.

The vivid past can dominate the probable future.

This is not unique to bureaucracy. Human bodies do it. Markets do it. Political systems do it. A severe event changes expectations more than a thousand uneventful days. The question is how the memory is translated into structure.

Organizations translate memory through documents, roles, routines, software, and authority.

A meeting cadence is memory.

A sign-off chain is memory.

A job description is memory.

A chart of accounts is memory.

A permission matrix is memory.

A legal precedent is memory.

Each turns a prior decision into a future constraint.

The strength of the constraint often depends on how far the memory has been externalized. A manager's preference disappears when the manager leaves. A preference converted into policy can survive longer. A policy converted into software can survive longer still because violating it requires technical change, not merely disagreement.

Code is institutional memory with enforcement.

This point matters increasingly because organizations are moving more of their procedures into software. A workflow engine does not merely remind employees of policy. It can make alternative sequences impossible. A database requires fields before a transaction proceeds. An automated risk rule rejects cases that do not meet thresholds. A permissions system hides actions from people not assigned the right role.

The institution's past becomes executable.

That phrase should make us slightly uneasy.

Executability is efficient. It reduces discretion, standardizes practice, and makes auditing easier. It also hardens assumptions. A human rule can be bent when reality does not fit. Software may require reality to be translated into its categories first.

The exception becomes an error message.

When people say an institution has a culture, part of what they may be sensing is this distributed executable history. Newcomers encounter not only what colleagues believe but what systems permit. They learn the organization through resistance.

Try to do something unusual and the ghosts introduce themselves.

The request has no category.

The budget cannot be used that way.

The vendor is not approved.

The form has no field.

The policy assumes a different sequence.

The database requires an identifier that the new thing does not yet have.

Novelty is institutionally illegible before it is institutionally difficult.

That distinction is important.

A new idea can fail not because anyone evaluated it and rejected it, but because the system has no route for it. Existing patterns possess forms, owners, precedents, metrics, and budget lines. The new pattern arrives without resonance infrastructure.

It has to invent its own bureaucracy before it can compete.

This is why genuinely new organizational behavior often begins in exceptions, pilots, side budgets, skunkworks, emergency powers, or informal networks. The old system cannot process it, so a temporary pocket is created outside ordinary procedure.

If the experiment succeeds, the institution faces a second challenge: converting novelty into memory without killing what made it novel.

A pilot becomes a program.

A program gets metrics.

Metrics create incentives.

Incentives change behavior.

The innovation is absorbed into the institution that once resisted it.

Now it can become another ghost.

This lifecycle resembles biological stabilization. A flexible response becomes a routine because the routine saves repeated deliberation. The routine then becomes costly to change.

The analogy should not be taken too far. Institutions are made of people, law, technology, and power, not cells. But the recurrence structure is useful: repeated success creates state that favors repetition.

Institutional memory is also political.

Some patterns persist because someone benefits from them.

Path dependence can sound mechanistic, as if history simply rolls downhill. But rules distribute authority and resources. Once a structure exists, groups may organize around it. People invest in skills suited to it. Firms build business models around it. Professionals gain status from it. Beneficiaries defend it.

A pattern that began accidentally can later persist intentionally.

The cause changes over time.

This complicates causal archaeology. The explanation for why an institution started doing something may not explain why it continues. Origins and persistence can be separate problems.

A rule began because of a crisis.

It survives because a profession formed around administering it.

A subsidy began as emergency relief.

It survives because firms invested in the subsidized structure.

A reporting system began to answer one question.

It survives because executives now use the metrics for performance management.

The ghost learns new jobs.

This matters whenever people search for one hidden cause behind a recurring pattern. History can hand a pattern from mechanism to mechanism. The chain stays continuous even while the reason changes.

Morphic resonance proposes a direct similarity-based influence from prior systems. Institutional recurrence shows how indirect influence can become almost indefinitely extended through relay. The old decision affects a policy. The policy affects a database. The database affects incentives. The incentives affect people who never heard of the original decision.

The later actor resembles the earlier one in behavior without being causally connected through conscious memory.

The connection is real anyway.

It just has many intermediaries.

This is a general warning about apparent action at a distance. Long causal chains disappear when observers examine only endpoints.

A person sees two similar institutional outcomes thirty years apart and says, “the organization reverted to type.” Perhaps it did. But *type* can be a compressed name for persistent hiring patterns, reporting structures, incentives, assets, legal constraints, and narratives.

The organization does not need an essence to reproduce itself.

It needs enough preserved state.

The same insight helps explain why leadership changes often produce less change than promised. Leaders occupy inherited systems. They can alter them, but not all at once. Contracts remain. Budgets are committed. staff has specific skills. Software has specific capabilities. Laws define authority. Customers expect continuity. Suppliers depend on schedules.

The institution limits the leader's degrees of freedom.

Then the institution is said to have “absorbed” the leader.

The ghost wins again.

Yet institutions can forget, and that is not always progress.

Experienced staff retire. Informal know-how disappears. Records are discarded. A once-rare emergency returns after the people who handled the last one are gone. A maintenance practice is dropped because nobody remembers what failure it prevented. A safety shortcut reappears after enough uneventful years make the rule seem excessive.

Forgetting can restore old risk.

A mature institution therefore needs two capabilities that pull against each other: retain the reason behind important constraints and periodically challenge whether the constraint still fits.

Memory without reasons becomes ritual.

Challenge without memory becomes repetition of old mistakes.

The best institutional memory may preserve provenance rather than only rules.

Not simply *do this*.

Also *this is what happened, this is the evidence, this is the condition under which the rule was adopted, and this is what would justify changing it.*

That form of memory is more expensive. It is also more adaptable because future people can distinguish principle from accident.

Science tries to do something similar through methods and citations. Engineering does it through incident reports and design rationale. Law does it imperfectly through opinions that explain holdings rather than merely announce outcomes. Software teams preserve reasoning in design documents and commit histories when they are disciplined enough.

Provenance gives ghosts names.

Once named, they can be questioned.

This is a practical lesson for any system that wants to learn without becoming haunted. Preserve enough history to know why the walls exist.

Then allow someone to ask whether the wall still belongs there.

The institution does not need a collective unconscious to carry the dead forward.

It has forms.

It has code.

It has budgets.

It has people whose livelihoods were built around yesterday's answer.

The ghosts are ordinary.

That is why they are powerful.
