# The Similarity Problem

Similarity is one of the easiest things in the world to recognize and one of the hardest things to define without cheating.

A child can tell that two dogs are more alike than a dog and a bicycle. A musician hears a melody transposed into another key and still recognizes it. A doctor sees two cases as clinically similar even when no measurement is identical. A lawyer distinguishes a precedent that is merely nearby from one that is controlling. A search engine decides which documents resemble a query. A machine-learning system places different images near one another in a representation space because statistical features make them useful for the same task.

We move through life by deciding what belongs with what.

Morphic resonance makes that ordinary act carry extraordinary weight. If similar systems influence later similar systems, similarity is not merely a way observers classify the world. It is part of the causal machinery.

That creates a problem large enough to swallow the theory.

Similar in what respect?

Two oak trees are similar as members of a species, but one may be a seedling in a wet English field and another a centuries-old tree in dry soil. Two people can be genetically similar and culturally distant. Two crystals can share a chemical formula yet differ in polymorphic form. Two economic recessions can look similar in unemployment and completely different in financial structure. Two songs can share rhythm but not melody, harmony but not instrumentation, mood but not structure. Two neural networks can produce similar outputs from different internal representations.

There is no single natural ruler marked *similarity*.

There are measurements chosen for purposes.

This is not philosophical nitpicking. It determines whether a theory predicts or merely narrates.

Suppose an experiment reports that one set of organisms became better at a task after similar organisms elsewhere learned it. If the effect appears, the researcher can point to species, task, timing, or form as the relevant similarity. If the effect does not appear, the researcher can say the conditions were not similar enough. Unless the metric was defined first, success and failure can both be absorbed.

A flexible similarity measure is a universal solvent for falsification.

The problem appears far beyond morphic resonance. Consider historical analogy. A commentator says a present political event resembles a famous past event. Which features count? Economic stress, polarization, institutional weakness, technology, charismatic leadership, geopolitical competition, demographic change? If enough variables are available, almost every period can be made to rhyme with almost every other period. The analogy becomes persuasive when matching features are emphasized and differences are treated as noise.

Or consider medicine. Patients can be grouped by diagnosis, symptoms, biomarkers, age, genetics, response to treatment, or combinations. Different classifications can reveal different causal structures. A subgroup discovered after the outcome can look dramatically responsive to a treatment even when the pattern will not reproduce.

Or consider recommendation systems. A platform does not need to know what two users “really” have in common. It needs a representation in which similarity predicts behavior. Two people may be demographically different yet close in a behavioral space because they pause on the same videos, buy related products, or listen to overlapping artists. The system's similarity metric is operational. It earns its existence by improving prediction.

That suggests a standard for morphic resonance.

A causal similarity should earn its existence by making novel predictions.

If physical form is the relevant dimension, then carefully changing form while holding other variables stable should alter the effect. If sequence matters, sequence similarity should predict influence better than visual resemblance. If behavioral pattern matters, two biologically different systems performing the same temporal structure might show more resonance than two genetically similar systems performing different structures. The theory becomes interesting when it chooses.

Without that choice, “like affects like” is too generous.

This chapter matters because the modern world is increasingly organized by machine-defined similarity. We are building infrastructures that turn resemblance into consequence.

A credit system groups a person with prior borrowers. A fraud model compares a transaction with historical patterns. A hiring system may rank applicants through features learned from past data. A music service decides which song belongs after another. An image model learns latent regularities across huge corpora. A social network suggests people because a graph implies closeness. A search engine retrieves pages because a query lands near them in a representation.

These systems make a literal version of something morphic resonance imagines abstractly: the past of similar cases influences the treatment of the present case.

The mechanism is not mysterious. It is computation.

That is precisely why it is useful for thinking.

Suppose a bank model sees a new applicant and estimates risk from similar historical applicants. The applicant is influenced by the past of people they never met. No direct communication is required between them. The past reaches the present through data, model training, feature selection, and institutional action. Similarity becomes causal because a system has been built to make it causal.

The applicant may experience the result as an invisible field of prior lives.

In a meaningful social sense, it is.

But the field has a database.

This kind of engineered resonance is everywhere. It also shows why hidden metrics matter. The person affected may not know which features made them similar to a prior group. The institution may not be able to explain the model cleanly. Similarity can become powerful before it becomes legible.

That raises an uncomfortable possibility for human judgments too. We may often be guided by similarity metrics we cannot articulate.

Expertise depends partly on pattern recognition. An experienced nurse notices that a patient looks wrong before being able to name the variable. A mechanic hears a sound and reaches for a particular cause. A chess player sees a position as belonging to a family of positions. These intuitions are not magical; they can arise from accumulated experience shaping perception and action. Yet the expert may not have introspective access to the features doing the work.

Tacit similarity feels like resonance because the classification arrives before the explanation.

This is one place where romantic accounts of collective memory can overreach. A person produces a solution without consciously remembering its source and concludes that the knowledge came from somewhere beyond personal experience. But learning routinely changes future judgment without preserving an accessible episodic record. We can be influenced by patterns we no longer remember encountering.

Forget the source, keep the weight.

Machine learning gives us a vivid analogy. A model does not store its training corpus as a neat library of remembered passages in the same way a database stores documents, though memorization can occur in some systems. Training changes parameters so that exposure to many examples alters future output probabilities. The model can reproduce structures that are statistically inherited from data without retrieving a conscious memory of an instance.

Human learning is not the same mechanism, but the analogy is useful: influence can survive as changed disposition.

Similarity activates dispositions.

This helps explain why culture can appear to speak through individuals. A writer has read thousands of sentences. Most are forgotten as episodes. Their rhythms, expectations, vocabulary, narrative habits, and judgments leave traces. When the writer encounters a new problem, similar prior structures can become active without a source citation appearing in consciousness.

The result may feel original because, at the level of explicit recall, it is.

Originality and inheritance are not opposites.

A person can genuinely construct something new from patterns absorbed so deeply that their origins are unavailable.

That statement can be abused to erase authorship. It should not be. Influence does not make creation automatic, and shared language does not make every sentence communal property. The point is cognitive: hidden inheritance is ordinary enough that an unexplained feeling of familiarity is weak evidence for a nonlocal field.

The similarity problem also changes how we interpret coincidence.

If two events look remarkably alike, we are tempted to ask for a connection. Sometimes that is correct. But the apparent improbability depends on how the resemblance was selected.

Imagine two strangers discover that they share a birthday, grew up on streets with the same name, own the same uncommon book, and both recently considered moving to the same city. The conjunction feels uncanny. But human lives contain thousands of attributes. If the matching features are chosen after the encounter, the number of possible comparisons is enormous. The probability of *these exact matches specified beforehand* may be tiny. The probability of *some striking set of matches noticed afterward* is much larger.

This is the multiple-comparisons problem wearing ordinary clothes.

Morphic resonance claims can accumulate in the same way. A large collection of cases is searched for similarity after the fact. The unusual matches survive into books and conversations. The mismatches disappear. The resulting narrative looks too structured to be random because randomness was filtered through human attention.

The cure is not to stop noticing. It is to specify the target before looking.

Prediction transforms resemblance into evidence.

There is another difficulty. Similarity depends on scale.

A person and a tree are dissimilar at the level of organismal form and similar at the level of being living systems made of cells. Two mammals are similar compared with a bacterium and dissimilar compared with identical twins. Two political parties are similar compared with monarchies and different compared with one another. A system can move from similar to dissimilar simply by changing the comparison class.

If morphic resonance operates across nested systems—as its broadest formulations suggest—the theory needs to say which scale dominates when similarities conflict.

Does a cell resonate most with prior cells of the same type, with the organism's own past state, with the species' historical cells, or with some structural pattern shared across species? Do these influences add? Compete? Decay? Is there a hierarchy? If multiple levels matter, can their contributions be measured separately?

These questions are not hostile. They are what turns a metaphor into a research program.

The same nested problem appears in social systems. A person may be similar to one group by profession, another by nationality, another by age, another by ideology, another by network position. Which past should shape expectations?

Institutions answer by choosing categories.

A tax form decides which distinctions matter. A census chooses boxes. A medical code groups symptoms. A platform chooses features. Once these categories are embedded, they do more than describe similarity. They manufacture it.

People begin organizing around the available labels. Funding follows categories. Research accumulates inside them. Policies target them. Data is collected according to them. The classification becomes part of the environment it measures.

This is resonance infrastructure at a high level: a similarity scheme acquires machinery.

Once machinery exists, similar cases are treated similarly, which can make them become more similar over time.

Feedback begins.

A neighborhood classified as risky may receive less investment, which can produce conditions later cited as evidence of risk. A genre label can influence what artists produce because platforms and audiences expect the genre. A disease category can shape research and clinical practice, improving recognition while also potentially narrowing attention. A corporate performance metric can cause teams to optimize toward the metric, making the measured pattern more prevalent.

The past does not merely influence similar things. Systems decide what counts as similar and then build channels that reinforce the decision.

This is more than a philosophical lesson for morphic resonance. It is a warning about civilization.

We are delegating similarity at scale to algorithms trained on history. Those algorithms increasingly determine which past cases are allowed to speak into the present. A model may decide that a new applicant resembles prior defaulters, that a new song resembles a successful cluster, that a new research paper resembles an established field, that a shopper resembles high-value customers, or that a social post resembles content associated with engagement.

Each judgment makes history active.

Each can also trap novelty by forcing the new into an old neighborhood.

A truly new pattern has a representation problem. If it resembles nothing valuable in the historical data, a similarity-driven system may rank it badly precisely because it is novel. Recommendation infrastructure naturally favors legibility to the past.

This is why innovation often arrives first as a category error.

The new thing looks wrong because the metric was built before it existed.

That insight will matter near the end of the book. For now, it clarifies the scientific problem.

If morphic resonance exists as an unknown natural process, experiments need a similarity metric that is not improvised from outcomes. If ordinary resonance infrastructure explains recurrence, researchers need to identify the representations through which systems classify cases and route historical influence.

In both cases the question is the same.

Who decided these things are alike?

Sometimes the answer is evolution. Sometimes development. Sometimes a nervous system. Sometimes a legal code. Sometimes a statistician. Sometimes a neural network. Sometimes an observer telling a story after the fact.

Those answers are not interchangeable.

A useful science of recurrence would make them explicit.

It would distinguish objective shared structure from observer-selected resemblance. It would measure how much a chosen similarity predicts future behavior. It would test whether changing the metric changes the effect. It would ask whether the same result appears under alternative representations. It would penalize explanations invented after outcomes. It would be suspicious of analogies that become stronger as their definitions become looser.

Most of all, it would recognize that similarity is not innocent.

Once attached to a mechanism, similarity becomes power.

A field, if it existed, would couple like to like.

A model already does.

A culture already does.

An institution already does.

A habit already does.

A category already does.

The radical question is no longer merely whether similar things influence similar things.

They obviously can.

The question is which similarity, through which channel, chosen by whom, and with what right to shape what comes next.
