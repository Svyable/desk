*How the Link Changed Everything*

The page is the wrong unit of history.

Digitizing the page gave us electronic documents. The link did something stranger and more consequential: it made a relationship between two things addressable, repeatable, shareable, and executable by another person later. A link can say *this supports that*, *go here next*, *this came from there*, *buy this*, *trust this*, *challenge this*, *open this app*, or simply *you may leave this page now*. Once those relations became cheap enough to create by the billion, text stopped behaving like a stack and began behaving like a world.

This book follows the link from cross-reference and footnote through Vannevar Bush, Ted Nelson, Douglas Engelbart, the World Wide Web, PageRank, search-engine optimization, link rot, deep linking, mobile apps, citation infrastructure, and machine agents. Its central claim is that the hyperlink was not merely a navigation convenience. It became a general-purpose primitive for routing attention, transferring reputation, preserving provenance, creating markets, and coordinating action across systems that did not share an owner.

The Web made publishing cheap. The link made independence useful.
