# Hypertext: How the Link Changed Everything

*How the Link Changed Everything*

**Sven Hardy Benson**

---

September 2026

The most important thing on the first Web page was not the page.

It was the exit.

The page lived on a NeXT computer at CERN. It explained the World Wide Web project, described the software, and pointed outward toward other places a visitor might go. Nothing about the screen itself would look miraculous now. It was text. There were headings. There were words that led somewhere else.

That last part is so ordinary that it is difficult to see.

A reader today has clicked, tapped, opened, copied, pasted, shared, shortened, expanded, previewed, crawled, ranked, blocked, sponsored, followed, unfollowed, redirected, and broken so many links that the act barely registers. A blue word is not a technology. A URL is often treated as clerical debris. We complain when a message contains too many links. We hide them behind buttons. We tell people the link is in the bio. Software unfurls them into cards because apparently the naked address is too ugly to be seen.

The familiarity has concealed the invention.

Before the link, a document could refer to another document. Scholars had done that for centuries. A footnote could name a book. A legal opinion could cite a case. An index could send you to another page. A library catalog could tell you which shelf to visit. A newspaper could tell you where an event occurred and a map could help you find it. Human culture was dense with references long before computers arrived.

What changed was not the existence of relation.

What changed was that the relation became executable.

Someone else could prepare the transition in advance. You could encounter the relation later and ask a machine to perform it. The distance between *this mentions that* and *take me to that* collapsed into an action small enough to disappear beneath a finger.

That sounds like convenience. It became infrastructure.

The difference matters because a page is a thing while a link is a relationship. Pages put information online. Links put relationships online. Once relationships could be named, published, copied, traversed, counted, ranked, sold, attacked, preserved, and automated, the digital world began to acquire properties that no individual page possessed.

Search is the obvious example. A search engine can read the words on a page, but the Web gave it something else to read: what other pages pointed there, what those pages were about, how the graph connected, and where a page sat inside a larger pattern of attention. A link written by one person for one reader could become evidence consumed later by a machine for an entirely different purpose. The relation escaped its original intention.

Commerce followed. If a link could route attention, attention could be priced. If links affected search, links could be bought. If a publisher could send a customer to a merchant, a link could become a referral contract. If a platform wanted to keep people inside its walls, the ability to link outward could become something to discourage, meter, bury, or redesign.

Reputation followed. A citation, backlink, recommendation, directory listing, review, blogroll, and profile link all express different relationships, but software can flatten them into edges and try to infer authority from the resulting graph. That inference is useful enough to build businesses around and crude enough to invite manipulation.

Security followed. A link can hide its destination behind its description. A familiar domain can redirect somewhere unfamiliar. A URL can carry parameters, identify a private object, trigger an application, open a payment flow, or become the first step in a fraud. The same mechanism that makes a transition effortless makes the wrong transition effortless too.

Memory followed. Every link is a small wager on the future. The author assumes that something will still answer at the other end. The Web itself cannot enforce that promise. Domains expire. organizations reorganize. files move. companies disappear. CMS migrations erase paths that once looked permanent. The resulting broken link is not merely a technical error. It is a severed relationship in a document that may otherwise remain intact.

Then the link escaped the browser.

A phone can use an ordinary web address to open a specific screen inside an installed app. Operating systems verify that the app and the website belong together, then route the address through native software instead of a browser. The link survives the fragmentation of the interface because the address is useful outside the interface that first made it famous.

Now the link is entering another transition.

Generative systems can answer without sending the user anywhere. Agents can traverse information and perform actions on a user's behalf. Search results can synthesize. Feeds can summarize. Assistants can read a set of sources and return a paragraph that contains the conclusion but not the experience of moving through the evidence. The blue underline is no longer guaranteed a central place on the screen.

That does not make hypertext obsolete.

It may reveal what hypertext was for.

The visible click was only one way of consuming a relationship. Crawlers have followed links without human fingers for decades. Search engines built maps of the Web by traversing them. Archival systems discover material through them. Security scanners inspect them. Bots preview them. Applications interpret them. Agents can follow them. A future in which fewer people manually click links could still be a future in which more machines depend on addressable relations than ever before.

The history of hypertext is usually told through inventors and systems, and those histories matter. Vannevar Bush imagined associative trails through a personal archive in 1945. Ted Nelson gave hypertext its name and pursued a far richer vision of connected literature than the Web eventually implemented. Douglas Engelbart's team built linked, addressable structures into a larger project for augmenting human intellect. Researchers at Brown kept hypertext systems alive. Tim Berners-Lee combined hypertext with Internet protocols and global addressing at CERN, then helped establish a Web simple and open enough to spread.

Those are not interchangeable achievements. One of the pleasures of the history is discovering how different their ambitions were.

Bush was thinking about memory and association. Nelson was thinking about literature, authorship, ownership, versions, and freedom from the fixed sequence of print. Engelbart was thinking about collective intelligence and the way tools could improve the work of people confronting complicated problems. Berners-Lee had an immediate institutional problem at CERN: information lived on incompatible systems, in different formats, under different local arrangements, while scientists came and went across organizational boundaries.

The thing that conquered the world was not the richest vision.

The Web left things out.

Its ordinary links were mostly one-way. A target did not automatically know who had linked to it. The source did not need the target's permission. A link did not guarantee payment, version history, permanence, ownership, or reciprocal visibility. Many of the properties that earlier hypertext thinkers wanted had to be rebuilt later, imperfectly, by search engines, analytics systems, archives, content-management systems, reputation markets, and platforms.

That incompleteness looks like a defect until you ask what it removed.

Coordination.

A person could publish a page and point at somebody else's public page without asking the other server to create a matching object. The author did not need both systems to share a database. The target could be built in another language, run by another institution, located in another country, and unaware that the source existed. The two parties needed a usable address and compatible protocols, not a business relationship.

This is the underappreciated political economy of the hyperlink.

A cheap outbound link lets independent things compose without becoming one thing.

That property is easy to miss because the Web eventually produced some of the most centralized companies in history. Yet the architecture that allowed those companies to grow also allowed a student to point at a professor, a critic to point at the object of criticism, a small shop to point at a payment service, a newspaper to point at a government document, a blogger to point at a rival, and a search engine to crawl them all.

The link did not abolish hierarchy. It created another source of power: topology.

Where you sit in a network of references can matter alongside who owns the server, who controls the publication, how much money you have, and whether an editor approves your work. That can democratize discovery. It can also create new hierarchies so powerful that people spend entire careers trying to influence the graph.

This book follows that change.

It begins before the blue underline, when references required a reader to do the moving. It passes through the machines and arguments that made linking interactive. It spends time on the Web's apparently modest decision to let a page point outward. It follows the moment when search engines began treating links as evidence, when markets began assigning that evidence a price, and when attackers learned that the same edge could carry deception.

Then it asks what survives if the click fades.

The answer I keep returning to is the edge itself.

Computer science has a plain word for a connection in a graph: an edge. The nodes may be pages, people, products, files, services, concepts, or pieces of evidence. The edge says that some relation exists between them. Human beings had always perceived those relations. The hyperlink industrialized one particular way of expressing them.

An edge could now cross ownership boundaries and remain usable.

It could be copied without copying the target.

It could be published before anybody knew who would follow it.

It could outlive the interface that displayed it.

It could be interpreted by a machine that did not exist when the author created it.

Those properties are larger than navigation.

They are a recipe for an open system.

Not a perfectly open system. Not a fair system. Not a permanent system. The Web contains gates, monopolies, dead ends, surveillance, spam, paywalls, fraud, broken addresses, and vast private databases. A link can mislead as easily as it can enlighten. A graph can concentrate power as easily as it can distribute it.

Still, the ability to create a usable relation between independently controlled things without first merging their control remains an extraordinary trick.

We use it so casually that we forget to be surprised.

This book is an attempt to become surprised again.
