# The Enemy in Our Hangar

## How America Learned to Fly the MiG—and What Reverse Engineering Actually Looks Like

**Sven Hardy Benson**

### Thesis

Cold War foreign-materiel exploitation gives us a rare, documented control case for the modern mythology of secret retrieval and reverse engineering. When the United States actually obtained adversary aircraft, it did not leap from possession to miraculous replication. It measured, flew, broke, repaired, instrumented, compared, trained, argued over classification, and converted narrow findings into tactics. The hard part was not owning the object. The hard part was translating an object into trustworthy knowledge.

This book follows the chain from the Iraqi MiG-21 defection that gave Israel—and then the United States—a flyable Fishbed, through HAVE DOUGHNUT, HAVE DRILL, HAVE FERRY, Soviet radar exploitation, the Red Eagles of CONSTANT PEG, and the training revolution that followed Vietnam. It then asks a deliberately uncomfortable question: if this is what a real retrieval-and-exploitation ecosystem looks like in the historical record, what evidentiary signatures should we demand before believing modern claims of exotic crash retrieval, reverse engineering, or hidden breakthrough technology?

### Evidence classes

1. **Verified / declassified / documented:** named programs, aircraft, test campaigns, official USAF histories, DIA/FTD evaluation records, declassified exploitation material, sortie counts, training outcomes, and the existence of foreign materiel programs.
2. **Credible but incomplete:** acquisition pathways where public records are partial, participant recollections, tactical influence that is difficult to isolate quantitatively, and classified successor activity inferred from institutional continuity.
3. **Disputed / unconfirmed:** specific modern crash-retrieval allegations, claims of non-human origin, claims of successful replication of exotic propulsion or materials, and narratives whose chain of custody is unavailable.
4. **Speculative synthesis:** documented foreign-materiel exploitation can serve as an epistemic benchmark—a control specimen—for evaluating extraordinary retrieval claims.

### Reader promise

No romance of Area 51. No reflexive debunking either. The book treats secrecy as an engineering condition: sometimes necessary, sometimes distorting, always costly. It asks what physically happens after intelligence services acquire a machine they did not design and cannot call the manufacturer about.
