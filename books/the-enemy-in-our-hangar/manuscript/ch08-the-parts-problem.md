# 8 — The Parts Problem

A secret aircraft is still an aircraft, which means eventually it needs a part nobody has.

This may be the least cinematic fact in the history of covert aviation and one of the most important.

The Red Eagles operated Soviet-designed fighters without the normal manufacturer, depot system, technical representatives, spares pipeline or training establishment that followed those aircraft in their native services. The U.S. Air Force Museum’s history of CONSTANT PEG says aircraft often arrived in poor condition and required lengthy repair and reconditioning. Replacement parts and expertise were scarce. Maintainers invented ways to keep a small, irreplaceable fleet flying.

Reverse engineering meets its first adult test at the supply shelf.

Can you reproduce the thing you understand?

Not conceptually. Reliably.

A bolt is not merely dimensions and alloy. It has heat treatment, surface finish, fatigue properties, quality control, lot history and installation torque. An engine blade is not merely shape and chemistry. Its grain structure, coatings, internal cooling geometry and manufacturing defects matter. A radar component may be replaceable only if the replacement behaves correctly in the system, under heat, vibration and voltage transients.

The gap between measuring a part and making a trustworthy replacement is where technological capability becomes industrial capability.

This is the third major corrective to “reverse engineering” as a magic phrase. Copying is not understanding, and understanding is not production.

The Cold War United States was one of the most industrially capable societies ever built. It could manufacture jet engines, supersonic aircraft, radars, missiles and nuclear weapons. Yet maintaining foreign aircraft remained hard because the local engineering context was missing.

That fact should discipline claims about recovered technology that is allegedly far beyond human manufacturing.

If a device depends on materials we cannot synthesize, tolerances we cannot hold or processes we cannot infer, possession may yield knowledge without reproduction. We might learn that something works and still fail to make another.

This is not a contradiction. It is normal.

A useful evidentiary taxonomy should therefore distinguish at least four achievements: **characterized**, **understood**, **replicated**, and **fielded**.

Characterized means measurable properties are known.

Understood means a causal model predicts behavior.

Replicated means humans can reproduce the effect or component.

Fielded means the replication can survive manufacturing variation, maintenance, safety requirements and operational use.

Modern extraordinary narratives often slide across all four in a paragraph.

The parts problem prevents that slide.

It also generates records. A fleet without native logistics creates substitution attempts, local fabrication, cannibalization, nondestructive inspection, special handling rules and safety decisions. It produces a distinctive engineering ecology because scarcity changes behavior.

The Red Eagles reportedly treated their MiGs like national treasures. That phrase can sound sentimental until one remembers the arithmetic. A conventional U.S. squadron can write off an aircraft and receive another. A covert foreign-materiel fleet may possess one example of a particular configuration. Lose it, and the experiment changes permanently.

That scarcity affects how boldly people test. It affects whether destructive analysis is authorized. It affects how much confidence can be placed in conclusions from a tiny sample.

The same questions should follow any alleged exotic retrieval.

How much material exists? Is destructive testing permitted? Are samples conserved because they are unique? Have different laboratories analyzed the same parent material? Has any claimed effect survived sample division? Has a component been consumed or damaged during testing? What did the failure teach?

Scarcity is not evidence that a claim is false. But genuine scarcity should have consequences.

A story in which a unique artifact is simultaneously too precious for open testing and yet has yielded sweeping conclusions deserves scrutiny. The less material available, the more conservative the inference should become.

The MiGs did not become less real because they were difficult to maintain. Their difficulty is part of what makes the history credible.

Real machines are needy.
