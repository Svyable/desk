# 5 — Fighting the Real Thing

The purpose of a captured fighter is not merely to admire it under fluorescent light.

At some point somebody has to fight it.

This is where foreign-materiel exploitation becomes psychologically interesting. Pilots do not meet aircraft as spreadsheets. They meet them as moving shapes with closure rates, aspect changes, threat envelopes and reputation. The first time a fighter pilot sees the red star he has been briefed about for years, cognition gets noisy.

The Constant Peg program later used the phrase “buck fever” for this phenomenon: the shock of first contact with the real adversary. The cure was exposure.

HAVE DOUGHNUT made exposure analytical before Constant Peg made it routine. The value of flying against the actual MiG-21 lay partly in the ability to discover its performance. But there was also a human correction underway. The enemy aircraft could stop being a legend.

This is a neglected function of intelligence. Good intelligence does not merely add information. It reduces the cognitive surplus generated by uncertainty.

Unknown systems become superweapons in the mind because every gap can be filled with the adversary’s best possible capability. Once the system is measured, the threat often becomes both more dangerous in specific places and less magical overall.

That asymmetry matters.

A pilot who thinks the MiG is superior everywhere may avoid an engagement he could win. A pilot who thinks it is inferior everywhere may die in the part of the envelope where it is not. Exploitation replaces global adjectives with local rules.

Do not fight it this way.

Force it to do this.

Do not accept this geometry.

Here is where your own aircraft has the advantage.

These are useful sentences because they can survive classification. You do not need to tell a line pilot the story of Munir Redfa, Israeli custody and Groom Lake to teach the maneuver that came from the evaluation.

The epistemic transformation is from noun to conditional.

“MiG-21” is a noun. “If the MiG-21 is in this energy state and you are in this aircraft, do not attempt this maneuver” is knowledge.

Modern conversations about anomalous aerospace often remain trapped at the noun stage. UAP. Tic Tac. Orb. Retrieval. Metamaterial. The terms accumulate semantic weight without acquiring conditional structure.

A mature technical claim should generate predictions. If an object uses a particular propulsion mechanism, what exhaust, waste heat, electromagnetic signature, acceleration profile or material requirement follows? If a material has an engineered function, under what field, temperature or frequency does the function appear? If a sensor return corresponds to a solid craft, which independent channel should co-register?

The MiG could be forced into predictions because it could be flown again tomorrow.

That repeatability is a luxury many anomaly cases do not offer. But when physical material is alleged to exist in custody, the excuse weakens. A retained sample can be tested repeatedly. An intact device can be instrumented. A claimed reverse-engineering program should eventually produce a performance curve rather than an adjective.

The real MiG also taught something about simulation. Before Constant Peg, aggressor units could imitate Soviet tactics in American aircraft. That was valuable. But a surrogate has a fidelity ceiling. It can reproduce doctrine better than hardware. If the surrogate turns differently, accelerates differently, presents a different visual signature and has different cockpit limitations, the student learns a hybrid threat.

Real foreign hardware closes that gap.

There is an analogy here to laboratory work on controversial materials. Computational modeling can suggest properties. Terrestrial analogs can provide controls. But if someone claims possession of the actual anomalous sample, eventually the actual sample must bear the evidentiary weight.

The stronger the claim, the less satisfying the surrogate.

HAVE DOUGHNUT’s great methodological contribution was brutally simple: stop arguing about the MiG and fly the MiG.

Reality has terrible public relations and excellent test data.
