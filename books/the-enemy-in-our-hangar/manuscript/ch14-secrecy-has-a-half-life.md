# 14 — Secrecy Has a Half-Life

CONSTANT PEG was declassified in 2006, almost two decades after it ended.

That delay is long enough to sustain myths and short enough to leave living participants.

Secrecy has a half-life because the reasons for protection change. Acquisition methods become obsolete. Adversary systems age. Facilities acquire new missions. Participants retire. The operational damage caused by disclosure falls. Eventually the institutional cost of maintaining the secret can exceed the benefit.

But declassification is not automatic truth serum.

Records can remain redacted. Files can be missing. Programs can be acknowledged without all acquisition pathways being revealed. Memoirs can appear before official documents. Public histories can simplify.

The useful lesson is not that every real secret eventually becomes public.

The lesson is that real secrets evolve.

They acquire bureaucratic age.

A program that persists for decades must renew authorities, budgets, facilities, clearances, safety processes and personnel. It experiences reorganizations. Its parent offices change names. Its technology migrates from revolutionary to legacy. Some facts become safe to acknowledge while others remain protected.

This evolution creates seams in the archive.

Modern claims about ancient, perfectly sealed retrieval programs often imagine secrecy as static. The same compartment allegedly survives presidents, corporate mergers, base closures, scientific revolutions and generations of personnel without producing a sufficiently authenticated documentary seam.

That is possible in principle.

It becomes less probable as claimed scale and duration grow.

The strongest response is usually “special access.” A deeply compartmented program can indeed restrict awareness and use cover mechanisms. But special access is not an exemption from institutional physics. People still require authorization to spend money and move hardware. Safety incidents still happen. Facilities still age. Expertise still has to be recruited.

The history of black aviation shows both sides: the state can keep breathtaking programs secret for years, and those programs still leave recoverable structure once the secrecy decays.

The U-2 existed. OXCART existed. HAVE BLUE existed. TACIT BLUE existed. HAVE DOUGHNUT existed. CONSTANT PEG existed.

Their eventual documentation does not prove every alleged program will surface on the same schedule. It does establish a reference distribution of what documented secrecy looks like after time.

A claim should be compared against that distribution rather than against an imaginary perfect black box.

Secrecy can delay evidence.

It should not be allowed to replace evidence.
