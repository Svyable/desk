# 15 — Reverse Engineering Is Mostly Failure

“Reverse engineering” sounds successful by grammar.

The verb arrives already pointed toward an answer.

Real programs spend much of their time not knowing.

They misidentify functions. They fabricate replacements that fail. They discover that a measured property belonged to damage rather than design. They learn that a material can be reproduced but not economically. They understand a component and still cannot reproduce the manufacturing process that made it reliable.

Failure is not an embarrassment around the edges of reverse engineering.

Failure is the dataset.

The foreign MiG programs make that visible because their hardware was comprehensible enough to expose the struggle. Maintainers had to improvise. Aircraft arrived in poor condition. Parts were scarce. Safety constraints mattered. Some types were eventually retired from the program partly because of age and risk.

If this much friction accompanied the exploitation of aircraft built with known twentieth-century physics, then a genuinely non-human or radically beyond-state-of-the-art artifact should generate extraordinary failure density.

This gives us a prediction.

A real long-running reverse-engineering effort should have a graveyard.

Dead-end materials programs. Broken test stands. Misread components. False mechanisms. Contamination scares. Competing teams. Replications that failed. Prototype concepts that never scaled.

The public mythology usually shows the opposite. The secret program is simultaneously impossibly difficult and mysteriously competent. Scientists allegedly decode gravity, inertia or propulsion while leaving no visible trail of the mistakes required to get there.

That is narratively efficient and technically suspicious.

The epistemic standard should therefore reward negative evidence from inside a program. A document that says *we tried this and it did not work* can be more probative than a testimonial claiming spectacular success. Failure records are hard to fake into a heroic story because they reduce the storyteller’s status.

This is one reason declassified technical archives are so valuable. They contain abandoned approaches and unresolved problems alongside successes.

A healthy investigation of controversial retrieval claims should actively seek the failures.

Which alleged materials produced no anomaly under independent testing?

Which witness accounts conflict?

Which supposed programs have naming or organizational inconsistencies?

Which patents were filed but never demonstrated?

Which claimed effects disappear under better controls?

If only confirming evidence survives curation, we are not looking at an engineering record. We are looking at a belief system.

The MiGs were valuable because they could disappoint the Americans studying them.

Any extraordinary artifact deserves the same privilege.
