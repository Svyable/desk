# 6 — HAVE DRILL and HAVE FERRY

One captured aircraft can become a story. Two start to become a method.

After HAVE DOUGHNUT came HAVE DRILL and HAVE FERRY, focused on MiG-17s. According to the National Security Archive’s synthesis of declassified material, the HAVE DRILL aircraft began flying at Groom Lake on February 17, 1969 and accumulated 172 sorties over fifty-five days. The HAVE FERRY aircraft began flying on April 9 and flew fifty-two sorties over twenty days.

Those numbers matter more than the code names.

A sortie is a unit of friction. Fuel has to be found. A pilot has to be cleared. Weather has to cooperate. Ground crews have to make the aircraft safe. Instrumentation has to work. The range has to be protected. Data has to be reduced. A failure discovered on sortie 41 has to be repaired before sortie 42.

This is the administrative shadow of a real secret program.

People often imagine classification as a way of making activity disappear. It is more accurate to think of it as a way of constraining who can see an activity that remains materially stubborn. Aircraft still consume tires. Engines still need inspection. People still get hurt. Buildings still exist. Budgets still have lineages even when their labels are opaque.

HAVE DRILL and HAVE FERRY also complicate the idea that exploitation aims only at advanced technology. By 1969 the MiG-17 was not the newest Soviet fighter. That did not make it irrelevant. North Vietnamese MiG-17s had demonstrated that an ostensibly older subsonic or transonic design could be lethal when matched to doctrine, geography, pilot skill and a particular fight.

The intelligence question was therefore not “Is this machine more advanced than ours?”

The question was “Can this machine kill us, and under what conditions?”

That is a healthier question for anomaly research too. Scientific novelty and operational importance should not be confused. A sensor artifact can be operationally important if it produces false alarms. A plasma phenomenon can matter to pilots without being exotic technology. A material can be scientifically interesting without belonging to a vehicle.

HAVE DRILL’s virtue was specificity.

The MiG-17 could be flown against American aircraft. Its low-speed maneuvering could be experienced rather than modeled. Its gun solution, visual presentation and energy behavior could be translated into warnings and tactics. The point was not to venerate the artifact but to use it as a hostile teacher.

There is a subtle epistemic difference between analyzing an opponent and learning from an opponent’s machine. Analysis begins with our categories. The machine can expose categories we forgot to create.

A feature dismissed as unsophisticated may turn out to be robust. A limitation may become an advantage under the right rules of engagement. A cockpit that seems primitive may reduce failure modes. A less advanced radar may matter less if the ground-control network carries the search burden.

The capture therefore becomes a check against technological narcissism.

This is one reason historical foreign-materiel programs are relevant to claims of non-human or radically advanced technology. If the hypothetical gap is enormous, our interpretive risk becomes enormous too. We would be even more likely to mistake a feature for a purpose, a byproduct for a mechanism, or a manufacturing artifact for a functional structure.

The responsible response to a large inferential gap is not greater certainty. It is more controls.

HAVE DRILL and HAVE FERRY were controls against American assumptions about Soviet air combat. Their real intellectual product was humility rendered operational.

The programs also remind us that redundancy matters. A single specimen may have idiosyncrasies. Multiple aircraft allow comparison, even if the sample remains tiny. The existence of a backup aircraft under HAVE FERRY is not glamorous, but backup is exactly what serious experimentation looks like.

Extraordinary programs should be full of such unglamorous redundancies.

If a purported retrieval ecosystem has operated for decades, there should be duplicate samples, independent laboratories, calibration standards, failed replications, destroyed components, archived raw data, contamination incidents and arguments over interpretation.

A program without failure may be a legend describing competence rather than competence itself.
