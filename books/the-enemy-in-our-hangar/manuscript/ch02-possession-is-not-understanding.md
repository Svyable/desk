# 2 — Possession Is Not Understanding

There is a childish model of reverse engineering in which the object is the answer key.

Put the machine on a table. Remove the panels. Measure every part. The secret falls out.

This is sometimes close enough for simple products. It becomes less true as the system grows complicated, tightly coupled, software-dependent, materials-sensitive, or embedded in doctrine. A fighter aircraft is not only a geometry. It is a bundle of tolerances, maintenance practices, supply assumptions, pilot training, radar modes, weapon envelopes, production choices and unwritten operating habits.

The United States knew this long before anyone used the phrase “foreign materiel exploitation” in public conversation. The intelligence problem was not just to identify what an adversary possessed. It was to learn what the equipment could actually do, where it failed, and what those facts meant for U.S. forces.

That last step is the one that matters most. An engine thrust number is not a tactic. A radar range is not an engagement result. Wing loading is not a pilot’s decision under pressure.

A recovered machine therefore creates at least five different questions.

What is it?

How is it made?

How does it perform?

How does it fail?

What should we do differently because we know these things?

Those questions belong to different communities. Technical intelligence may care about metallurgy, radar waveforms, electronic components and manufacturing quality. Test pilots care about departure behavior, energy retention and visibility. Weapons officers care about missile zones and geometry. Maintainers care about whether the aircraft can be kept alive without its native supply chain. Intelligence officers care about whether one specimen represents a fleet.

The temptation is to collapse these communities into “scientists studying the craft.” Real exploitation is messier.

HAVE DOUGHNUT’s own structure makes the point. Public summaries distinguish technical and tactical evaluation. The tactical report did not ask merely how the MiG-21 worked. It asked whether existing U.S. maneuvers and weapons worked against it, what its tactical limitations were, and how tactics should change. That is not reverse engineering in the commercial sense. It is adversary translation.

This matters because the phrase *reverse engineering* is used too generously in modern extraordinary claims. Sometimes it means material characterization. Sometimes it means recreating a component. Sometimes it means understanding a principle. Sometimes it means producing a deployable system. Those are radically different milestones.

Imagine handing a 2026 smartphone to an excellent electrical engineer from 1970. The engineer can weigh it, section it, identify classes of materials, trace some conductors and understand that electromagnetic radiation is involved. But reproducing the integrated circuits requires lithography, purity, metrology, design software, process knowledge and an entire industrial stack. Understanding that the phone computes does not give you a semiconductor fab.

A foreign fighter acquired in 1968 was vastly closer to American technological capability than that hypothetical gap. Yet exploitation still had bottlenecks.

The bottlenecks are the story.

Some knowledge is tacit. A pilot learns the onset of buffet in the body. A maintainer learns that a fitting which looks acceptable by specification is trouble after three heat cycles. A test engineer learns that a sensor channel lies under vibration. The original designers may never have written these things down because their own ecosystem supplied them culturally.

A captured object arrives stripped of that culture.

The intelligence service can sometimes recover manuals. CIA records include an effort to acquire Soviet technical manuals for a MiG-21PFM in 1969. A manual is valuable because it restores part of the missing context. But a manual is not a factory traveler, a depot mechanic, a design office, a supplier qualification program or ten years of fleet experience.

This is the second corrective to retrieval mythology: documentation can matter almost as much as hardware.

It is also why chain of custody is not clerical fussiness. If you do not know where an object came from, which model it is, what happened to it before recovery, or whether a sample was altered, the space of explanations expands. In intelligence exploitation, provenance constrains inference.

The MiG-21 loan from Israel had unusually good provenance. It was an intact operational aircraft flown by its pilot into Israel. Its identity was not inferred from a fragment sold through intermediaries. Its broad technological family was known. That made the exploitation problem tractable.

Even then, one aircraft could not reveal everything about Soviet fighter aviation.

This is the part that should haunt any conversation about alleged anomalous debris. A fragment with uncertain provenance sits at the opposite end of the evidentiary spectrum from an intact, operating, traceable aircraft. A strange isotope ratio may be interesting. A layered material may be unusual. Neither is a vehicle until the chain from sample to system is demonstrated.

Possession is powerful. But possession does not eliminate interpretation. It merely gives interpretation something harder to argue with.
