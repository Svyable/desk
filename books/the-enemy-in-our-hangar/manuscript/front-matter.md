# Front Matter

## The Enemy in Our Hangar
### How America Learned to Fly the MiG—and What Reverse Engineering Actually Looks Like

Sven Hardy Benson

There is a particular kind of sentence that becomes irresistible once secrecy enters the room.

*They recovered it.*

The sentence is short enough to feel like proof. It is not. Recovery is only the first verb in a long chain of verbs: inventory, preserve, identify, measure, disassemble, sample, instrument, fly, compare, reproduce, fail, revise, classify, disseminate. The difference between a rumor and a program often lives in those verbs.

This book began with a mundane historical fact that has become strange only because we have forgotten how strange it was. The United States secretly flew Soviet fighters over Nevada. Not replicas. Not F-5s painted red and flown with adversary tactics. Real MiGs, acquired through foreign governments and intelligence channels, maintained by Americans who had no normal logistics pipeline, flown by handpicked pilots, instrumented, compared against front-line U.S. aircraft, then used to teach thousands of aircrew how not to die.

The program was secret. The hardware was foreign. The acquisition pathways were sensitive. The work happened at places that later accumulated every species of legend. Yet when the records became public, the story did not reveal a frictionless technological miracle. It revealed maintenance logs, tactical evaluations, missing parts, safety limits, classification fights, sortie rates, dead pilots, and the dull heroism of people trying to understand a machine whose designers were not available for questions.

That is why the story matters now.

Modern claims about hidden retrieval programs often compress the hard middle. An object is allegedly recovered; then, somewhere behind a security fence, scientists supposedly extract capabilities that would redraw physics, materials science, propulsion, or energy. Sometimes the claim is merely that a program exists. Sometimes the claim expands into decades of successful reverse engineering. These are not the same proposition.

We have a control case.

HAVE DOUGHNUT, HAVE DRILL, HAVE FERRY, Soviet radar exploitation, and CONSTANT PEG give us a public record of what competent, well-funded states actually did when they obtained advanced adversary technology. The record is not complete. It should not be treated as complete. But it is concrete enough to discipline the imagination.

The book therefore keeps four ledgers. The first is documented fact. The second is credible but incomplete reporting. The third is disputed allegation. The fourth is synthesis—the place where I am allowed to make an argument so long as I mark it as an argument.

The rule is simple: testimony is not converted into hardware; classification is not converted into truth; a patent is not converted into a working device; a crash-retrieval story is not converted into non-human origin merely because the story is old, specific, or repeated by serious people.

The opposite error is also prohibited. A subject does not become false because it attracts nonsense. Foreign materiel exploitation was once among the blackest of black aviation activities. The United States did hide Soviet aircraft at secret ranges. It did construct an airfield for a program whose students often did not know until late in the process what they were about to see. It did acquire foreign radars and arrange them into threat complexes. It did keep real capabilities out of normal channels.

Secrecy can hide reality. It can also create a climate in which unreal things borrow credibility from the hidden real.

The pages ahead are about learning the difference.
