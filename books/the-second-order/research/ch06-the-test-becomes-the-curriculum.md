# Chapter 6 research — The Test Becomes the Curriculum

## Core evidence

### Brian A. Jacob — Chicago accountability and strategic response

- **Source:** Brian A. Jacob, “Accountability, Incentives and Behavior: The Impact of High-Stakes Testing in the Chicago Public Schools,” NBER Working Paper 8968 (2002), published in *Journal of Public Economics* 89(5–6), 2005, pp. 761–796.
- **Record:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w8968
- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.3386/w8968
- **Supports:** the chapter's claim that Chicago's high-stakes accountability policy was followed by sharp gains on the high-stakes mathematics and reading tests, but that gains were driven largely by test-specific skills and student effort and did not produce comparable improvement on a low-stakes state exam. The study also reports strategic responses including special-education placement, retention, and substitution away from lower-stakes subjects.
- **Caution:** the study does not show that all measured gains were artificial or that accountability produced no learning.

### Thomas S. Dee and Brian A. Jacob — No Child Left Behind counterevidence

- **Source:** Thomas S. Dee and Brian A. Jacob, “The Impact of No Child Left Behind on Student Achievement,” NBER Working Paper 15531 (2009), later *Journal of Policy Analysis and Management* 30(3), 2011, pp. 418–446.
- **Record:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w15531
- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.3386/w15531
- **Supports:** the counterevidence that consequential accountability can generate gains visible on an external assessment: the authors report statistically significant improvements in fourth-grade mathematics and evidence of eighth-grade mathematics gains, while finding no evidence of increased reading achievement.
- **Caution:** effects differ by subject, grade, baseline policy environment, and outcome; the chapter does not use these results as a universal estimate of accountability effects.

### Brian A. Jacob and Steven D. Levitt — detecting teacher cheating

- **Sources:** Brian A. Jacob and Steven D. Levitt, “Rotten Apples: An Investigation of the Prevalence and Predictors of Teacher Cheating,” NBER Working Paper 9413 (2003), and “Catching Cheating Teachers: The Results of an Unusual Experiment in Implementing Theory,” NBER Working Paper 9414 (2003).
- **Records:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w9413 and https://www.nber.org/papers/w9414
- **Supports:** the chapter's account of statistically detecting suspicious classroom answer patterns and prospectively retesting more than one hundred Chicago classrooms. Classrooms identified as likely cheating cases experienced large declines under monitored retesting, while comparison classrooms with large gains but low cheating probability largely retained those gains.
- **Caution:** unusual score gains are not themselves evidence of cheating; the prospective retest is important precisely because it distinguishes suspicious patterns from genuine improvement.

### Edward P. Lazear — teaching to the test can be productive

- **Source:** Edward P. Lazear, “Speeding, Tax Fraud, and Teaching to the Test,” NBER Working Paper 10932 (2004), published as “Speeding, Terrorism, and Teaching to the Test,” *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 121(3), 2006, pp. 1029–1061.
- **Record:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w10932
- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.3386/w10932
- **Supports:** the chapter's counterargument that narrowing attention toward tested material is not intrinsically harmful. In Lazear's model, more explicit and concentrated testing can improve learning for students facing high learning costs or weak incentives, while broader less predictable testing can be preferable for other learners.
- **Caution:** this is a theoretical model, not direct evidence that any particular testing regime is optimal.

## Falsification / counterevidence note

The chapter should weaken if gains on consequential tests generally transfer fully to independent assessments, persist across untested domains, and arrive without meaningful changes in curriculum allocation, student classification, threshold targeting, or test-specific preparation. It should also weaken if teachers and schools show little behavioral response to high-stakes assessment rules.

The opposite evidence matters just as much. External-assessment gains under No Child Left Behind and the possibility that explicit testing focuses useful effort both count against a simplistic claim that “teaching to the test” is necessarily fake learning. The narrower claim is that a consequential test becomes part of instruction and resource allocation, so its score must be interpreted as the output of an adaptive system rather than a passive sample of unchanged educational practice.
