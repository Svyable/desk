# Chapter 14 — Reflexive Markets: research and falsification note

## Argument boundary

The chapter argues that market prices can be both measurements and causal inputs. The claim is conditional, not a blanket assertion that prices are detached from fundamentals. Feedback should matter most where market valuations alter financing, collateral, liquidity, leverage, confidence, or rule-bound trading, and where arbitrage is constrained or slow.

The chapter deliberately preserves the countermechanism: markets also contain strong negative feedback. Arbitrage, entry, substitution, issuance, short selling, competition, and fundamental cash flows can correct errors and erase profitable rules. Reflexivity is useful only if it identifies conditions under which reinforcing feedback overwhelms those stabilizers for a meaningful interval.

## Sources checked

### Grossman & Stiglitz (1980), “On the Impossibility of Informationally Efficient Markets”

- *American Economic Review* 70(3), 393–408.
- Core use: information acquisition is costly. A market in which prices perfectly and freely revealed all costly information would undermine the incentive to acquire that information. The chapter uses this as an early second-order example: the informativeness of prices changes incentives to produce information.
- Boundary: this is not evidence that prices are generally uninformative. The point is precisely that informative prices require an equilibrium in which some informational imperfection remains.
- Bibliographic verification: EconPapers/AER metadata, current page checked 2026-08-30: https://econpapers.repec.org/article/aeaaecrev/v_3a70_3ay_3a1980_3ai_3a3_3ap_3a393-408.htm

### De Long, Shleifer, Summers & Waldmann (1990), “Positive Feedback Investment Strategies and Destabilizing Rational Speculation”

- *Journal of Finance* 45(2), 379–395; working-paper version NBER Working Paper 2880 (March 1989).
- Core use: rational speculators do not necessarily stabilize prices when noise traders use positive-feedback strategies. Forward-looking traders may profit by buying ahead of feedback traders, and their actions can increase volatility around fundamentals.
- This supports the chapter’s narrower point that sophisticated traders can knowingly participate in a movement they do not regard as a clean revelation of fundamental value.
- Primary/authoritative pages checked 2026-08-30:
  - NBER: https://www.nber.org/papers/w2880
  - Wiley journal record: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1990.tb03695.x

### Smith, Suchanek & Williams (1988), “Bubbles, Crashes, and Endogenous Expectations in Experimental Spot Asset Markets”

- *Econometrica* 56(5), 1119–1151.
- Relevant as experimental evidence that asset markets can generate bubbles and crashes even when the fundamental dividend process is made comparatively transparent to participants.
- Boundary: laboratory markets are deliberately simplified and should not be treated as direct estimates of bubble prevalence or magnitude in modern financial markets.

### Limits-to-arbitrage / stabilizing countermechanism

The chapter keeps the standard stabilizing intuition in view rather than treating positive feedback as universal. Profit opportunities attract capital; competition can erase predictable excess returns; fundamental cash flows and financing constraints eventually matter. The empirical question is therefore which mechanism dominates at a given horizon and institutional configuration.

## Claims intentionally kept qualitative

- No numerical claim is made about the frequency or magnitude of bubbles.
- No claim is made that momentum strategies are always destabilizing.
- No claim is made that market efficiency and reflexivity are mutually exclusive.
- Housing, banking, collateral, volatility-targeting, index demand, and rating effects are used as mechanisms rather than assigned unsupported universal effect sizes.
- The chapter does not attribute the term or a universal theory of “reflexivity” to a single thinker; it uses the word descriptively and defines the mechanism directly.

## Strongest counterevidence

The strongest countercase is the ordinary operation of competitive markets. Prices often do incorporate information quickly. Mispricing creates incentives for opposing trades. A trading rule that becomes widely known may lose profitability precisely because market participants adapt. Firms can issue supply when valuations rise; consumers substitute when prices change; creditors and investors can distinguish temporary market movement from durable cash flows. These negative-feedback channels are not exceptions to the chapter’s argument—they determine its scope.

The chapter would be weakened if reinforcing feedback remained important even where valuation had no effect on financing or operations, arbitrage was unconstrained, traders were unlevered and heterogeneous, horizons were long, and cash flows were stable and observable. In that environment the causal channel from price to fundamentals should be small, and a conventional information-aggregation account should do most of the explanatory work.

## Falsification note

Evidence against the chapter’s stronger mechanism would include:

1. robust findings that financing, collateral, risk-control, and confidence channels do not materially transmit market-price changes into real decisions even in highly leveraged or market-dependent settings;
2. evidence that apparent positive-feedback episodes disappear once public information and fundamentals are adequately modeled;
3. evidence that arbitrage capital reliably neutralizes crowded feedback strategies before they meaningfully affect prices or real activity;
4. deployed forecasting or trading systems retaining historical performance and calibration at large scale without measurable market impact, crowding, adaptation, or capacity effects.

Such evidence would not show that prices contain no feedback at all. It would push the chapter toward the narrower claim that reflexivity is a special case rather than a central deployment problem in financial prediction.
