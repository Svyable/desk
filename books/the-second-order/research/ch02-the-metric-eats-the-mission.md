# Chapter 2 research — The Metric Eats the Mission

## Claim under examination

Consequential metrics do more than report performance. By changing the relative reward attached to measurable tasks, they can redirect effort away from important but less observable parts of a mission even when reporting is honest.

## Evidence spine

Steven Kerr's 1975 management paper provides the organizational version of the problem: institutions often reward one behavior while hoping for another. The chapter uses the paper for the durable reward-system mismatch, not as evidence for every illustrative example in the manuscript.

Holmstrom and Milgrom's 1991 multitask principal-agent analysis supplies the deeper mechanism. When an agent performs several tasks and performance measures differ in informativeness, strengthening incentives on the well-measured task can distort effort away from poorly measured tasks. This supports the chapter's claim that an accurate metric does not automatically justify a strong incentive.

Bevan and Hood's 2006 study of targets in the English public health-care system provides an institutional case in which target-based governance produced reported improvements alongside measurement and gaming concerns. The manuscript keeps the claim at that level rather than importing unverified anecdotes about particular hospitals or staff.

## Counterevidence and limits

Targets can improve performance. A metric may be close enough to the true objective that optimizing it is exactly the desired behavior. Public reporting can break complacent equilibria and expose failures that discretion previously hid. In narrow, inspectable tasks, strong performance incentives can be appropriate.

The chapter therefore does not claim that measurement or targets are inherently corrosive. Its narrower claim is that incentive strength should account for what the metric omits and for the extent to which measured and unmeasured tasks compete for the same scarce effort.

## Falsification prompt

The chapter's argument would need substantial revision if strong incentives on one accurately measured dimension generally improved multidimensional jobs without degrading, displacing, or strategically recoding less-measured dimensions. Evidence that certain organizational designs reliably prevent such substitution should be treated as design evidence, not dismissed as an exception.
