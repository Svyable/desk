# Chapter 15 — Policy as a Game With Memory

## Claim under test

Durable public policy changes more than the outcome immediately targeted. It can change expectations, investments, organizations, civic capacities, identities, and political coalitions, so later policy encounters a population and institutional environment partly produced by earlier rules.

The chapter does **not** claim that every policy creates self-reinforcing lock-in, that beneficiaries always support programs they use, or that historical relationships become useless whenever policy changes.

## Research anchors

### Robert E. Lucas Jr. — “Econometric Policy Evaluation: A Critique” (1976)

Published in *Carnegie-Rochester Conference Series on Public Policy*, vol. 1, pp. 19–46. DOI: 10.1016/S0167-2231(76)80003-6.

Use: the policy non-invariance problem. Behavioral relationships estimated under one policy regime may change when the regime changes because agents alter decisions and expectations. This supports the chapter's narrower claim that historical correlations are not automatically structural laws for policy evaluation.

### Suzanne Mettler and Joe Soss — “The Consequences of Public Policy for Democratic Citizenship” (2004)

*Perspectives on Politics* 2(1), 55–73. DOI: 10.1017/S1537592704000623.

Use: policy feedback beyond material transfers. Their framework emphasizes how policies can define membership, affect political cohesion and divisions, build or undermine civic capacities, frame agendas and evaluations, and structure participation.

### Suzanne Mettler and Eric Welch — “Civic Generation: Policy Feedback Effects of the GI Bill on Political Involvement over the Life Course” (2004)

*British Journal of Political Science* 34(3), 497–518. DOI: 10.1017/S0007123404000158.

Use: concrete longitudinal example. The authors report changing mechanisms over time through which GI Bill educational benefits were associated with political participation, including early interpretive effects and later resource effects linked especially to education.

### Paul Pierson — “Increasing Returns, Path Dependence, and the Study of Politics” (2000)

*American Political Science Review* 94(2), 251–267. DOI: 10.2307/2586011.

Use: disciplined definition of path dependence through increasing returns, timing, sequence, and rising costs of reversal. The chapter deliberately avoids using “path dependence” merely as a synonym for “history matters.”

### Erik Gahner Larsen — “Policy Feedback Effects on Mass Publics: A Quantitative Review” (2019)

*Policy Studies Journal* 47(2), 372–394. DOI: 10.1111/psj.12280. First published online October 21, 2018.

Use: counterweight to universal feedback claims. Review of 65 published studies finds substantial heterogeneity across domains and outcomes, with stronger feedback in participation/engagement outcomes than in some other categories.

### Suzanne Mettler — “Making What Government Does Apparent to Citizens” (2019)

*The ANNALS of the American Academy of Political and Social Science* 685(1). DOI: 10.1177/0002716219860108.

Use: visibility and limitation. Policy feedback is conditional; design and delivery affect whether citizens perceive government action, while polarization and distrust can interfere with expected feedback.

## Counterevidence and boundary conditions

- Feedback can be weak, negative, or absent. Material benefit does not guarantee political support.
- Policies delivered indirectly or opaquely may not be recognized as government action.
- Strategic adaptation is bounded by attention, information, habit, administrative friction, norms, and uncertainty.
- Path dependence is not irreversibility. Coalitions break, technologies change, crises alter option sets, and entrenched policies are sometimes repealed or redesigned.
- Static incidence and reduced-form historical estimates remain useful for many questions. The objection is to transporting them across changed regimes without asking what behavior or institutions the regime itself helped produce.
- Randomization and quasi-experimental methods can identify many policy effects, but scaling, spillovers, treatment anticipation, and political feedback can limit transport from a bounded experiment to a durable regime.

## Falsification note

The chapter's thesis would weaken if durable policy changes generally left expectations, investment patterns, organizations, participation, and political coalitions unchanged; if relationships estimated under one policy regime routinely transported without degradation across major regime changes; or if policy-feedback research converged on negligible effects across domains. Existing evidence instead supports a conditional claim: feedback is heterogeneous, but sufficiently common and consequential that policy evaluation should test for it rather than assume it away.

## Prose discipline

Keep the distinction among immediate mechanical effects, behavioral responses, and later institutional/political feedback. Do not turn “memory” into mysticism. It refers to observable persistence in expectations, capital, organizations, records, identities, and rules.
