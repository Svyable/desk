# Chapter 19 research — Institutions That Can Be Surprised

## Claim map

### Multiple reporting channels can reveal different classes of safety information

- NASA, Aviation Safety Reporting System, program overview and reporting materials. The ASRS was established in 1976 and uses voluntary, confidential safety reporting with identifying information removed from reports used for safety analysis.
  - https://asrs.arc.nasa.gov/overview/summary.html
  - https://asrs.arc.nasa.gov/overview/confidentiality.html
- Federal Aviation Administration, Aviation Safety Reporting Program guidance.
  - https://www.faa.gov/about/initiatives/asrp

Use: supports the chapter's concrete aviation example and the narrower claim that reporting systems with different incentives can surface information that a single mandatory outcome stream may miss.

### High-reliability organizations deliberately attend to weak signals and resist premature simplification

- Karl E. Weick, Kathleen M. Sutcliffe, and David Obstfeld, "Organizing for High Reliability: Processes of Collective Mindfulness," *Research in Organizational Behavior* 21 (1999), pp. 81–123.
- Karl E. Weick and Kathleen M. Sutcliffe, *Managing the Unexpected*, 2nd ed., Jossey-Bass, 2007.

Use: background for the discussion of sensitivity to operations, small failures, and the limits of simplifying interpretations. The chapter avoids treating high reliability as a magic organizational type.

### Complex safeguards can themselves create interactions and new failure modes

- Charles Perrow, *Normal Accidents: Living with High-Risk Technologies*, Basic Books, 1984; revised edition, Princeton University Press, 1999.

Use: counterweight to the simple prescription to add more oversight or redundancy. Tightly coupled complex systems can fail through interactions that are not reducible to a single operator mistake.

### Challenger should not be compressed into a morality play

- Presidential Commission on the Space Shuttle Challenger Accident, *Report to the President*, 1986.
  - https://history.nasa.gov/rogersrep/genindex.htm
- Diane Vaughan, *The Challenger Launch Decision: Risky Technology, Culture, and Deviance at NASA*, University of Chicago Press, 1996.

Use: supports the chapter's restrained treatment of Challenger as an organizational and technical history involving evidence, normalization, communication, and decision process rather than an invented simple scene of villains ignoring obvious truth.

### Random or independent sampling can counter selective observation

- This chapter treats random auditing primarily as a design inference from the selective-label and feedback problems established earlier in the book, not as a claim that a universal audit percentage is empirically optimal.
- Relevant conceptual background: exploration versus exploitation in sequential decision-making and active learning. No fixed exploration rate is asserted.

## Counterevidence / boundary conditions

1. Redundancy and oversight are costly. Additional review layers can diffuse responsibility, create alert fatigue, or become ceremonial compliance.
2. Frontline dissent is evidence, not automatically truth. Local experts can be biased, attached to legacy routines, or overfocused on salient edge cases.
3. Revisability is not always desirable. Some commitments need stability because constant reopening can invite capture, short-termism, or strategic pressure.
4. Confidential reporting systems have selection effects of their own. Voluntary reports do not provide representative incidence rates and should not be treated as population samples.
5. A successful preventive control can suppress the outcome it was designed to prevent, making later causal interpretation difficult; the chapter presents this as a counterfactual problem rather than proof that any specific control worked.

## Falsification note

The chapter's argument would be materially weakened if organizations relying on a single model-selected evidence stream routinely detected important out-of-distribution failures as early and reliably as organizations maintaining independent reporting, audit, or exploration channels, with no meaningful cost to epistemic coverage. It would also be weakened if override logging, anomaly reporting, and independent sampling systematically produced no information beyond what the primary targeting model already captured.

The practical claim is deliberately narrower: institutions facing consequential feedback loops should preserve at least some route by which evidence not selected by the current model can become visible, and should periodically test whether that route is actually informative.
