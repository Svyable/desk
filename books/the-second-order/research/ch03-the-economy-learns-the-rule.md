# Chapter 3 research — The Economy Learns the Rule

## Claim under examination

Policy changes behavior not only through its immediate mechanical effect but through what people learn about the rule governing future policy. The chapter treats expectations, credibility, commitment, and precedent as causal inputs rather than as decorative language around policy.

## Evidence spine

Robert Lucas's 1976 critique remains the conceptual bridge from Chapter 1: behavioral relationships estimated under one policy regime may shift when the regime changes because expectations and decisions change.

Kydland and Prescott's 1977 “Rules Rather than Discretion” supplies the time-consistency mechanism. Their central point is that economic planning is a game involving rational agents rather than a control problem against passive nature; a future incentive to deviate can change behavior before the deviation occurs.

The Federal Reserve's historical record of the October 1979 operating-procedure change under Paul Volcker provides the concrete institutional case. The Fed's own retrospective study says the earlier gradualist strategy was judged inadequate for fighting inflation and reversing inflation expectations, and that the new regime had to permit substantial short-term rate increases and convince financial-market participants that the shift was real.

Thomas Sargent's 1982 “The Ends of Four Big Inflations” is used carefully as a broader historical argument that durable changes in policy regimes can matter differently from temporary actions when expectations depend on whether the old process will continue.

## Counterevidence and limits

Expectations are heterogeneous and imperfect. Most households do not infer policy rules with economist-like precision. The Volcker disinflation coincided with recession, energy-market changes, fiscal developments, and other forces, so the chapter does not claim that credibility alone caused the decline in inflation. Rules can also be harmful when they bind institutions to a bad objective or prevent adaptation to genuine shocks.

## Falsification prompt

The chapter's thesis would weaken if policy-regime changes with large consequences systematically left forward-looking behavior unchanged, or if promises and repeated exceptions had no effect on how consequential actors priced, invested, contracted, or negotiated. Strong evidence that discretion can retain credibility without any commitment mechanism is important counterevidence for later institutional-design chapters.
