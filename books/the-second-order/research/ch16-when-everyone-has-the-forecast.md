# Chapter 16 research — When Everyone Has the Forecast

## Core evidence

### Stephen Morris and Hyun Song Shin — public information and coordination

- **Source:** Stephen Morris and Hyun Song Shin, “Social Value of Public Information,” *American Economic Review* 92(5), 2002, pp. 1521–1534.
- **Record:** https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/000282802762024610
- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1257/000282802762024610
- **Supports:** the chapter's mechanism that a public signal can matter not only because it contains information about fundamentals but because everyone knows that others observe it. In their coordination model, sufficiently noisy public information can under some parameter settings receive excessive weight relative to private information and reduce welfare.
- **Caution:** the result is conditional on the model's coordination incentives and information structure. It is not a general theorem that transparency is socially harmful.

### Lars E. O. Svensson — transparency counterevidence

- **Source:** Lars E. O. Svensson, “Social Value of Public Information: Comment: Morris and Shin (2002) Is Actually Pro-Transparency, Not Con,” *American Economic Review* 96(1), 2006, pp. 448–452.
- **Record:** https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/000282806776157650
- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1257/000282806776157650
- **Supports:** the chapter's counterevidence that the welfare-reducing region in the Morris-Shin setup is narrow under plausible assumptions and that their result should not be used as a broad argument against transparency.
- **Caution:** Svensson's comment addresses a particular theoretical setup rather than proving that more public information is always beneficial in every strategic environment.

### Stephen Morris, Hyun Song Shin, and Hui Tong — clarification of the transparency debate

- **Source:** Stephen Morris, Hyun Song Shin, and Hui Tong, “Social Value of Public Information: Morris and Shin (2002) Is Actually Pro-Transparency, Not Con: Reply,” *American Economic Review* 96(1), 2006, pp. 453–455.
- **Record:** https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/000282806776157597
- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1257/000282806776157597
- **Supports:** the chapter's bounded interpretation of the original result. The reply emphasizes that Morris and Shin's argument was not a general case for opacity and helps preserve the distinction between conditional coordination risk and a blanket anti-disclosure thesis.
- **Caution:** the chapter uses the exchange to discipline the claim rather than to settle all questions about central-bank or public forecast communication.

### K. Goeree and Theo Offerman — public information can improve efficiency

- **Source:** Jacob K. Goeree and Theo Offerman, “Efficiency in Auctions with Private and Common Values: An Experimental Study,” *American Economic Review* 92(3), 2002, pp. 625–643.
- **Record:** https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/00028280260136435
- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1257/00028280260136435
- **Supports:** concrete counterevidence that public release of information about a common value can improve efficiency in strategic environments. The chapter therefore treats common information as capable of solving coordination and information problems as well as creating correlated error.
- **Caution:** an auction laboratory is not a direct model of macroeconomic forecasting or central-bank communication.

## Falsification / counterevidence note

The chapter should weaken if widely shared forecasts generally leave strategic behavior unchanged, if actors do not place extra weight on common signals when coordination matters, or if publication effects are empirically negligible in settings where recipients can act on one another's expected responses.

The opposite evidence is equally important. Public information often improves welfare, coordination, and efficiency; Svensson's critique and the Morris-Shin reply both caution against turning a conditional theoretical result into an anti-transparency doctrine. The durable claim is narrower: once a forecast is public enough to become common knowledge, its value and risk depend partly on the actions it coordinates, so forecast evaluation cannot always be reduced to statistical error against an outcome assumed to be behaviorally independent of publication.
