# Back Matter

This book makes a claim about deployed representations, not about prediction in the abstract.

The claim is that a model, metric, ranking, forecast, test, benchmark, or decision rule can become part of the causal process it was built to observe. Once consequential decisions attach to the representation, people and institutions may respond. That response can alter effort, selection, treatment, reporting, exposure, incentives, search, investment, classification, or the future examples available for training.

The research method followed that structure.

Each chapter asks three questions.

First: what was the representation trying to measure or predict?

Second: what decisions became attached to it?

Third: what evidence exists that those decisions changed later behavior or observations?

That third question is the important one. Without it, every case risks becoming a metaphor for Goodhart's law. The book therefore uses different evidence for different mechanisms rather than treating all adaptation as one phenomenon.

The policy chapters draw on work showing that behavioral relationships can depend on the regime under which they were estimated. The rankings chapters use organizational and admissions evidence about public measures changing the institutions they compare. The education chapter distinguishes external learning gains from test-specific gains, strategic classification, and outright cheating. The medicine chapter separates untreated risk from outcomes observed after treatment. The policing chapter distinguishes independently reported events from incidents discovered through model-directed search. The recommendation and experimentation chapters focus on exposure, interference, and population response. The benchmark and agent chapters examine how optimization changes the examples and objectives that later systems inherit.

These literatures do not prove one universal law.

They provide repeated evidence for a conditional mechanism.

The strongest version of the book's thesis would be wrong if consequential deployment routinely left the relevant data-generating process unchanged even when affected actors could perceive incentives and respond. It would also be wrong if post-deployment data could generally be interpreted exactly as though the model had never changed treatment, search, selection, effort, exposure, or participation.

The evidence does not support that stronger static view across the domains examined here.

But the opposite overstatement would be wrong too.

Adaptation is not synonymous with corruption.

A patient responding to treatment is not gaming the model. A student learning important mathematics because an exam made the material salient is not evidence that testing failed. A driver avoiding a flooded road after a warning is not distorting the forecast. A fraudster giving up because detection improved is a desirable response. A company improving a real quality dimension because customers can now compare it is exactly the kind of behavior measurement can be meant to induce.

The central distinction is therefore between improvement in the objective and improvement in the representation of the objective.

Sometimes they move together.

Sometimes they separate.

The research trail preserves counterevidence for that reason. Studies showing external mathematics gains under accountability belong beside studies showing test-specific gains. Randomized predictive-policing trials belong beside work on selective observation and runaway feedback. The value of public information belongs beside models of coordination failure. Useful benchmarks belong beside benchmark saturation and construct-validity problems. Productive synthetic data belong beside recursive training failures.

A theory of feedback that survives only by excluding beneficial feedback is not much of a theory.

The same discipline applies to causality.

Many chapters contain relationships that are suggestive rather than fully identified. The source notes distinguish formal models, observational studies, randomized experiments, institutional histories, and interpretive synthesis. Where a paper establishes association rather than a clean causal effect, the prose avoids silently upgrading the claim. Where the evidence comes from one institutional setting, the mechanism is generalized more cautiously than the setting.

The book also avoids treating a current equilibrium as proof of correctness.

A system can stabilize because it is well designed. It can also stabilize because actors have adapted to its incentives, because excluded cases disappear from the data, or because the institution has become better at producing the evidence the model expects. Stability is evidence about the operating system. It is not, by itself, evidence that the operating system is optimal.

This is why independent evidence becomes so important in the later chapters.

Independent does not mean perfectly neutral. No measurement channel is free of incentives, selection, or error. It means generated through a sufficiently different process that it can reveal blind spots in the primary one.

An external assessment can test whether high-stakes gains transfer. A monitored retest can distinguish suspicious score patterns from genuine improvement. A random audit can reveal what targeted inspection never sees. A confidential reporting system can surface anomalies that mandatory outcome data miss. A rotated benchmark can test whether progress transfers beyond a familiar task. Logged overrides can show where frontline judgment repeatedly disagrees with a model.

These channels cost money and attention. They can become bureaucratic. They can generate false alarms. They can themselves be gamed.

The recommendation is not maximum oversight.

It is enough epistemic independence that the institution retains some way to discover that its main representation has become self-confirming.

The same restraint applies to transparency.

Transparency can improve accountability, contestability, and trust. It can also make a rule easier to game or a detector easier to evade. Secrecy can preserve some robustness while making arbitrary decisions harder to challenge. The book does not offer one answer because the legitimate balance differs among public-benefit eligibility, medical care, fraud detection, recommendation, examinations, and adversarial security.

The more general principle is to model the response to transparency rather than treating transparency itself as an unqualified good or bad.

Several terms recur throughout the book and deserve one final boundary.

A proxy is not inherently deceptive. It is a practical representation of an objective that may be expensive or impossible to measure directly.

Gaming is not every behavioral response. It is response that exploits the representation in a way that weakens its relationship to the objective.

Performative effects are not proof that a model is bad. A model can intentionally produce a better distribution than the one it predicted.

Feedback is not necessarily instability. A feedback loop can converge.

And prediction is not governance until decisions and consequences are attached to it.

Those distinctions matter because the practical lesson of the book is not to distrust numbers.

It is to distrust a picture of numbers in which the people affected by them remain motionless.

The research files accompanying the manuscript record sources at the chapter where they materially support factual claims, along with cautions, counterevidence, and falsification conditions. They are not intended as an exhaustive bibliography of the large literatures touched here. They are an audit trail for the specific claims the manuscript asks the reader to carry.

The argument can be summarized without a formula.

Observe the system.

Deploy a representation.

Watch what decisions attach to it.

Watch what those decisions make people do.

Then ask whether the next data still mean what the old data meant.

That is where the second order begins.
