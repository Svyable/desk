# Prices That Create Their Own Evidence

A market price is supposed to tell us something.

It condenses bids, offers, inventories, beliefs, constraints, fear, patience, financing, and whatever information participants think matters into one number that can be observed by people who were nowhere near the underlying decisions. That is why prices are so useful. They allow coordination without requiring everyone to know everything.

But a price can also change the thing it appears to describe.

Consider a financial intermediary holding a large portfolio of assets financed partly with borrowed money. If those assets rise in price, the firm's measured net worth improves. Its leverage can fall mechanically even if it has not sold anything or raised new equity. Lenders may become more comfortable. Haircuts can fall. Risk limits may loosen. The institution can borrow more and buy more assets.

The higher price has not merely reported stronger conditions.

It has created balance-sheet capacity.

If that new capacity produces more buying, the buying can push prices higher again. The price now contains evidence partly generated by the response to the earlier price.

Reverse the direction and the loop becomes more dangerous.

Asset prices fall. Marked-to-market net worth shrinks. Leverage rises. Lenders demand more collateral or reduce funding. Risk systems call for smaller positions. The intermediary sells assets. If enough institutions are under similar pressure, the sales push prices down further. The new lower price appears to confirm that conditions are worse, and mechanically makes some balance sheets worse as well.

A price that began as information becomes an input into the next round of information.

This is not a metaphor. It is plumbing.

Tobias Adrian and Hyun Song Shin documented this kind of procyclical balance-sheet behavior in work published by the Federal Reserve Bank of New York. In a market-based financial system where assets are marked to market, they argued, price changes show up quickly in net worth and prompt financial intermediaries to adjust balance-sheet size. Their empirical work found leverage to be strongly procyclical for important classes of intermediaries.

The mechanism helps explain why finance is full of feedback loops that can make apparently fundamental movements larger than the initial shock.

Suppose an asset is genuinely worth less because new information has arrived. A price decline is appropriate. But if the decline forces leveraged holders to sell, the next decline may reflect two things at once: the new information and the financing response caused by the first decline.

At that point, the observed market price is not a pure estimate of fundamental value. It is also a record of who had to sell.

This distinction matters because financial markets are often described as if prices simply aggregate beliefs. They do that, but beliefs are only one ingredient. A buyer may think an asset is cheap and still be unable to buy because funding is constrained. A holder may think an asset is valuable and still be forced to sell because a lender has changed a haircut. A risk manager can reduce exposure because volatility has risen, even while believing expected value remains attractive.

Prices therefore aggregate constraints as well as opinions.

That is what makes them capable of creating their own evidence.

A falling price can increase measured volatility. Higher measured volatility can tighten risk limits. Tighter limits can reduce demand. Lower demand can push the price down. A wider credit spread can weaken a borrower's financing conditions. Worse financing can damage the borrower's actual prospects. Those weaker prospects can justify a wider spread.

The loop can begin with information and end with reality changing in the direction the information predicted.

This is not always irrational. Each participant can be acting sensibly from a local perspective.

A lender that increases collateral requirements after prices fall may be protecting itself. A fund that reduces leverage when measured risk rises may be following a prudent risk policy. A dealer that shrinks inventory when funding becomes expensive may be preserving liquidity. None of those decisions requires panic.

The system-level effect can still be destabilizing.

This is one of the recurring themes of second-order systems: individually reasonable responses can alter the environment enough to change what counts as reasonable next.

The Bank for International Settlements has studied related mechanisms under the language of procyclicality. A 2009 Committee on the Global Financial System report examined how market-sensitive valuation and leverage practices could amplify financial cycles. A 2010 report on margins and haircuts described how secured-lending terms affect access to credit and risk-taking and can reinforce leverage in good times and deleveraging in bad times.

The key word is reinforce.

A financial boom does not need to be wholly detached from fundamentals to become self-strengthening. Rising profits can justify higher prices. Higher prices can improve collateral values. Better collateral values can support more borrowing. More borrowing can support more investment and more asset purchases. Those purchases can improve reported returns and make the original optimism appear prescient.

The process can contain real improvement.

A company whose stock price rises can issue equity on better terms. A household with appreciated collateral may refinance. A bank with stronger market value may find funding easier. A growing firm can hire, invest, and acquire competitors. Financial conditions are not merely spectators of the real economy.

This is why reflexive price dynamics are difficult to separate into “real” and “fake.”

The price can help create the fundamentals that later seem to justify the price.

The same is true on the way down.

A company whose debt price collapses may face higher refinancing costs. Those costs can make default more likely. A bank whose funding becomes suspect may lose counterparties, deposits, or access to short-term credit. A property owner facing falling collateral values may be unable to refinance and may sell, adding supply to a weak market. A sovereign facing rapidly rising yields may confront a larger fiscal burden simply because refinancing has become more expensive.

Expectation becomes condition.

Condition becomes evidence.

The strongest version of this argument can become mystical if it is not disciplined. Markets are not infinitely self-creating. A bad company cannot become permanently profitable merely because its stock rises. A genuinely insolvent borrower cannot borrow forever because investors remain optimistic. Cash flows, productive capacity, legal claims, physical assets, and institutional limits continue to matter.

Prices have power because they are connected to contracts and balance sheets, not because belief overrides arithmetic.

That connection is what makes the feedback precise.

One useful way to see it is to imagine three layers.

At the first layer, a price reflects information about an asset.

At the second layer, the price changes constraints: collateral values, leverage, margin requirements, borrowing capacity, risk limits, compensation, investor flows, or access to new capital.

At the third layer, those changed constraints alter behavior in the underlying system: buying, selling, hiring, investment, liquidation, refinancing, production, or default.

The altered behavior generates new information, which returns to the first layer.

That loop is the second order.

Fire sales are one of its clearest forms.

A fire sale does not mean that every seller is irrational or that every low price is wrong. It means that some holders are selling because they need liquidity or must reduce exposure, not because they independently concluded that the asset's long-run value fell by the full amount of the price decline.

If potential buyers are also constrained, the price may need to fall far enough to attract the limited capital still able to act.

Research from the BIS has described distress selling as a staged feedback process: some trigger forces sales, prices fall, and those lower prices can generate further rounds of selling. The importance of the framework is that it identifies a mechanical route by which market outcomes can become endogenous to the financing structure around the market.

This creates an evaluation problem.

Suppose a risk model flags a portfolio as dangerous after its measured volatility rises. The institution sells. The sale contributes to the price decline. The decline raises measured volatility further. Later, analysts look back and say the model correctly identified a dangerous asset because the price collapsed.

Maybe it did.

But the model also helped cause part of the collapse.

That does not make the warning useless. It makes causal interpretation harder.

A successful alarm can create the fire drill that makes the hallway chaotic.

This problem becomes especially sharp when many institutions use similar models.

If one fund follows a volatility limit, its trades may be too small to matter. If thousands of portfolios respond to similar volatility, leverage, or benchmark rules, the rules can synchronize behavior. The market then contains a population of actors who may receive the same instruction from different internal systems at roughly the same time.

Diversity of ownership does not guarantee diversity of response.

The common rule can become a hidden coordination mechanism.

This is where models stop being private descriptions and become market structure.

Value-at-Risk systems provide one example. A model estimates how much a portfolio could lose over a chosen horizon under a given confidence level. Institutions can use that estimate to set limits. If measured risk rises, allowed exposure may fall. Adrian and Shin's later work on intermediary leverage and Value-at-Risk examined how balance-sheet size and leverage can move with measured risk and how such behavior can amplify deleveraging.

Again, the point is not that risk limits are foolish.

The alternative can be uncontrolled leverage.

The question is whether a rule that protects one institution can create correlated selling across many institutions.

This is the same structure we saw with organizational metrics in Chapter 2. A sensible local objective can produce a system-level distortion when everyone optimizes under the same signal.

Finance makes the loop unusually fast because the feedback can happen through prices in seconds.

Other domains move more slowly.

A university ranking changes applications, which change selectivity, which affects the next ranking. A neighborhood's rising home prices attract investment, change who can buy, alter tax revenue, and reshape schools and amenities. A sovereign credit rating can affect financing costs, which affect fiscal conditions, which affect the next rating. A platform's seller ranking can direct customers toward highly ranked sellers, generating sales history that supports the ranking.

In each case, the score or price is not merely observing a fixed object.

It allocates resources toward the object.

The allocation then changes the object.

This creates a danger in backtesting systems that make decisions through markets.

Historical data can show how a strategy would have behaved when its trades did not exist. But a strategy deployed at scale can move prices, change liquidity, attract imitators, and eliminate the very pattern the backtest found. The more capital the strategy controls, the less valid the assumption that the historical market would remain unchanged around it.

A forecast with no market impact is a different object from a forecast attached to billions of dollars.

The scale of the response becomes part of model validity.

This is why capacity matters in finance. A strategy can be genuinely predictive at small scale and self-defeating at large scale. Buying what the model says is underpriced pushes the price toward the model's estimate. That is success in one sense. It is also the destruction of future excess return.

The model arbitrages away its own evidence.

The opposite can happen when the strategy depends on continuation rather than correction. If participants buy because prices are rising and their buying helps prices continue to rise, recent performance can recruit the capital that sustains recent performance. Momentum can be partly informational and partly flow-driven.

The observed trend then contains information about the trend-following behavior reacting to the trend.

This does not imply that every bubble is mechanically predictable or that every price increase is self-fulfilling. It means market data can be endogenous to the strategies using market data.

That recursive structure is easy to miss because a price arrives as a single clean number.

The number hides the path.

A price of 80 can result from a broad reassessment of future cash flows. It can result from a forced liquidation into a thin market. It can result from changing collateral terms. It can result from benchmark rebalancing. It can result from a crowded strategy unwinding. Usually it reflects several forces at once.

Yet downstream systems often treat the number as if its causal history did not matter.

Collateral systems ask what the asset is worth now. Risk systems ask how volatile it has been. accounting systems mark the position. lenders change terms. investors see losses. Each response can be reasonable given the current number.

The current number becomes more consequential precisely because everyone trusts it enough to act.

There is no simple escape from this loop.

Using stale prices can hide losses. Ignoring market signals can preserve bad positions. Disabling margins can transfer risk to lenders. Preventing all forced sales can subsidize leverage. Market-sensitive systems exist for good reasons.

The design problem is to reduce amplification without pretending prices are irrelevant.

That can mean countercyclical capital buffers, through-the-cycle risk estimates, minimum margin floors in good times, liquidity reserves, stress tests that assume correlated exits, or rules that recognize the difference between temporary market dislocation and permanent impairment.

Each intervention has costs and can create new incentives of its own.

There is no viewpoint outside the system.

That is the deeper lesson.

A financial model that reads prices is reading a world partly shaped by other financial models reading prices. A lender that uses collateral values is using numbers partly shaped by the financing conditions attached to collateral values. An investor who treats a rising price as evidence of quality may be observing a rise partly caused by other investors treating the same rise as evidence.

The mirror faces another mirror.

That image is tempting, but it can make the phenomenon sound abstract. The actual mechanism is simpler: contracts attach consequences to prices, consequences change behavior, and behavior changes prices.

Once those links are strong enough, a market price is not only a measurement.

It is an instruction.
