# When Everyone Has the Forecast

A forecast feels most useful when few people have it.

The farmer who sees a storm before the neighboring farms can protect equipment first. The trader who notices a change before the market does can move before prices adjust. The company that spots a shortage before competitors can secure inventory. In each case, information creates an advantage because behavior has not yet fully adapted to it.

Now give the same forecast to everyone.

The information may become more socially useful. More farmers move equipment. More factories order inputs. More households evacuate. More lenders prepare for stress. But the forecast also changes the behavior of the whole population at once, and that collective response can alter the event being forecast, the price attached to it, or the evidence available afterward.

This is where prediction stops being merely informational and becomes coordinative.

A public forecast is not only a statement about the future. It can become a focal point around which people organize present action.

That is usually desirable. Weather warnings are supposed to coordinate. Central-bank communication is supposed to shape expectations. Public-health advisories are supposed to change behavior. Emergency alerts are valuable precisely because many people receive them.

But a shared forecast has a second-order property that a private forecast does not: everyone can reason about everyone else receiving it.

The individual question changes from “What do I think will happen?” to “What will everyone do because they think this will happen?”

Financial markets make the transition obvious. If a widely followed analyst predicts that an asset will rise, a trader does not need to believe the analyst has discovered eternal truth. The trader needs to believe enough other traders will act on the forecast before the price adjusts. A forecast can therefore matter because of beliefs about beliefs.

The same logic appears in bank runs, currency attacks, product launches, hiring booms, election expectations, traffic routing, and shortages. A prediction about a crowded system can become a piece of common knowledge inside the system.

Common knowledge is stronger than shared information.

It is not merely that many people know something. They know that others know it, know that others know that others know it, and so on far enough for the recursive structure to affect behavior.

Economists Stephen Morris and Hyun Song Shin examined one version of this problem in their 2002 paper on the social value of public information. Their model begins with a useful distinction between two goals an individual may care about. One is matching an underlying fundamental state. The other is coordinating with what other people are doing.

If only the first goal mattered, more accurate public information would have a straightforward value. Better signals improve decisions.

But when coordination itself matters, a public signal can receive disproportionate weight because everyone knows everyone else has seen it.

Suppose a business has private information about demand in its own market and also hears a public forecast about the economy. The private signal may be more informative about the business's circumstances. Yet the public forecast says something about what suppliers, customers, competitors, lenders, and investors are likely to believe. If the business's payoff depends partly on moving with them, the public signal acquires strategic importance beyond its statistical precision.

Everyone leans toward the same number because everyone knows everyone else can see it.

Morris and Shin showed that under some parameter settings, this coordination motive can make additional public information socially harmful. A sufficiently noisy public signal can pull many actors together around an error. The signal becomes a coordination device so powerful that its common visibility outweighs its informational weakness.

The result was provocative because it cuts against a deep instinct: transparency should improve decisions.

It also attracted an important correction.

Lars Svensson argued in 2006 that the harmful region in the Morris-Shin model was narrower than many readers had taken it to be. Under plausible conditions, public information remained socially beneficial, and the original analysis should not be read as a broad case against transparency. Morris, Shin, and Hui Tong replied that they agreed the earlier result had sometimes been interpreted too strongly and clarified that their concern was conditional, not a blanket rejection of disclosure.

That debate matters for this book because it prevents an easy slogan.

The lesson is not that public forecasts are bad.

The lesson is that public forecasts can change the objective function of the people receiving them.

A forecast becomes one input into beliefs about the future and another input into beliefs about the crowd.

That dual role is enough to change behavior.

Consider a central bank.

For much of monetary history, policy makers tried to influence economic conditions partly through actions whose implications the public inferred after the fact. Modern central banks communicate much more explicitly. They publish projections, policy statements, meeting minutes, speeches, and sometimes projected policy-rate paths. The goal is not merely to tell historians what officials thought. Communication is part of the transmission mechanism.

A statement about future rates can change borrowing today.

A statement about inflation can change wage bargaining today.

A statement about future balance-sheet policy can change asset prices before any bond is bought or sold.

The forecast acts in advance of the event.

This creates a strange evaluation problem. Suppose a central bank predicts inflation will fall and communicates a policy path consistent with achieving that result. Firms alter prices, workers alter wage demands, investors alter bond positions, households alter borrowing, and the bank itself adjusts policy as new data arrive. Inflation later falls.

Was the forecast accurate?

Yes, perhaps.

But the observed outcome is not independent confirmation of a passive prediction. The forecast and associated policy helped produce the world in which the prediction was judged.

That does not make the forecast illegitimate. It makes the causal interpretation more demanding.

A weather forecaster can usually treat the storm as indifferent to the forecast. A central bank cannot assume inflation is indifferent to communication about inflation.

This distinction becomes especially important when institutions are rewarded for forecast accuracy.

If a prediction changes the behavior that determines the target, then accuracy can arise through several paths. The model may have described the world correctly. The institution may have changed the world toward the prediction. Other actors may have coordinated around the prediction. Or the forecast may have been revised until it converged with the emerging outcome.

These paths have different meanings even if they produce the same final error statistic.

The same problem appears in markets with consensus forecasts.

Imagine every major firm expects a recession next year. Each company independently sees some weakness, but the forecast also becomes public through surveys, earnings calls, analyst notes, and news coverage. Managers slow hiring. Capital expenditures are postponed. Inventories are cut. Households hear that firms are preparing for recession and become cautious. Lenders tighten standards. Suppliers receive fewer orders and reduce production.

The forecast may have been correct because the economy was already deteriorating.

It may also have contributed to the deterioration.

The two explanations are not mutually exclusive.

This is reflexivity operating through shared expectations rather than balance sheets alone.

Once enough actors move on the same signal, a forecast can create the conditions that make itself look prescient.

The reverse is possible too.

Suppose everyone expects a shortage and orders early. The resulting surge in demand empties shelves, strains logistics, and confirms the impression of scarcity. Or suppose everyone expects congestion and avoids a route, leaving it unexpectedly clear. In one case the forecast helps cause the predicted condition. In the other it helps prevent it.

Prediction can be self-fulfilling or self-defeating.

Both cases create trouble for naive evaluation.

A forecast that prevents the event may look wrong even if it was useful. A hurricane warning that causes evacuation is not discredited because few people are harmed. A cyber alert that leads a company to patch a vulnerability may make the predicted breach disappear. A risk score that causes intensive treatment may be followed by survival.

The better the intervention works, the less the observed outcome resembles the untreated forecast.

This is familiar from medicine. It becomes more subtle when the intervention is distributed across millions of people who coordinate with one another.

Public forecasts also create incentives to forecast the forecasters.

A professional investor may spend less time asking what inflation will be and more time asking what the Federal Reserve will conclude inflation will be. A company may care less about the unemployment rate itself than about what policymakers will infer from the next release. A political campaign may care about voter preferences but also about how polling averages will change donor behavior, media attention, turnout efforts, and strategic voting.

The object of prediction moves up one level.

Then another.

What will happen?

What will the authorities think will happen?

What will markets think the authorities think will happen?

What will everyone else do because they expect everyone else to think that?

Systems with many recursive layers can become exquisitely sensitive to public signals.

This sensitivity does not imply irrationality.

If your payoff depends on what others do, it is rational to care about what others believe.

A restaurant considering whether to open in a new neighborhood cares about local purchasing power, but it also cares about whether other businesses will move in. A developer deciding whether to build near a proposed transit line cares about future ridership, but also about whether employers and residents expect the line to matter. A lender financing the developer cares about both sets of expectations.

Shared forecasts help coordinate complementary investment.

That can be enormously productive.

Many useful equilibria require people to move together.

A technology standard becomes valuable when manufacturers and customers expect others to adopt it. A new payment system works when merchants and consumers both participate. A neighborhood can revive when residents, businesses, lenders, and government all expect others to invest. Public information can help break coordination failures by giving people a common basis for action.

This is why the Morris-Shin result must be handled carefully. The fact that public information can coordinate people around a noisy error does not erase the much larger class of situations in which coordination is the point.

A fire alarm is useful because everyone hears it.

The danger lies in forgetting that a public forecast can do more than reduce uncertainty.

It can change strategic dependence.

Once that is understood, several design questions follow.

The first concerns precision.

An institution with a powerful public voice should be especially careful about attaching false exactness to uncertain forecasts. A noisy private belief harms one decision maker. A noisy public focal point can organize thousands of decisions around the same error.

The damage is not necessarily proportional to ordinary forecast error.

A central estimate of 2.6 can become qualitatively different from a range of plausible outcomes if contracts, thresholds, or media narratives crystallize around the point estimate. The public may treat a modeled midpoint as a commitment. Traders may treat a projected path as a promise. Managers may build budgets around a number that was meant only as a conditional scenario.

Communication therefore changes the model's social meaning.

The second question concerns diversity of information.

When everyone uses the same forecast, private information can become less influential. This may improve coordination while reducing the system's ability to aggregate dispersed evidence.

Financial markets depend partly on disagreement. Different investors hold different models, horizons, constraints, and information. Their trades help produce a price. If all of them mechanically follow the same model, the market may become less informative even as individual decisions become more standardized.

The system gains consistency and loses independent error.

This is closely related to the problem of correlated models in risk management. Ten institutions using ten nominally separate models may still share data, assumptions, vendors, benchmark methods, and regulatory constraints. Their forecasts can fail together.

Diversity is valuable not because disagreement is inherently wise, but because uncorrelated mistakes provide information.

A crowd of independent estimates can cancel noise.

A crowd staring at one dashboard cannot.

The third question concerns release timing.

Information released gradually can allow actors to combine it with private evidence. Information released all at once can coordinate quickly but may create abrupt synchronized movement. Emergency systems often prefer synchronization. Financial systems sometimes fear it.

There is no universal optimum.

The design depends on whether delay creates danger or whether simultaneity creates fragility.

The fourth question concerns conditionality.

Forecasts that depend on policy choices should say so.

If a central bank projects inflation under an assumed rate path, the projection is different from a weather forecast. If a public-health agency projects hospitalizations under a given vaccination rate, the number is contingent on behavior. If a traffic model predicts congestion assuming current route choices, the forecast may become obsolete as soon as drivers see it.

Conditional forecasts become misleading when audiences read them as unconditional prophecy.

The distinction is difficult to preserve because public communication rewards simplicity.

A headline wants one number.

The system may contain ten contingencies.

The fifth question concerns feedback measurement.

Institutions that publish consequential forecasts need to evaluate not only whether the forecast matched the eventual outcome but how behavior changed after publication.

Did firms alter hiring?

Did investors change duration exposure?

Did households refinance?

Did travelers reroute?

Did patients seek care earlier?

Did local officials move resources?

Without this information, a forecast may be judged as if it observed a world that publication itself helped alter.

The sixth question concerns humility about counterfactuals.

A successful public forecast often destroys the comparison world needed to know what would have happened without it.

If an evacuation order moves a million people out of danger, we do not observe the casualty count under non-evacuation. If a bank announces a credible liquidity facility and panic subsides, we do not observe the run that might have occurred without the announcement. If a central bank commitment anchors expectations, we do not observe the inflation path in the absence of credibility.

This does not make evaluation impossible.

It means prediction and intervention cannot be separated by habit.

The institution needs experiments, natural variation, historical comparison, structural reasoning, or other evidence that distinguishes informational accuracy from behavioral impact.

The distinction becomes more important as forecasting becomes cheap.

For much of history, forecasts were scarce because data collection and computation were expensive. Today the same economic release can feed thousands of models within seconds. Weather forecasts appear on every phone. Sports probabilities update live. Companies can purchase demand forecasts by region, product, and hour. Individuals receive algorithmic estimates of travel time, health risk, creditworthiness, churn, fraud, and likely preferences.

The problem is no longer merely that people lack forecasts.

Increasingly, everyone has one.

Often they have the same one.

That changes what forecast quality should mean.

A model can be individually accurate and systemically destabilizing.

A forecast can be statistically noisy and socially useful because it coordinates action.

A prediction can become wrong because people successfully respond to it.

A prediction can become right because people collectively enact it.

Accuracy remains important. It is simply not the whole object anymore.

The system designer has to ask a second set of questions.

Who receives the forecast?

What can they do with it?

Do their actions interact?

Will the forecast become common knowledge?

Will it suppress private information or help aggregate it?

Does the desired outcome require coordination or diversity?

Can the institution distinguish a forecast that described the future from one that helped create it?

These questions are awkward because they move prediction away from the clean geometry of error rates and into strategic behavior.

But that is where widely shared forecasts live.

A forecast released into a crowd is no longer only a forecast.

It becomes part of the crowd's environment.

And once everyone has it, the future being predicted begins to include the fact that everyone has it.
