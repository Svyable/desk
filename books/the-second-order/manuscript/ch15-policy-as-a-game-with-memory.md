# Policy as a Game With Memory

In January 1981, the United States changed the rules of a game that had been running for years. Inflation was high. Unemployment was painful. The Federal Reserve had already begun forcing interest rates upward under Paul Volcker. Ronald Reagan entered office promising tax cuts, deregulation, and a different relationship between government and the economy.

None of the people responding to those policies were blank slates.

Workers had lived through the inflation of the 1970s. Firms had learned to expect wage demands and price increases. Bond traders had watched governments promise restraint before. Households had mortgages, habits, contracts, and memories. The economy confronting the new policy was partly the product of the old one.

That sounds obvious when put historically. It becomes surprisingly easy to forget when policy is evaluated statistically.

A policy maker often wants to know what will happen if a rule changes. Raise a tax. Lower a rate. Add a benefit. Remove a subsidy. Tighten an eligibility standard. Change a disclosure requirement. The natural temptation is to look backward: under the old rule, when variable X moved, variable Y usually followed. If that relationship has been stable enough, perhaps it can be projected into the new regime.

But the new rule can change the relationship itself.

Robert Lucas made this objection famous in economics in 1976. His target was a style of macroeconomic policy evaluation that treated estimated behavioral relationships as if they would remain fixed when policy changed. If households and firms adjust their expectations and decisions to the policy regime, then parameters estimated under one regime need not survive another. The historical relationship is not a law of nature. It is partly a record of people responding to the rules they faced.

The point reaches beyond macroeconomics. Policy is a game with memory because people remember what institutions did, learn which promises were durable, acquire assets and skills under existing rules, organize around benefits and burdens, and form expectations about what will happen next. The policy does not merely act on a population. It helps produce the population that will encounter its successor.

That makes government one of the clearest second-order systems in public life.

Consider a simple benefit program. At the moment of enactment, an analyst can estimate the immediate transfer: who qualifies, how much they receive, what the budgetary cost will be. Those are first-order effects. Over time, recipients may make choices that depend on the benefit. Employers may adjust compensation. State governments may reorganize complementary programs. Advocacy groups may form. Administrative agencies may build specialized expertise. Voters may come to see the benefit as normal, inadequate, invisible, stigmatizing, or indispensable.

Years later, a proposal to change the program does not encounter the society that existed before the program. It encounters the society that the program helped make.

Political scientists call one part of this process policy feedback. Suzanne Mettler and Joe Soss describe policies as capable of shaping how citizens understand government, how they identify themselves and others, what capacities they possess, and whether and how they participate in politics. The arrow usually drawn from preferences to policy can point backward too. Policy changes preferences, resources, organizations, and participation; those changes alter the politics that determines future policy.

The GI Bill offers a concrete example. In research following veterans across decades, Mettler and Eric Welch found that educational benefits were associated with later political participation through mechanisms that changed over time. Early effects were tied to how the program was experienced; later effects were linked more selectively to resources, especially educational attainment. A policy adopted for veterans' readjustment after World War II became part of the civic biography of the people who used it.

This is stronger than saying that people like benefits they receive. Sometimes they do not. Sometimes they barely perceive the government role at all.

Mettler's later work on the visibility of public policy makes the qualification important. Programs delivered through obscure tax provisions, employers, banks, or other intermediaries can fail to produce the political feedback one might expect from their material value. Citizens may use a government-created benefit without recognizing it as government action. Polarization and prior distrust can further weaken or reverse the expected response.

Policy feedback is therefore not a machine that automatically manufactures supportive constituencies.

That matters because the easy version of the argument is too neat. Enact a program, create beneficiaries, beneficiaries defend program, program becomes permanent. Real political systems are messier. Policies can stigmatize recipients. Administrative burdens can discourage participation. Benefits can be too diffuse to mobilize anyone. A visible tax can create opposition more effectively than an invisible subsidy creates support. A program can alter the resources of organized interests without changing mass opinion. Feedback can be positive, negative, delayed, or politically cross-cutting.

A quantitative review by Erik Gahner Larsen of dozens of published studies found substantial heterogeneity in both domains and outcomes. Feedback effects appeared stronger for political participation and engagement than for some other outcomes, but there was no single universal effect size waiting to be inserted into a policy model.

The useful lesson is not that every policy locks itself in. It is that policy evaluation becomes incomplete when it treats the political environment as fixed.

This is where memory enters in several forms.

The first is expectation.

A government that announces a temporary tax cut may get a different response from one believed to be permanent. A central bank that has repeatedly tolerated inflation may find a new promise of restraint discounted. A regulator known for reversing rules after elections creates different incentives from one whose decisions are expected to survive changes in administration. The same formal rule can produce different behavior depending on what people believe about its future.

Expectations are not free-floating opinions. They are often earned histories.

When a government repeatedly rescues institutions that take certain risks, firms can rationally incorporate rescue probabilities into future decisions. When tax provisions are routinely extended just before expiration, taxpayers learn something about the meaning of an expiration date. When enforcement arrives in bursts and then fades, regulated actors learn the rhythm. When a benefit has survived attacks for a generation, households may plan around it differently than around a pilot program authorized for two years.

The second form of memory is investment.

Rules cause people to build things.

A tax credit for a technology can encourage factories, supplier networks, training programs, financing structures, and political associations. A zoning rule can shape the physical form of a city for decades after the politics that produced it has changed. A reimbursement formula can encourage hospitals to buy particular equipment and hire particular specialists. Once those investments exist, changing the rule has effects that cannot be inferred from the world before the investment.

The old policy leaves capital behind.

Sometimes that capital is physical. Sometimes it is organizational. An industry association created to obtain a subsidy can survive after the subsidy changes. A bureaucracy built to administer a program acquires routines, data, constituencies, and professional identities. A compliance department created under one regulatory regime may become an advocate for predictable rules even if the firm originally opposed regulation.

The third form of memory is distribution.

Policies create winners and losers, but those categories are not merely accounting outcomes. They can become political identities and organizational resources.

A rule that transfers a small amount from millions of people to a concentrated industry may create intense defense by the beneficiaries and almost no organized opposition among those paying the diffuse cost. Another policy may do the reverse: a highly visible burden on a narrow group can mobilize resistance even when benefits are broad. The politics of the next round depends on who can see the stakes, who can organize, and who has acquired the resources to stay in the game.

The fourth form is interpretation.

People learn from institutions not only what they receive but what kind of relationship they have with the state. A program can communicate inclusion or suspicion. An application process can make a benefit feel like a right, a transaction, a favor, or an interrogation. Police practices can teach communities whether contact with government is likely to be protective or punitive. Tax administration can make compliance feel routine or adversarial.

These experiences affect later behavior in ways a fiscal ledger will not show.

The fifth form is path dependence.

Paul Pierson argued that political processes can exhibit increasing returns: once a particular institutional path is taken, the benefits of staying on it or the costs of leaving it can grow. Timing and sequence then matter. Early events that might have been reversible at low cost can become difficult to undo after organizations, expectations, legal arrangements, and investments accumulate around them.

Path dependence is often abused as a fancy way to say that history matters. The stronger claim is narrower. A process is path dependent when earlier steps change the relative attractiveness or feasibility of later choices. The sequence helps produce the option set.

A keyboard layout is a familiar technological analogy, though its history is more complicated than the textbook story suggests. In policy, the mechanism can be clearer. Build a pension system around payroll contributions and decades of workers acquire contribution histories, employers build reporting systems, agencies develop records, and retirees structure household finances around promised payments. A later reform is not choosing among the same alternatives that existed at the program's birth. It is choosing among alternatives after a society has invested in one of them.

This does not mean reform is impossible. It means the cost function has changed.

The distinction is important because the rhetoric of lock-in can become fatalistic. Institutions that appear entrenched do change. Benefits can be cut. Regulations can be repealed. Tax systems can be redesigned. Political coalitions fracture. Technologies make old administrative arrangements obsolete. Crises can compress decades of institutional change into months.

Memory constrains politics; it does not determine it.

A better image is a game whose board is altered by prior moves.

Chess has memory in a weak sense: the current position contains the consequences of everything that happened before. Public policy has a stronger kind. The players themselves can change. They can learn the rules, form coalitions, acquire resources, leave the game, enter it, change what they value, and alter the institution that defines legal moves.

This is why apparently successful policies can undermine the evidence for their own continuation.

Suppose a safety regulation sharply reduces a hazard. Twenty years later, the original danger is rare. A critic can point to the low current incidence and argue that the rule is unnecessary. The policy has helped erase the observable world that justified it.

The opposite can happen too. A subsidy meant to establish an infant industry may succeed so thoroughly that the industry becomes a powerful political actor capable of defending the subsidy long after the original rationale has weakened. Success produces a constituency for continuation.

In one case, success makes the policy look unnecessary. In the other, success makes repeal harder.

Both are second-order effects.

Public health repeatedly encounters the first pattern. Prevention changes the prevalence of the thing being prevented. Infrastructure does as well. A flood barrier that works for decades can make the avoided floods politically abstract. Maintenance begins to look like spending on nothing happening. The evidence of success is absence.

This creates a peculiar epistemic problem for democratic government. Voters and legislators must judge policies partly by counterfactuals they cannot directly observe. How many bank failures did capital rules prevent? How many infections did vaccination avert? How much inflation did credible monetary restraint suppress? How many industrial accidents did a safety standard stop?

The better the intervention, the less visible some of its benefits may become.

But the reverse danger is equally serious. Once policy makers embrace counterfactual reasoning, almost any program can be defended by claiming invisible disasters avoided. The absence of failure cannot itself prove success.

That is why second-order policy analysis needs more than stories about feedback. It needs designs that preserve comparison.

Randomized trials can sometimes help, especially for bounded interventions. Natural experiments, phased rollouts, threshold rules, and credible comparison groups can recover causal information that before-and-after stories cannot. Administrative data can reveal heterogeneous effects. Sunset clauses can force reconsideration, though they can also create uncertainty that changes behavior before the sunset arrives. Pilot programs can generate evidence before institutions become deeply invested.

None of these methods abolishes adaptation.

A pilot can produce different behavior precisely because participants know it is a pilot. A randomized program can create spillovers into untreated groups. A temporary rule can generate temporary responses. A policy tested at small scale may change prices, wages, congestion, or political organization when expanded. The experiment enters the world too.

The practical question becomes: what is stable enough to transport?

Lucas's critique was one answer in macroeconomics. If behavior depends on policy rules, policy analysis should model the decision structure that generates behavior rather than treating historical correlations as invariant. The ambition was to find parameters more robust to changes in regime.

That ambition is useful even outside the particular macroeconomic program built around it. We want to know which relationships are likely to survive intervention and which are artifacts of the old rules.

Yet there is a danger in taking the critique too far. People are adaptive, but they are not infinitely strategic. Institutions are remembered, but imperfectly. Many policies change at the margin without causing everyone to recompute an optimal lifetime plan. Habits, confusion, bounded attention, social norms, administrative friction, and simple inertia can make behavior more stable than a fully strategic model predicts.

Policy feedback research adds another complication: adaptation is not limited to optimizing individuals. Rules change organizations and identities. They alter who has expertise, who has money, who meets whom, and which categories become politically salient. A model that perfectly represents household expectations but ignores the creation of a trade association can still miss the next round.

The unit of adaptation is often the institution itself.

Consider environmental regulation. A new emissions rule may initially be evaluated as a cost imposed on firms for a public benefit. Over time, firms invent compliance technologies. Engineers learn new methods. Suppliers specialize. Some incumbents discover that the regulation raises barriers to entry and begin defending parts of it. Environmental groups learn which provisions are enforceable. Regulators accumulate data. Courts create precedent. Investors price regulatory risk.

A decade later, repeal is not simply the negative of enactment.

The regulated world has changed.

This asymmetry is easy to miss in political argument. If a policy cost ten billion dollars to introduce, opponents may assume repeal returns ten billion dollars. But some compliance investments are sunk. Some new technologies are cheaper than the old ones. Some organizations now depend on the rule. Some behavioral changes persist without enforcement. Other effects vanish immediately.

The path back is not necessarily the path in reverse.

That is one reason policy debates conducted entirely through static incidence tables can be misleading. Static tables are often necessary. Someone should know who pays and who receives under the law as written. But a policy with meaningful duration also changes the future incidence table.

Taxes alter portfolios and corporate structures. School funding formulas alter where families live and how districts organize. Housing rules alter what gets built, which changes prices and political constituencies. Immigration rules alter networks that influence later migration. Procurement rules create vendor ecosystems. Patent rules influence research strategies. Insurance mandates change both coverage and the markets supplying it.

Every durable policy is partly an intervention in the next dataset.

The question is how much.

A useful discipline is to separate three levels.

At the first level, ask what the policy does holding behavior and institutions mostly fixed. This is the immediate mechanical effect. It is often the easiest to calculate and should not be dismissed merely because it is incomplete.

At the second level, ask how people and firms respond. They substitute, avoid, comply, invest, bargain, move, learn, or wait. Economics has spent generations developing tools for these responses.

At the third level, ask how those responses alter the political and institutional environment in which the policy will later be judged. Who gains capacity? What organizations form? Which expectations harden? Which categories become visible? What evidence disappears because the policy worked? What new evidence is generated because people learned the rule?

The third level is where policy becomes a game with memory.

It is also where prediction becomes most fragile.

The temptation is to answer fragility with ever more elaborate models. Sometimes that is appropriate. But no model can enumerate every political organization, technological complement, interpretive shift, or future coalition a rule might produce. The deeper response is institutional rather than computational.

Build policies that can learn.

That does not mean perpetual technocratic tinkering. Constant rule changes can destroy the very predictability people need to invest and plan. A government that revises policy every time a metric twitches creates its own instability. Learning requires enough persistence to observe effects and enough flexibility to respond when the world moves.

Those goals pull against each other.

A durable institution therefore needs memory of its own.

It needs records of why a policy was adopted, what alternatives were considered, what outcomes were expected, and which observations would count as failure. It needs data that survive leadership changes. It needs evaluation designs chosen before everyone knows which result would be politically convenient. It needs mechanisms for hearing from people who bear costs that aggregate metrics hide. It needs enough independence to report unwelcome findings and enough democratic legitimacy that revision is not simply delegated to experts.

Most of all, it needs to remember that the population it is measuring has already met the policy.

This changes how we should read history.

When an old program appears to have worked, we should not ask only whether its beneficiaries did better than some comparison group. We should ask what political capacities it created, what expectations it normalized, what investments it induced, and what later options it made easier or harder. When a reform failed, we should ask whether it failed because its first-order mechanism was wrong or because the response to the mechanism changed the environment.

And when we borrow an estimate from the past, we should ask a question that sounds almost embarrassingly simple: under what rules was this behavior observed?

The answer can determine whether the estimate is evidence or archaeology.

Policy makers do not get to move first forever. They announce a rule. People respond. Institutions accumulate consequences. The next policy arrives in a world shaped by the previous one.

The game remembers.
