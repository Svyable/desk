# Institutions That Can Be Surprised

The dangerous institution is not the one that makes a bad forecast. Every institution makes bad forecasts. The dangerous institution is the one that has arranged its procedures so that a bad forecast cannot become legible as a bad forecast.

By the time a model reaches an organization, it has usually acquired allies. A budget has been built around it. A team has learned how to operate it. A dashboard has turned its categories into familiar nouns. Managers have learned which number to cite in meetings. Frontline workers have learned which exceptions require explanation and which do not. What began as an estimate becomes, gradually and without ceremony, part of the institution's account of reality.

This is useful. Institutions cannot reopen every question every morning. A hospital needs protocols. A bank needs underwriting rules. A city needs a way to decide which bridge gets inspected first. An airline cannot treat each maintenance decision as a philosophical inquiry into the nature of risk. Procedures are compressed experience. Models are compressed evidence. Without compression, coordinated action becomes prohibitively expensive.

But compression has a price. The rule that makes ordinary cases manageable can make the extraordinary case harder to see.

The problem is deeper than stubbornness. An institution can employ intelligent, conscientious people and still become difficult to surprise because its information system decides in advance what counts as information. A risk score may determine which cases receive scrutiny. A performance metric may determine which outcomes reach senior management. A classification system may decide whether a new failure looks like a known problem or disappears into an "other" category nobody owns. The organization then learns from a world partly filtered by its own prior beliefs.

That is the institutional version of the second-order problem. A model changes action; action changes observation; observation changes the evidence available to revise the model. If the loop is tight enough, an institution can become very good at confirming the environment it has helped create.

The obvious remedy is to demand better prediction. Often that helps. It is not enough.

An institution that expects uncertainty needs something more awkward: channels through which reality can disagree with the system that is currently organizing reality.

Consider aviation. Commercial aviation is frequently invoked as a model of safety culture, sometimes so casually that the phrase loses its meaning. The useful part is not that aviation eliminated error. It did not. Nor is it that pilots and engineers became uniquely virtuous. The more interesting achievement was institutional: the industry developed multiple ways for anomalous events, near misses, equipment problems, and operational hazards to enter collective memory before they became a neat row in a performance dashboard.

In the United States, NASA has administered the Aviation Safety Reporting System since 1976. The system accepts voluntary safety reports from pilots, controllers, mechanics, cabin crew, dispatchers, and others. Its design is deliberately different from an enforcement pipeline. Reports are de-identified, and the program's confidentiality protections are intended to encourage people to describe hazards and mistakes that might otherwise remain hidden. The Federal Aviation Administration separately maintains mandatory occurrence and safety processes. The point is not that one reporting channel replaces another. It is that safety information arrives through channels with different incentives and different blind spots.

That plurality matters.

A mandatory system is good at collecting events that fit a reporting rule. A voluntary confidential system can catch events that a worker experienced as dangerous even when the event did not cleanly trigger the formal rule. Investigations can reconstruct severe failures in depth. Routine operational data can reveal rates and trends. None of these sources is a complete picture. Their disagreement is sometimes the useful part.

Near-miss reporting is especially important because outcomes are sparse and luck is noisy. If a runway incursion ends without collision, a database built only from fatalities records a successful day. The crew may have experienced something very different. An institution that learns only from realized losses throws away information about the mechanisms that nearly produced them.

This distinction recurs far outside aviation. A cybersecurity team that counts only successful intrusions ignores blocked attempts that reveal a changing adversary. A hospital that studies only deaths may miss repeated rescue events in which staff intervention prevented the same latent failure. A financial institution that measures credit risk only through realized defaults can misread a period in which refinancing, forbearance, or rising collateral values suppress the losses its underwriting choices helped create.

Outcome data are indispensable. They are also late.

The harder design question is how to give weak, inconvenient signals somewhere to go.

Organizations often say they want bad news early. Their internal economics can say the opposite. Escalating an anomaly consumes time, creates work, threatens a deadline, and may embarrass the team that owns the system. If most anomalies turn out to be harmless, the employee who keeps raising them can look less competent than the employee who keeps things moving. The institution may therefore select, quite rationally at the individual level, for silence.

This is one reason "speak up" is an incomplete safety policy. Voice depends on structure. Who receives the report? What happens to the reporter? Does anybody have authority to investigate? Can the issue be closed by the same chain of command whose performance is being questioned? Is there a record that allows repeated weak signals to accumulate? Does the organization distinguish an accusation from an observation?

The answers determine whether surprise is operationally possible.

High-reliability research has long emphasized a related idea: organizations facing hazardous, tightly coupled work need an unusual sensitivity to small failures and to the limits of simplification. The phrase can sound mystical when detached from practice. In practice it means refusing to let a clean aggregate erase a dirty local fact.

Suppose a hospital's deterioration model performs well across the patient population. Its calibration plot is respectable. Its alert burden has been tuned down after clinicians complained about fatigue. Then nurses on one ward begin overriding it for a particular class of postoperative patients.

There are several possible explanations. The nurses may be overreacting. The ward may have an unusual patient mix. The model may be missing a variable. The surgical protocol may have changed. The overrides may be correct for reasons nobody has yet measured. Or the apparent cluster may be random.

A brittle institution asks whether the model is validated and, if the answer is yes, treats the overrides as noncompliance.

A credulous institution assumes frontline intuition is superior and abandons the model.

An institution capable of surprise preserves the disagreement long enough to learn from it.

That requires keeping some data the decision rule did not ask for. It requires logging overrides rather than merely preventing them. It may require reviewing cases selected for reasons other than model score. It may require a temporary audit sample drawn at random, because a system that investigates only flagged cases cannot discover what its flagging rule systematically misses.

Randomness is underrated as an institutional sense organ.

We usually think of randomization as a tool for causal inference. It is also a defense against selective attention. A lender can audit a random sample of approvals and denials. A content platform can inspect a random sample of recommendations rather than only items reported by users or detected by classifiers. A regulator can inspect some facilities that do not look suspicious. The sample will often seem inefficient because many cases will be ordinary. That inefficiency buys a view outside the targeting system.

The same principle appears in quality control. If every inspection is allocated according to predicted risk, the institution becomes increasingly knowledgeable about high-scoring cases and comparatively ignorant about the rest. Some targeted inspection is sensible; pure targeting can make the map self-sealing. A modest exploration budget keeps open the possibility that the risk model is wrong about where risk lives.

Machine learning has a name for a related tradeoff: exploration versus exploitation. Institutions have faced it much longer than machine learning has. Exploitation uses what the organization currently believes to allocate scarce attention. Exploration spends some attention learning whether those beliefs remain warranted.

The balance is not fixed. When stakes are high and the environment stable, aggressive exploitation may be appropriate. When a system is new, incentives are changing, adversaries are adapting, or the cost of a blind spot is catastrophic, exploration becomes more valuable. The important point is not that every organization should randomize ten percent of everything. It is that a system with no mechanism for looking where it does not expect to find trouble has made an epistemic bet it may not realize it made.

Surprise also requires preserving dissent without romanticizing it.

Dissenters can be wrong. Whistleblowers can misunderstand. Frontline workers can defend outdated routines. Experts can become attached to rare edge cases and underweight population evidence. An institution that treats every objection as revelation becomes paralyzed.

The alternative to suppressing dissent is not obeying dissent. It is making disagreement inspectable.

This distinction matters because organizations often confuse authority with epistemology. Somebody must decide. That does not mean the decision maker must also determine which evidence remains visible after the decision. A chief risk officer may approve the model while an independent validation group records unresolved limitations. A medical director may retain a protocol while a safety committee tracks exceptions. A public agency may use an allocation formula while an audit office examines who falls outside its categories.

The institutional design is doing two jobs at once: enabling action now and preserving the possibility of correction later.

Those jobs pull against each other.

Action rewards closure. Learning rewards residual uncertainty.

A meeting ends more cleanly when the slide says the model works. A decision memo is easier to defend when one estimate is presented rather than three. A budget is easier to allocate when a category has a stable definition. But every unresolved caveat that gets polished away makes the institution slightly less capable of recognizing the condition under which its policy should change.

This is why precommitment can help. Before a model is deployed, an institution can specify what evidence would trigger review. A calibration drift threshold. A rise in overrides. A disparity that persists after case-mix adjustment. A new failure mode. A change in the process generating the input data. A sustained gap between predicted and realized outcomes.

The exact trigger matters less than the act of naming one before the evidence becomes politically inconvenient.

A falsification condition turns "we will keep monitoring" into something closer to an institutional promise. It does not remove judgment. Thresholds can be noisy; environments can change in ways the designers did not anticipate. But a precommitted review rule makes it harder to reinterpret every adverse signal as an exception after the fact.

The same logic supports sunset clauses and scheduled reauthorization. A rule that must periodically earn continuation is easier to revise than a rule whose continued existence is the default. This is not always desirable. Some institutions need stability precisely because constant reopening invites capture or short-term pressure. Constitutional rights should not expire every quarter because a dashboard moved. Neither should safety standards be casually weakened by the latest noisy measurement.

Revisability is not the same as impermanence.

A good institution distinguishes between commitments that should be hard to change and models that should remain empirically vulnerable. It can be constitutionally stubborn about values while being technically curious about means.

That distinction becomes essential when metrics are attached to incentives. Once careers, budgets, or legitimacy depend on a number, evidence about the number is no longer neutral. A department whose funding depends on reducing a measured backlog has reason to prefer definitions that make the backlog fall. A platform team evaluated on engagement has reason to interpret rising engagement as success. A regulator rewarded for enforcement counts may become less interested in whether enforcement changed underlying harm.

The people inside these systems need not be corrupt. They are responding to the institution's theory of success.

Independent measurement can therefore matter as much as independent review. If the same unit sets the target, produces the data, evaluates performance, and explains anomalies, the feedback loop has no external friction. Separating some of those functions creates disagreement by design.

There is a cost. Independent auditors can become bureaucratic. External reviewers can lack context. Redundant measurement can waste money. Multiple data systems can produce endless reconciliation work. The cure for institutional blindness can become an institution devoted to paperwork.

That counterevidence should be taken seriously because redundancy is not free.

Charles Perrow's work on complex systems made famous the idea that some accidents arise not from a single incompetent actor but from interactions among tightly coupled components. Later safety practice has often responded by adding defenses, checks, and layers. Yet layers themselves can interact. A second approval can diffuse responsibility rather than strengthen it. A warning system can create alert fatigue. A backup can encourage riskier primary operation. An audit can become ceremonial once everyone learns its checklist.

There is no institutional architecture outside the second order. The safeguard changes behavior too.

This is why the goal cannot be "add oversight" in the abstract. Oversight must itself remain observable. Does the confidential reporting system produce actionable information? Are reports investigated? Do random audits find materially different problems from targeted audits? Are review triggers ever allowed to trigger? Does the independent validator have incentives to discover weaknesses, or merely to document compliance? Do people route around the safeguard once it becomes burdensome?

The institution needs feedback on its feedback.

That phrase can produce an infinite regress if taken literally. It should not. At some point a decision has to be made and work has to continue. The practical aim is smaller: prevent any single representation of the world from becoming the only channel through which the world can answer back.

Several design habits follow.

Keep raw observations when feasible, not only transformed scores. Preserve reasons for overrides. Maintain at least one evidence stream that is not selected entirely by the model under evaluation. Give anomalies a durable place to accumulate. Separate some measurement from the incentives attached to the measure. Specify review conditions before deployment. Let people report weak signals without requiring them to prove the entire causal story. Revisit classifications when too much important material is being forced into "other."

None of these guarantees wisdom.

The deeper requirement is cultural but not merely cultural: an institution must be able to experience contradiction without immediately converting it into either disobedience or noise.

This is harder than celebrating failure in a corporate slogan. Real contradiction is irritating. It arrives at the wrong time. It comes from people who may be difficult. It threatens sunk costs. It can be statistically ambiguous. It can look exactly like the random variation a competent system is supposed to ignore.

The Challenger disaster is often invoked in discussions of organizational learning, sometimes with more confidence than the history warrants. The accident involved technical evidence about O-ring performance in cold temperatures, contractor and NASA decision processes, schedule pressure, and communication failures that were reconstructed in painful detail afterward. It is tempting to turn the event into a simple morality play in which managers ignored engineers. The record is more complicated, and reducing it to a slogan repeats the error this chapter is concerned with: compressing a messy warning history into a story that is easy to remember.

The durable lesson is narrower. Organizations do not receive warnings in the form of future accident reports. They receive incomplete evidence, disputed interpretations, weak signals, and people arguing about whether the situation is actually unusual.

An institution that can be surprised must function in that interval.

After catastrophe, anomaly is obvious. Before catastrophe, anomaly competes with normal variation.

That is why retrospective accountability, though necessary, cannot substitute for prospective curiosity. Firing the person who made the wrong call may satisfy a demand for responsibility. It does not tell the institution whether the next weak signal will travel farther. A new dashboard may demonstrate action. It does not tell anyone whether the dashboard's categories will contain the next problem.

The relevant capability is not clairvoyance. It is permeability.

Can evidence cross organizational boundaries? Can a fact discovered at the edge alter a belief held at the center? Can the system distinguish a model's confidence from the institution's confidence in the model? Can a decision be reversed without requiring everyone who supported it to confess incompetence?

That last question matters more than it appears.

Organizations learn socially. If changing a policy is framed as proof that its authors failed, authors will defend policies longer. If revision can instead be treated as an expected response to new evidence, the political cost of learning falls. This does not mean eliminating accountability. Negligence, concealment, and reckless decisions still require consequences. But an institution that punishes every revision as an admission of error teaches people to disguise revision as continuity.

Language becomes part of the control system.

So does memory.

Institutions often preserve decisions better than they preserve the assumptions behind decisions. Years later, a threshold remains in software after the population that justified it has changed. A reporting category survives because downstream systems depend on it. A risk appetite statement is copied forward. Nobody is certain which empirical claim originally made the rule sensible.

Decision records can help if they capture not only what was chosen but why: the evidence available, the uncertainties acknowledged, the alternatives rejected, and the conditions under which the choice should be reconsidered. Such records are not glamorous. They are a way of allowing a future institution to disagree with its past without first having to reconstruct the past from artifacts.

Memory without reasons becomes tradition. Reasons without memory become folklore.

The second-order institution needs both because its own actions alter the environment its future members inherit.

A successful intervention can even erase the evidence that made the intervention necessary. When prevention works, the bad outcome does not occur. Later observers may conclude the precaution was excessive. Public health repeatedly encounters this structure. So does infrastructure maintenance. So does financial regulation after a long quiet period. The absence of disaster can be evidence of low risk, or evidence that the control is working. Outcome data alone cannot always distinguish them.

This is another reason institutional memory matters. The organization must retain some account of the counterfactual it was trying to prevent.

But memory can also become dogma. An old disaster can dominate attention long after the relevant technology changes. A risk control built for one failure mode can block adaptation to another. Institutions can overlearn.

Surprise therefore requires forgetting, too—not amnesia, but the ability to demote an old lesson when new evidence makes it less useful.

The tension cannot be solved by a universal rule. Preserve history, but do not worship it. Trust expertise, but record dissent. Exploit what the model knows, but spend some attention where it predicts nothing interesting. Standardize enough to coordinate, but leave paths for anomalies that do not fit the standard. Make decisions, but retain the evidence that could make tomorrow's decision different.

These are not elegant instructions. That is partly the point.

The systems described throughout this book fail when a representation becomes too successful at organizing behavior. A metric becomes the mission. A forecast becomes a trading input. A ranking changes applicants. A test reshapes instruction. A risk model changes treatment. A recommendation system changes the preferences it later measures. An institution becomes fragile when its model of the world gains the additional power to decide which disagreements with the model deserve to be seen.

The answer is not permanent skepticism. Institutions that refuse to commit cannot build bridges, run hospitals, allocate credit, or govern. The answer is to make commitment and revisability coexist.

Act with the best model available. Keep enough of the world outside it.

The institution that can be surprised is not the institution without beliefs. It is the institution whose beliefs have not acquired a monopoly on evidence.
