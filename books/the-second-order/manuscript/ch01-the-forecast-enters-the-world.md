# The Forecast Enters the World

A forecast looks passive when it is sitting on a page.

A number in a table appears to describe something outside itself: next quarter's inflation, the probability that a borrower will default, the chance that a patient will be readmitted, the ranking of a school, the expected return on an asset. We are accustomed to treating such numbers as windows. Some are clearer than others, some are distorted, but the world is presumed to be on one side of the glass and the model on the other.

Then somebody acts on the number.

A central bank tightens policy because inflation is expected to rise. A lender declines applicants whose predicted default risk is high. A hospital assigns extra attention to patients a model labels fragile. A university changes admissions practices after a ranking methodology shifts. An investor buys because a forecast says other investors are likely to buy. The number has crossed the glass. It is no longer only an account of the world. It has become one of the causes operating inside it.

That crossing is the subject of this book.

The first-order question is familiar: is the model right? The second-order question begins a moment later: what happens because people believe it, fear it, optimize against it, route money through it, or redesign their behavior around it?

The distinction sounds academic until a model becomes important enough to matter. Then it is everywhere.

In the mid-1970s, economists and policymakers were arguing intensely about how governments should manage inflation, unemployment, money, and demand. The period is useful not because every modern problem resembles monetary policy, but because several thinkers were forced to confront the same uncomfortable fact at roughly the same time. Relationships observed under one set of rules could change when the rules changed. Measures that had been useful for understanding a system could become less useful once institutions tried to control the system by targeting those measures. People were not molecules. They watched policy, formed expectations, shifted assets, changed contracts, and adapted.

Charles Goodhart was working inside the Bank of England during those years, in the middle of debates over monetary targets and the practical meaning of monetary aggregates. The Bank's archive from late 1975 and early 1976 preserves the texture of that argument: notes about money-supply targets, monetary aims, policy instruments, and what one file calls “Targetry.” These were not philosophical exercises. Officials were trying to decide which quantities could serve as handles on an economy while the economy itself kept changing its grip.

A monetary aggregate seems, at first, like a thing that can simply be measured. Count the relevant forms of money, watch how quickly the total expands, and use the result as evidence about financial conditions. But the category is partly institutional. If regulation, technology, interest rates, or bank behavior make one type of account less attractive, people can move toward another. A boundary that once separated economically distinct forms of money may become porous. The act of controlling one aggregate can increase the incentive to route activity around it.

The problem is deeper than clever evasion. There does not have to be a villain gaming the measure. Adaptation can be ordinary and rational. A bank changes its funding because rules change. A household moves savings because yields change. A firm alters financing because one instrument becomes expensive relative to another. The measured relationship moves because the environment that produced it has moved.

This is why the popular summary of Goodhart's law—when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure—is useful but incomplete. The memorable line compresses several mechanisms into one warning. Sometimes people manipulate the measure directly. Sometimes they substitute around it. Sometimes the institution improves the measured outcome without improving the underlying objective. Sometimes the old correlation simply breaks because the policy regime has altered incentives. The danger is not measurement itself. The danger is believing that a relationship discovered while a measure was merely descriptive will remain unchanged after large consequences are attached to it.

The same decade produced a related argument from Robert Lucas. In his 1976 paper “Econometric Policy Evaluation: A Critique,” Lucas challenged a common way of using large econometric models to compare proposed policies. If a model estimated how households and firms had behaved under one policy regime, policymakers could not safely assume those behavioral relationships would remain fixed after policy changed. People form expectations about rules. Change the rules and they may change their decisions. A coefficient that looked stable in historical data could be partly a record of the old policy environment rather than a timeless feature of human behavior.

The point was not that prediction is impossible. It was that policy is an intervention in the process generating the data.

Suppose a city observes that commuters tend to leave home at a certain time, uses that pattern to set a toll, and announces that the toll will be dramatically higher during the predicted peak. The historical traffic pattern is no longer a neutral input. Some drivers will leave earlier. Some will leave later. Some will take transit. Some will work from home. The policy may be successful precisely because the forecast becomes wrong.

This is an odd result if accuracy is the only thing we know how to reward. The city's model could predict a peak at 8:15, provoke enough adaptation to flatten that peak, and then look mistaken when evaluated against the world it helped create. A naïve scorekeeper might conclude that the model failed. A better account would ask whether the prediction was accurate conditional on no intervention, whether the intervention changed behavior in the intended direction, and whether the resulting distribution of costs and benefits was acceptable.

Once prediction is linked to action, there are at least two worlds hiding inside every evaluation: the world in which the forecast is ignored and the world in which it is used.

We rarely get to observe both.

This is why second-order systems are so easy to misunderstand. We often evaluate them with first-order evidence. We ask whether people labeled high-risk later had bad outcomes, without asking what additional scrutiny the label triggered. We ask whether a forecast matched the final number, without asking whether the forecast changed policy before the final number arrived. We ask whether a recommendation predicted preference, even though the recommendation itself helped shape what the person encountered, learned, and came to prefer.

The model enters the causal chain and then leaves fingerprints on the evidence used to judge it.

Donald Campbell, the social scientist best known for work on research design and program evaluation, approached the problem from another direction. In a 1976 paper on assessing planned social change, he examined the difficulties that arise when quantitative indicators become consequential in public administration. The broad lesson later associated with Campbell's law is not that indicators are corrupting by nature. It is that the more an indicator is used for social decision-making, the more pressure there is to alter behavior in ways that improve the indicator, including ways that can distort the process it was intended to monitor.

Imagine a school system that wants children to learn more mathematics. It needs evidence. Standardized tests are one possible instrument because they create comparable observations across classrooms and years. If the test has no consequences, teachers and students have relatively little reason to reorganize their behavior around the exact scoring rule. The scores may be noisy, incomplete measures of mathematical understanding, but they can still contain useful information.

Now attach graduation, teacher evaluation, school funding, public rankings, and political careers to the result.

Nothing about the arithmetic of the score has changed. Everything about the institution around it has.

Teachers may rationally spend more time on tested material. Administrators may direct resources toward students near proficiency thresholds. Families with means may buy targeted preparation. Schools may become more careful about which students sit for which exams. Some of these responses may improve learning. Some may improve scores without much transfer beyond the test. Some may be outright manipulation. Most will live in the uncomfortable middle: behavior that is understandable under the incentives and difficult to classify from the score alone.

The test has become part of the curriculum even if nobody intended it to.

This does not make tests useless. The opposite conclusion is too easy. Measurement can reveal neglect, inequality, improvement, and failure that would otherwise remain hidden behind anecdotes or institutional self-protection. A hospital without outcome measures can tell itself comforting stories. A police department without records can hide patterns. A company without metrics can promote the manager with the best presentation. The alternative to a flawed metric is often not wisdom. It is unmeasured discretion.

The interesting problem begins after we reject both extremes: metrics are neither transparent truth nor inherently corrupting. They are instruments placed inside adaptive systems.

An instrument changes the environment most when three conditions coincide. People can detect, directly or indirectly, what is being rewarded. They have some ability to alter behavior in response. And the measured proxy is not identical to the underlying objective.

Those conditions are common because objectives are usually difficult to measure precisely. If the true objective were easy to observe, the proxy would be unnecessary.

A company wants durable customer value but can measure monthly engagement. A university wants intellectual development but can count citations, completion rates, salaries, and selectivity. A government wants public safety but can count arrests, reported crimes, response times, and clearance rates. A hospital wants health but is reimbursed through coded procedures and measurable outcomes. A research lab wants discovery but can count papers, grants, benchmarks, and patents. Each institution is trying to steer by quantities that are related to what it cares about without being the thing itself.

The gap between objective and proxy is where the second order lives.

Sometimes the response to a metric is exactly what its designer wanted. A factory posts a defect rate, managers investigate the sources of defects, workers improve a process, and the defect rate falls because the products really are better. A safety checklist changes behavior by design. A tax on pollution is meant to make firms alter production. A congestion charge is successful if commuters respond. In such cases, behavior changing because of a measure is not a pathology. It is the mechanism.

The challenge is that the same feedback can make the measure harder to interpret.

If a lender uses a risk model to deny credit to applicants predicted to default, the institution will never observe how many rejected applicants would actually have repaid. The training data produced by the policy contains repayment outcomes mainly for people who were approved. If future models are trained on that selective record without care, the system can begin learning from outcomes created by its own earlier decisions.

If a hospital gives intensive follow-up to patients classified as likely to be readmitted, those patients may be less likely to return. A crude retrospective analysis could make the model look poorly calibrated precisely where the intervention worked. If clinicians learn that a risk score triggers scarce resources, they may also change how they document borderline cases. The score is now entangled with treatment, documentation, and resource allocation.

If a trading strategy identifies an apparent price discrepancy and enough capital follows the signal, the discrepancy may disappear. The model's success destroys the opportunity it recognized. If the strategy becomes crowded, a relationship that once produced excess returns can reverse when many participants attempt the same trade at once. Prediction becomes pressure.

These are not identical problems. Grouping them carelessly would produce a vague theory in which everything is “reflexive” and therefore nothing can be analyzed. The mechanisms matter.

One mechanism is gaming: actors deliberately manipulate a proxy while avoiding the underlying objective. Another is substitution: activity moves into categories the metric does not capture. Another is treatment response: the prediction triggers an intervention that changes the predicted outcome. Another is equilibrium response: many people change behavior, producing a new system-level pattern. Another is selection: decisions based on the model determine which outcomes become observable. Another is learning: people infer the rule over time and become better at responding to it. Another is performativity: the model supplies a language or procedure that participants begin to use, helping organize the world into something more like the model.

A good second-order analysis has to ask which of these is happening rather than reaching for a single slogan.

There is also a question of time.

A model can look excellent immediately after deployment because nobody has adapted to it yet. Its first users behave much like the historical population on which it was trained. Then the institution reorganizes. Employees learn which cases are rewarded. Competitors imitate successful tactics. Consultants publish playbooks. Software vendors build tools around the scoring rule. Applicants learn what the system prefers. The environment becomes increasingly endogenous to the model.

The half-life of an insight can depend on how valuable the insight is.

This is obvious in financial markets, where profitable public information tends to attract attention and capital. It is less obvious in bureaucracies, where the adaptation may take years. A hospital coding rule, school accountability system, university ranking, or corporate key performance indicator can slowly reorganize work until the institution is producing exactly the data it was told to produce.

That can feel like success from the dashboard.

The second-order question is whether the underlying mission moved with it.

The temptation at this point is to become suspicious of all explicit rules. That would be a mistake. Hidden rules are still rules. If a hiring manager claims to use holistic judgment, applicants will still infer patterns from who gets hired. If a grant committee refuses to publish criteria, researchers will still learn what kinds of proposals tend to win. If a platform hides its ranking algorithm, creators will experiment until they discover regularities. Opacity can slow adaptation, but it does not abolish incentives. It can instead concentrate knowledge among insiders who have enough experience to reverse-engineer the system.

The choice is rarely between a world with metrics and a world without them. It is between different architectures of measurement, discretion, feedback, and revision.

That is why the Lucas critique remains useful far outside the specific macroeconomic models Lucas was criticizing. Its strongest general lesson is modest: do not treat behavioral relationships as invariant when the policy regime that helps produce them is changing. It does not follow that every parameter is unstable, that structural modeling solves everything, or that policymakers can never learn from historical correlations. Some relationships survive interventions better than others. Some policies are too small to provoke meaningful adaptation. Some actors do not know the rule or cannot respond to it. Institutions are full of friction.

Those frictions are often what make prediction possible.

A weather forecast can be acted upon without changing the hurricane. An astronomer's prediction does not alter an eclipse. A bridge does not study the engineering model and redistribute its load out of strategic spite. Much of classical science became powerful by studying systems that do not change their governing behavior merely because they have been measured.

Social and economic systems are different not because humans are magical, but because information is one of their inputs.

Tell people tomorrow's weather and they carry umbrellas. Tell investors tomorrow's price and there may be no tomorrow's price of that kind. Tell students which questions determine admission and preparation shifts toward those questions. Tell a company which metric determines bonuses and the metric acquires a workforce.

Machine learning makes this issue more important, not less.

For much of the history of statistics, a model could be built, published, and used by a relatively small number of specialists. Modern predictive systems can sit in the path of millions of decisions every day. They rank feeds, route deliveries, screen transactions, suggest sentences, price advertisements, identify fraud, prioritize leads, recommend music, flag accounts, and shape which pieces of the world become visible to whom.

At that scale, a model is an environment.

A recommendation system does not merely estimate which videos people might enjoy. It chooses part of the sequence from which future preferences and behaviors emerge. The next training set contains traces of prior recommendations. A fraud model changes which transactions are blocked, which teaches criminals where friction exists and alters which fraudulent attempts succeed. An automated hiring filter changes who receives interviews, which changes the observed pool of successful employees later available for analysis. A generative model trained partly on material produced in response to earlier models enters an even tighter loop: machine output can become part of the environment from which later machines learn.

None of this proves that feedback loops are necessarily harmful. A navigation app reroutes drivers because of observed congestion; drivers respond; the system updates again. The feedback can make the whole network work better. Online marketplaces use reputation systems that change seller behavior precisely because ratings matter. Spam filters improve, spammers adapt, filters improve again. The moving target is difficult, but the movement is sometimes the point.

The mistake is assuming stationarity where there is strategy.

Stationarity is a technical word for a simple comfort: the process generating observations remains similar enough over time that yesterday can teach us about tomorrow. Many useful statistical methods depend, explicitly or quietly, on some version of that continuity. But consequential models can spend their own accuracy. They identify regularities, institutions act on those regularities, people adapt, and the regularity weakens or changes sign.

The better the model is at finding exploitable structure, the more pressure it can place on that structure once deployed.

This creates an inversion that appears throughout the chapters ahead. Prediction quality before deployment and policy quality after deployment are not the same thing. A model can be beautifully calibrated and attached to a terrible decision rule. A rough model can support a useful intervention. A measure can become less predictive because the institution successfully prevented the outcome. Another can remain highly predictive because the institution has accidentally reinforced the condition it was measuring.

We need separate scoreboards.

One scoreboard asks whether the model represented the world well at the moment of observation. Another asks whether the decision rule built around it improved the objective. A third asks how the system adapted after deployment. A fourth asks who bore the cost of that adaptation. These questions overlap, but collapsing them into a single accuracy number invites confusion.

Consider a simple risk score used to decide who receives additional inspection. If inspectors find more violations among high-scoring cases, the model may appear validated. But inspection itself determines which violations are discovered. The low-scoring group receives less scrutiny, so its hidden violation rate is less observable. More enforcement in one place creates more recorded evidence there, which can justify more enforcement in the next round. The system has begun producing the distribution it claims merely to detect.

There are technical ways to reason about this problem: randomized audits, exploration policies, causal inference, counterfactual estimation, holdout groups, policy experiments. We will get to them. But the conceptual move comes first. Data collected after a consequential model is deployed is not innocent simply because it is empirical.

It is history written under a policy.

This is also why resistance and gaming deserve more sympathy than institutions usually give them. Gaming is often evidence that a rule has become legible. If employees find a way to hit a call-center target while making customers miserable, the immediate response may be to punish the employees or add another metric. Sometimes that is appropriate. But the gaming also reveals information about the mismatch between the target and the mission. It is a form of adversarial testing performed by people whose livelihoods are attached to the score.

The useful question is not merely, “How do we stop them from gaming this?” It is, “What did their response teach us about what this measure actually rewards?”

A resilient metric is not one nobody can influence. If nobody can influence it, it may be useless for management. A resilient metric is one for which the easiest ways to improve the score are reasonably aligned with improving the underlying objective, and for which obvious forms of substitution or manipulation are visible enough to trigger revision.

That is a design problem rather than a morality play.

It also changes the ideal relationship between institutions and surprise. Organizations often build models because they want fewer surprises. A credit model narrows uncertainty. A forecast turns a range of possible futures into a distribution. A ranking compresses many dimensions into an ordered list. A dashboard tells an executive where to look. The compression is useful. Nobody can run a complex institution while reconsidering the whole world from first principles each morning.

But compression becomes dangerous when the institution loses the ability to notice what the model has excluded.

The strongest systems keep some channel open to evidence that does not fit. They audit low-risk cases occasionally. They compare measured performance with qualitative complaints. They rotate metrics. They examine residuals rather than only averages. They preserve human escalation for cases that violate the model's categories. They treat unexplained drift as information rather than an inconvenience to be smoothed away.

In other words, they expect the second order.

They assume that a rule will teach people how to respond to it. They assume that successful interventions can invalidate old forecasts. They assume competitors will learn. They assume categories will leak. They assume the world will occasionally use their own model as an input.

This stance is less comfortable than believing in a permanent scoring rule, but it is not anti-model. It demands better models and better institutions around them.

The distinction matters because we are moving toward a world in which models do not merely advise decision-makers. They increasingly execute decisions, communicate with other models, adapt from feedback, and operate fast enough that a human may see only the aggregate result. An automated system can change a price, observe the response, update a prediction, and change the price again before a committee could finish discussing the first move. The loop becomes shorter. The opportunity for second-order effects becomes larger.

The old warning about a measure becoming a target now has machine speed.

Yet the old escape remains available too: observe the response.

A reactive world is harder to model, but its reactions are themselves data. The people shifting around a rule are telling you where the rule bites. The market erasing an arbitrage is telling you the information has been absorbed. The school teaching to the test is telling you which parts of the curriculum the accountability system has made valuable. The patient whose risk changes after treatment is telling you that prediction and intervention cannot be evaluated independently.

The model does not have to remain outside the world to be useful. It has to know, in some disciplined way, that it has entered it.

That is the move from first order to second.

Before asking what happens next, ask who can see the forecast. Ask what decisions are attached to it. Ask who can change behavior. Ask what remains unmeasured. Ask what evidence will disappear because the model acted on it. Ask which correlations depend on the old rule. Ask how long it will take people to learn the new one.

Then ask the question that will recur through this book: after the model changes the world, what will we mistake for proof that the model was right?
