# The Metric Eats the Mission

The trouble with a metric usually begins after everybody agrees it is useful.

At first, the number is a relief. An organization has been arguing in adjectives: service is slow, quality is slipping, customers are frustrated, research is improving, safety feels better. A metric gives the argument edges. Average response time is twelve minutes. Defects are 2.4 percent. Ninety-one percent of cases are closed within the target window. The number can be put on a chart, compared with last month, assigned to a manager, and discussed without everyone having to begin from personal impressions.

This is genuine progress.

Then the number becomes a target.

Bonuses depend on it. Promotions depend on it. Budgets depend on it. Public embarrassment depends on it. A regional director must explain it on Monday morning. A minister is asked about it in Parliament. A chief executive puts it on the first page of the operating review. Employees who once treated the measure as information now experience it as consequence.

The metric has not changed. Its meaning has.

The first chapter described what happens when a forecast enters the causal chain. A target is an even more direct intervention. It tells an organization which part of reality will be counted and, by implication, which part is most likely to matter when rewards and punishments arrive.

The organization listens.

That sentence can sound cynical, as if workers and managers are standing around waiting for an excuse to cheat. Most of the time the problem is more ordinary. People have limited hours. Departments have finite budgets. A nurse, engineer, teacher, salesperson, researcher, inspector, or civil servant cannot maximize every desirable property at once. Once a metric carries consequences, it helps settle arguments over where the next hour goes.

This is why badly designed metrics can damage a mission even when nobody lies.

In 1975, organizational scholar Steven Kerr published a paper with one of the durable titles in management research: “On the Folly of Rewarding A, While Hoping for B.” The title survives because it describes a mistake that can be recognized before any mathematics is introduced. Institutions routinely announce one objective while building a reward system around something easier to count.

They hope for cooperation and reward individual output. They hope for careful judgment and reward speed. They hope for long-term value and reward quarterly results. They hope for truthful risk reporting and punish the appearance of bad news. They hope for scientific discovery and reward a stack of visible publications. The reward system does not have to perfectly determine behavior to matter. It only has to tilt the local tradeoffs often enough.

A metric is powerful because work is full of those tradeoffs.

Imagine a maintenance team responsible for a fleet of machines. The mission is simple enough to say: keep the equipment useful, safe, and available over time. But usefulness, safety, availability, cost, speed, preventive maintenance, documentation, and staff development are different dimensions. Management chooses downtime because downtime is visible and expensive. The metric is reasonable. Nobody wants broken machines.

Now managers are evaluated almost entirely on reported downtime.

They can improve the number in several ways. They can fix underlying causes faster. They can carry better spare parts. They can schedule preventive work intelligently. Those are mission-aligned responses. They can also postpone maintenance that requires taking a machine offline. They can classify an outage as planned rather than unplanned if the accounting rule treats the categories differently. They can keep a marginal machine technically “available” while operators learn not to depend on it. They can cannibalize another machine to restore the one being counted. They can reduce training time because an experienced technician closes incidents faster today, even if the department becomes more fragile later.

Only some of these are cheating. Most are reallocations inside a multidimensional job.

That distinction matters because the obvious cure for cheating—better surveillance—does not solve the deeper problem. If the incentive contract overweights one measurable dimension, employees can respond sincerely and still pull effort away from dimensions that are harder to observe.

Economists Bengt Holmstrom and Paul Milgrom gave this problem a formal treatment in their 1991 work on multitask principal-agent relationships. The important intuition is accessible without the equations. When a worker is responsible for several tasks and some are easier to measure than others, strong incentives tied to the measurable tasks can distort the allocation of effort. If quantity is precisely measured while quality is difficult to verify, paying aggressively for quantity can make quality suffer. The weakness of the quality measure changes how strong the quantity incentive should be.

This is a subtle result because it runs against a common managerial instinct. If a measure is reliable, why not use it more heavily?

Because the relevant question is not only whether the measure is accurate. It is what strengthening the incentive around that measure does to the rest of the job.

A thermometer can be extremely accurate and still be a terrible basis for paying a doctor.

The metric problem therefore has at least two layers. One is measurement error: does the number represent what we think it represents? The other is incentive distortion: even if the number is measured perfectly, what behavior does rewarding it produce across the dimensions we are not measuring as well?

Organizations often notice the first layer and miss the second.

They clean the data. They standardize definitions. They improve dashboards. They audit reporting. These are useful. But a perfectly audited proxy remains a proxy.

This is where the mission starts to disappear behind its representation.

The sequence is familiar. A broad objective is difficult to manage, so leaders choose a tractable measure. The measure becomes a target because targets create accountability. The target becomes a performance contract because contracts need consequences. Employees reorganize work around the contract. Over time, the organization becomes unusually good at producing the measured outcome. Then leaders look at the improving metric and conclude that the organization is becoming unusually good at the mission.

Sometimes they are right.

Sometimes the metric has eaten the mission and taken its name.

The English National Health Service became an important case for researchers studying this problem in the 2000s. Gwyn Bevan and Christopher Hood examined the use of performance targets in English public health care and the opportunities for gaming that followed. Their 2006 paper was pointedly titled “What’s Measured Is What Matters.” The phrase should not be read as an argument against measuring health services. Health systems need waiting-time data, quality measures, mortality statistics, staffing information, and financial controls. The question was what happened when selected indicators became the basis of a high-pressure performance regime.

The attraction of targets in a public system is obvious. A government that promises shorter waits needs some way to know whether waits are actually shorter. Citizens should not have to accept “trust us” from a service with monopoly power. Targets can force neglected problems onto the agenda and give managers permission to reorganize complacent systems.

That is the strongest case for them, and it should not be caricatured.

A target can break an equilibrium in which everybody knows performance is poor but nobody bears a specific cost for fixing it. Public reporting can make delay visible. A deadline can coordinate people who previously optimized their own departments separately. Measurement can protect patients against organizations that would otherwise define success on their own terms.

The problem appears when the target is treated as if it were the whole service.

A health system does not merely produce waiting times. It produces diagnosis, treatment, communication, continuity, safety, dignity, prioritization, prevention, and a thousand small judgments about which patient needs what now. Some of these can be measured. None can be collapsed into one number without loss.

The more severe the consequences attached to one indicator, the more rational it becomes to move scarce capacity toward that indicator. This can be a feature. It can also create shadows.

A queue target can encourage genuine process improvement. It can also encourage organizations to manage where the queue is counted. A response-time target can speed real service. It can also shift attention toward cases sitting near the threshold because moving them across the line changes the reported result more than helping someone far from it. A target can improve the mean while making the tails stranger. It can make a particular stage of a process look efficient by pushing delay into the stage before the clock starts or after it stops.

The metric creates a border, and borders attract activity.

This is one reason threshold targets deserve special suspicion. A continuous mission becomes a binary administrative event. Forty-nine minutes and fifty-one minutes may be almost identical for the person receiving a service, yet radically different inside an organization if fifty minutes is the line separating success from failure. Whenever consequences jump at a threshold, effort tends to gather around it.

That gathering is information.

A suspicious analyst may call it gaming. A more useful analyst asks what the threshold has made locally valuable. If staff suddenly discover dozens of ways to move cases from just above the line to just below it, the organization has revealed how much discretionary effort was available and where the incentive points.

The deeper error is believing that the target merely records performance. It is allocating attention.

Attention is the hidden currency of almost every metric system.

Senior leaders can discuss perhaps a few dozen measures regularly. Frontline managers can actively steer a smaller set. Employees learn quickly which numbers trigger phone calls and which sit quietly in appendices. The chosen metrics become an attention map. Work that moves them becomes legible upward. Work that does not may remain essential but politically invisible.

This creates a peculiar kind of organizational gravity. The measured tasks pull resources toward themselves.

Suppose a customer-support organization tracks average handling time because long calls are expensive and often indicate process problems. The measure can identify training needs and broken workflows. Then bonuses are tied tightly to reducing it. Agents now face a direct tradeoff between resolving a complicated problem and ending the call quickly. Some will become more efficient. Some will transfer difficult customers. Some will avoid careful explanations. Some will solve the immediate issue while leaving the underlying account problem untouched because prevention takes longer than closure.

Management sees average handling time fall.

Customers may still be spending more total time getting help, only now that time is fragmented across repeated contacts.

If the organization responds by adding “first-contact resolution,” it has improved the measurement system. It has also created a second target. Agents now optimize two visible quantities. Add customer satisfaction, upsell rate, compliance, escalation rate, and schedule adherence, and the dashboard becomes more balanced—but the job has not become fully measurable. It has become a higher-dimensional optimization problem.

There is no final dashboard on which the mission becomes identical to the metrics.

This can sound discouraging, but it points toward a better way to use measurement. Metrics are samples from the mission, not substitutes for it.

A sample can be very informative. A doctor does not need to measure every molecule in a patient to learn from blood pressure. An engineer does not reject instrumentation because no sensor captures the entire machine. The mistake is not using indicators. It is forgetting the gap between the indicator and the object of concern.

That gap should influence how incentives are designed.

If a task is narrow and measurable, strong incentives may work well. Piece rates make more sense when units are genuinely comparable and quality is easy to inspect. A delivery route with a clear completion criterion can be managed more directly than a research program whose best outcome may arrive years late and look unpromising for most of its life.

As the job becomes more multidimensional and its important qualities become harder to observe, stronger incentives on one measured dimension can become more dangerous.

This is the uncomfortable implication of multitask incentive theory. Sometimes the correct response to an excellent metric is a weaker incentive.

Managers often dislike this because weak incentives sound like weak management. But the choice is not between caring and not caring. It is between acknowledging incomplete observability and pretending that the observable slice is the whole job.

Professional norms, discretion, peer review, rotation, audits, qualitative judgment, and fixed salaries can look inefficient compared with crisp performance pay. Yet these softer institutions sometimes exist because the mission is too complex to contract completely.

The old bureaucratic rulebook is not automatically wise. Neither is the new dashboard.

Every measurement regime contains a theory of what can safely be made explicit.

There is another way metrics eat missions: they alter language.

Once a number becomes central, employees begin speaking through it. A manager no longer says that a team is exhausted and making brittle decisions; she says productivity is down six percent. A researcher no longer says an idea is strange but promising; he says the preliminary benchmark is below baseline. A school does not say students are curious but struggling with writing; it says proficiency rose in mathematics and fell in literacy.

The numerical language is useful because it compresses. Compression makes large organizations possible. A chief executive cannot personally inspect every customer interaction. A health minister cannot sit beside every patient. Metrics let distant principals see something.

But compression discards information.

As the compressed representation becomes the language of authority, people learn to translate messy reality into whatever survives the trip upward. Details that do not fit a field in the reporting system become stories, and stories travel badly in metric-heavy organizations. Eventually workers may stop producing the stories because they know they will not affect a decision.

The loss is not only human texture. It can be operational warning.

A system can hit every target while becoming more fragile in unmeasured ways. Maintenance is deferred. Experienced staff leave. Customers learn to navigate around the official process. Suppliers become brittle. Employees stop reporting near misses because the safety metric punishes them for candor. The dashboard looks green until a problem crosses a threshold large enough to become visible.

This is why good metrics need companions that can disagree with them.

One companion is a counter-metric. If speed is rewarded, measure rework. If output is rewarded, inspect quality. If utilization is rewarded, watch resilience. The point is not to create an infinite balanced scorecard. It is to identify the most likely way an apparently improving metric could conceal a worsening mission.

Another companion is random inspection. If people know that only targeted cases will ever be examined, they can optimize the selection rule. A small amount of random sampling preserves information about the regions the metric would otherwise ignore.

Another is qualitative escalation. Frontline workers need a way to say, “The number is right and the situation is still bad.” This sentence should be treated as potentially valuable evidence, not insubordination.

Another is metric rotation. A permanent target becomes part of the environment. Rotating attention can reduce the value of narrow long-term gaming and reveal whether improvement survives when the spotlight moves away.

Another is periodic amnesty. If organizations are punished forever for revealing that a metric has become unreliable, they will defend the metric. Leaders need moments when admitting a broken indicator is rewarded more than protecting its historical trend.

Most important is a habit of asking how the metric could improve while the mission gets worse.

That question sounds adversarial because it is. It should be asked before deployment, not after scandal.

For a sales target, perhaps revenue rises through discounts that destroy margin or through deals that churn quickly. For a safety target, perhaps incidents fall because reporting falls. For a research target, perhaps paper counts rise through smaller, safer projects. For a hiring target, perhaps time-to-fill improves because standards fall or because difficult roles are quietly reclassified. For a productivity target, perhaps measured output rises because maintenance, mentorship, documentation, and exploration are being consumed as invisible fuel.

A metric becomes dangerous when the organization cannot imagine these paths.

It becomes more dangerous when leadership treats imagining them as disloyalty.

This is where metric systems turn moral. People begin to divide into believers and cynics. The believers insist that resistance to the target is resistance to accountability. The cynics insist that all targets are games. Both positions are convenient because they avoid design work.

The harder position is conditional.

Some targets are worth having. Some deserve strong incentives. Some should be used only diagnostically. Some should trigger questions rather than rewards. Some should be retired once behavior adapts. Some need random audits. Some should be paired with measures of downstream harm. Some objectives are too complex to compress into a contract without leaving substantial discretion to people who understand the work.

The shape of the metric regime should depend on the shape of the job.

This also means that gaming is not a single phenomenon. There is fraudulent gaming, where data are falsified. There is definitional gaming, where cases are recategorized within the rules. There is threshold gaming, where effort concentrates just enough to cross the line. There is effort substitution, where measured tasks crowd out unmeasured ones. There is temporal gaming, where work is pulled forward to hit a period target and the next period pays the bill. There is population gaming, where difficult cases are avoided. There is goal displacement, where people internalize the proxy so completely that nobody remembers the original objective.

Only the first of these is solved mainly by honesty.

The rest require better system design.

The distinction changes how leaders should react when a target produces strange behavior. The instinctive response is often to write a more detailed rule. If employees exploit an ambiguity, close it. If they find another, close that too. Over time the measurement system grows elaborate, with definitions, exemptions, audit trails, and exception codes.

Sometimes this is necessary. But complexity has its own second-order effects. It rewards specialists in the measurement system. It increases the advantage of organizations with enough administrative capacity to manage the reporting machinery. It can turn operational work into evidence production for auditors. The institution begins spending more effort proving that it is doing the mission than doing it.

The metric has eaten not only the mission but the lunch break.

There is no technique that eliminates this tension because delegation itself creates it. The person setting the objective cannot observe everything the person doing the work can see. The person doing the work does not automatically share every priority of the person setting the objective. Metrics are one technology for bridging that gap. They make some performance visible and therefore governable.

Their power comes from the same incompleteness that makes them dangerous.

A useful metric is a disciplined reduction. It says: this aspect of the mission is important enough, measurable enough, and stable enough that we are willing to steer partly by it. The word “partly” does a great deal of work.

When the word disappears, the metric starts making claims it cannot support.

This is why an organization should know the answer to four questions before attaching serious consequences to a number.

What part of the mission does this metric represent well?

What important part does it leave out?

How can a competent, self-interested person improve the metric without improving the mission?

What evidence would tell us that this has begun to happen?

These questions do not prevent adaptation. They make adaptation observable.

That is the second-order discipline.

The first-order manager sees a number moving in the desired direction and asks how to make it move faster. The second-order manager asks what behavior the number is purchasing, what behavior it is crowding out, and whether the relationship between the number and the mission is still intact.

The distinction becomes more important as measurement becomes cheaper. Software can count almost everything that passes through a digital system: clicks, calls, commits, tickets, keystrokes, response times, conversion rates, resolution rates, lines changed, messages sent, leads touched, minutes active. Cheap measurement creates the temptation to manage what is available rather than measure what matters.

An abundant metric can become a scarce organization's objective almost by accident.

The irony is that better instrumentation can make this worse. Once leaders can see a number in real time, waiting for slower evidence becomes emotionally difficult. The dashboard offers immediate reinforcement. Customer trust, employee capability, research quality, institutional legitimacy, and resilience move more slowly and resist clean measurement. The fast metric wins meetings because it arrives on time.

So the metric eats the mission one bite at a time.

Not because the mission was false. Not because everyone became corrupt. Not because measurement failed.

Because a partial representation acquired consequences, and the organization rationally reorganized around what could be seen.

The cure is not blindness. It is to keep the representation subordinate to the thing represented.

Measure aggressively where the mapping is strong. Incentivize cautiously where tasks compete. Audit the blind spots. Preserve evidence from outside the dashboard. Reward people who discover that a target is no longer serving its purpose. And when the number looks almost too good, ask what else had to move to make it so.

A metric is at its most useful when it can still be contradicted by the mission.
