# The Metric Eats the Mission

In the summer of 2011, the state of Georgia released the results of an investigation into Atlanta Public Schools that should have been impossible to reconcile with the district's public story.

For years, rising test scores had been presented as evidence of dramatic improvement. Schools were praised. Administrators were rewarded. The system had the clean visual grammar of modern management: goals, targets, dashboards, comparisons, upward lines.

Then investigators began looking at the answer sheets.

They found cheating in 44 of the 56 schools they examined. The state's summary identified 178 teachers and principals as participants and said 82 confessed. Investigators concluded that the pressure to meet targets in a data-driven environment was a primary cause, alongside a broader failure of leadership and a culture of fear, intimidation, and retaliation.

The tests had been designed to measure learning. In parts of the system, the organization learned to produce test scores instead.

That distinction is the center of the problem.

A mission is usually too large to fit on a dashboard. A school wants students to learn, retain knowledge, reason, become capable adults, and acquire enough command of a subject to use it outside the exact conditions under which it was taught. A hospital wants people to become healthier. A police department wants people to be safer. A company wants to create durable value for customers without destroying the conditions that make the business viable. A research institution wants discoveries that matter.

None of those objectives arrives in a clean column.

So institutions construct proxies. They count test scores, waiting times, arrests, quarterly sales, publications, citations, defects, customer contacts, cases closed, calls handled, miles driven, conversion rates, clicks, engagement, utilization, churn, and thousands of other quantities that can stand close enough to an objective to make management possible.

This is not bureaucratic stupidity. It is a response to a real problem. Large institutions cannot steer by intuition alone. A superintendent cannot personally sit in every classroom. A hospital chief executive cannot follow every patient home. A minister cannot inspect every police encounter. A board cannot infer a company's health from the confidence of its executives. Measurement is one of the technologies that lets organizations become larger than the direct perception of the people running them.

The trouble begins when the proxy acquires consequences.

The more money, status, promotion, punishment, attention, or survival depends on a number, the more work reorganizes around the number. The first response may be exactly what the designer wanted. A defect target makes engineers investigate defects. A waiting-time target causes a clinic to rethink scheduling. A school with weak reading results directs more time toward reading. Measurement can pull neglected work into view.

Then the organization keeps learning.

Employees discover which actions move the score fastest. Managers learn which categories count and which do not. Vendors build tools around the reporting rule. Consultants teach optimization strategies. Ambiguous cases migrate toward favorable classifications. Work that improves the mission but does not move the metric becomes harder to defend. Work that moves the metric but does not improve the mission becomes easier to justify.

A useful measure can become a local objective long before anyone formally announces that the mission has changed.

Donald Campbell saw the outline of this problem in the 1970s while thinking about social indicators and program evaluation. His warning is often summarized as a law about corruption pressure: the more a quantitative social indicator is used for decision-making, the more pressure it experiences and the more likely it is to distort the process it is meant to monitor. The popular wording varies. The mechanism matters more than the slogan.

Pressure changes behavior.

The Atlanta case is unusually vivid because the endpoint was literal cheating. It is tempting, for that reason, to treat it as a morality play about bad actors. That would miss the harder lesson.

Most metric distortion does not begin with someone deciding to falsify an answer sheet.

It begins with selection.

Suppose a school is judged heavily on the percentage of students crossing a proficiency threshold. A teacher with limited time now faces an altered allocation problem. Helping a student far below the threshold may produce a large educational benefit without changing the accountability statistic. Helping a student just below the line cross it may produce a smaller educational benefit and a larger measured gain. If the metric determines reputation, bonuses, interventions, or job security, the threshold becomes more than a description. It becomes a map of where effort pays institutionally.

The teacher does not need to be cynical. The incentive is embedded in the arithmetic.

This pattern appears whenever a continuous objective is compressed into a boundary. Hospitals can be judged by whether a process happens within a specified time. Call centers can be judged by average handling time. sales teams can be rewarded for crossing quota. Researchers can be assessed by publication counts. Once a line matters, cases near the line become disproportionately valuable.

The organization starts seeing cliffs where the underlying world is smooth.

Another form of distortion is reclassification.

If one category is penalized and another is not, the boundary between them becomes economically meaningful. A case that could reasonably be coded either way will tend to migrate toward the category with the better consequence. Sometimes that is fraud. Often it is discretion under pressure.

This is one reason target systems can produce suspiciously sharp improvements without any dramatic change in the underlying reality. A department can appear to reduce a problem by redefining what counts as the problem. A company can improve a key performance indicator by changing which transactions enter the denominator. A platform can report healthier engagement by changing the event that counts as an active user. None of these maneuvers requires altering the physical world very much. They alter the measurement boundary.

A third response is substitution.

When managers reward what they can see, workers shift effort toward visible tasks and away from invisible ones. A police unit ordered to reduce a counted category of crime may devote more attention to that category while neglecting harms that are harder to measure. A university department rewarded for publication volume can break work into smaller papers. A software team measured by tickets closed can choose smaller tickets. A customer-service team measured by speed can end calls sooner.

The measured activity improves. The displaced activity becomes harder to notice precisely because it is less measured.

This creates a peculiar informational trap. The dashboard gets cleaner at the same time the institution becomes less legible.

The problem can become self-reinforcing because senior leaders often respond to disappointing metrics by increasing the pressure attached to them. A number looks weak, so the target is raised, monitoring becomes more frequent, rankings are published, bonuses become more sensitive, and managers are asked for action plans. If the metric still contains slack—if genuine improvement remains easier than distortion—this can work.

If genuine improvement is difficult while the proxy remains manipulable, pressure migrates toward the proxy.

The organization has created a contest to discover the cheapest way to move the number.

This is why metric systems should be understood as incentive systems even when no explicit bonus exists. Attention is a reward. Promotion is a reward. Avoiding humiliation is a reward. Not being called into a meeting is a reward. Keeping one's school off an intervention list is a reward. Maintaining professional identity in front of peers is a reward.

People are exquisitely sensitive to what the institution actually notices.

The phrase “what gets measured gets managed” is usually offered as a defense of measurement. It is also a warning. Management does not stop at the intended mechanism. Once measurement changes consequences, it changes the search process inside the organization. People begin exploring for actions that score well.

Some of those actions will be excellent.

That point matters because the usual critique of metrics can become lazy. A target is not corrupt merely because people respond to it. The response may be the entire point.

A factory that measures defects wants teams to change their behavior. A surgeon who sees infection rates may adopt a better protocol. A city that prices congestion wants drivers to alter when and how they travel. A utility that pays customers for reducing peak demand hopes the number causes substitution. A carbon price is designed to make a previously external cost enter decisions.

If behavior did not change, these instruments would fail.

The question is whether movement in the measure remains informative about movement in the mission.

That relationship can survive pressure. It survives best when the proxy is hard to improve without improving the objective, when manipulation is costly, when multiple independent measures constrain each other, when the measured outcome is close to the thing that actually matters, and when evaluators preserve enough discretion to notice strange patterns.

A manufacturing defect is often easier to tie to the mission than a university ranking is. If a component physically fails a validated stress test, reducing that failure rate may represent genuine improvement. The room for semantic maneuver is narrower. Even then, engineers can optimize to the test regime while missing failure modes outside it. But the distance between proxy and objective is smaller.

This suggests a useful way to think about measurement quality: not only correlation before deployment, but resistance to optimization after deployment.

A metric that predicts the objective beautifully when nobody cares about it may be fragile. A slightly noisier measure that remains meaningful under pressure may be more valuable for governance.

That is a different criterion from ordinary statistical accuracy.

Imagine two metrics used to evaluate a customer-service organization. The first predicts customer satisfaction extremely well in historical data but is easy for agents to manipulate once they know the rule. The second predicts satisfaction somewhat less well but is difficult to improve without actually solving the customer's problem. If management selects only on historical fit, it may choose the first and then watch the relationship decay.

The act of choosing the metric changes which metric was best.

This is the same second-order move introduced in the previous chapter, now operating inside organizations. A descriptive relationship becomes a control surface. The control surface invites adaptation. Adaptation changes the relationship.

The Atlanta investigation showed the extreme end of the chain. The target did not merely redirect teaching time. In some schools, according to the state's findings, educators changed students' answers. Once that happens, the indicator becomes worse than noisy. It becomes a false reassurance machine. Students who needed help can appear proficient. Administrators can believe a reform is working. Outside funders can reward the wrong practices. Political leaders can point to apparent gains. The distorted measure does not merely fail to reveal reality. It creates evidence for an incorrect story about reality.

This is one reason corrupted metrics are so dangerous in hierarchical systems. Information generally becomes more compressed as it moves upward.

A classroom contains hundreds of details: which student understands fractions but freezes under time pressure, which child can decode text but not infer meaning, which teacher has improved a difficult class even though the proficiency rate remains low. By the time information reaches a district office, much of that texture has been converted into percentages and trend lines. At the state level, the compression is greater still.

Compression is necessary. But once the compressed signal is gamed, leaders can lose access to the very detail that would reveal the gaming.

The map becomes cleanest at the level farthest from the terrain.

This produces an asymmetry between good and bad news. A troubling anecdote can be dismissed as unrepresentative when the aggregate numbers look strong. A whistleblower can be portrayed as resistant to reform. A sudden jump can be celebrated as evidence that ambitious targets work. The metric does not merely measure performance; it can become a shield against contradictory information.

An institution that truly wants to learn therefore needs channels through which local evidence can challenge the dashboard.

That may mean audits, random sampling, independent measurements, qualitative review, protected whistleblowing, rotating indicators, or simply leaders who treat unusually good results as something to investigate rather than immediately reward.

The last habit is underrated.

Organizations are generally built to investigate failure. They are less practiced at investigating miracles.

A number that suddenly improves far faster than the underlying process normally permits should create curiosity. What changed? Is the improvement broad or concentrated near a threshold? Did definitions change? Did the population change? Did reporting change? Did an intervention really produce a breakthrough? Are independent measures moving in the same direction?

Suspicion need not be cynicism. It can be respect for how adaptive organizations are.

There is also a design lesson in the difference between targets and diagnostics.

A diagnostic measure helps someone understand a system. A target attaches a desired direction and usually a consequence. The same quantity can serve both roles, but the transition matters. Once a diagnostic becomes a target, its statistical properties are no longer enough. Designers must ask how people can respond to it.

What would I do if my career depended on this number?

What would a clever employee discover after six months?

Which valuable work would become invisible?

Which category boundaries would become contested?

What behavior could improve the score while making the underlying objective worse?

What would become harder to observe after decisions started using the metric?

These are not questions about employee virtue. They are questions about system design.

Well-designed measurement does not assume bad people. It assumes adaptive people.

That distinction matters because moralizing about gaming often protects the metric from criticism. Leaders can say the system is sound and a few unethical employees abused it. Sometimes that is true. But if the same incentive repeatedly generates the same distortion across different people, the design deserves scrutiny too.

A bridge designer does not blame water for finding a crack.

The strongest metric systems make undesirable optimization expensive and genuine improvement comparatively cheap. They use several signals that fail differently. They separate some measures used for diagnosis from those used for reward. They audit edge cases and threshold behavior. They keep definitions stable enough for comparison while allowing revision when a measure has plainly become an object of the game. They preserve unstructured evidence that can contradict the headline number.

They also accept that no metric should be asked to carry an entire mission.

This is difficult for modern institutions because metrics promise comparability, and comparability is politically powerful. A ranking lets a minister compare schools. A score lets a lender process thousands of applicants. A KPI lets a board summarize divisions. A benchmark lets customers compare products. A number allows decisions to travel farther than the person who generated the evidence.

The more scalable the institution, the stronger the demand for compression.

So the answer cannot be to abandon metrics and return to judgment. Judgment has its own pathologies: favoritism, inconsistency, opacity, memory error, status bias, and the ability of powerful people to tell flattering stories without contradiction. Unmeasured institutions can fail invisibly.

The alternative is to treat measurement as part of the system being measured.

The score changes behavior. The behavior changes the score. The manager reacts. The workers react to the manager's reaction. The organization learns what counts. The metric acquires a history.

At that point, a number is not simply a reading from an instrument. It is the outcome of a negotiation between the mission, the proxy, the incentives, and the people living under them.

That is why the title of this chapter is deliberately stronger than the conclusion.

The metric does not always eat the mission.

Sometimes it feeds it.

The real danger is forgetting that it can do either.
