# The Test Becomes the Curriculum

A test begins as a sample.

No exam can contain everything a student is supposed to know. It selects. A few algebra problems stand in for a much larger mathematical ability. A passage and several questions stand in for reading comprehension. A timed essay stands in for writing. The test is useful because it is incomplete. It compresses a broad domain into something that can be administered, scored, compared, and repeated.

That incompleteness becomes important the moment consequences attach to the score.

If a school is judged by mathematics and reading tests, those subjects become more expensive to neglect. If some grades count and others do not, the counted grades attract attention. If one exam determines promotion or intervention, its format becomes operational knowledge. Teachers study released items. Districts buy preparation materials. Administrators ask which standards are most likely to appear. Students learn not only mathematics, but the shape of the mathematics the institution will inspect.

The test starts to teach the school what school is for.

This is one of the cleanest examples of a second-order measurement problem because the response is neither mysterious nor necessarily corrupt. An assessment is designed to direct attention toward valued knowledge. If teachers ignored it completely, accountability would have no mechanism. The difficulty is that the test contains a sample of the objective, while the school can reorganize around the sample.

The closer the sample is to the objective, the less dangerous that reorganization is.

The farther apart they are, the more the test can become the curriculum.

In 1996, Chicago Public Schools introduced a high-stakes accountability policy that tied consequences to standardized test performance. Brian Jacob later studied the policy using student-level administrative data. The results are unusually useful because they show improvement and distortion in the same system rather than offering a simple morality tale.

Achievement on the high-stakes mathematics and reading tests rose sharply after the policy began. That is real evidence in favor of accountability. Schools did not simply ignore the new incentives. Students and teachers responded.

But Jacob found that the gains were driven largely by test-specific skills and student effort, and that they did not produce comparable gains on a separate state-administered low-stakes exam. He also found strategic responses along other dimensions: increases in special-education placement, preemptive retention of students, and substitution away from lower-stakes subjects such as science and social studies.

The school system learned the rule.

That phrase can sound accusatory. It should not.

Imagine being a principal in a school serving students who are already behind. A public accountability system announces that mathematics and reading scores will determine whether your school is sanctioned, praised, restructured, or placed under pressure. You have limited instructional time, limited money, uneven staffing, children with different needs, and a calendar that does not expand because the number of objectives has increased.

What should you do?

You allocate attention.

You ask teachers to spend more time on the tested material. You study item formats. You identify students close to important thresholds. You look for schedules, curricula, tutoring arrangements, and interventions that move the measured outcome. If the test measures important skills, much of that effort can be educationally valuable.

The problem is not that the school responds.

The problem is that response changes what the test score means.

Before high stakes, a test score may partly reflect broad knowledge sampled under relatively ordinary instruction. After high stakes, the same score can reflect broad knowledge plus targeted preparation, familiarity with item types, effort induced by consequences, selective attention to tested topics, student sorting, and the institution's growing knowledge of the assessment itself.

The number is still empirical.

Its interpretation has changed.

This is why arguments over “teaching to the test” often become confused. The phrase covers several very different activities.

One form is simple alignment. A curriculum says students should know fractions, the test measures fractions, and teachers spend more time teaching fractions. If students genuinely learn fractions, the test has done exactly what its designers hoped.

Another form is format coaching. Students learn how to eliminate distractors, manage time, recognize recurring item structures, and exploit predictable conventions. Some of this is legitimate test literacy. Some of it raises scores without much transfer to the underlying skill.

A third form is curricular narrowing. Tested subjects displace untested subjects because only some outcomes enter the accountability system.

A fourth form is threshold targeting. Resources concentrate on students whose score is most likely to change the school's measured status.

A fifth form is exclusion or reclassification. Students who threaten the metric may be retained, moved, reclassified, or otherwise shifted relative to the testing rule.

A sixth form is outright manipulation or cheating.

These responses sit on a continuum, but they are not equivalent.

A serious analysis has to distinguish them because the remedy for one can worsen another. Making tests more predictable can improve instructional alignment while increasing narrow coaching. Making them less predictable can reduce coaching while making expectations less clear. Testing more subjects can reduce substitution while consuming more instructional time and administrative capacity. Using more metrics can make gaming harder while creating a bureaucracy of measurement that overwhelms the classroom.

There is no costless test architecture.

The second-order question is therefore not whether incentives alter instruction. They will. It is whether the instruction they induce generalizes beyond the instrument.

That idea of generalization matters more than the slogan.

Suppose students practice ten kinds of arithmetic problems and then improve on a test that contains those same forms. The result is encouraging but ambiguous. Did they learn a mathematical principle that transfers to new problems? Did they memorize procedures for familiar structures? Did they become faster at the test's conventions? Did they simply try harder because the exam mattered more?

A score cannot answer those questions by itself.

You need evidence from outside the loop.

That is what makes low-stakes comparison tests valuable. If a high-stakes score rises while a differently designed low-stakes measure of the same broad domain barely moves, the gap contains information. It suggests that at least part of the apparent gain is specific to the tested instrument or the conditions around it.

Jacob's Chicago findings are important for precisely this reason. They do not say the policy produced no learning. They say the response was more test-specific than the headline score alone would reveal.

That distinction should make both enthusiasts and critics uncomfortable.

The enthusiast wants the rising score to be a clean measure of improved education.

The critic wants teaching to the test to mean nothing of value happened.

The evidence supports neither extreme.

A system can produce genuine improvement and genuine distortion at the same time.

Thomas Dee and Brian Jacob later examined the federal No Child Left Behind era using state-level National Assessment of Educational Progress data. Their results complicate the story further. They found statistically significant gains in fourth-grade mathematics and evidence of improvements in eighth-grade mathematics, especially among lower-achieving groups, while finding no evidence of increased reading achievement in either fourth or eighth grade.

That matters because it shows that consequential accountability did not merely manufacture local test-score illusions everywhere. Some gains appeared on NAEP, an external assessment not itself used as the primary state accountability test.

The right conclusion is not that high-stakes testing works or fails.

It is that the effects depend on domain, design, incentives, baseline performance, and what teachers and students can do in response.

This is where the second-order frame becomes useful.

A test is not only an observation technology. It is also a message about priorities.

The message can be explicit. Mathematics and reading are important enough to count every year.

It can also be implicit. Science matters, but not in the same way. Writing matters, but perhaps not this year. Art matters, but it does not appear on the dashboard. History matters, but the accountability clock is running somewhere else.

Schools do not need to be cynical for those differences to affect time allocation.

Time is a budget.

A forty-five-minute block added to test preparation has to come from somewhere. A specialist reassigned to tested subjects is no longer doing another job. A Saturday program aimed at students near proficiency thresholds may be educationally sensible while still revealing how the metric changes the marginal value of different students and activities.

This is why accountability can create what economists would call shadow prices inside a school.

The official budget says one hour is one hour.

The incentive system says an hour spent in one place may be worth far more than an hour spent in another.

Teachers feel those prices long before researchers estimate them.

A classroom under pressure becomes a small optimization problem.

Which standard has not been covered? Which students are likely to move? Which unit can be compressed? Which assignment can be dropped? Which practice item resembles the state format? Which child needs remediation? Which child is already safely above the threshold? Which meeting can be canceled so teachers have more preparation time?

None of these decisions is visible in the final score.

The score is downstream of thousands of reallocations.

That is the key move. The test creates a field of local decisions, and those decisions alter the educational process that later produces the next test result.

Bright-line rules intensify the effect.

If a school is labeled failing when a measured percentage falls below a threshold, the difference between one student just below proficiency and one student far below proficiency is not the same from the institution's point of view. Helping either child may be educationally valuable. But helping the first child cross the line can also alter the school's formal status.

The metric creates a cliff that the underlying skill distribution does not naturally contain.

A child does not become a different kind of mathematician because a score moves from one side of an administrative cut point to the other. The institution becomes a different kind of institution.

That asymmetry changes behavior.

It can also change data.

Students may be retained before reaching a tested grade. Some may be moved into categories with different testing rules. Administrators may focus attendance efforts around exam days. Teachers may spend disproportionate time on students near thresholds. These are all ways the test becomes an organizing principle rather than merely an assessment.

At the far edge sits cheating.

Jacob and Steven Levitt developed statistical methods for identifying suspicious answer patterns in Chicago classrooms and then helped test the method prospectively. In 2002, more than one hundred classrooms were selected for retesting based on the algorithm and comparison groups. Classrooms identified as likely cheaters experienced large score declines on the closely monitored retest, while classrooms with large original gains but low cheating probability largely maintained them.

The result is useful for two reasons.

First, it demonstrates that some apparently extraordinary achievement can be false.

Second, it demonstrates that not every extraordinary achievement is false.

That second point is easy to lose.

A system that learns to detect gaming must avoid turning skepticism into another crude metric. Unusual improvement can be a signal of manipulation, but it can also be excellent teaching. The retesting design mattered because it created an independent way to distinguish the two.

This suggests a general rule for second-order systems: when the primary measure becomes consequential, preserve a measurement channel that is harder to optimize directly.

In education, that can mean low-stakes audits, rotating item pools, external assessments, sampling untested domains, student work reviewed outside the accountability chain, or longitudinal outcomes that are difficult to manufacture quickly.

The purpose is not to catch everyone.

It is to keep the institution capable of learning whether the headline measure still represents the objective.

The distinction between test and curriculum becomes especially important because a curriculum is broader than a list of content standards.

It includes habits of attention. It includes how long a student sits with a difficult problem before receiving a hint. It includes whether a teacher has time to follow an unexpected question. It includes argument, curiosity, memory, revision, cooperation, background knowledge, and the ability to apply an idea when the cues are unfamiliar.

Many of these qualities are measurable in some form.

Measuring all of them at scale is another matter.

A standardized test succeeds by reducing variation in administration and scoring. That makes comparisons more defensible. But the same standardization leaves out some forms of learning that are expensive to evaluate reliably across thousands or millions of students.

The omitted dimensions do not disappear from education.

They become less visible to the accountability system.

This is a familiar problem from Chapter 2, but classrooms make the tradeoff unusually tangible because time cannot be stored. When a teacher devotes April to test preparation, April is gone.

The benefits may be real.

So are the opportunity costs.

That is why the strongest defense of high-stakes testing is not that teaching to the test never happens. It is that some teaching to the test is desirable when the test samples important knowledge and when the alternative is diffuse instruction with weak accountability.

Edward Lazear made this point formally in work on teaching to the test. His model shows why announcing what will be tested can be useful for students for whom learning is costly or motivation is weak. Concentrating attention on a subset of material can produce more learning than a broad, vague standard that induces little effort anywhere.

This is uncomfortable but important.

Breadth is not always quality.

A curriculum that theoretically covers everything but leaves struggling students mastering almost nothing is not saved by its elegance. A narrow target can focus effort where focus is needed.

The problem returns when the narrow target is mistaken for the whole objective.

A school may rationally begin with a limited set of foundational skills. If success on the measure then becomes the definition of success, the temporary scaffold can harden into the curriculum.

Measurement has a ratchet effect because organizations build around what can be defended.

A principal can justify a test-preparation program with a trend line. A teacher protecting time for an untested seminar, novel, lab, project, or discussion often needs a more complicated argument.

The measurable activity arrives at the budget meeting with evidence already attached.

The less measurable activity arrives with judgment.

In systems under pressure, evidence usually wins.

That does not mean the evidence is wrong. It means the evidence has an institutional advantage.

Over time, that advantage can reshape professional identity. Teachers become evaluated by score growth. Schools become described by proficiency rates. Districts compare themselves through dashboards. Parents learn to use ratings. Real-estate markets incorporate school scores into neighborhood demand. Political debates turn around annual releases of test data.

The assessment escapes the testing room.

It becomes part of the social definition of the school.

Once that happens, even reforms intended to reduce test pressure have to work through a world already organized around the scores.

This is one reason measurement systems are sticky. They create constituencies, routines, software, contracts, reporting calendars, expertise, and categories. Vendors build products. District offices create departments. Journalists expect annual numbers. Researchers build longitudinal datasets. Parents learn how to interpret the labels.

A test can be changed in a year.

The institution that grew around it may take much longer.

There is also an equity problem that simple anti-testing arguments can miss.

Without common measures, powerful institutions can hide behind local judgment. Schools serving affluent communities often have more resources, stronger political voice, and more capacity to present qualitative evidence of success. Standardized assessments can reveal achievement gaps that anecdotes obscure. They can make neglect visible. They can force systems to report outcomes for groups that would otherwise disappear inside averages.

Removing the metric can therefore restore discretion without restoring accountability.

That is not obviously progress.

The design challenge is to preserve the diagnostic value of common measurement while reducing the incentives to collapse education into the measured slice.

One way is to separate some tests used for diagnosis from those used for punishment.

Another is to vary the evidence. If schools know that only one fixed instrument determines consequences, optimization becomes easier. If evaluation combines external assessments, sampling, classroom work, broader outcomes, and periodic audits, no single proxy carries the whole mission.

A third is to inspect transfer deliberately. Do gains survive on a different test? Do they persist the following year? Do students perform better on unfamiliar problems? Does improvement appear in later courses? Do graduation, attendance, or other outcomes move in the same direction?

A fourth is to watch what disappears. If mathematics scores rise while science instruction shrinks, the tradeoff belongs in the evaluation. If proficiency improves while advanced students stagnate, that matters. If low-performing students are reclassified or excluded, the data architecture should make the movement visible.

These are not arguments for an infinite dashboard.

Too many measures create their own pathology.

A school can drown in indicators and still miss the child in front of it.

The point is to design measurement with the expectation that people will respond intelligently to it.

That means asking, before deployment, what the easiest score-improving strategies will be.

Which of those strategies represent genuine learning?

Which merely improve familiarity with the instrument?

Which displace valuable work?

Which alter who is tested?

Which create cliffs around thresholds?

Which can be detected through independent evidence?

The answers should shape the test before the test shapes the school.

This is the broader lesson for any institution that uses evaluation to guide behavior.

A benchmark in machine learning can become the research agenda. A publication metric can become the definition of scientific productivity. A sales quota can become the customer strategy. A hospital quality measure can become the treatment protocol. In each case, the measure starts as a sample of performance and ends by influencing what performance means operationally.

Education simply makes the loop easier to see because the phrase is literal.

The test becomes the curriculum.

But not all at once.

It happens lesson by lesson, meeting by meeting, calendar decision by calendar decision. A principal asks for another practice block. A teacher drops one unit and extends another. A district buys software aligned to the state exam. Students learn which questions have consequences. Parents hire tutors who know the format. The system becomes better at producing the thing that is inspected.

Sometimes that thing is worth producing.

Sometimes it is only the shadow of what the school was meant to teach.

A mature accountability system has to know the difference.
