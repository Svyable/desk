# The Economy Learns the Rule

A tax schedule is one of the clearest examples of a model becoming an environment.

On paper it is a set of instructions. Earn this much and one rate applies. Cross another threshold and a different rate applies. Claim this deduction and taxable income falls. Organize a transaction one way and one treatment applies; organize it another way and the treatment changes.

To the government, the schedule is a policy instrument.

To everyone living under it, the schedule is also a map.

The map does not tell people what they must do. It changes the prices around their choices. Work an extra hour, realize a gain this year rather than next, incorporate a business, take compensation as salary or something else, spend time documenting an expense, remain just below a threshold, cross it anyway. The formal rule enters ordinary decisions and becomes one of the facts the economy is reacting to.

This is where economic policy becomes second-order in a very literal way.

A policymaker can estimate how people behaved before a rule changed and then use those relationships to forecast what will happen after the change. But the forecast itself depends on an assumption: that people will keep behaving in roughly the same way under the new incentives.

Sometimes they do.

Sometimes they do not.

And often the most interesting evidence is visible not in what people say about the rule, but in the shape of the data after they have had time to adapt.

Economists have a useful word for one such shape: bunching.

Imagine an income distribution that would otherwise be fairly smooth. Now place a tax threshold at one point along it. If crossing the threshold changes the marginal reward to earning or reporting another dollar, people who can control the timing or form of income may respond. The distribution can begin to pile up near the boundary.

The rule leaves a fingerprint.

Emmanuel Saez studied this question using U.S. tax-return data. His published work found clear bunching at some parts of the tax schedule and weak or no bunching at others. The distinction matters. A simple story in which everyone instantly and precisely optimizes against tax rates is wrong. Some people have more control over reported income than others. Some thresholds are salient. Some margins of adjustment are cheap. Others are expensive, invisible, or simply not worth the trouble.

The economy learns the rule unevenly.

That unevenness is important because models of policy response often imagine a cleaner world than the one people inhabit. A tax rate changes, therefore labor supply changes by some elasticity. A subsidy changes, therefore adoption changes. An interest rate changes, therefore borrowing changes. The equations can be useful, but the pathway from rule to behavior passes through institutions, attention, contracts, accounting systems, habits, and the amount of control people actually possess.

A salaried employee whose pay is fixed by an employer may have little ability to place annual income just below a tax kink. A self-employed worker can often choose timing, expenses, or reported business income with more flexibility. A large firm may have a tax department that notices a tiny rule change immediately. A household may not learn about the same change until a refund looks different months later.

The same policy therefore enters different decision systems at different speeds.

This is one reason the strongest economic rules often create not only incentives but industries of interpretation. Accountants, tax lawyers, payroll vendors, compliance teams, brokers, benefits consultants, and software systems convert policy into actionable choices. They are part of the transmission mechanism.

A rule that is too obscure to change behavior directly may still change behavior once an intermediary translates it.

The policy does not need to be secret for this process to take time. Knowing that a threshold exists is different from knowing how to reorganize around it. The capacity to respond is itself unequally distributed.

This complicates a familiar picture of rational adaptation. People do not stand outside the economy with complete rulebooks and frictionless control. They learn selectively. They imitate. They discover strategies from peers. Firms build procedures. Software makes some responses automatic. Over time, an incentive that initially produced little movement can become easier to exploit.

The first year of a policy may therefore be a poor guide to its mature effects.

Henrik Kleven and Mazhar Waseem found a particularly useful version of this problem in Pakistan's tax system. Their work examined tax notches, where crossing a threshold can create a discrete jump in liability rather than a smooth change in the marginal rate. A notch creates a region in which, under a frictionless optimization model, some choices should be obviously unattractive. Yet the data showed two things at once: sharp bunching near the boundary and many taxpayers still sitting in ranges that a frictionless model would describe as dominated.

People were responding strongly, but not completely.

That combination is more revealing than either result alone.

If nobody responded, a policymaker might conclude that incentives did not matter. If everyone perfectly rearranged behavior, a theorist might conclude that economic agents were nearly frictionless optimizers. The observed world contained both adaptation and inertia.

The rule mattered. So did the cost of reacting to the rule.

This is the part of second-order policy analysis that slogans tend to miss. The Lucas critique is often remembered as a warning that policy changes behavior. True. But how behavior changes is an empirical question, and the answer depends on what people know, what they can control, what adjustment costs they face, and what institutions translate the policy into action.

A second-order model therefore needs two models at once.

It needs a model of the environment before the intervention.

And it needs a model of how people will respond to the intervention itself.

The second model is usually harder.

Consider an income-tax increase aimed at raising revenue from a particular group. A static calculation can multiply the old tax base by the new rate. That arithmetic is not useless. It is the first pass.

Then the responses begin.

Some people may work less. Some may shift compensation. Some may change when income is realized. Some may claim deductions they previously ignored. Some may relocate taxable activity. Some may incorporate. Some may hire advisers. Some may do nothing. Some may not even notice the change.

The final revenue depends not only on the rate but on the menu of responses and how costly each one is.

This does not imply that every tax cuts its own base enough to defeat its purpose. That is another overreaction. Behavioral response is not magical. Governments raise substantial revenue through taxes precisely because many taxable activities remain taxable and many people cannot or do not fully adjust.

The important point is narrower: the base is not independent of the rule imposed on it.

That principle extends far beyond taxation.

Regulation changes which products firms design. Capital requirements change bank balance sheets. Emissions rules change production technologies. Benefit eligibility rules change reporting and sometimes household choices. Zoning rules change where developers build. Procurement rules change which firms bother to bid. Monetary policy changes borrowing, saving, asset allocation, exchange rates, and expectations.

A policy is not merely placed on top of an economy. It becomes one of the conditions inside the economy.

This seems obvious stated plainly, yet institutional forecasting repeatedly forgets it because historical data are so seductive.

Historical data provide a record of behavior under old rules.

The moment the rules change substantially, part of the dataset becomes a study of a world that no longer exists.

Robert Lucas's 1976 critique attacked exactly this complacency in macroeconomic policy evaluation. Econometric models estimated relationships from observed history and then used those relationships to compare alternative policies. Lucas argued that if people's decision rules depended on the policy regime, those historical coefficients could not be assumed to remain fixed when policy changed.

A stable relationship may be stable only because the rule behind it was stable.

Change the rule and the apparent law can dissolve.

This is especially important when policy is predictable enough for people to anticipate.

Suppose a government repeatedly responds to rising inflation in the same way. Firms and workers can begin incorporating that response into wage bargains, prices, contracts, borrowing, and inventories. Suppose a central bank credibly changes its reaction function. The effect of an interest-rate move can depend partly on what the move reveals about future policy. Suppose a tax credit is temporary and its expiration is known. Purchases may shift across time rather than simply increase.

The public does not merely react to today's policy. It forms beliefs about the rule generating tomorrow's policy.

That makes economic governance resemble a repeated game more than a one-time intervention.

Once a pattern is recognized, the pattern itself changes behavior.

This does not require the strong claim that everyone has rational expectations in a technical sense. Even crude learning is enough. Businesses remember that a subsidy tends to expire. Traders notice how a central bank responds to inflation surprises. Households learn that a tax deadline creates advantages to acting before December. Banks learn which forms of financing receive favorable treatment.

People do not need perfect models of government. They need only enough memory to recognize a recurring incentive.

The economy becomes harder to surprise with the same rule twice.

That phrase can sound like a celebration of cleverness. It should not. Adaptation can make policy better as well as weaker.

A pollution rule is supposed to cause firms to develop cleaner processes. A congestion charge is supposed to redirect travel. A tax credit for investment is supposed to alter investment decisions. A safety standard is supposed to change product design. In these cases, the economy learning the rule is the mechanism of success.

The policy works by changing the world that generated the old data.

The evaluation problem is to distinguish intended adaptation from evasive adaptation and from adaptation that creates new costs elsewhere.

A firm that installs cleaner equipment because pollution is taxed has responded exactly as intended. A firm that moves the polluting activity into an unmeasured category has changed the reported outcome without necessarily changing the underlying harm. A firm that shuts down a productive process entirely may reduce pollution while imposing other costs. The same headline metric can conceal very different behavioral paths.

This is why policy evaluation needs more than a before-and-after comparison.

If the rule changes behavior, the composition of observed activity may change too. Who participates, what gets reported, which transactions occur, and where activity migrates can all move. The new data are generated under a different equilibrium.

Policy success can even make an old prediction look wrong.

Suppose a government forecasts a shortage if no action is taken and announces a subsidy intended to expand supply. Producers respond, investment arrives, and the shortage never materializes. A superficial scorecard says the forecast was inaccurate. A better analysis asks what would have happened without the policy and whether the forecast correctly identified the risk conditional on inaction.

The same structure appeared in Chapter 1 with forecasts that trigger intervention. Here the emphasis is different: the economy learns not only the forecast but the rule attached to it.

Once enough people learn, the baseline moves.

This creates a challenge for anyone who wants policy to be both stable and adaptive.

Stable rules are valuable because people can plan around them. Arbitrary policy makes contracts difficult and investment riskier. But a stable rule also gives people time to optimize against its edges. If the rule contains loopholes, thresholds, or exploitable classifications, stability can deepen the response.

Constantly changing the rule is not a solution. That creates its own uncertainty and invites another form of gaming: betting on political revision.

The deeper requirement is to design rules whose induced adaptation is tolerable.

That means asking what the economy will learn.

Which margins can move?

Who has the cheapest adjustment technology?

Which intermediaries will teach people how to respond?

What happens when the response becomes automated?

Which historical relationships depend on the current rule staying in place?

Where will activity migrate if one channel is constrained?

How much of the apparent response is real behavior, and how much is reporting behavior?

These questions are not complications added after the policy model. They are part of the policy model.

The tax-bunching evidence provides a small visual metaphor for the whole problem. A rule is drawn across a distribution. The distribution bends around it.

Not perfectly. Not uniformly. Not immediately.

But enough to reveal that the line is no longer outside the system.

The rule has become part of the terrain.
