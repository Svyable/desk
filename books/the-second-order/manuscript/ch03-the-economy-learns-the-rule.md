# The Economy Learns the Rule

A policy can fail because people disobey it.

It can also fail because people understand it.

That second possibility is harder for institutions to accept. It is comforting to imagine policy as a lever attached to a machine. Pull the lever, observe the response, adjust the pressure, and repeat. If the response differs from the forecast, perhaps the lever was moved too little or too late. The machinery itself is assumed to be stable.

Economies do not offer that comfort.

They are filled with people trying to infer what the policymaker will do next. Businesses choose when to invest. Workers negotiate wages. Banks set loan terms. Investors choose maturities. Households decide whether to borrow, save, buy now, or wait. Every one of those choices can depend partly on expectations about taxes, inflation, interest rates, regulation, exchange rates, or government support.

A policy rule therefore enters the economy twice.

It enters once through the immediate action: the tax changes, the rate rises, the subsidy expires, the spending begins.

It enters again through what people learn about the rule behind the action.

The second entry can matter more than the first.

This was one of the central lessons emerging from macroeconomics in the 1970s. Robert Lucas's critique, introduced in the first chapter, is often remembered as a technical objection to a generation of econometric models. Its broader force is easier to see if we imagine a policymaker observing a regular historical relationship and deciding to exploit it.

Suppose higher inflation has often coincided with lower unemployment in the historical data. A government might be tempted to use expansionary policy to push unemployment lower, accepting somewhat more inflation as the trade. If households and firms simply repeat their past behavior, the historical relationship may appear usable.

But once people begin to anticipate the policy, wage demands, prices, contracts, and investment decisions can change. The government is no longer moving through the old statistical landscape. Its repeated attempt to exploit the relationship can help change the relationship itself.

The economy has learned the rule.

This idea is easy to overstate. Nobody wakes up knowing the government's reaction function. Expectations are uneven. Most people do not read central-bank minutes. Firms have different models. Policymakers themselves are uncertain and can change their minds. Information moves slowly through some parts of the economy and instantly through others.

But perfect understanding is not required.

Enough people making enough consequential decisions can infer enough of the pattern for the old policy effect to weaken.

That is what makes macroeconomic management different from controlling a furnace. A furnace does not notice that the operator raises the temperature every time output falls below trend. It does not preemptively alter its chemistry in response. A bond trader can. So can a wage negotiator, a bank, or a company deciding whether a cost increase is temporary or likely to become embedded.

In 1977, Finn Kydland and Edward Prescott made the point in a different form. Their paper “Rules Rather than Discretion: The Inconsistency of Optimal Plans” argued that economic planning is not a game against nature. It is a game involving people whose behavior depends on what they expect policymakers to do.

The phrase “rules rather than discretion” can sound like a simple ideological preference for mechanical policy. The underlying problem is more general: a plan that looks optimal before people act may not remain optimal once their actions are fixed. If everyone knows the policymaker will later have an incentive to change course, they can anticipate that change at the beginning. The promise loses force.

Consider a government that wants to encourage investment in a particular activity. Before companies invest, it promises favorable treatment. Once the factories are built and the capital cannot easily move, the temptation to tax or regulate the activity more heavily may increase. If investors anticipate this, the original promise is worth less. The government does not need to break its promise many times. The expectation that it might do so can change behavior before the first factory is built.

The policy is partly the public's model of future policy.

Central banking makes this especially visible because expectations are woven directly into many financial prices. The interest rate on a long-term loan is not only about today's policy rate. It contains judgments about inflation, future short rates, risk, and the credibility of the institutions making policy. A central bank can move one overnight rate with an announcement, but the rates that households and firms face across years reflect a wider argument about what comes next.

This is why credibility became such a powerful word in monetary policy.

Credibility is not the same as popularity. It is not even the same as being believed in every statement. It is the degree to which people think a policy commitment will survive the moments when abandoning it becomes tempting.

The United States in the late 1970s provides a concrete case of an institution trying to change not only current financial conditions but expectations about its future behavior.

Inflation had risen through the decade. Federal Reserve histories of the period describe policymakers struggling with the combination of inflation, weak growth, energy shocks, and the political cost of tighter policy. The central bank raised rates at times, but a gradualist strategy had not convinced markets or the public that inflation would be brought under control.

Paul Volcker became Federal Reserve chairman in August 1979. On October 6, after an unscheduled meeting of the Federal Open Market Committee, he announced a change in operating procedures. The Fed would place greater emphasis on controlling bank reserves and monetary aggregates rather than keeping the federal funds rate within the narrower day-to-day range that had characterized previous operating practice.

The technical details mattered. So did the signal.

A Federal Reserve historical study of the October 1979 reform says the committee had concluded that its earlier gradualist approach was inadequate for fighting inflation and reversing inflation expectations. The new plan needed to allow the possibility of substantial increases in short-term interest rates and convince financial-market participants that the change was real.

The phrase “convince financial-market participants” is the second order in plain language.

The Fed was not merely choosing a current setting. It was trying to alter the model that other people carried of the Fed.

That effort was costly. Interest rates became highly volatile. The economy experienced severe recessions in the early 1980s, and unemployment rose sharply. The political pressure on the central bank was intense. Any tidy retelling that turns the episode into a painless demonstration of credibility misses what credibility required: policymakers had to continue tightening even after the costs became obvious enough to create a strong temptation to retreat.

A promise becomes credible partly through what an institution is willing to endure to keep it.

Inflation eventually fell substantially. Federal Reserve historical accounts treat the persistence of the Volcker-era tightening as central to restoring credibility for lower inflation. The episode remains debated in its details—oil prices, fiscal policy, monetary aggregates, labor-market changes, and recession all matter—but the basic second-order point does not depend on a single causal story.

People's expectations about policy were themselves an object of policy.

This creates a strange problem for evaluation.

Suppose a central bank announces a forceful anti-inflation regime and inflation expectations fall before the full mechanical effect of tighter policy arrives. Was the policy effective because the public believed it? Yes, at least partly. But if expectations fall quickly enough, the policy may not need to be as tight for as long as a model based on the old regime predicted.

The forecast changes because the forecast was used to design a credible intervention.

The reverse can happen too. A government announces a temporary stimulus, but households expect taxes to rise later or doubt that the policy will persist. The behavioral response can differ from the response estimated during earlier episodes. A regulator promises that a bailout rule will be strict, but market participants expect exceptions in a crisis. The expectation of rescue can affect risk-taking before any rescue occurs.

Rules acquire reputations.

Once a reputation exists, each new policy decision is interpreted partly as evidence about the rule.

This means that governments are always doing two things when they act. They are changing today's conditions, and they are updating the public's estimate of how government behaves.

A one-time exception is therefore rarely only one time.

Imagine a city that says a zoning rule will be enforced strictly but grants a politically connected developer an exception. The immediate consequence is one project. The second-order consequence is that every future developer updates the probability that lobbying can change the rule. Land prices can move. Legal strategies change. Campaign contributions may become more valuable. The exception teaches the system how rigid the rule really is.

The same logic appears in tax amnesties. A government may offer one to collect unpaid tax quickly. If taxpayers come to expect future amnesties, the immediate revenue gain can alter future compliance incentives. A policy designed to solve yesterday's noncompliance can help shape tomorrow's.

It appears in debt guarantees. A government rescues an institution to prevent a broader collapse. The rescue may be justified. It may also teach creditors and managers something about which losses will be socialized in the next crisis.

It appears in trade policy. Firms do not respond only to today's tariff. They invest according to their beliefs about whether the tariff is temporary, retaliatory, negotiable, or the beginning of a durable new regime.

Policy is a message even when nobody writes the message down.

That does not mean policymakers should never make exceptions. Mechanical consistency can be foolish. A rule designed for normal times may be destructive in a crisis. A central bank that refuses to provide liquidity because it fears future moral hazard can turn a solvency problem into a system-wide collapse. A regulator that applies yesterday's rule after technology changes can preserve consistency at the expense of reality.

The problem is not discretion itself.

The problem is discretion without an account of how today's exception changes tomorrow's expectations.

Kydland and Prescott's argument is often treated as a case for commitment devices: rules, independent institutions, contracts, reputations, or other arrangements that make certain future deviations harder. Commitment works by changing what other actors believe is possible.

A constitution is partly a commitment technology. So is an independent central bank. So is a long-term contract with penalties for exit. So is an automatic fiscal rule. So is a company promising customers that old data will not be used for a new purpose and creating technical controls that make the promise costly to break.

The more credible the constraint, the more behavior can change before enforcement is needed.

This is one of the quiet powers of institutions. A good rule can govern through anticipation.

But commitment has a price: it binds future decision-makers who may know more than current ones. The rule that prevents opportunistic reversal can also prevent intelligent adaptation.

This is why policy design tends to oscillate between two fears. One fear is opportunism: if officials retain discretion, people will not trust promises. The other is rigidity: if officials bind themselves too tightly, they will be unable to respond when the world changes.

Second-order design lives between them.

A robust institution needs enough commitment that people can plan around it and enough adaptability that the rule can survive contact with new evidence.

This sounds like a contradiction until we separate the object of commitment from the instrument.

An institution can commit strongly to an objective while allowing instruments to change. A central bank can commit to price stability without promising a fixed interest rate forever. A safety regulator can commit to a risk standard while updating the approved testing method. A platform can commit to privacy constraints while changing how a particular feature is implemented.

The important question is what people need to be able to predict.

If everyone needs to know the exact future action, flexibility is low. If they mainly need confidence about the objective, process, or boundary conditions, the institution has more room to adapt.

This is why process credibility can sometimes substitute for policy rigidity.

A government may not be able to promise the exact tax rate ten years from now. It can make the process for changing taxes transparent, slow, legally constrained, and subject to public debate. A regulator may not promise never to revise a rule, but it can promise notice, evidence, transition periods, and consistent treatment. Predictability can come from procedure rather than outcome.

The distinction matters because people learn procedures too.

Markets learn how a central bank reacts to inflation surprises. Companies learn which regulatory violations receive waivers. Citizens learn whether a nominally independent agency changes course after political criticism. Contractors learn whether deadlines are real. Students learn whether a professor's stated late policy survives the first sympathetic request.

Every repeated interaction estimates the rule.

The economy does this at scale.

That is why policies can have effects before formal implementation. If a credible future regulation is announced, firms may invest in compliance early. If a tax change is expected, transactions can move across the effective date. If investors believe a central bank will tighten next year, longer-term rates and exchange rates can move today. The future rule becomes part of the current environment.

The more forward-looking the decision, the more important the expected rule becomes.

A family buying groceries can change behavior quickly when policy changes. A company building a semiconductor fabrication plant is making a commitment across years. Its decision depends on an entire imagined path of energy prices, trade rules, subsidies, taxes, demand, and geopolitics. The company is not merely responding to policy. It is forecasting policymakers.

This complicates the idea that uncertainty is always reduced by flexibility. From the policymaker's side, discretion preserves options. From the investor's side, discretion can widen the set of plausible futures.

The same freedom has different value depending on which side of the rule you occupy.

One reason institutions publish frameworks, reaction functions, forward guidance, rulemaking calendars, and long-term targets is to narrow that uncertainty without surrendering all freedom. They are trying to make some aspects of future behavior legible.

Legibility has second-order effects too.

If a central bank explains exactly which data will trigger a response, markets may begin trading aggressively around those data. If a regulator publishes a threshold, companies can optimize to the threshold. If a government announces a subsidy formula, projects can be designed to satisfy the formula rather than the underlying policy purpose.

Transparency helps people plan. It also helps people game.

This tension cannot be eliminated. An opaque rule reduces direct optimization but increases uncertainty and concentrates advantage among insiders who are better at inferring how decisions are actually made. A transparent rule democratizes the model of the institution but makes strategic response easier.

The right amount of transparency depends on what adaptation you want.

That is a more useful question than whether transparency is good in the abstract.

The same is true of credibility. We often speak as if credibility were simply a stock an institution should maximize. But credibility attached to a bad commitment can make harm more efficient. A government that credibly promises to defend an unsustainable exchange rate may induce actors to build positions around a policy that eventually breaks. A company that becomes famous for never changing course can transform consistency into stubbornness.

Credibility is an amplifier. It increases the degree to which expectations respond to announced policy.

The value depends on what is being amplified.

Thomas Sargent's study “The Ends of Four Big Inflations” examined historical episodes in which very high inflation ended after broader policy regimes changed. The cases were not the United States of the Volcker era; they involved postwar European inflations. Sargent's argument emphasized that ending inflation required credible changes in the policy regime, including fiscal arrangements, rather than merely a temporary tightening.

The historical details differ sharply across those countries, and the work has generated decades of debate. Its relevance here is conceptual. If inflation is partly sustained by expectations that the old policy process will continue, a durable regime change can matter differently from a one-off action with the same immediate mechanical size.

People do not only ask what the government did.

They ask what kind of government does this now.

That is a second-order variable.

The same logic is increasingly important in technology governance. An AI company that announces a safety threshold is not only describing its current system. Researchers, competitors, regulators, and customers begin modeling the company's future behavior around that threshold. If the company repeatedly waives the rule near product launches, observers learn the real rule. If it delays launches when the threshold is crossed, the commitment becomes more credible. If the threshold itself is easy to optimize against, internal teams may begin building for the test rather than the underlying safety objective.

The economy learning the rule and the metric eating the mission are not separate stories. They are layers of the same adaptive process.

People infer what is rewarded. They infer what will be enforced. They infer what exceptions are available. Then they act.

The policy designer sees the resulting behavior and updates the rule.

Then everyone updates again.

This loop means that policy should be evaluated not only by its direct effect but by the equilibrium it teaches.

A generous emergency rescue may be correct today and still require a new resolution regime tomorrow to prevent the rescue from becoming the expected default. A temporary tax incentive may accelerate investment and still need a credible sunset to avoid turning every future investment cycle into a lobbying contest. A regulator may wisely use discretion in an exceptional case and still need to explain the principle that made the exception exceptional.

Explanation is part of policy because people are estimating the rule.

The best institutions know this.

They do not assume every audience fully understands the model. They assume consequential actors are learning from repeated decisions. They think about precedent. They separate objectives from instruments. They build procedures for changing rules. They know that a promise without a mechanism for keeping it is weaker than a promise backed by institutional cost.

They also know that being predictable is not the same as being frozen.

This is the deeper lesson of the policy debates that began to crystallize in the 1970s. An economy is not simply a collection of equations awaiting control. It is a population of model builders, most of them informal, all of them trying to make decisions under uncertainty.

Policy enters those models.

The more important the policy, the more effort people spend forecasting it.

So every durable rule has two targets: the immediate variable it is trying to move and the expectation about future behavior it is trying to create.

Ignore the second target and the first may not stay where you put it.
