# Rankings Build the Thing They Rank

A ranking looks like a report card issued after the fact.

The objects already exist. Universities have faculty, students, libraries, laboratories, traditions, debts, strengths, weaknesses, and reputations. Somebody gathers information, chooses a formula, sorts the institutions, and prints the result. The ranking appears to arrive at the end of the process, after the real work has happened.

Then the institutions read it.

So do applicants. Donors read it. Trustees read it. Employers read it. Journalists read it. Alumni read it. Deans read it. Competing deans read it. A number that began as an attempt to compare institutions becomes part of the environment in which those institutions compete.

The next class is recruited under the shadow of the previous rank.

The next budget is argued over with the previous rank in the room.

The next strategic plan contains an implicit or explicit theory of how the institution can move.

The ranking does not merely describe a field. It helps organize the field.

Wendy Nelson Espeland and Michael Sauder spent years studying this process in American legal education. Their 2007 article on rankings and reactivity used law schools to make a broader point: public measures can change the people and organizations being measured. They identified two especially important mechanisms. One was self-fulfilling prophecy. A ranking changes beliefs and behavior in ways that can help produce the differences the ranking claims to reveal. The other was commensuration, the conversion of different qualities into a common metric that makes comparison easier and changes what people pay attention to.

A law school is not naturally a single number.

It is a collection of classrooms, clinics, professors, students, professional networks, geographic ties, institutional missions, debt burdens, hiring outcomes, libraries, journals, alumni, and legal cultures. Much of what matters is difficult to compare directly. A ranking makes the field legible by forcing these differences into a common scale.

That is useful.

It is also consequential.

Once the scale is public, the objects being ranked begin to see themselves through it.

A school that had thought of itself as regionally important may begin comparing itself primarily with institutions several positions above and below. An admissions officer can no longer treat median test scores as one descriptive characteristic among many if those scores have visible ranking consequences. A dean considering where to place scarce scholarship money may have to think about how that decision affects both enrollment and measured selectivity. Faculty hiring, expenditures, employment statistics, class size, and reporting practices can all acquire a second meaning: they matter for the school, and they matter for the representation of the school.

The representation enters management.

This is one reason rankings are different from ordinary descriptions.

If a historian writes that one law school had an unusually influential faculty in 1985, administrators can disagree without changing next year's budget. If a widely watched ranking places the school at number thirty-eight, the statement comes with an immediate comparison set, a public status signal, and the possibility of movement. The number is portable. It travels into board meetings and applicant conversations. It can be copied into headlines and fundraising letters.

A ranking compresses complexity into a form that can move through institutions faster than the complexity itself.

That portability is part of its power.

It also creates a loop.

Suppose applicants treat a higher-ranked school as more desirable. More applicants make the school more selective. Greater selectivity can improve some inputs to the next ranking. A stronger incoming class may improve outcomes, employer interest, bar performance, or peer perceptions. Those changes can support the next rank.

The initial ranking has helped create conditions that make the ranking easier to justify later.

This does not mean the first ranking was arbitrary.

The school may genuinely have been excellent. The ranking may have detected real differences. The second-order point is that measurement can amplify those differences once the measure becomes socially consequential.

James Monks and Ronald Ehrenberg examined this dynamic empirically in the late 1990s using selective private colleges. Their NBER study found that less favorable U.S. News rankings were associated with weaker admissions outcomes: schools accepted a greater share of applicants, a smaller share of admitted students enrolled, and the resulting entering class had lower average SAT scores. Institutions also responded through financial aid, offering more generous grant aid rather than simply lowering the sticker price.

The direction matters.

A ranking did not only tell students something about a college. It changed student behavior enough to alter the admissions environment the college faced. The institution then changed its own pricing behavior in response.

The report card became a market signal.

The market signal changed the institution.

A decade later, Nicholas Bowman and Michael Bastedo found another version of the effect. Using admissions data from top-tier colleges, they reported that moving onto the front page of the U.S. News rankings produced a substantial improvement in following-year admissions indicators. They also found that movement within the top tier mattered especially for institutions near the top.

The number changed attention.

Attention changed applications.

Applications changed selectivity.

Selectivity could then become part of the next number.

This is a self-reinforcing structure, but it should not be mistaken for a perfect one.

Rankings move for many reasons. Applicants care about price, geography, programs, family ties, labor markets, campus culture, aid, safety, athletics, and thousands of details that no league table can fully absorb. Institutions differ in how exposed they are to ranking pressure. A local school serving working adults may care less about national prestige than a residential university competing for students across the country. Some rankings matter enormously to one field and barely at all to another.

The mechanism is conditional.

It becomes strongest when the audience believes the ranking, when actors have choices, and when the ranked institution depends on those actors.

A ranking without an audience is a spreadsheet.

A ranking with an audience is a coordination device.

It tells applicants where to look. It tells employers what counts as prestigious. It tells trustees what peers to compare against. It tells administrators which direction is “up.” It tells journalists which institutions deserve attention when a story needs an example of excellence or decline.

That coordination can create real value.

Comparison is hard. A student choosing among two hundred institutions cannot personally investigate every classroom, professor, graduation outcome, housing policy, aid package, and alumni network. A ranking lowers search costs. It can expose institutions that perform badly on outcomes they would rather discuss vaguely. It can reward genuine improvement. It can force organizations to collect information they had never bothered to measure.

Transparency is not the enemy here.

The problem is that a public ranking does two jobs at once.

It informs outsiders.

It governs insiders.

Those jobs are related but not identical.

A ranking can be informative for a student even while producing distorted incentives for an institution. The fact that applicants respond rationally to the ranking does not mean the ranking captures everything that matters. The fact that administrators optimize against it does not mean every optimization is corrupt.

Some optimization is improvement.

A school whose poor employment results damage its rank may invest more heavily in career services, strengthen employer relationships, redesign curricula, or collect better outcome data. A hospital that drops in a quality ranking may improve infection control. A city embarrassed by a safety ranking may fix dangerous intersections. Competitive comparison can make complacency more expensive.

The harder question is whether the easiest path to a higher rank also improves the underlying object.

That path depends on the formula.

Every ranking contains weights.

Even when the weights are presented as neutral methodology, they are a theory of value. One measure receives ten percent, another twenty. Some dimensions are excluded because they are hard to observe. Others receive proxies. A ranking may count expenditures without knowing whether the money was useful. It may count selectivity without knowing whether exclusion itself improves education. It may use peer reputation, which can lag reality or become influenced by prior rankings. It may use employment outcomes that depend heavily on regional labor markets.

The formula is not merely measuring quality.

It is deciding which forms of quality are legible.

Once institutions know the formula, legibility becomes strategic.

If class size matters, class size becomes a budget question. If median test scores matter, the admissions office faces pressure around the median. If expenditures matter, accounting categories matter. If employment rates matter, the definition of employment matters. If reputation matters, visibility to survey respondents matters.

The organization begins allocating effort around the measurement architecture.

This is the same basic problem as the metric in the previous chapters, but rankings add competition.

A target says, “Reach this number.”

A ranking says, “Beat those people.”

That difference matters because a ranking is positional.

Not everyone can be first.

If every school improves equally on the underlying objective, the rank order may barely move. Institutions can therefore experience real improvement without receiving the visible status reward. A school can graduate more students, reduce debt, improve teaching, and still fall because peers improved faster or because the methodology changed.

This makes ranking competition more psychologically intense than many absolute targets.

There is no finish line.

The relevant comparison is the moving field.

Sauder and Espeland's later work on the discipline of rankings described how law schools became tightly coupled to the ranking process. They emphasized surveillance and normalization: administrators watched their position, watched competitors, and internalized the ranking as a standard through which legal education itself was interpreted.

That internalization is more important than direct coercion.

Nobody has to order a dean to care.

If applicants care, trustees care.

If trustees care, the dean cares.

If employers care, students care.

If students care, admissions offices care.

The ranking can govern without having legal authority over anyone.

Its power travels through dependent relationships.

That is why refusing to participate can be difficult.

An institution may dislike the methodology and still fear that nonparticipation will be interpreted as weakness. The more competitors participate, the more absence itself becomes information. Even a critic may need to track the ranking closely enough to explain why it should be ignored.

Resistance can therefore deepen attention.

The school says the number is bad while building a committee to monitor the number.

This is a peculiar form of institutional capture: the measure can become central even to people who distrust it.

The effect reaches beyond administrators.

Rankings can reshape categories of aspiration.

Before a league table becomes dominant, a student may think in terms of fit, geography, price, program strength, or family tradition. After rankings become a common language, “top ten,” “top twenty-five,” and “top fifty” become categories that feel natural. Employers can adopt the same thresholds. Donors can. Faculty candidates can.

The categories then create cliffs.

The difference between twenty-four and twenty-six may be tiny in the underlying data while the social interpretation is much larger because one institution is “top twenty-five” and the other is not.

That discontinuity can change behavior out of proportion to the measured difference.

A ranking converts a smooth distribution into named status groups.

Once those groups are meaningful, people act as though the boundaries reveal something real.

Sometimes they do.

Often they reveal the structure of the list.

This is commensuration in practice. Different institutions become comparable because a ranking translates them into a common language. The translation enables decisions, but it also erases texture.

A school that is extraordinary for one kind of student may be mediocre in the aggregate formula. A regional institution with unusually strong employer ties may look weak next to a national brand. A program optimized for affordability may be penalized by a system that rewards expenditures or selectivity. A university with a broad public mission may appear inefficient beside one that serves a narrower population.

The ranking does not have to be malicious to produce this flattening.

Compression always discards information.

The danger begins when discarded information becomes institutionally invisible because the ranking is easier to discuss than the thing itself.

Boards can ask why the school fell three places more easily than they can ask whether students are becoming better lawyers.

The first question has a number attached.

The second requires judgment.

Numbers win meetings.

This creates an asymmetry in management. Activities that affect the ranking can be defended in a sentence. Activities that matter to the mission but not the ranking need a story.

The story may be better.

It is still harder to budget around.

Consider scholarship aid.

An institution can use aid to expand access, reduce debt, recruit a more diverse class, support a public-service mission, or attract students whose measurable credentials improve the entering profile. Those goals can overlap. They can also conflict.

Once ranking consequences become salient, the aid budget acquires a positional component. A dollar is not only helping a student. It may also be helping the institution win a student away from a peer, protect a median, or improve yield.

The same money now operates in two systems: education and ranking competition.

That second system can become expensive.

If every institution spends more to protect relative position, the field can end up in an arms race in which large sums move students among institutions without producing equivalent gains in education. Each individual decision can be rational. The collective result can still be wasteful.

This is another recurring second-order pattern.

Competition around a measure can create costs that the measure does not record.

Rankings also create opportunities for strategic reporting.

Whenever categories carry consequences, institutions have incentives to understand category boundaries. Which students count? Which expenditures are included? Which employment outcomes qualify? Which reporting period matters? How are joint programs treated? What happens when data are missing?

Some of this is ordinary compliance.

Some is aggressive interpretation.

Some becomes deception.

The important point is that ambiguity in the ranking method is not neutral once position is valuable. The ambiguity acquires a shadow price.

A school can invest in improving reality, invest in improving the representation of reality, or do both.

Those activities are difficult to distinguish from the outside.

That is why verification matters.

A ranking provider that wants its measure to survive contact with institutions has to expect strategic response. It needs definitions that are hard to manipulate, independent audits where stakes are high, methodological revision when a proxy becomes corrupted, and enough transparency for users to understand what movement in the rank actually means.

But transparency itself creates a tradeoff.

If the methodology is fully public, institutions can optimize against it more precisely. If it is opaque, users cannot evaluate the ranking and insiders with more resources may still reverse-engineer the system.

There is no design in which adaptation disappears.

The question is whether the adaptation becomes informative.

A good ranking should make the cheapest route upward look as much as possible like genuine improvement in the thing users care about.

That is difficult because users do not all care about the same thing.

One student cares about debt. Another cares about research opportunities. Another needs a school near home. Another wants a specific specialty. Another cares about class size. Another wants access to a national employer network. A single ordinal list implies a common objective where the underlying decision is heterogeneous.

Personalized comparison would solve part of this problem.

Instead of asking which school is number one, the user could ask which school is best on the dimensions that matter to this decision. That reduces the ranking's false universality.

But institutions would still respond to whatever dimensions become visible and consequential.

There is no neutral scoreboard once the scoreboard affects behavior.

The deeper issue is status.

Status is not merely a belief floating above material reality. It changes access.

A prestigious school can attract stronger applicants because it is prestigious. It can recruit faculty more easily. Alumni may give more. Employers may visit more often. Journalists may call its professors. Those advantages can improve the institution in ways that make the prestige less arbitrary over time.

Status accumulates capital.

This is why rankings can harden an existing hierarchy even while presenting themselves as fresh measurements each year.

A high rank can produce resources that help preserve the high rank.

A low rank can make improvement harder by reducing the very inputs needed to improve.

The feedback is not absolute. Institutions rise and fall. Methodologies change. New programs emerge. Local strengths can overcome national weakness. Students sometimes discover value that rankings underweight.

Still, a ranking can become part of the production function of the thing it ranks.

That should change how we interpret persistence.

If the same institutions remain near the top year after year, one explanation is durable quality. Another is durable advantage reinforced by the ranking's own effects. Usually both are present.

The causal arrow runs in both directions.

Quality affects rank.

Rank affects quality-producing resources.

That makes the ranking harder to validate than it first appears.

A naïve validation test might ask whether highly ranked institutions later attract better students or produce stronger outcomes. But if the rank itself helps attract those students and resources, later superiority does not cleanly prove that the initial ranking was merely descriptive.

The forecast has entered the world again.

This is why the most interesting question about a ranking is not whether it is “right.”

It is what kind of system it creates when people act as though it is right.

Does it reward real improvement or cosmetic movement?

Does it lower search costs or narrow attention too aggressively?

Does it create useful competition or a positional arms race?

Does it help outsiders see institutions more clearly or teach institutions to perform for outsiders?

Does it make hidden differences legible, or does it make legible differences the only differences that receive resources?

A ranking can do all of these at once.

The answer will change as the participants learn the rule.

That is the part rankings rarely display.

The published list shows this year's order.

It does not show the committees formed because of last year's order, the scholarships reallocated, the admissions thresholds protected, the categories reinterpreted, the employers influenced, the applicants redirected, or the strategic plans rewritten.

Those responses are hidden in the next dataset.

By the time the ranking arrives again, some of the evidence it uses has already been shaped by the previous ranking.

The list is reading its own wake.

That does not make it useless.

It makes it causal.
