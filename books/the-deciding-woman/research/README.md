# The Deciding Woman — research constitution

## Core question

For each ten-year interval in the first 250 years of the United States, which single woman’s actions inside that interval produced the greatest downstream consequence for American life?

The intervals are birthday decades, not calendar decades: `[1776-07-04, 1786-07-04)`, continuing in ten-year increments through `[2016-07-04, 2026-07-04)`.

## Selection criteria

The manuscript uses five dimensions as an argumentative discipline rather than a fake numerical science.

1. **Reach** — number and breadth of people, institutions, territories, markets, laws, or practices touched.
2. **Depth** — magnitude of the change for those touched.
3. **Durability** — persistence of the downstream consequence.
4. **Counterfactual force** — how much slower, smaller, or less likely the change appears without the individual’s specific action.
5. **National signal** — whether the action changed expectations about citizenship, women, government, culture, rights, science, or national identity.

A candidate does not need to score highest on all five. Each chapter must state which dimensions carry the verdict.

## Evidence hierarchy

Prefer, in order:

- contemporaneous primary sources;
- government, archival, library, museum, university, court, and institutional records;
- peer-reviewed scholarship and serious specialist histories;
- reputable biographies and historical syntheses;
- journalism for later periods when reporting itself is part of the evidentiary record.

Avoid using modern inspirational summaries as proof of contested causal claims.

## Narrative rules

- No invented dialogue, interior monologue, sensory detail, or composite scene.
- If an anecdote survives mainly because it became legend, label the legend and then state the documentary minimum.
- Do not attribute a movement’s work to one woman merely because she became its symbol.
- Name collaborators and predecessors when the causal chain depends on them.
- Distinguish action during the decade from lifetime reputation accumulated later.
- Do not count a title such as “first woman to…” as sufficient evidence of consequence.
- Importance is not moral endorsement.

## Falsification rules

A chapter’s winner should be replaced or qualified if deeper research shows any of the following:

- the central action occurred outside the designated window;
- the familiar story materially exaggerates her role;
- a runner-up had substantially greater reach, depth, durability, and counterfactual force;
- the claimed downstream effect is mostly symbolic while a rival changed law, survival, institutional capacity, or mass behavior;
- the chapter depends on a causal link not supported by primary or specialist sources;
- credit properly belongs to a collective in a way that makes the single-person lens misleading rather than illuminating.

## Novelty discipline

A preliminary title/concept search did not surface an obvious trade history using this exact device: one causally argued woman for each of the United States’ twenty-five birthday decades. That is not enough to claim “the first book ever” or “never before written.” Any marketing claim of bibliographic originality requires a fuller catalog search across Library of Congress, WorldCat, major publishers, booksellers, and scholarly databases.

The safer novelty claim is structural: this manuscript uses a deliberately forced 25-decade, one-winner-per-window causal method and makes the runner-up argument part of every chapter.

## Opening evidence already checked

### Esther de Berdt Reed
Library of Congress material describes Reed’s 1780 leadership of a systematic Philadelphia women’s canvass for Washington’s poorly provisioned troops, the circulation of *Sentiments of an American Woman*, and the spread of related drives to other states. Use this to establish the first chapter’s institutional-network argument. Do not inflate depreciated paper-currency totals into modern-dollar equivalents without specialist economic conversion.

### Ona Judge
National Park Service material documents Judge’s May 21, 1796 escape from the presidential household, Washington’s attempts to recover her, resistance by officials and allies in New Hampshire, and her continued freedom. Use the episode to expose the collision between revolutionary liberty, slavery, federal authority, and an enslaved woman’s self-emancipation. Do not claim Judge herself changed federal fugitive-slave law; her importance in the chapter is as a direct test of the founding regime’s contradiction.

### Sacagawea
National Park Service material specifically warns against the simplified “guide who led the expedition” legend. The journals support narrower but consequential roles: her presence signaled peaceful intent, her language and geographic knowledge helped contacts with the Shoshone, and the expedition’s access to horses mattered to crossing the mountains. Preserve that correction in the chapter.

### Dolley Madison
White House Historical Association material supports her role as a skilled hostess whose drawing rooms helped build Washington social life and political influence. The famous 1814 portrait story should be phrased carefully: she directed workers to save the Gilbert Stuart portrait before fleeing rather than personally carrying it out.

### Emma Willard
Library of Congress material supports the 1821 relocation/renaming of her school as Troy Female Seminary and her curricular innovation in geography and history. The chapter should connect women’s advanced education to the republic’s later professional and reform capacity without pretending one school created mass female education alone.

### Frances Willard
Library of Congress material describes the WCTU as the largest women’s organization in the United States by the late nineteenth century and Willard as its president from 1879 to 1898. The chapter must also investigate racial conflict, temperance coercion, and Ida B. Wells’s criticism so organizational scale is not confused with uncomplicated progress.

### Frances Perkins
The U.S. Department of Labor identifies Perkins as the first woman Cabinet member and principal architect of the Social Security Act, with major roles in wage-and-hour and labor policy. The chapter’s high bar is to distinguish her individual design and coalition work from Roosevelt, Congress, organized labor, and other New Deal actors.

### Rosa Parks
National Park Service material documents Parks as an experienced NAACP member, her December 1, 1955 refusal, and the mass boycott that followed. The chapter must reject the “tired seamstress accidentally starts a movement” simplification and name Claudette Colvin, Jo Ann Robinson, E. D. Nixon, the Women’s Political Council, the Montgomery Improvement Association, litigants in *Browder v. Gayle*, and thousands of boycotters.

### Nancy Pelosi
U.S. House historical records confirm Pelosi became the first woman Speaker on January 4, 2007. The chapter’s importance case cannot rest on firstness alone; later drafting must test her role in major legislation, agenda control, caucus discipline, and the institutional normalization of a woman holding one of the federal government’s most powerful constitutional offices.

### Kizzmekia Corbett
NIH and peer-reviewed records identify Corbett as a Vaccine Research Center scientist in the team led by Barney Graham that designed a stabilized SARS-CoV-2 spike immunogen and collaborated with Moderna; Corbett is first author on the 2020 *Nature* paper describing the mRNA vaccine design. The chapter must be explicit that vaccine development was team science involving NIH, Moderna, academic collaborators, trial participants, manufacturers, regulators, health workers, and decades of prior coronavirus and mRNA research. Her selection is an argument about consequential contribution, not sole invention.
