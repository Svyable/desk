*The Most Important Woman of Every American Decade, 1776–2026*

**Twenty-five women. Twenty-five arguments. One country remade in public and in private.**

Most books about important women begin with a roster. *The Deciding Woman* begins with a fight over the roster.

The premise is intentionally severe: divide the first 250 years of the United States into twenty-five ten-year birthday windows beginning July 4, 1776. For each window, choose exactly one woman whose actions inside that interval most changed the trajectory of American life. No repeats. No lifetime-achievement prizes. No requirement that the winner be admirable, famous, politically convenient, or even primarily remembered as a women’s-history figure.

This is therefore not a hall of fame. It is a book of causal arguments.

“Important” means downstream consequence. Each chapter tests five things: **reach** (how many lives or institutions changed), **depth** (how much they changed), **durability** (how long the change lasted), **counterfactual force** (how differently events might have unfolded without her), and **national signal** (whether her action changed what Americans believed a woman, a citizen, an institution, or the country itself could be). Fame is evidence only when fame itself became a mechanism of consequence.

The result is a deliberately disputable alternative history of the United States. It begins not with Abigail Adams but with Esther de Berdt Reed, whose 1780 organizing turned private female patriotism into a coordinated wartime network. It moves through an enslaved woman who forced George Washington to confront the limits of presidential power over her body; a Shoshone teenager whose presence changed the diplomacy and survival odds of the Corps of Discovery; women who built schools, movements, hospitals, regulatory systems, voting campaigns, social insurance, civil-rights flashpoints, environmental law, judicial power, mass media, congressional power, and vaccine science.

The last chapter refuses the easy temptation to award the decade merely to the woman who held the highest office. It asks whether Kizzmekia Corbett’s role in the team that designed the stabilized SARS-CoV-2 spike used in the Moderna vaccine produced a larger direct consequence for American survival than any symbolic or electoral breakthrough of the same period. That is exactly the kind of argument the book is built to provoke.

The manuscript does not claim that one person “caused” a decade. Every chapter includes the coalition, institution, adversary, predecessor, and runner-up that make the individual story honest. The one-woman rule is a narrative instrument: a forced choice that makes causality visible by making the reader argue back.

## Editorial contract

1. **Importance is not goodness.** A chapter may name a woman whose legacy is morally mixed or whose movement caused harms alongside gains.
2. **No invented scenes.** Reconstructed narrative detail must have a documentary source. Unknown thoughts remain unknown.
3. **No lone-genius history.** Every chapter names the coalition, institution, adversary, and structural conditions around the central figure.
4. **No present-day moral laundering.** Historical actors are neither excused by their period nor flattened into current slogans.
5. **No “first woman” shortcut.** Being first matters only when the breakthrough altered access, power, precedent, or expectations downstream.
6. **One winner, visible dissent.** Each chapter states the strongest runner-up and the case against the chosen woman.
7. **Birthday decades, not calendar decades.** Windows run from one July 4 anniversary to the next ten-year anniversary, beginning July 4, 1776 and ending July 4, 2026.
8. **The reader should argue back.** Disagreement is part of the design. A successful chapter leaves the reader with a sharper model of consequence even when they reject the verdict.
