## The rule

Choose one woman.

Not one woman for the Revolution, one for abolition, one for science, one for politics, one for business, one for culture. That would be sensible. It would also let us avoid the question.

Choose one woman for every ten years America has existed.

Twenty-five birthday decades. Twenty-five names. No repeats.

Then defend every choice as though another historian is already standing up to object.

That is this book.

The first surprise is that “most important” is harder than “greatest.” Greatness invites admiration. Importance asks what moved because a person was there. A saint can be less important than an operator. A beloved symbol can matter less than a bureaucrat who changed the rules of retirement. A president’s wife can be more politically consequential before women can vote than a famous reformer whose argument arrives before the machinery exists to carry it. A scientist inside a team can matter more to a decade’s body count than the most recognizable woman in the country.

Importance is not a medal. It is a claim about consequence.

That distinction matters because American history has repeatedly converted women into symbols after first denying them ordinary agency. We remember the seamstress, the first lady, the nurse, the guide, the schoolteacher, the television host. The nouns are often accurate and still too small. They describe how a woman was legible to her era, not what her actions did to the era.

This book tries a different unit of measurement.

For each ten-year window I ask five questions.

**Reach:** How many people, institutions, or decisions did her action touch?

**Depth:** Did it change a preference, or did it change a rule, a route, a market, a body, a law, a public expectation?

**Durability:** Did the consequence vanish with the news cycle, or did it become infrastructure?

**Counterfactual force:** If she disappears from the story, how much slower, smaller, stranger, or less likely does the change become?

**National signal:** Did the action alter what Americans believed was possible—not merely for women, but for the country?

None of those questions produces a machine-readable winner. History refuses that convenience. The point of the scorecard is not to disguise judgment as mathematics. It is to discipline judgment enough that the reader can see where to attack it.

Every chapter therefore has an opponent. Sometimes it is a more famous woman. Sometimes it is the claim that no individual deserves the credit at all. The book names runners-up because a forced choice becomes dishonest if the alternatives vanish from view.

The one-woman rule also creates a second danger: lone-genius history. The women in these pages did not move America alone. Esther de Berdt Reed needed hundreds of canvassers and more than a thousand contributors. Ona Judge needed a free Black community willing to help her disappear. Sacagawea traveled with a corps, through the homelands and assistance of Native nations whose agency older histories often reduced to scenery. Rosa Parks’s refusal became history because organizers were already prepared to turn an arrest into collective action. Kizzmekia Corbett’s chapter is, by necessity, a chapter about team science.

The central figure is a lens, not a monopoly on credit.

A third danger is cleaner and more seductive: turning the book into twenty-five moral victories. That would falsify the country. Some women here held prejudices common to their time and prejudices challenged in their own time. Some movements expanded one group’s power while narrowing another’s freedom. Frances Willard’s extraordinary organizing cannot be separated from the racial politics surrounding temperance and her public conflict with Ida B. Wells. Margaret Sanger’s campaign for reproductive autonomy cannot be narrated without the later entanglements of birth-control advocacy, eugenic thought, coercion, class, and race. Political power is never pure because power is never exercised in a laboratory.

Importance is not innocence.

The final rule is chronological and slightly strange. These are not the 1780s, 1790s, 1800s, and so on. America did not begin on January 1 of a year ending in zero. The clock starts July 4, 1776. The first window runs to the eve of July 4, 1786. The twenty-fifth ends at the 250th anniversary in 2026.

That choice changes the opening.

Abigail Adams wrote “remember the ladies” in March 1776, before the United States formally declared itself. Her sentence belongs in the prologue to the country. But when the first actual American decade begins, another woman produces the larger institutional act. In 1780 Esther de Berdt Reed helps organize the Ladies Association of Philadelphia, publishes an argument for women’s patriotic political action, and builds a systematic canvassing campaign at a moment when Washington’s army is badly supplied.

That is the kind of displacement this method is designed to create.

The familiar woman does not disappear. She becomes the runner-up, the precondition, the counterargument, the shadow across the page. The unfamiliar woman has to earn the center.

By the end, the book reaches a question that could not have existed at the founding. In a decade containing the first woman vice president, the first woman to wield the House speakership across two eras, women running trillion-dollar financial institutions and strategic technology companies, and movements capable of changing national language in days, what counts as importance?

The answer offered here is Kizzmekia Corbett.

Not because she acted alone. The opposite. Because in the pandemic decade, a team-science contribution to the vaccine platform used by Moderna reached the most intimate unit of national consequence: whether millions of bodies became seriously ill, survived, returned to work, or carried protection into the next exposure.

You may reject that verdict before you reach it.

Good.

This book is not twenty-five answers.

It is twenty-five arguments about how America actually changes.
