# 1866–1876: Susan B. Anthony — Put the Defendant on the Constitution

On November 5, 1872, Susan B. Anthony voted.

The law did not accept the ballot as an ordinary act of citizenship.

That was why she cast it.

The Civil War had ended slavery as a legal institution through the Thirteenth Amendment. The Fourteenth Amendment defined national citizenship and promised equal protection. The Fifteenth prohibited denying the vote on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude—but not sex. Reconstruction rewrote the constitutional relationship between individual and nation while leaving women politically outside the electorate in most places.

The women’s-rights movement fractured over what to do with that fact.

Anthony and Elizabeth Cady Stanton opposed ratification of the Fifteenth Amendment without women’s suffrage, a position entangled with racialized rhetoric that remains part of the movement’s history and should not be polished away. Other reformers supported Black male suffrage while continuing to pursue women’s voting rights. Black women confronted racism inside suffrage organizing and sexism outside it.

There was no single clean movement advancing in one moral direction.

Then came a legal theory.

Some suffragists argued that the Fourteenth Amendment’s citizenship language already protected women’s right to vote. If women were citizens, they reasoned, voting should be among the privileges of citizenship. Instead of waiting only for a new constitutional amendment, they could test the existing one.

Anthony turned the theory into a prosecution.

In Rochester, New York, she and other women sought to register. Election officials ultimately allowed them. Anthony and thirteen other women cast ballots. Publicity encouraged other women to try registering as well. Federal authorities arrested Anthony.

The government now had a problem it had not faced in the abstract.

It had to explain, in court, why a citizen voting was a crime.

Anthony used the months before trial as a speaking tour, turning the prosecution into public argument. At trial in 1873, Justice Ward Hunt effectively directed the jury toward a guilty verdict rather than allowing the jury to decide the constitutional question in the ordinary way. Anthony was fined one hundred dollars. She refused to pay.

The case did not create women’s suffrage.

She lost.

That is precisely why it belongs here.

Political movements need ways to convert diffuse exclusion into a visible point of conflict. The ballot test did that. It placed citizenship, sex, constitutional interpretation, federal power, and the ordinary physical act of voting inside one event the press and public could understand.

Anthony made the defendant a constitutional exhibit.

Her strongest runner-up is Elizabeth Cady Stanton, whose intellectual leadership and writing remained foundational. Victoria Woodhull became the first woman to address a congressional committee on suffrage and ran for president in 1872 under conditions that made the campaign both groundbreaking and chaotic. Frances Ellen Watkins Harper offered a more inclusive critique connecting race, sex, class, and freedom and stands as a reminder that the movement’s most famous white leaders did not own the argument.

Anthony wins the decade because she weaponized procedure.

A movement can petition forever and remain outside the machinery it wants to change. A test case enters the machinery. It forces officials to register a response. It creates a record that later advocates can analyze, condemn, distinguish, or build upon.

The Supreme Court soon closed the particular constitutional route. In *Minor v. Happersett* in 1875, the Court held that citizenship did not itself confer a federal constitutional right to vote. Women would need political change through state law and, ultimately, a new constitutional amendment.

The loss clarified the battlefield.

That is an underappreciated kind of consequence.

A failed argument can be valuable when it eliminates a false shortcut.

If the Fourteenth Amendment would not be read to guarantee women the ballot, the movement had to organize around the harder task: changing voting laws and the Constitution directly.

The chapter should not turn Anthony into the sole author of the Nineteenth Amendment that would arrive nearly half a century later. She died in 1906. Generations of organizers built state campaigns, national associations, pickets, lobbying operations, publications, legal arguments, and coalitions. Black women faced disfranchisement after 1920 despite the formal sex barrier falling. Native women’s citizenship and voting rights remained contested. Asian American women encountered exclusion tied to citizenship law. The amendment was enormous and incomplete.

Anthony’s importance in this decade is narrower and more immediate.

She made women’s exclusion from the electorate criminally concrete.

The country said she could not vote.

She voted.

The country said that made her a defendant.

She used the dock as a platform.

The constitutional order was forced to answer.

**Verdict:** Susan B. Anthony wins 1866–1876 because her deliberate 1872 voting test transformed women’s disfranchisement from a petitioned grievance into a constitutional prosecution, clarifying both the power and the limits of postwar citizenship law.
