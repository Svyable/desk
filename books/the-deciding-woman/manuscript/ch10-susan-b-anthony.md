# 1866–1876: Susan B. Anthony — Put the Defendant on the Constitution

On November 5, 1872, Susan B. Anthony voted.

The law did not accept the ballot as an ordinary act of citizenship.

That was why she cast it.

The Civil War had ended slavery as a legal institution through the Thirteenth Amendment. The Fourteenth Amendment defined national citizenship and promised equal protection. The Fifteenth prohibited denying the vote on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude—but not sex. Reconstruction rewrote the constitutional relationship between individual and nation while leaving women politically outside the electorate in most places.

The women's-rights movement fractured over what to do with that fact.

Anthony and Elizabeth Cady Stanton opposed ratification of the Fifteenth Amendment without women's suffrage, a position entangled with racialized rhetoric that remains part of the movement's history and should not be polished away. Other reformers supported Black male suffrage while continuing to pursue women's voting rights. Black women confronted racism inside suffrage organizing and sexism outside it.

There was no single clean movement advancing in one moral direction.

That disorder is necessary to the chapter.

The temptation in retrospective history is to imagine suffrage as a relay race in which one generation handed a morally coherent baton to the next until the Nineteenth Amendment crossed the finish line.

The actual movement contained rival organizations, strategic disagreements, class interests, racial prejudice, regional differences, religious arguments, constitutional theories, and competing definitions of whose enfranchisement was most urgent.

Anthony was important partly because she was extraordinarily good at organizing inside that conflict.

She was also capable of contributing to it.

A serious account has to hold both facts at once.

Her partnership with Stanton had already become one of the central relationships in nineteenth-century women's-rights politics. Stanton was the more prolific theorist and writer. Anthony became an exceptional organizer, lecturer, fundraiser, network-builder, and political operator. The partnership distributed labor.

One wrote.

One traveled.

One developed arguments.

One kept the movement moving.

The distinction is imperfect, but useful.

Movements require both ideas and operations.

Anthony's historical force often came from turning a claim into a procedure.

After the Civil War, one such claim emerged from the new constitutional amendments.

Some suffragists argued that the Fourteenth Amendment's citizenship language already protected women's right to vote. The theory became known as the New Departure.

If women were citizens, the argument ran, then voting should be among the privileges or immunities of citizenship protected against state interference. The Fifteenth Amendment's explicit protection against racial discrimination in voting did not necessarily mean other citizens could be excluded arbitrarily.

Instead of waiting only for a new constitutional amendment, women could assert a right they claimed already existed.

That was strategically attractive.

Constitutional amendments are difficult.

If the right was already present in the Fourteenth Amendment, the movement could move from persuasion to enforcement.

Register.

Vote.

Litigate.

Force the legal system to answer.

The theory converted citizenship into an experiment.

Anthony turned the experiment into a prosecution.

In Rochester, New York, she and other women sought to register for the 1872 election. Election inspectors debated whether they could legally accept the registrations. Anthony pressed the constitutional argument. The inspectors ultimately registered the women.

Anthony then voted in the presidential election on November 5.

The physical act was ordinary.

That was its radical quality.

She did not smash a ballot box.

She did not seize an office.

She participated in the normal ritual of citizenship while the legal system still classified the ritual as unavailable to her sex.

Civil disobedience is often most effective when the prohibited act looks harmless.

The state has to reveal the coercion required to preserve the rule.

Anthony cast a ballot.

Federal authorities arrested her.

The government now had a problem it had not faced in the abstract.

It had to explain, in court, why a citizen voting was a crime.

The prosecution made constitutional exclusion personal.

A petition can be filed away.

A defendant has a court date.

That procedural difference is why the event matters.

Anthony used the months before trial as a speaking campaign. She traveled through the area where the jury would be drawn, lecturing on the constitutional question and arguing that the government was prosecuting a citizen for exercising a right.

The government responded by moving the trial to another federal court location.

The maneuver itself reveals the political character of the case.

The courtroom was not insulated from the public campaign surrounding it.

Anthony wanted the trial to be a civic lesson.

Federal officials wanted a manageable prosecution.

At trial in 1873, Justice Ward Hunt refused to allow Anthony to testify on her own behalf under the rules then governing defendants in federal criminal cases. After the evidence and legal arguments, Hunt directed the jury toward a guilty verdict rather than leaving the central question to the jury in the ordinary way.

Anthony was convicted and fined one hundred dollars plus costs.

She refused to pay.

Hunt did not order her jailed for nonpayment, a decision that had an important practical consequence.

Without imprisonment or another final coercive order, Anthony had less opportunity to carry the case upward through habeas corpus or appeal in a way that might have brought the constitutional question before the Supreme Court through her own prosecution.

The legal system had found a way to punish without giving the movement the appellate vehicle it wanted.

Procedure answered procedure.

That is one reason the chapter title matters.

Put the defendant on the Constitution.

Anthony tried to turn herself into a test case.

The courts did not have to cooperate with the movement's preferred route.

This is a recurring reality of legal activism.

A strategic litigant can choose a claim.

Judges still control jurisdiction, procedure, remedies, and the form of the decision.

The state is not a neutral stage waiting for the movement's argument.

It is another actor.

Anthony nevertheless succeeded in creating the public record she wanted.

The trial became part of suffrage organizing. Her speeches circulated. The prosecution dramatized the contradiction between citizenship and disfranchisement. The movement could point to a woman treated as a citizen for purposes of criminal punishment but not for purposes of political participation.

That asymmetry was politically useful.

If the law can prosecute her, why can it not count her vote?

The question did not answer itself constitutionally.

It worked rhetorically because the categories felt unstable.

The instability came directly from Reconstruction.

Before the Civil War, national citizenship had been weakly defined and racially restricted in law and practice. The Supreme Court's *Dred Scott* decision had denied that Black people descended from enslaved Africans could be citizens within the Constitution's meaning. The Fourteenth Amendment repudiated that framework by establishing birthright citizenship and national protections.

Women's-rights advocates immediately had to interpret what the new citizenship meant for sex discrimination.

Reconstruction therefore opened a constitutional door without deciding how wide it was.

Anthony tried to walk through.

The Supreme Court soon narrowed the route.

In *Minor v. Happersett* in 1875, the Court considered the claim of Virginia Minor, a Missouri woman who had been denied voter registration. The Court held that women were citizens but that citizenship did not itself confer a federal constitutional right to vote.

That distinction killed the strongest version of the New Departure strategy.

Women could be citizens and still be legally excluded from voting unless constitutions or statutes changed.

The loss clarified the battlefield.

That is an underappreciated form of consequence.

A failed legal theory can save a movement from spending decades on the wrong shortcut.

Once the Fourteenth Amendment route had been rejected, suffragists had to place greater emphasis on state campaigns and a federal constitutional amendment explicitly prohibiting sex-based disfranchisement.

The problem became harder and clearer.

That clarity shaped the movement's next half-century.

Anthony's choice also reveals the strengths and limitations of rights-based constitutional strategy.

A rights claim can universalize a grievance.

Instead of saying women deserve the vote because women are morally superior, socially useful, educated, taxpaying, property-owning, or mothers of citizens, the citizenship argument said women possess political standing because they are citizens.

That is a cleaner democratic principle.

It also runs into the Constitution's allocation of election rules among states and federal provisions.

The courts can interpret citizenship narrowly.

A movement then has to change the text or political coalition.

Constitutional language does not enforce itself.

People make claims under it.

Institutions answer.

Movements revise.

Anthony's prosecution compressed that entire cycle into one decade.

Her racial politics complicate any celebratory account.

The split over the Fifteenth Amendment placed white suffragists in direct argument with abolitionists and Black activists who believed Black male enfranchisement was an urgent protection against racial terror and the restoration of white supremacy after slavery.

Anthony and Stanton sometimes used rhetoric suggesting it was intolerable for uneducated or immigrant men to receive political rights before educated white women. Such language drew on class prejudice, nativism, and racism even while they were arguing for a real democratic injustice.

Historical actors do not become morally coherent because their cause eventually wins.

Anthony could be right that sex-based disfranchisement was unjust and wrong in the way she sometimes framed competition over the ballot.

The contradiction matters because suffrage history has often been written as a story of widening democracy while minimizing which women the movement centered.

Black women had to fight two exclusions at once.

Frances Ellen Watkins Harper made this clear in movement debates. Sojourner Truth had already embodied the inseparability of race and sex. Later Black suffragists including Mary Church Terrell, Ida B. Wells, and many clubwomen would organize in a movement where white organizations sometimes tolerated segregation or ignored racial violence.

The eventual Nineteenth Amendment did not end those inequalities.

Black women in the South faced poll taxes, literacy tests, intimidation, white primaries, violence, and other forms of disfranchisement long after 1920. Native American women encountered citizenship barriers and state restrictions. Asian immigrant women were shaped by exclusion and naturalization laws that limited citizenship itself.

The sentence "women won the vote" is legally important and historically incomplete.

Anthony belongs to the incomplete story.

That does not weaken her importance.

It defines it more accurately.

She was central to a movement that expanded American democracy while carrying some of the country's existing hierarchies inside itself.

Movements are not outside society.

They are made of people formed by it.

Her strongest runner-up in the birthday window is Elizabeth Cady Stanton, whose intellectual leadership and writing remained foundational. Stanton's arguments reached across suffrage, marriage law, religion, property, and women's legal status. In a book measuring intellectual originality, Stanton could win.

Victoria Woodhull offers another remarkable case. She became the first woman to address a congressional committee on suffrage and ran for president in 1872 under conditions that made the campaign both groundbreaking and chaotic. Her public challenge to sexual and political conventions was extraordinary.

Frances Ellen Watkins Harper offered a more inclusive critique connecting race, sex, class, and freedom and stands as a reminder that the movement's most famous white leaders did not own the argument.

Why Anthony?

Because she weaponized procedure.

A movement can petition forever and remain outside the machinery it wants to change.

A test case enters the machinery.

It forces officials to register a response.

It creates a record that later advocates can analyze, condemn, distinguish, or build upon.

Anthony understood institutions as things to be used, not merely appealed to.

This was an operational insight consistent with her broader organizing style.

She traveled relentlessly.

She scheduled conventions.

She raised money.

She recruited speakers.

She cultivated newspapers.

She managed organizational disputes.

She treated reform as work.

That labor is easy to romanticize away because movements prefer memorable speeches and dramatic victories.

Institutions change through calendars, train tickets, mailing lists, dues, petitions, meeting halls, parliamentary rules, and people willing to repeat the same argument in the next town.

Anthony's importance cannot be separated from that repetition.

The 1872 vote was dramatic because years of organizing had made it legible.

The press knew who she was.

The movement knew what the theory meant.

Supporters could distribute the case.

An isolated illegal ballot would have been a local curiosity.

Anthony's ballot became national argument because she had built a network capable of carrying it.

That is organizational power.

The case also shows why voting itself became such a compelling suffrage demand.

Women's legal inequality extended far beyond the ballot. Property rights, wages, marriage, divorce, custody, education, professional access, and bodily autonomy were all contested. Voting did not automatically solve those problems.

Suffragists increasingly treated the ballot as leverage over the rest.

A voter can punish a legislator.

A constituency can organize around candidates.

An officeholder has incentives to respond to people who can remove him.

Political power does not guarantee policy power.

Its absence weakens almost every other demand.

Anthony's ballot therefore represented more than participation in one presidential election.

It represented access to the feedback mechanism of representative government.

Without the vote, women could influence politics through family, petition, persuasion, organizations, philanthropy, journalism, protest, and moral authority.

With the vote, they would also enter electoral calculation.

The distinction is structural.

This is why the prosecution made the exclusion look increasingly strange.

Anthony was politically active enough to lecture across states, organize national associations, influence newspapers, and shape constitutional debate.

The law could tolerate all of that.

It objected when she put a paper ballot into a box.

The boundary had become symbolic as much as practical.

Symbols can survive long after their original rationale weakens because institutions reproduce them through habit.

A movement sometimes has to touch the symbol directly.

Anthony touched it.

The country prosecuted her for doing so.

The legal loss also gave the movement a long-term strategic lesson.

Rights can be discovered through interpretation.

They can also have to be written explicitly.

The Nineteenth Amendment would eventually take the second route. First introduced in Congress in 1878 in language associated with Anthony, it would not be ratified until 1920.

That interval is a warning against treating constitutional change as the inevitable consequence of a good argument.

Forty-two years separated introduction from ratification.

Movements age.

Leaders die.

Coalitions shift.

States become laboratories.

Opponents organize.

Wars alter political possibilities.

New generations inherit old slogans and change their tactics.

Anthony died in 1906.

She did not see the amendment ratified.

That fact is often used sentimentally.

Its more important meaning is institutional.

Democratic reform can require labor whose reward arrives after the laborer is gone.

The movement had to become bigger than its founders.

That is how durable change works.

Anthony's importance in this decade is therefore narrower than lifetime sainthood and deeper than a "first woman to vote" anecdote, which would be historically wrong in any case. Women had voted in various places and circumstances before her, and other women participated in the 1872 New Departure tests.

Her importance lies in deliberate constitutional confrontation.

The country said women were citizens.

She asked what citizenship did.

The country said the answer did not include voting.

She forced that answer into a criminal record.

Then the movement had to decide what to do with the loss.

That is causal consequence.

The vote did not win suffrage.

It eliminated ambiguity about one route and made the remaining route harder to avoid.

A failed case clarified a successful movement.

**Verdict:** Susan B. Anthony wins 1866–1876 because her deliberate 1872 voting test transformed women's disfranchisement from a petitioned grievance into a constitutional prosecution, forcing postwar citizenship law to reveal its limits and helping push the movement from interpretive shortcut toward explicit constitutional change.
