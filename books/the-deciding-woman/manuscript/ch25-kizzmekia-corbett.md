# 2016–2026: Kizzmekia Corbett — The Spike

The most important woman of America’s twenty-fifth decade was not elected to anything.

That is the wager of the final chapter.

The decade contained the first woman vice president. It contained a Nobel-winning gene editor whose work opened a new era in biotechnology, a semiconductor executive at the center of the computing economy, women leading universities and companies, women shaping Congress and the courts, and millions of women doing work that became newly visible only when the country could not function without it.

Then a virus arrived.

Early in 2020, SARS-CoV-2 began spreading through the United States. Hospitals filled. Schools closed or went remote. Workplaces disappeared into laptops or became dangerous precisely because they could not. Families cancelled funerals, weddings, visits, and holidays. Grocery workers, nurses, physicians, respiratory therapists, nursing-home aides, transit workers, delivery drivers, laboratory technicians, public-health staff, and many others entered a category that acquired a new name—essential—because ordinary life had stopped being ordinary.

The pandemic changed the scale on which importance had to be measured.

How quickly could science reduce the probability that infection would become severe disease or death?

Kizzmekia Corbett was part of the answer.

The wording matters.

Part of the answer.

At the National Institutes of Health Vaccine Research Center, Corbett worked in the Viral Pathogenesis Laboratory led by Barney Graham. The team had spent years studying coronaviruses and thinking about a problem that attracts less public attention than outbreak response: how can researchers prepare for a pathogen that does not yet exist in its final dangerous form?

The answer was not to predict the exact virus.

It was to know the family.

This is the logic of prototype-pathogen preparedness. Researchers study representative viruses deeply enough to understand structures, immune targets, vaccine designs, and platform technologies that may be reused when a related threat appears. The work can look abstract during quiet years. Nobody knows which preparation will become urgent or whether a particular viral family will produce the next emergency.

Preparedness is a bet placed against ignorance.

Corbett’s team had made such a bet on coronaviruses.

The critical object was the spike protein, the structure projecting from the virus that helps it enter host cells and that can also become a target for immune recognition. Vaccine designers wanted to present the immune system with a useful version of that target. But viral proteins do not sit still like diagrams. They change shape. A vaccine antigen can work better if researchers can preserve a form that displays the relevant structure before the virus completes the process of entering a cell.

That problem had already generated important work before SARS-CoV-2 appeared. Barney Graham, structural biologist Jason McLellan, and collaborators studying earlier coronaviruses developed a strategy for stabilizing spike proteins in their prefusion form through specific proline substitutions. The scientific lineage matters because the later Moderna vaccine did not emerge from a single clever decision made after January 2020. It drew on years of structural biology, immunology, virology, and platform development involving many laboratories and institutions.

Corbett joined and helped advance that lineage.

When Chinese scientists posted the genetic sequence of the new coronavirus in January 2020, the NIH group did not begin by asking what a coronavirus was. Researchers could move quickly because earlier work had already reduced the number of unknowns. The NIH team and Moderna collaborated on an mRNA vaccine design encoding a prefusion-stabilized version of the SARS-CoV-2 spike.

Speed came from work that had happened before speed was needed.

That is the central fact of the chapter.

The public story of 2020 often used the language of a race. Companies raced. Governments raced. Scientists raced. Clinical trials raced. Manufacturing raced. The metaphor captured the urgency, but it obscured the starting line. Some teams appeared fast because the necessary intellectual infrastructure had been built over years.

A sprinter can only accelerate from the block she already has.

Corbett became first author, with co-first authors Darin Edwards and Sarah Leist, on the 2020 *Nature* paper whose title stated the mechanism plainly: “SARS-CoV-2 mRNA vaccine design enabled by prototype pathogen preparedness.” The author list extended across NIH, Moderna, the University of North Carolina, and other collaborators. She was also first author on a large preclinical study of mRNA-1273 in nonhuman primates, again with a long list of coauthors.

The lists are not clutter to be edited out.

They are the history.

Modern vaccine science is collaborative at a scale that resists the old biographical instinct to identify a lone inventor. Structural biologists determine a target. Immunologists characterize responses. Virologists develop assays. Animal researchers test protection. Platform engineers determine how genetic instructions can be delivered. Clinical investigators run trials. Biostatisticians assess results. Regulators evaluate evidence. Manufacturers scale production. Supply chains secure glass, lipids, cold storage, syringes, shipping, and countless ordinary components. Health systems schedule appointments. Pharmacists and nurses inject arms. Public-health agencies track outcomes and adverse events. Volunteers accept the uncertainty of clinical trials.

The Moderna vaccine did not belong causally to one person.

Neither did the Pfizer-BioNTech vaccine, the Johnson & Johnson vaccine, or the broader vaccination campaign.

So why choose Corbett?

Because the book is not awarding patents, Nobel Prizes, or shares of causal credit. It is forcing one individual into the foreground to test what kind of work mattered most in a decade. Corbett offers an unusually clear lens onto preparedness, team science, public research, private collaboration, and the compression of time during crisis.

The causal claim should remain specific.

The vaccine candidate known as mRNA-1273 encoded a prefusion-stabilized SARS-CoV-2 spike. In the large phase 3 clinical trial, the vaccine showed 94.1 percent efficacy against symptomatic COVID-19 at the primary analysis. The Food and Drug Administration authorized it for emergency use on December 18, 2020. By January 10, 2021, more than four million first doses of the Moderna vaccine had already been administered in the United States.

Those numbers do not prove that Corbett personally saved a calculable number of lives.

The chapter should refuse that temptation.

Observed vaccine impact belongs to the entire chain, and estimates of deaths prevented by vaccination combine multiple products, public-health conditions, timing assumptions, variants, prior infection, uptake, and statistical models. Assigning a national body count to one scientist would turn team science into mythology.

The stronger claim does not need a heroic number.

A vaccine with high efficacy against symptomatic disease and severe outcomes entered American use during an active pandemic, and Corbett’s documented scientific work sits inside the design and preclinical chain that made that vaccine possible.

That is deep consequence.

It is also different from the scientific achievement represented by Jennifer Doudna, one of the hardest runners-up.

In October 2020, Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for developing CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing. The technology gave researchers a programmable way to alter DNA and has already reshaped biological research, agriculture, diagnostics, and the development of therapies. Its eventual consequences may be larger than those of any single vaccine platform. It may change medicine over generations.

The phrase “may be” decides the comparison.

This book judges action inside a ten-year window rather than eventual historical prestige. CRISPR’s long-run consequence was still unfolding by July 4, 2026. Corbett’s case is brutally immediate. Her work fed into a product deployed during the same emergency the research was trying to solve.

Doudna represents possibility multiplied across the future.

Corbett represents prepared capacity meeting a present danger.

Lisa Su presents a different challenge. As chief executive of AMD, she helped lead the company through a remarkable transformation in high-performance computing during a decade when semiconductors became obvious strategic infrastructure. Chips sit underneath cloud computing, artificial intelligence, communications, defense systems, scientific research, automobiles, and the digital economy. A nation that cannot design, manufacture, or secure access to advanced semiconductors discovers quickly that “technology sector” is too small a phrase for the dependence.

Su’s business leadership therefore has enormous reach and durability.

But the same counterfactual problem that appeared in the Yellen chapter appears here. Corporate success emerges from engineering teams, product road maps, manufacturing partners, capital markets, customers, competitors, and industry cycles. Su’s leadership is central to AMD’s turnaround, but translating that corporate consequence into one person’s effect on American life requires a longer causal bridge than the vaccine case demands.

The strongest public-office rival is Kamala Harris.

On January 20, 2021, Harris became vice president of the United States, the first woman, the first Black person, and the first person of South Asian descent to hold that office. After twenty-four previous chapters in which women entered public speech, education, reform organizations, Cabinet government, the Supreme Court, and the Speakership, the vice presidency is not a small culmination. The office places its holder one heartbeat from the presidency and gives the vice president a constitutional role in the Senate. Harris cast tie-breaking votes in an evenly divided Senate during the first two years of the Biden administration, giving her office practical legislative consequence as well as representational force.

Why not end the book there?

Because the ending would be too easy.

A history of women in America naturally accumulates toward the highest office a woman has held. The structure itself tempts the author to treat representation as the final measure of importance. But the premise of this book was designed to resist exactly that shortcut. Firstness counts when it changes access, authority, precedent, or expectations. It does not automatically defeat every form of material consequence happening outside elected office.

Harris changed the answer to a question the country had been asking for more than two centuries: can a woman hold the vice presidency?

After January 20, 2021, yes.

Corbett’s work participated in answering a different question: could accumulated scientific preparation be converted quickly enough into protection during a once-in-a-century public-health emergency?

The answer affected bodies before it affected symbols.

That gives Corbett the edge on depth.

There is a moral danger in saying that, too. Pandemic history can become a celebration of biomedical rescue that forgets the uneven structure of the disaster. Americans did not face the same exposure or the same ability to isolate. Race, income, occupation, housing, age, disability, geography, incarceration, access to health care, and employer power all shaped risk. Vaccines arrived into a country already marked by unequal death and unequal trust in institutions.

A vaccine is not a social policy.

It cannot retroactively provide paid sick leave. It cannot make a crowded workplace spacious or a multigenerational home less vulnerable to transmission. It cannot repair a history of medical abuse that makes some communities distrust public-health authority. It cannot make people take it. It cannot by itself decide who receives the first appointments or which neighborhoods have convenient access.

Technology can be extraordinarily effective and still enter an unequal system.

That distinction belongs at the end of this book because so many earlier chapters made the same point in other forms. A constitutional amendment can prohibit sex discrimination in voting while Black women remain disfranchised by racist state systems. A social-insurance law can create national security while excluding millions of workers. A legal right can exist before people possess the money, information, safety, or institutional access required to use it.

Capacity is never just invention.

It is distribution.

The Moderna story makes that visible from another direction. Designing an antigen is one achievement. Encoding it in an mRNA platform is another. Manufacturing doses at scale is another. Authorizing them is another. Delivering them is another. Persuading people to accept them is another. Monitoring safety and effectiveness as conditions change is another. No stage can be substituted for all the others.

The more complex the system, the less plausible the lone hero becomes.

Corbett is therefore an unusual winner for a book organized around individuals. Her chapter works only if it spends much of its time refusing to make her singular.

That refusal is not self-contradiction.

It is the point.

By the twenty-first century, some of the most consequential work in American life occurs inside teams so large that individual biography can distort the mechanism. The one-woman rule becomes hardest exactly where the country’s capacity becomes most sophisticated. A vaccine team, semiconductor company, federal agency, or research collaboration may require thousands of people performing specialized work that no one participant could reproduce alone.

Importance changes shape.

Esther de Berdt Reed could write a broadside, organize a canvass, correspond, raise funds, and see a civic network spread because the young nation’s institutions were thin. Kizzmekia Corbett worked in an ecosystem of public laboratories, private biotechnology, genomic sequencing, structural biology, international publication, high-throughput assays, clinical trial networks, regulatory science, advanced manufacturing, and digital data systems.

The distance between those worlds is one way to measure 250 years.

Yet the first and last chapters share a mechanism.

Preparation before emergency.

Reed’s organizing mattered because soldiers needed supplies before a mature federal logistics system existed. Corbett’s coronavirus work mattered because scientists had to begin responding to SARS-CoV-2 before there was time to invent every necessary technique from scratch.

Preparedness is politically difficult because its success can erase the evidence that justified the investment.

A bridge repaired before it collapses does not produce a disaster story. A stockpile used smoothly may look excessive the year before. A laboratory studying a virus family that has not yet produced a pandemic can seem remote from immediate need. Public institutions are often asked to defend capacity against budgets organized around visible present problems.

Then the emergency arrives and time acquires a price.

Every month matters.

Every unresolved scientific problem becomes a delay.

Every process rehearsed in advance becomes an option.

The speed of the COVID vaccine effort was not evidence that normal standards had become unnecessary. It was evidence that years of prior research, parallel work, enormous funding, regulatory attention, manufacturing risk, and unprecedented urgency allowed stages to be compressed and overlapped in ways ordinary product development rarely permits.

Speed was purchased partly in advance.

Corbett’s scientific career also changes the national signal in a way the chapter should not reduce to a diversity slogan. A Black woman stood publicly associated with one of the most scrutinized scientific projects in the world. During the pandemic she also spoke to audiences about vaccine science, including communities in which medical trust could not be assumed.

Representation matters differently in a laboratory than in elected office.

A scientist does not represent a constituency in the constitutional sense. Evidence does not become more valid because of the identity of the person presenting it. But institutions still have histories. American medicine and science have excluded Black people and women from authority while sometimes exploiting Black communities as research subjects. Visibility cannot repair that history, but it changes who can be seen occupying technical authority inside the institution.

For a young Black girl looking at the public face of vaccine science, Corbett’s presence alters possibility.

For the causal ranking in this book, that is an addition rather than the foundation.

The foundation remains the work.

The book also has to resist hindsight about the pandemic itself. Vaccines did not end COVID-19 in 2021. Variants emerged. Protection against infection changed over time. Boosters became part of policy. Breakthrough infections occurred. Public guidance shifted as evidence changed. Political identities hardened around masks, mandates, vaccines, school closures, and institutional trust. The country did not move from crisis to clean resolution.

A technology can be successful without ending the problem it addresses.

That is another useful correction to heroic history.

The right counterfactual is not a world in which one vaccine makes the pandemic disappear. It is a world in which one major vaccine platform arrives later, works less well, or does not arrive at all, while the virus continues to spread.

That alternative is enough.

The final chapter began by asking why the winner was not the woman who held the highest office.

It can end with a different question.

What does a country discover about importance when survival depends on something built before most citizens knew they would need it?

The answer is not merely “science.” Science can produce knowledge without producing capacity. The answer is sustained institutions: laboratories that retain expertise, public funding that tolerates uncertain payoffs, researchers who learn viral families before the emergency, private platforms capable of rapid development, regulators capable of evaluating evidence under pressure, manufacturing systems capable of scale, and health workers capable of turning a vial into protection.

Corbett’s name sits inside that chain rather than above it.

That is where it belongs.

The forced-choice method has spent twenty-five chapters looking for one woman in each decade. The last chapter discovers the limit of the method and keeps the method anyway. We choose one person not because history was made by one person, but because choosing forces us to ask exactly which part of a system we think mattered most.

In this decade, the answer is prepared scientific capacity.

Corbett is the woman through whom that capacity becomes visible.

Two hundred and fifty years after a woman organized material support for an army in a country with almost no national administrative capacity, another woman worked inside a research system complex enough to turn a genetic sequence into a vaccine candidate with extraordinary speed.

The nation did not become less dependent on organized labor.

It became more capable of organizing it.

That is the arc.

Not from women outside history to women inside it. They were always inside it.

From a country that could use women’s capacity while denying women authority to a country in which a Black woman scientist could be publicly identifiable at the technical center of a national emergency.

The transformation is incomplete. The institutions are unequal. The verdicts remain arguable.

They should.

The point was never to produce a perfect list.

It was to make consequence visible enough that the reader could fight about it.

**Verdict:** Kizzmekia Corbett wins 2016–2026 because her documented work inside the NIH-led coronavirus vaccine team exemplifies the decade’s deepest mechanism of consequence: years of prototype-pathogen preparation converted, with Moderna and a vast scientific, clinical, regulatory, manufacturing, and public-health coalition, into a highly effective vaccine during an active national survival crisis. The choice is deliberately narrower than a lone-inventor claim and deliberately harder than awarding the decade by office rank.
