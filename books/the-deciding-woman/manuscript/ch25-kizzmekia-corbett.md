# 2016–2026: Kizzmekia Corbett — The Spike

The most important woman of America’s twenty-fifth decade was not elected to anything.

That is the wager of the final chapter.

The decade contained the first woman vice president. It contained women leading major technology and financial institutions, women shaping Congress and the courts, women organizing mass movements, women winning scientific prizes, women commanding audiences larger than national newspapers, and women holding authority unimaginable in 1776.

Then a virus arrived.

In early 2020, SARS-CoV-2 began spreading through the United States. Hospitals filled. Workplaces closed or reorganized. Schools went remote. Families separated. Death became a daily statistic. The central national question narrowed with terrifying speed.

How quickly can science reduce the probability of severe disease and death?

Kizzmekia Corbett was part of the answer.

At the National Institutes of Health Vaccine Research Center, Corbett worked in the team led by Barney Graham on coronavirus vaccine research. Years of prior work on prototype pathogens, coronavirus spike proteins, and structural biology meant the group did not begin from zero when the SARS-CoV-2 genetic sequence became available.

That prehistory is essential.

The popular image of vaccine development is a frantic 2020 race in which brilliant scientists invented everything at unprecedented speed. The real achievement was preparedness compounded over years.

When the sequence arrived, the NIH team and collaborators designed a stabilized version of the viral spike protein to use as an immunogen. NIH collaborated with Moderna, whose mRNA platform could encode the antigen quickly. Corbett became first author on the 2020 *Nature* paper describing the mRNA vaccine design enabled by prototype-pathogen preparedness and was also first author on major preclinical work evaluating mRNA-1273 in nonhuman primates.

The Moderna vaccine that followed became one of the principal COVID-19 vaccines used in the United States.

That sentence contains a trap.

It can make team science sound like individual invention.

Corbett did not invent the COVID-19 vaccine alone.

Barney Graham, Jason McLellan and structural-biology collaborators, Moderna scientists, NIH teams, academic laboratories, clinical-trial investigators, manufacturers, regulators, funders, trial volunteers, logistics workers, pharmacists, nurses, physicians, public-health agencies, and decades of mRNA and coronavirus research all belong in the causal chain.

The stabilized spike itself grew from earlier coronavirus work.

The vaccine’s impact depended on manufacturing, trials, authorization, distribution, uptake, and public trust.

If this chapter turns Corbett into a solitary genius, it fails the book’s central rule.

Why choose her, then?

Because the one-woman method is not asking who deserves all the credit.

It is asking which individual contribution offers the strongest lens onto the decade’s largest consequence.

Pandemic vaccination operated at the depth of survival.

That gives Corbett a difficult-to-match advantage over symbolic and institutional firsts. A vice presidency changes representation at the top of government. A corporate leader can change strategic industries. A scientific discovery can reshape future medicine.

A vaccine platform deployed during an active pandemic changes the probability that people live through the year.

The strongest runner-up is Kamala Harris, who in January 2021 became the first woman, first Black person, and first person of South Asian descent to serve as vice president of the United States. Her election broke a barrier that had survived every prior decade in this book.

Lisa Su is another extraordinary rival. Under her leadership AMD became a central competitor in high-performance and AI computing during a period when semiconductors became strategic national infrastructure.

Jennifer Doudna’s CRISPR work helped create a programmable gene-editing revolution with consequences that may eventually exceed everything else in this chapter.

Why Corbett now?

Because this book measures consequence inside the window, not anticipated lifetime importance.

The pandemic’s body count was immediate.

The intervention was immediate.

The scientific work translated directly into a national tool for reducing risk.

The choice also completes an arc begun in 1776.

Esther de Berdt Reed organized women to supply an army because the new state lacked enough logistical capacity.

Kizzmekia Corbett worked inside one of the world’s most sophisticated public research institutions, connected to private biotechnology, global scientific publication, advanced manufacturing, clinical trials, and regulatory systems.

In the first decade, a woman’s national importance had to hide inside voluntary service.

In the twenty-fifth, a Black woman scientist could stand visibly inside the technical core of national survival.

The country changed.

The underlying mechanism did not.

Capacity before crisis.

Reed’s network mattered because it existed when soldiers needed shirts.

Corbett’s team mattered because years of coronavirus research existed when a new genome appeared.

Preparedness looks wasteful until the emergency arrives.

Then time becomes the most expensive variable in the country.

The final chapter therefore ends not with a celebration of representation, though representation matters enormously, but with a theory of importance.

The most important person may be the person who shortened the distance between danger and capacity.

Sometimes history announces that person from a podium.

Sometimes she is in a laboratory, working on a protein nobody outside the field expects to become a national object.

Then the decade turns.

The object becomes the country’s problem.

The preparation becomes the country’s advantage.

And a woman whose name most Americans did not know in 2019 becomes part of the reason millions could imagine life after 2020.

**Verdict:** Kizzmekia Corbett wins 2016–2026 because her documented contribution inside the NIH-led team that converted years of coronavirus preparedness into the Moderna vaccine platform sits closest to the decade’s deepest measurable consequence: reducing the human cost of a national survival crisis.
