# 1786–1796: Ona Judge — The President Could Not Own the Ending

Power becomes easiest to see when someone refuses it.

On May 21, 1796, while George and Martha Washington ate dinner in Philadelphia, Ona Judge left the presidential household.

She did not have legal permission to go.

That was the point.

Judge was an enslaved woman in Martha Washington’s household, a skilled seamstress and personal attendant who had been brought to Philadelphia while George Washington served as president. She learned that Martha intended eventually to give her to a granddaughter. The transfer threatened to make permanent what was already intolerable: another person claiming the authority to decide where Judge would live, whom she would serve, and what would happen to her children.

So Judge decided first.

Friends in Philadelphia’s free Black community helped her prepare. She left while the household was occupied and found passage north. Within days, an advertisement appeared offering a reward for her capture. The president of a republic founded on liberty wanted an escaped human being returned as property.

There are broader events in this decade. The Constitution took effect. The first federal government was built. The Bill of Rights was ratified. Parties began to emerge. The Whiskey Rebellion tested federal authority. Foreign policy hardened around the wars of Europe. A case for “most important woman” could reasonably point to Judith Sargent Murray’s 1790 argument for intellectual equality between the sexes or to Martha Washington’s role in creating expectations around a new national presidency.

Ona Judge is a stranger winner because she held no office, led no formal movement, and did not publish a famous theory in the decade.

Her importance lies in the directness of the collision she created.

The abstract contradiction of the founding is familiar: a republic of liberty was also a slave society. Judge made that contradiction personal inside the executive household. The man who embodied federal authority could command armies, sign laws, appoint officials, and shape the presidency, yet recovering one woman required him to operate through a network of personal letters, federal officeholders, local officials, secrecy, persuasion, and attempted seizure.

Judge kept saying no.

When the Washingtons’ agents located her in New Hampshire, she did not agree simply to return. According to later accounts and surviving correspondence, she was willing to discuss return only if freedom would eventually be guaranteed. The Washingtons rejected the condition. Another attempt followed. Judge received warning and escaped again.

She remained free.

It would be too much to say that Ona Judge defeated slavery, defeated the Fugitive Slave Act, or changed federal law. She did none of those things by herself. Millions remained enslaved. Federal law continued to protect enslavers. The constitutional structure continued to empower slaveholding interests.

But historical importance is not reserved for people who win the whole war.

Judge exposed the limits and dependencies of power.

A legal regime can authorize domination without making domination effortless. Enforcement still requires people. It requires information, cooperation, risk, legitimacy, and local compliance. The effort to recover Judge depended on officials and intermediaries deciding how far they would go. Some resisted or warned her. A free Black community had already made her departure possible. Geography mattered. Reputation mattered. The president’s own awareness of public reaction mattered.

The story therefore belongs not only to slavery but to state capacity.

The early federal government was becoming stronger. Judge’s escape showed that even strong legal claims become fragile when the people needed to enforce them do not behave like gears.

That is one reason enslaved people’s self-emancipation matters so much to the history of American freedom. Freedom was not merely granted downward through statutes and proclamations. People seized it, ran toward it, hid it, defended it, financed it, transported it, and created communities capable of making legal ownership harder to realize in practice.

Judge’s decision also rearranges the usual camera angle on the Washington presidency.

Presidential history tends to place the president at the center and everybody else in orbit. In Judge’s chapter, Washington becomes part of her story. His letters and requests matter because they are evidence of the force she compelled him to spend. The president wanted an ending. The woman he claimed as property produced another one.

The strongest runner-up, Judith Sargent Murray, offers almost the opposite kind of importance. Her 1790 essay “On the Equality of the Sexes” articulated a claim that women’s apparent intellectual inferiority was a product of unequal education rather than nature. That idea belongs to a long intellectual road toward women’s equality.

But this book privileges consequence inside the decade. Murray’s argument widened what could be said. Judge’s action revealed what the new government would do when one woman made the founding language of liberty literal for herself.

There is also a risk in turning Judge into a symbol so polished that the person disappears. Her life after escape was not a triumphal montage. Freedom did not mean wealth. She married, had children, endured hardship, and outlived members of her family. The point is not that escape made life easy.

The point is that it made the life hers.

Every political system has an official theory of where authority resides. The early United States announced one theory in its public documents and practiced another in households, plantations, courts, and markets.

Ona Judge forced the theories to meet in the president’s dining room.

Then she walked out.

**Verdict:** Ona Judge wins 1786–1796 because her self-emancipation turned the founding contradiction between liberty and slavery into a direct test of presidential power, federal enforcement, free Black mutual aid, and the practical limits of legal ownership.
