# 1786–1796: Ona Judge — The President Could Not Own the Ending

On May 24, 1796, readers of the *Pennsylvania Gazette* encountered a notice from the household of the president of the United States. It described a young woman in the language of recovery. She was about twenty years old, slender, freckled, with black eyes and bushy black hair. She possessed many changes of clothing. Ship captains were warned not to admit her aboard. A reward was offered.

The woman had left three nights earlier.

Her name was Ona Judge.

The notice said there had been “no provocation” for her departure. That phrase contains almost the entire contradiction of the early republic. Judge was enslaved. She belonged, in law and in the Washington household’s understanding, to Martha Washington. The people advertising for her return could describe bondage itself as insufficient provocation because bondage was the premise they did not intend to examine.

Judge examined it for them.

Her escape from the presidential household is not the largest event of 1786–1796. It is barely a footnote in most histories of the decade. The Constitution was written and ratified. George Washington became the first president. Congress created departments, courts, taxes, a national bank, and the machinery of a federal state. The Bill of Rights was ratified. The Whiskey Rebellion tested federal enforcement. Political parties began to harden. American diplomacy navigated war in Europe and conflict on the continent.

The strongest argument against Ona Judge is that no individual enslaved person, however courageous, can plausibly outrank the founding of the federal government in a decade-by-decade history.

But this book is not choosing the most important event. It is choosing the woman whose action most changes what the decade reveals about America.

Judge’s importance comes from where she stood when she refused.

She had been born into slavery at Mount Vernon around 1773, the daughter of Betty, an enslaved seamstress, and Andrew Judge, a white indentured tailor. As a child she became one of Martha Washington’s personal attendants. When George Washington assumed the presidency, Judge entered the new national household, first in New York and then in Philadelphia.

The geography mattered.

Pennsylvania had adopted a gradual abolition law in 1780. The statute did not simply free every enslaved person who entered the state, but it created legal conditions that slaveholding officials had to manage. The Washington household rotated enslaved workers in and out of Pennsylvania partly to prevent them from establishing claims to freedom under state law. The first president of the United States, presiding over a federal system built on divided sovereignty, was privately attentive to the legal boundary between a slave state’s property regime and a northern state’s gradual emancipation policy.

Judge lived inside that contradiction, not as an abstract constitutional puzzle but as a person whose location could change the legal risk surrounding her captivity.

Philadelphia also contained a substantial free Black community. Black churches, mariners, workers, families, and mutual-aid networks made the city a place where an enslaved person could imagine alternatives and potentially find assistance. Judge later recalled that she had friends among the city’s free Black population. Her escape required secrecy and practical help. She left while the Washingtons were eating dinner on May 21, 1796, and found passage north by ship.

The immediate cause was personal and structural at once. Judge learned that Martha Washington intended to give her as a wedding gift to her granddaughter, Elizabeth Parke Custis Law. The transfer would have confirmed that Judge’s future could be inherited inside another family line. She would not control where she lived. She could be carried back south. Any children she had could inherit enslaved status through her.

Judge did not petition the president to recognize the inconsistency.

She removed herself from his control.

That difference is why she wins the decade over a writer such as Judith Sargent Murray, whose 1790 essay “On the Equality of the Sexes” made a sophisticated argument that women’s apparent intellectual inferiority reflected unequal education rather than nature. Murray deserves a place in the intellectual history of women’s equality. Her argument widened what could be said.

Judge changed what the president had to do.

Once she reached Portsmouth, New Hampshire, the Washingtons faced a practical enforcement problem. The Fugitive Slave Act of 1793 provided federal support for the recovery of people claimed as fugitives from slavery. Washington had signed the law. Yet a statute does not seize anyone by itself. Recovery required information, agents, local cooperation, timing, and a willingness to endure public scrutiny.

Washington pursued Judge quietly.

Correspondence shows him using intermediaries, including Treasury Secretary Oliver Wolcott Jr. and local officials, to locate her and explore her return. He worried about provoking controversy if force were used openly. Joseph Whipple, a federal customs official in Portsmouth, interviewed Judge. She did not deny leaving. She reportedly expressed willingness to consider returning only if the Washingtons guaranteed her eventual freedom. They would not.

That negotiation is astonishing because of how completely it reverses the expected hierarchy.

The president’s household claimed Judge had no legal right to set terms at all.

Judge set terms anyway.

The Washingtons’ refusal did not restore their control. Another effort to seize her was attempted later through Martha Washington’s nephew, Burwell Bassett Jr. Judge received warning and avoided capture. Senator John Langdon, according to accounts, helped ensure she knew of the danger. Local relationships, geography, free Black assistance, and the political sensitivity of the case all constrained the practical reach of the nation’s most powerful man.

The birthday boundary makes the chronology unusually important here. Judge’s act of self-emancipation and the Washington household’s public advertisement both fall before July 4, 1796. The more elaborate recovery correspondence with federal officials in New Hampshire came later in 1796, and the Bassett attempt came still later. Those episodes belong in the chapter as downstream evidence of what the escape set in motion, not as achievements smuggled backward into the winning interval.

That restriction actually sharpens the case.

By July 4, the irreversible fact was not that Washington had already exhausted every means of recapture. It was that the president’s household had lost physical control of Judge and had been forced into public notice. Three days after her departure, the advertisement described her body, clothes, possible route, and ability to pay passage. The notice warned shipmasters not to help her and offered ten dollars for her recovery. Its wording insisted that there had been no provocation.

The paper was doing two jobs at once.

It was an instrument of capture, meant to circulate information that could turn strangers into enforcement agents. It was also an accidental record of enslaver surprise. The household that had organized Judge’s labor closely enough to know her appearance and wardrobe had failed to perceive her intention. The claim of “no provocation” therefore tells us less about Judge than about the limits of the Washingtons’ own interpretation. They could see service and apparently not the desire to end it.

The advertisement also demonstrates why escape was a network problem rather than a solitary sprint. A person fleeing slavery had to move through a world in which information could travel ahead of her. Ports, roads, inns, newspapers, customs officials, ship captains, and local acquaintances could become parts of a recovery system. Success required counter-networks: people willing to keep silent, carry possessions, arrange transportation, offer shelter, or simply decline to assist the claimant.

Judge later said she had friends among Philadelphia’s Black community and had moved her belongings before leaving. That detail is modest and decisive. The popular image of escape often begins when the fugitive crosses a threshold. The practical escape begins earlier, when somebody agrees not to betray the plan.

This gives the chapter a second kind of power to examine. Washington’s household possessed law, status, money, and national prestige. Judge’s side possessed secrecy, mobility, and social ties the household did not fully control. Those resources were not equal. If discovered, Judge faced consequences Washington did not. But asymmetry does not mean the weaker party has no leverage. It means the leverage has to come from places the stronger party cannot easily command.

Philadelphia’s free Black community mattered in that sense even when the surviving record does not identify every helper. The city contained churches, families, workers, sailors, mutual-aid institutions, and households through which information and assistance could move. The absence of names is not permission to invent a rescue committee around Judge. It is evidence of an archival limit: successful clandestine help often survives precisely because it was not formally documented.

That limit is worth preserving. A biography built from an enslaver’s records and later interviews will almost always know more about the people trying to recover a fugitive than about everyone who helped the fugitive disappear. Washington wrote letters. Officials retained correspondence. Newspapers printed advertisements. The people protecting Judge had reasons not to create comparable paperwork.

The archive therefore inherits the surveillance priorities of power.

Judge’s story partly reverses them because she lived long enough to speak to abolitionist interviewers decades later. Her recollections give historians a rare first-person route into motives the presidential records could only guess at. Even then, memory, transcription, editorial choices, and the distance of decades require care. The safer method is to let the Washington papers establish what the household tried to do and Judge’s interviews establish what she said about her own decision, without forcing either source to answer questions it cannot answer.

That discipline changes the moral texture of the chapter. The household’s claim that she had no provocation and Judge’s later explanation that she wanted liberty are not two equally plausible descriptions of the same employment dispute. One side asserted ownership over the other person. The uncertainty lies in details of memory, assistance, timing, and motive—not in whether slavery constituted coercion.

Judith Sargent Murray remains the most intellectually formidable rival because her 1790 essay attacks another structure of presumed natural hierarchy. Murray argued, in effect, that society confused denied education with deficient ability. The claim would echo through later fights over women’s schooling and professional access. Emma Willard’s chapter will show what happens when that argument becomes an institution.

But Murray’s mechanism is persuasion. Judge’s is withdrawal.

Those mechanisms leave different counterfactuals. Without Murray’s particular essay, arguments for women’s intellectual equality still existed in transatlantic political thought and would continue to develop. Her wording and publication mattered, but the intellectual current did not depend on one text. Without Judge’s decision on May 21, there is no Ona Judge escape from the president’s household, no May 24 advertisement, and no later presidential recovery problem attached to her person.

Counterfactual force does not make Judge’s action nationally larger than Murray’s ideas. It makes the causal chain more personal and less substitutable.

The chapter’s place immediately after Esther Reed also creates a useful reversal. Reed converted household trust into public capacity for a national cause. Judge used household familiarity in the opposite direction. The Washingtons believed they knew the young woman who attended Martha Washington closely enough to assume continuity. Judge used that assumption as cover while arranging to leave.

One woman’s domestic position became a network for service.

Another woman’s domestic position became an opportunity for exit.

Both stories expose how much early American public life rested on activities classified as private. The founding generation did not keep politics in a separate room from households. Households produced labor, inheritance, property, reputation, hospitality, patronage, and social connection. In a slaveholding elite household, they also produced coercive control. To look only at constitutional chambers is to miss part of the machinery that made those chambers possible.

Judge’s escape makes that machinery visible because one of its supposed components refused to remain in place.

Judge remained free for the rest of her life.

It is essential not to inflate that outcome. She did not invalidate the Fugitive Slave Act. She did not establish a court precedent. She did not end slavery in New Hampshire, Pennsylvania, Virginia, or anywhere else. The legal system overwhelmingly favored enslavers. Washington’s inability to recover one woman did not make the federal government weak in general or slavery unenforceable in particular.

What it revealed was more precise: legal power is not the same thing as frictionless control.

Every enforcement regime depends on people between the rule and the target. Officials have discretion. Neighbors possess information. Communities can hide, warn, transport, delay, refuse, or cooperate. Geography changes cost. Reputation changes political risk. A person who is supposedly powerless can exploit the gaps.

This is why self-emancipation belongs at the center of slavery’s history. A version of emancipation told only through statutes, elections, presidents, generals, and courts makes freedom something public institutions eventually bestowed. Enslaved people repeatedly acted before those institutions were ready. They ran to British lines during the Revolution. They built maroon communities. They used waterways, cities, kinship networks, forged papers, free Black institutions, and changing military fronts. During the Civil War, mass self-emancipation would change federal policy by forcing Union armies and politicians to decide what to do with people who had already left slavery behind them.

Ona Judge’s escape is an early presidential version of that dynamic.

She made the contradiction walk into the executive household.

The Washingtons’ own behavior after the escape reveals how much they understood about the moral and political sensitivity. Washington instructed that the matter be handled without creating a public disturbance. He wanted Judge recovered, but he did not want the president publicly seen using force in a northern community to drag a young woman back into slavery.

Image mattered because the republic had language about liberty.

That language did not free Judge. It created a reputational cost around openly contradicting it.

The distinction matters. Ideals can constrain power before they control law. A government may violate its own principles while still having to explain, conceal, euphemize, or limit the violation. Those rhetorical costs can become political resources for people resisting the system.

Judge also complicates the way Americans remember Washington. National memory often turns founders into monuments before readers encounter them as employers, debtors, neighbors, spouses, speculators, enslavers, litigants, and political operators. The monumental Washington can appear to belong only to the nation. Judge brings the household back into view.

The presidency was not separated from domestic slavery by a clean wall. Enslaved workers cooked, dressed, cleaned, transported, served, and maintained the households through which elite political life operated. The executive office’s dignity rested partly on labor performed by people who had no consent in the arrangement.

Judge’s story therefore changes the camera angle.

Instead of asking what Washington decided about slavery, the chapter asks what happened when an enslaved woman decided something about Washington.

She decided he would no longer be able to direct her daily life.

The choice had consequences that extended into her later years. Judge married Jack Staines, a free Black sailor, and had children. Freedom did not produce prosperity. She experienced poverty and loss. Two of her children died before she did; her husband died in 1803. Later interviewers found her living modestly in New Hampshire.

Her life after escape is important because triumphalist history often treats crossing into freedom as an ending. It was a beginning under harsh conditions. Free Black Americans faced discrimination, economic insecurity, kidnapping risk, racist violence, and restricted rights. Judge’s freedom meant she owned her decisions. It did not mean the country made those decisions easy.

In interviews late in life, she was asked whether she regretted leaving the Washington household given the hardship that followed. Her reported answer has often been paraphrased into a statement that she was free and trusted Providence. The exact wording varies by source and should be quoted only from a verified transcript. The underlying historical fact is clear enough without polishing it into an aphorism: she never returned.

That permanence is her most powerful argument.

The strongest rival remains Murray because Murray’s published case for women’s intellectual equality belongs to the decade’s long democratic future. Martha Washington could be considered for the way she helped create the social expectations of the presidency, though her public role was substantially more constrained than Dolley Madison’s would become. Free Black women in northern cities were building churches, businesses, families, schools, and mutual-aid institutions whose collective importance is larger than the surviving biography of any single participant.

Judge wins because she intersects with the decade’s central institution at its most intimate point.

The federal government was being invented. The president was becoming a model for executive authority. Questions of federal power, state law, individual rights, citizenship, and slavery were being embedded into the new constitutional order.

Judge forced those abstractions into one practical question: could the president get her back?

The answer was legally supposed to be yes.

In practice, it became no.

That gap is a window into how power actually works.

The story also warns against a comforting misunderstanding of resistance. Judge did not need to know that future Americans would find her escape historically meaningful. She could not know that. She was making a decision under uncertainty about her own life. The ship could have betrayed her. An agent could have seized her. A friend could have talked. A local official could have cooperated more aggressively. She was risking capture and punishment without a guarantee that history would eventually put her on the morally vindicated side.

Hindsight makes courage look less risky than it was.

The people inside the event had no hindsight.

That is one reason Judge belongs ahead of more famous intellectuals. She did not merely imagine a different political order. She bet her body on one.

Her escape also changes the meaning of consent in the founding era. The Constitution famously begins “We the People,” a phrase that would expand through struggle. Judge’s story asks who had the practical ability to withdraw consent from private power even when public law refused to recognize the withdrawal.

She could not vote against Washington.

She could leave him.

The act did not make her a citizen in the full legal sense. It made her an agent in a history that had classified her as property.

For the first president, that was a logistical inconvenience, a property loss, a family grievance, and a political risk.

For Ona Judge, it was the rest of her life.

**Verdict:** Ona Judge wins 1786–1796 because her 1796 self-emancipation forced the founding republic’s contradictions into direct contact with presidential power. Her escape tested the practical reach of federal fugitive-slave enforcement, depended on free Black and local networks, imposed reputational constraints on the Washington household, and demonstrated that people excluded from the constitutional promise of liberty were already acting as though liberty could be seized rather than awaited.
