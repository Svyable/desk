# 1976–1986: Sandra Day O’Connor — The First Woman at the Final Court

The Supreme Court had been interpreting the Constitution for nearly two centuries before a woman sat among the justices.

That fact was not written anywhere in Article III.

The Constitution did not say the Court was male.

Habit did.

On September 25, 1981, Sandra Day O’Connor took the judicial oath and joined the Supreme Court of the United States. The sentence is so familiar now that it can collapse into anniversary language: first woman, historic appointment, barrier broken.

A court is not an anniversary.

It turns disputes into binding law.

O’Connor’s importance begins with the first and becomes much larger because of the second.

Her path to the Court made the contradiction visible. She had graduated from Stanford Law School near the top of her class in the early 1950s and nevertheless encountered law firms unwilling to hire a woman as a lawyer. She built a career through public service, private practice, Arizona politics, the state legislature, a trial court, and eventually the Arizona Court of Appeals.

By the opening of this birthday decade in July 1976, she was already a judge on the Maricopa County Superior Court. In 1979 she joined the Arizona Court of Appeals.

The pipeline existed, but only barely.

Women had entered American law in increasing numbers, argued before the Supreme Court, served as judges, and built constitutional doctrine. Belva Lockwood had become the first woman admitted to the Supreme Court Bar in 1879 and the first to argue a case there. Women lawyers including Ruth Bader Ginsburg had spent the 1970s reshaping sex-equality law through litigation.

The country had women capable of serving on the Court long before a president decided to appoint one.

Ronald Reagan turned the capability into a political commitment during the 1980 presidential campaign, promising to appoint a woman to the Supreme Court. When Justice Potter Stewart retired in 1981, the administration considered candidates and selected O’Connor.

The nomination itself changed what later nominations would mean.

Before O’Connor, every president choosing a justice could treat an all-male Court as historical continuity.

After O’Connor, an all-male future Court would have to be produced through active reversal rather than inherited custom.

That is the institutional power of a first.

A first does not prove equality.

It destroys one explanation for inequality: the claim that the excluded person cannot plausibly perform the role.

The Senate Judiciary Committee held hearings in September 1981. The Senate confirmed O’Connor unanimously, 99–0. Four days later she took her seat.

The unanimity can be misread as evidence that the appointment was apolitical.

No Supreme Court appointment is outside politics. Reagan had made the sex of the nominee part of an electoral promise. Conservative activists examined O’Connor’s record, particularly on abortion. Senators assessed judicial philosophy, qualifications, and the symbolism of the appointment. The broad vote reflected the coalition around a highly qualified nominee and a breakthrough the country was prepared to celebrate.

Celebration ended when cases arrived.

That is the moment symbolic representation becomes actual authority.

A Supreme Court justice does not represent women in the way a congresswoman represents a district. The justice has no constituency and no obligation to deliver a movement’s preferred outcomes. This distinction was especially important for O’Connor because “first woman” created an expectation that she would reveal something about what women judges would do.

Her jurisprudence refused the category.

Within a year, she wrote a major sex-discrimination opinion in a case where the government classification favored women.

In *Mississippi University for Women v. Hogan* in 1982, a male applicant challenged the state nursing school’s policy of admitting only women. O’Connor wrote for the Court in holding that the sex-based admissions policy violated equal protection. The decision applied heightened scrutiny to a classification defended partly as compensatory for women.

The case is an unusually good test of what representation means after a first.

A shallow version of representation would expect the first woman justice to protect a women-only institution because its beneficiaries were women.

O’Connor treated the constitutional question as a problem of sex classification rather than group loyalty.

One can disagree with the decision and still see the institutional consequence.

A woman justice was exercising authority over sex equality, not appearing as the sex whose equality the men would define for her.

That is a different position in the constitutional order.

The same independence made O’Connor difficult to classify ideologically. Over her later career she would become known for pragmatic, fact-sensitive decisions and often for occupying the Court’s center in closely divided cases. That full reputation belongs mostly beyond this birthday window and should not be imported backward.

The early years already show the pattern.

In abortion cases, she was more skeptical of *Roe v. Wade*’s trimester framework than the Court’s liberals but did not immediately adopt the most sweeping position urged by abortion opponents. Her 1983 dissent in *City of Akron v. Akron Center for Reproductive Health* developed an “undue burden” concept that would become important in later abortion doctrine, though the later controlling framework and her joint opinion in *Planned Parenthood v. Casey* fall well outside this decade.

In religion cases, O’Connor developed reasoning around whether government action communicates endorsement of religion, an approach that influenced Establishment Clause analysis for years.

In federalism, she emphasized state sovereignty in ways that sometimes placed her against broader federal power.

The details matter because they show that institutional entry became doctrinal production almost immediately.

O’Connor did not merely make the bench look different.

She added votes and opinions to the law.

The difference is obvious but often lost in histories of firsts.

A first woman pilot, astronaut, judge, executive, speaker, or vice president is frequently remembered through the threshold photograph. The threshold is only the beginning if the office carries power.

The real historical question is what happens the next morning when the person receives ordinary work.

O’Connor’s work was deciding cases no other institution could resolve finally.

That is why she defeats Sally Ride, the most emotionally compelling runner-up.

Ride became the first American woman in space in June 1983 aboard the space shuttle *Challenger*. The image transformed American technological imagination. Girls who had been told astronaut was implicitly male could look at an actual mission rather than a hypothetical promise. NASA’s astronaut corps had only recently begun including women, and Ride’s flight showed that the change had reached the operational frontier.

The achievement had enormous national signal.

O’Connor’s had greater institutional authority.

Ride demonstrated that a woman could enter space.

O’Connor participated in deciding what every government in the United States could constitutionally do.

The difference is not a ranking of courage or intelligence.

It is jurisdiction.

Geraldine Ferraro presents another serious challenge. In 1984 Walter Mondale selected the New York congresswoman as his running mate, making Ferraro the first woman nominated for vice president by a major American political party. Her campaign placed a woman on a national ticket before tens of millions of voters and forced the press, parties, and public to imagine female executive power at unprecedented scale.

The Mondale-Ferraro ticket lost decisively to Reagan and George H. W. Bush.

That loss does not erase the precedent. Like Shirley Chisholm’s 1972 presidential campaign, Ferraro’s nomination changed the practical category of possibility.

O’Connor still wins because her breakthrough came with a lifetime appointment and immediate binding authority.

A losing vice-presidential campaign demonstrates electoral possibility.

A justice’s vote changes the law whether the public likes the vote or not.

Barbara McClintock is the most intellectually formidable runner-up. She received the 1983 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for discoveries involving mobile genetic elements, work that had been conducted much earlier and whose scientific significance grew as molecular genetics caught up with insights initially regarded skeptically.

Her Nobel recognition changed the public image of women in science and honored research with enormous biological importance.

The birthday method weakens her claim because the central experimental actions occurred decades before the prize. The award is a consequential recognition inside 1976–1986, not the decade in which the scientific breakthrough itself was made.

O’Connor’s decisive action occurred squarely inside the window.

Her appointment also exposed the physical assumptions built into institutions.

The Supreme Court building, like many workplaces designed around a male professional elite, had spaces and routines that presumed the justices would be men. Accounts of O’Connor’s arrival describe practical adjustments to facilities near the Court.

The detail is almost too convenient as a metaphor, which is why it should not carry more weight than it deserves.

Architecture encodes expectations.

A restroom, robing room, uniform, schedule, security protocol, leave policy, or social custom can reveal who the institution imagined would occupy the job before any explicit rule says others are excluded.

The first outsider often discovers barriers everybody inside has stopped seeing.

O’Connor’s appointment did not simply change the person in the room.

It forced the room to acknowledge its design.

The larger professional pipeline was changing at the same time. Women’s law-school enrollment had risen rapidly during the 1970s. Federal and state courts were beginning to include more women. Law firms, prosecutors’ offices, academia, government agencies, and corporate legal departments were confronting a generation of women lawyers who could no longer be treated as isolated exceptions.

A Supreme Court appointment amplifies that pipeline because the Court is the profession’s most visible summit.

The summit does not create the climbers.

It changes what institutions below it can plausibly say about where the climbers belong.

This is one of the reasons “first woman” should not be dismissed as merely symbolic.

Professional expectations are partly produced by what people observe. A hiring partner who has never seen a woman argue a major case can call male authority traditional. A student who has never seen a woman justice can internalize the office as male without anyone explicitly telling her so. A president can imagine a short list through precedents established by earlier presidents.

Visibility changes priors.

It does not guarantee outcomes.

O’Connor herself resisted being reduced to gender representation. That resistance is part of the achievement.

The first member of a group often faces contradictory demands.

Be excellent enough to justify the group’s inclusion.

Represent the group’s interests.

Do not mention the group too often.

Be grateful for the breakthrough.

Do not appear to have benefited from identity.

Be symbolically available while behaving like an ordinary professional.

No person can satisfy all of those expectations coherently.

Institutional equality becomes more mature when the person can be evaluated for decisions rather than forced to embody a category.

O’Connor’s record helped move the Court in that direction because her jurisprudence could disappoint advocates who had celebrated her appointment and please people who had opposed the idea that women judges would necessarily transform law in a particular ideological direction.

A female justice could be conservative in one case, moderate in another, protective of state power in one domain, skeptical of a sex classification in another, and hard to predict from sex alone.

That is normalization.

It is also one reason her breakthrough differs from a representational office like Congress.

Shirley Chisholm could legitimately argue that Brooklyn’s voters had sent her to represent their interests.

O’Connor’s oath required a different theory of role. Her authority derived from constitutional office and judicial judgment, not from representing women as a political constituency.

The distinction matters for evaluating later women judges as well.

If the public treats a woman justice as a delegate of women, disagreement becomes betrayal.

If the public treats her as a justice, disagreement becomes jurisprudence.

Equality includes the right to be wrong in ordinary professional ways.

By July 1986, O’Connor had been on the Court for nearly five terms. Her later status as a frequent decisive vote in the Rehnquist Court was still developing. The nomination of Antonin Scalia and elevation of William Rehnquist later that year would change the Court’s composition just after the birthday window closed.

This timing is useful.

The chapter does not need the mature “swing justice” reputation to make the case.

The first five years are sufficient.

A woman had entered the final court.

She had written constitutional opinions.

She had participated in decisions touching sex discrimination, abortion, religion, federalism, criminal law, and government power.

The Court’s authority had not diminished because a woman exercised it.

That sounds absurdly obvious now.

Historical breakthroughs often sound obvious after institutions metabolize them.

The absence that lasted almost two hundred years begins to look inexplicable rather than natural.

That reversal is one of the signs the precedent worked.

There is a temptation to finish a chapter like this with a celebratory staircase: O’Connor opened the door, then Ruth Bader Ginsburg arrived, then more women followed, until the Court eventually included several women at once.

The sequence is real and too neat.

Women’s representation on the Court did not rise smoothly. O’Connor served alone as the only woman justice until Ginsburg joined in 1993. When O’Connor retired in 2006, Ginsburg again became the only woman until Sonia Sotomayor joined in 2009. Representation can advance and recede.

A first is irreversible as history and reversible as composition.

That distinction keeps the achievement from becoming a fairy tale.

O’Connor did not guarantee the Court would represent the population evenly.

She made it impossible for an all-male tradition to defend itself as untested necessity.

The next president had evidence.

The next Senate had precedent.

The next woman lawyer had an office in the realm of the possible.

And the next case involving sex equality could be decided by a Court that included a woman not as witness, litigant, or attorney, but as one of the people saying what the Constitution meant.

**Verdict:** Sandra Day O’Connor wins 1976–1986 because her 1981 appointment converted female authority on the Supreme Court from aspiration into binding constitutional power. The firstness mattered, but the stronger case is operational: within the window she was already writing and casting votes in major constitutional cases, including a sex-discrimination decision that demonstrated why representation cannot mean ideological delegation. Sally Ride and Geraldine Ferraro changed national imagination at comparable scale; O’Connor wins on jurisdiction. She entered the institution whose decisions constrain every other branch and level of American government, and after she did, “justice” could no longer pretend its historic maleness was part of the job description.
