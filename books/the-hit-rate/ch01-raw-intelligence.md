# Raw Intelligence

On 5 March 1980, somebody sat down to describe a place he could not see.

The target was not a card in a psychology laboratory. It was tied to the hostage crisis at the American embassy in Tehran. The surviving session report is stamped by the habits of bureaucracy: classification markings, distribution restrictions, a session number, a purpose, a protocol. Then comes the sentence that should have followed the remote-viewing program everywhere it went.

The viewer's impressions were raw intelligence data. They had not been subjected to intermediate analysis, evaluation or collation. Interpretation and use were the requester's responsibility.

This is an extraordinary disclaimer because it contains, in miniature, the entire problem.

Imagine that the viewer writes down a corridor, a courtyard, a man, a vehicle, a fence, a smell of fuel, a sense of water nearby. Some details may correspond to the target. Some may not. Some are so generic that they will correspond to thousands of places. Some may be specific but wrong. Some may become impressive only after the analyst knows what happened. The transcript can therefore be interesting before it is useful, and useful-looking before it is true.

That distinction is not peculiar to psychic spying. It is the ordinary misery of intelligence analysis. Satellites produce images that need interpretation. Intercepts arrive in fragments. Human sources lie, misunderstand, exaggerate and occasionally know exactly what they claim to know. Radar returns have clutter. A sensor does not hand an analyst reality. It hands over a measurement shaped by the sensor.

Remote viewing added a particularly vicious sensor problem: the instrument was a person producing language.

Language is generous. It tolerates approximation. It can be scored at several resolutions. A viewer who says "large vertical structure" might have meant a tower, a crane, a chimney, a mast, a cliff, a tall building, or the visual impression of any of them. Once the target is revealed, the phrase can suddenly feel precise. Before reveal, it may barely constrain the world.

That is why the hit rate matters, and why the phrase itself is dangerous.

A hit rate requires a denominator. How many sessions? Which sessions count? What was decided in advance? Who scored them? Against how many possible targets? Were judges blind? Were the transcripts edited? Was feedback available? Were viewers selected because they had previously performed well? Were unsuccessful viewers quietly dropped? Was the question whether the target could be identified from a fixed set, whether individual descriptors matched, or whether an intelligence officer found something in the report worth following?

Change the denominator and the story changes.

The U.S. government did not merely entertain remote viewing as cocktail-party weirdness. Declassified records show operationally framed sessions conducted in response to real intelligence requests. Reports in the CIA's STARGATE collection document sessions connected to the Tehran hostage situation. They also show the bureaucracy trying to discipline the material with protocols, session numbers, cueing records and warnings about interpretation. That is evidence that the activity occurred. It is not evidence that psychic perception worked.

This distinction sounds elementary until one reads popular accounts of classified research. Classification exerts a peculiar glamour. If a report was SECRET, readers often unconsciously promote the claims inside it. But classification answers a security question, not an epistemic one. A classified report can contain excellent intelligence, rumors, mistakes, raw leads, failed experiments, deliberate deception, or a transcript of somebody being confidently wrong. Governments classify uncertainty too.

One surviving training report is useful precisely because it is bad. In a session identified as LIII, the summary states that the target images appeared to show "absolutely no target correlation." The viewer was working without an interviewer for the first time and worried about falling asleep. The record is not a debunking of the entire program. It is more valuable than that. It shows that failure was legible enough to be written into the archive.

A serious history has to count those pages with the spectacular ones.

This becomes difficult because memorable correspondence is a selection machine. Suppose a viewer produces twenty impressions and two are astonishing. Those two will survive retelling. The eighteen misses become atmospheric noise. Suppose ten viewers work a target and one says something uncannily close. The successful transcript becomes the anecdote. Suppose one operational request appears to help locate something while fifty generate nothing actionable. The one case acquires a name.

None of this proves fraud. It describes how humans remember.

The scientific question is whether a scoring procedure established before target revelation can reliably distinguish remote-viewing output from chance or controls. The intelligence question is harsher: can the material improve a decision enough to justify the attention, money, false leads, and confidence it consumes?

Those are not the same test.

The difference became explicit when the program was reviewed in the 1990s. CIA's own later public summary says the independent evaluation found enough accurate remote-viewing experiences to defy randomness, while also concluding that the phenomenon was too unreliable, inconsistent and sporadic to be useful for intelligence. The agency did not restore the program.

That sentence is routinely recruited by both sides. Advocates stop after "defy randomness." Skeptics begin at "too unreliable." The interesting territory is the conjunction.

What would it mean for both to be true?

Take a fictional sensor that guesses whether a concealed target is a building, landscape, machine, or person. Chance is twenty-five percent. Over a large experiment the sensor scores thirty percent. With enough trials, the difference may be statistically difficult to attribute to chance under the model being used. The scientific result could deserve investigation.

Now hand that sensor to an intelligence officer looking for a hostage.

The officer does not care that the aggregate distribution moved five percentage points. She needs to know whether today's answer is one of the correct ones. If the sensor cannot attach calibrated confidence to individual outputs, its modest aggregate edge may not translate into a usable decision. Worse, the output may contain rich descriptive material that invites analysts to construct explanations around it. A weak statistical effect wrapped in vivid language can create more subjective confidence than a weak statistical effect deserves.

The problem is not that thirty percent is "too low" in some universal sense. Intelligence routinely uses weak indicators. A slight edge can be valuable when repeated cheaply, combined with independent evidence, and calibrated. The problem is conversion.

How does an effect size become a probability attached to a claim?

How does a probability become a search decision?

How does the search decision perform against a baseline without the psychic input?

How many false leads does it create?

Does the analyst know which parts of the transcript deserve weight before the target is known?

The remote-viewing literature is full of arguments about the first question and surprisingly thin, at least in the public record, on the complete chain.

That gap is where this book lives.

There is another complication. Remote viewing was never one perfectly stable experimental object. Protocols changed. Sponsors changed. Contractors changed. Operational demands intruded. Viewers were selected and trained. Some sessions involved outbound beacons; others used coordinates or other cueing. Some work belonged to laboratory research, some to training, some to intelligence requests. Later summaries compress this history into a program name, STARGATE, as though a single apparatus ran unchanged for two decades.

It did not.

Even the names invite confusion. The public archive contains material under GRILL FLAME and STARGATE among other labels. CIA's own popular history notes transfers between CIA and DIA and changing project names. A clean narrative is attractive because the administrative reality is ugly. The ugliness matters. When a phenomenon is weak, procedural differences can become the whole experiment.

The same problem appears in the scientific literature after the government program. A 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis in the Journal of Scientific Exploration reported 36 studies and 40 effect sizes and concluded that remote-viewing protocols showed an average effect above chance. That is a claim worth examining, not a verdict to inherit. Meta-analysis can aggregate evidence; it can also aggregate differences in protocol, quality, selection and scoring. Its conclusions depend on what enters the pool and how the outcomes are made commensurable.

The broader anomalous-perception literature demonstrates why this is not a trivial concern. Ganzfeld research has produced decades of argument over replication, methodology, selection and meta-analysis. One influential later meta-analysis found above-chance effects in a selected set of studies; other reviews have failed to confirm earlier claims under different inclusion periods or methods. The existence of a literature is not the same thing as convergence.

There is a temptation here to choose a tribe. It should be resisted.

The skeptical version of the story says the government wasted years chasing nonsense until competent reviewers killed it. That version has a problem: it often treats every positive experimental result as contaminated in advance and every institutional continuation as mere credulity. It can become unfalsifiable skepticism.

The believer's version says the government proved psychic functioning, used it operationally, then shut the public program while the real work continued in secret. That version has the opposite problem: termination becomes evidence of continuation, missing records become evidence of deeper classification, and every failure can be blamed on the inherently elusive nature of psi. It can become unfalsifiable belief.

A useful book needs a place to stand where both stories can lose.

Here is mine.

If preregistered or otherwise methodologically strong remote-viewing experiments repeatedly produce a stable effect across independent laboratories, with scoring procedures that do not depend on post hoc generosity, then the claim that the entire phenomenon is ordinary noise becomes increasingly expensive. Mechanism can remain unknown. Science has lived with unexplained effects before.

But if that effect cannot be converted into calibrated information at the individual-session level, then claims of operational intelligence value should remain weak no matter how impressive the aggregate p-value becomes.

Conversely, if archival operational records show that remote-viewing input repeatedly changed decisions for the better under conditions where conventional information leakage, selective reporting and retrospective scoring can be excluded, the operational case strengthens even if laboratory theory remains confused.

And if the best results shrink toward chance as protocols tighten, independent replication expands, target sets become harder to game, and all trials are counted, then the paranormal interpretation should lose status.

These are defeat conditions. They are more important than atmosphere.

The Tehran files make a good place to begin because they strip away the romance. A hostage crisis is not a spoon-bending demonstration. The requester needs information. The viewer produces impressions. The report warns that the impressions are raw. Somewhere downstream, somebody has to decide whether to believe any of them.

That downstream step is rarely the star of the story. It is paperwork. Comparison. Collation. The analyst has to decide whether "water" means the target is near a river or whether the viewer simply said water. If the target is later revealed to be near a fountain, a port, a drainage ditch, a swimming pool, or a blue roof, somebody has to decide whether the descriptor earns a point.

This is where a paranormal claim becomes an epistemology problem.

The word "raw" is doing enormous work. Raw intelligence is not useless intelligence. Signals intelligence begins raw. Imagery begins raw. Human reporting begins raw. But established intelligence disciplines possess elaborate methods for provenance, confidence, cross-cueing, source validation and error correction. They know, imperfectly, what their sensors are measuring.

Remote viewing asked the system to ingest a source whose transfer function was unknown.

A transfer function describes how an input becomes an output. For a camera, optics and electronics transform photons into an image. For radar, transmitted energy and reflected returns become measurements. For a human source, access, memory, motive and reporting behavior intervene. For a remote viewer, if the claimed phenomenon exists, we do not know what physical or cognitive channel carries the target information. We therefore do not know which distortions to expect.

That ignorance matters operationally more than it matters statistically.

If a camera has a known blur kernel, analysts can compensate. If a radar has a known false-alarm distribution, operators can set thresholds. If a source has a history of being right about missile deployments and wrong about leadership politics, the history becomes calibration. But what is the error model for a viewer who sometimes gives striking spatial correspondences and sometimes gives "absolutely no target correlation"?

The program tried to build one through training, protocol and evaluation. Whether it succeeded is the story ahead.

The first task is more modest. We have to stop treating every apparent hit as the same kind of object.

There is a **descriptor hit**: a word or sketch resembles a target feature.

There is a **target-identification hit**: blinded judging selects the correct target from alternatives at a rate above chance.

There is an **operational hit**: information reaches a requester in time and causes or materially improves a useful action.

There is a **retrospective hit**: after the outcome is known, participants recognize something in an earlier transcript as meaningful.

There is a **narrative hit**: years later, a case becomes the story people use to explain why the program mattered.

Those categories can overlap. They are not interchangeable.

The most famous remote-viewing stories often become powerful because the last category swallows the first four. A narrative hit is portable. It fits in an interview. It can survive without the full target pool, failed sessions, timestamps, analyst notes, or base rates. A quantitative audit is less cooperative. It asks for the boring pages.

So this book will ask for boring pages.

For every operational case that carries argumentative weight, the publication burden is the same: the request as it existed before the session; the cue given to the viewer; the complete transcript rather than selected descriptors; timing; all participating viewers; all sessions tied to the task; the scoring or evaluation rule; feedback available before later sessions; conventional intelligence already known to participants and analysts; the action, if any, taken because of the material; and the eventual ground truth.

If those pieces are missing, the case may still be interesting. It cannot carry the weight assigned to a controlled experiment.

For experimental work, the burden changes: target-selection procedure, randomization, blinding, judge independence, complete trial accounting, preregistered or fixed scoring, effect size, uncertainty, replication, and evidence of analytic flexibility. A tiny p-value is not a substitute for design.

This sounds severe only because the subject has spent decades being granted exceptions. It should not receive them. If remote viewing is real, weak standards do it no favors. If it is not, weak standards are how it survives.

There is one more reason to care about this old program now.

Modern institutions are drowning in systems that produce probabilistic outputs nobody fully understands. Machine-learning models generate classifications with measurable aggregate accuracy and unpredictable individual failures. Intelligence services fuse heterogeneous sensors whose errors are correlated in ways analysts may not see. Automated systems can be statistically impressive and operationally brittle. We are relearning, at industrial scale, the distinction between a benchmark score and a trustworthy answer.

Remote viewing is an extreme version of that problem. The proposed sensor was a human mind. The mechanism was unknown. The outputs were semantically rich. The error distribution was obscure. The stakes were real. The institution spent years trying to determine whether a measurable edge could become dependable information.

That is a better story than psychic spies.

It may also be a more consequential one.

In the 1980 Tehran report, responsibility for interpretation was pushed downstream to the requester. That bureaucratic sentence was prudent. It was also an admission. The system did not yet know how to convert the viewer into an instrument.

Forty-six years later, the argument over remote viewing still turns on the same conversion.

Not whether somebody once said something uncanny.

How often. Compared with what. Scored by whom. Known when. Useful how.

The hit rate is not a number until those questions have answers.
