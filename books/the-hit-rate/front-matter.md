# THE HIT RATE

## When a Psychic Signal Is Not Intelligence

Sven Hardy Benson

Drafting

There is a sentence buried in the declassified remote-viewing archive that is more useful than most of the mythology built around the program.

The impressions, the reports repeatedly warn, are **raw intelligence data**. They have not been subjected to intermediate analysis, evaluation, or collation. Interpretation belongs to the requester.

That warning is not an embarrassment to be edited out. It is the book.

The United States spent years investigating whether people could describe distant or hidden targets without conventional sensory access. Some evaluators later argued that controlled experiments contained effects that could not comfortably be dismissed as chance. The same institutional history ended with a judgment that the material was too unreliable, inconsistent, and sporadic to support intelligence operations. Those two claims are often presented as though one must annihilate the other. They do not.

A phenomenon can produce a statistical residue and still fail every practical test that matters. A laboratory can detect a weak signal that an operator cannot use. A scoring method can distinguish two populations while being unable to tell an analyst which sentence in tomorrow's transcript is true. A viewer can occasionally describe something striking and still produce enough attractive wrong material to make the stream dangerous. Intelligence has an asymmetrical problem that parapsychology does not: the cost of acting on the wrong hit can exceed the value of ten correct impressions.

This book therefore treats remote viewing as an information problem before it treats it as a paranormal claim.

Four evidence classes govern the manuscript.

**Documented facts** include declassified program records, session reports, official histories, contracts, congressional or agency material, and peer-reviewed papers whose existence and stated results can be checked. A document proves that a document existed and said something. It does not automatically prove the phenomenon described inside it.

**Credible but incomplete evidence** includes attributed testimony, retrospective accounts by participants, reporting based on identifiable sources, and records whose surrounding operational context remains missing. These can guide questions. They cannot carry conclusions alone.

**Disputed allegations** include claims of spectacular operational successes, paranormal mechanisms, specific intelligence coups, foreign breakthroughs, or hidden continuations that lack independent confirmation. They remain claims even when repeated by decorated officers, scientists, contractors, journalists, or former intelligence personnel.

**Speculative synthesis** is mine. The main synthesis here is that the remote-viewing controversy has been damaged by collapsing three different quantities into one word—success. Experimental effect, descriptive correspondence, and decision usefulness are not interchangeable. The book will try to build a conversion chain between them. If that chain cannot be built from the surviving evidence, the thesis will be demoted.

No classified access is claimed or implied. Everything used here is lawfully public. Redactions are treated as missing information, not as evidence for whatever dramatic fact would make the story better.

The title refers to the number everybody wants and almost nobody can define without making a choice first: the hit rate.
