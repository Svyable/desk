## Thesis

Cold War intelligence agencies repeatedly tried to turn living animals into sensors, carriers, recovery systems, and covert platforms not because officials had become whimsical, but because evolution had already solved sensing and mobility problems that electronics could not yet solve. The failures are as revealing as the successes: a dolphin could outperform sonar in cluttered water, a pigeon could carry a camera beneath the altitude regime of aircraft, and a cat could carry a microphone yet refuse to become an obedient intelligence platform. The neglected history is therefore about the boundary between sensing and taskability: biology can possess extraordinary capability without becoming a controllable machine.

## Evidence classes

1. **Verified/declassified/documented:** CIA and Navy records describing animal programs, hardware, missions, tests, and acknowledged operational uses.
2. **Credible but incomplete:** official retrospectives and participant accounts whose underlying operational files remain partial or classified.
3. **Disputed allegations:** offensive dolphin claims, assassination stories, embellished Acoustikitty lore, and other claims without independent documentary confirmation.
4. **Speculative synthesis:** what animal programs teach about embodied intelligence, autonomy, biohybrid systems, and the limits of replacing evolved sensing with machines.

## Defeat condition

The book's central argument weakens if the record shows that animal programs were principally publicity curiosities with no durable operational advantage, or if modern autonomous systems have cleanly reproduced the relevant biological capabilities under equivalent field conditions.
