# The Ethics of Recruiting Another Species

The animal cannot enlist.

That fact is easy to acknowledge and difficult to operationalize. Working animals exist throughout human society, and military service is not automatically the worst thing humans ask of them. But secrecy removes ordinary observation. A classified mission can make welfare evidence harder to inspect at exactly the moment the institution has a reason to emphasize mission success.

The ethical ledger therefore has to be concrete: acquisition, housing, transport, training methods, invasive procedures, deployment risk, injury, mortality, veterinary care, retirement and disposition. The Acoustikitty story makes this obvious because surgery was part of the platform. The marine-mammal program makes it larger because the institution persisted for decades.

The Navy points to a substantial scientific and veterinary literature generated around its animals. That work matters and should be read rather than waved away as public relations. So should independent welfare criticism. The interesting question is not whether military veterinarians care about animals. Many plainly do. It is how an institution measures acceptable animal risk when the mission belongs to humans.

A serious book cannot make the animals adorable when it wants narrative warmth and turn them into equipment when it wants engineering clarity. They remain animals in both sentences.
