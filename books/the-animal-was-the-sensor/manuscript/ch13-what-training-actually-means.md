# What Training Actually Means

Training is the interface layer between two nervous systems.

That sounds grander than the work looks. Cue, search, response, reward, repeat. Yet the procedure solves something robotics still finds difficult: how to specify a useful behavior without specifying every movement required to produce it.

A handler does not calculate the dolphin's acoustic scene. The animal does. The handler defines a task boundary and reinforces an outcome. This is a form of programming, but the executable system arrived with drives, perception, memory and its own error modes.

Acoustikitty failed at this interface. The electronics could be made small enough; the behavior could not be made reliable enough. Navy dolphins succeeded because the species, task and training regime aligned differently. “Trainability” is therefore not a decorative animal trait. It is a systems requirement.

Modern machine learning makes the analogy tempting, and the analogy should be handled carefully. An animal is not a neural network in fur. But both histories force engineers to confront specification by examples, reward, generalization and behavior outside the training distribution. The older animal programs are useful because their failures are impossible to pretend are merely software bugs.
