# The Soviet Dolphin Problem

Every secret program attracts a twin behind the adversary's fence.

Claims about Soviet and later Russian military dolphins range from well-supported training programs to lurid stories of animals carrying weapons against divers. This is exactly where a comparative book can become dishonest by accumulation: one American document, one Russian newspaper account, one former trainer, one photograph, and suddenly a coherent hidden doctrine appears that no source actually established.

The Soviet case must therefore be built from the bottom up. Facility existence is one question. Species and training are another. Mine detection, object recovery, swimmer detection and offensive action are separate claims. Soviet, Russian, Ukrainian and Western intelligence sources have different access and incentives. Crimea adds another layer because animals, facilities and institutional custody became entangled with territorial conflict.

Until that ledger is complete, the Soviet material belongs in a chapter about uncertainty rather than proof. Convergent state interest would be meaningful if independently documented: two rival systems discovering that marine mammals solved similar underwater sensing problems would tell us something about the underlying engineering niche. But convergence cannot be manufactured by repeating each side's folklore.

The comparative test is demanding on purpose. If the same capability emerges independently, show the records. If it does not, leave the symmetry broken.
