# First Come, First Served

The rule sounds almost childlike: whoever got here first goes first.

That simplicity is the source of its strength. First come, first served requires no committee to measure merit, no market to discover a price, no expert to judge urgency, no lottery machine, and no identity system more sophisticated than an ability to preserve order. It asks strangers to agree on one fact about the past: arrival sequence.

The rule is so common that it can feel natural, as if a line were discovered rather than invented. It is not natural. Crowds do not automatically become queues. In some settings, people form orderly lines. In others, they cluster around the point of service and negotiate position through proximity, attention, social cues, rank or persistence. Queue discipline is learned, enforced and culturally interpreted.

What makes chronological order attractive is not that earlier people are inherently more deserving. It is that arrival time is often a cheap proxy for claim strength. The person who has already waited has incurred a cost. Moving someone behind a later arrival imposes an additional cost on the earlier person and signals that waiting does not reliably earn progress.

First come, first served turns endurance into a property right of sorts.

The right is narrow. You do not own the service. You own a position in the sequence, conditional on continuing to participate. That position can be surprisingly valuable. People hold places for friends, hire others to stand in line, create virtual waiting rooms, resell reservations, run bots for scarce online releases, and organize informal systems of numbers and names when institutions fail to provide one.

Whenever a position becomes valuable, a secondary economy appears around it.

This tells us something about queues that the clean mathematics can hide. Waiting time is not merely delay. It can become an investment. A person who has already waited three hours is reluctant to leave even if the expected remaining wait becomes unpleasant. The sunk-cost logic is partly psychological and partly rational. Leaving means forfeiting a scarce position.

The line therefore creates commitment.

That commitment can stabilize the system. If people believe their position is secure, they need not fight for physical proximity to the server. Tickets and numbered queues make this especially clear. A paper number allows the body to leave the exact spot while preserving the temporal claim. A virtual queue goes further, separating order from place entirely.

This is one of the profound shifts in the history of waiting: the queue becomes an informational object rather than a physical arrangement.

Once position can be recorded, institutions can manipulate it. They can merge multiple lines into one. They can create priority classes. They can estimate waits. They can invite people to arrive later. They can call the next eligible person from a dispersed population. They can allow rescheduling. They can silently reorder cases.

The physical line has a virtue that sophisticated queues sometimes lose: violations are visible.

If somebody walks to the front of a bakery line, the crowd sees the exception. If an application in a digital system is expedited, everyone else may have no idea. The ability to reorder invisibly is operationally useful. It can also make fairness harder to audit.

This tension runs through nearly every modern queue. Efficiency favors flexibility. Legitimacy often favors legibility.

A single serpentine line feeding several cashiers is a good example of a design that can improve both. In separate lines, customers must choose a server before knowing which line will move fastest. One difficult transaction can trap everyone behind it while another cashier goes quickly. People become amateur forecasters. They study cart sizes, customer behavior, employee speed and the mysterious risk that somebody ahead will request a price check.

A pooled line removes the forecasting burden. The next available server takes the next customer. This can reduce variation in waiting and eliminate the painful experience of choosing the “wrong” line. The rule still feels chronological because the shared sequence is visible.

Pooling is powerful because it shares variability.

The same principle appears in call centers, computing systems, hospital resource pools and logistics networks. A larger shared pool can often handle random fluctuations better than several isolated pools with the same total capacity. One server's temporary difficulty is absorbed by others.

But pooling has limits. Skills differ. Locations differ. Customers differ. A single giant queue can become administratively efficient while psychologically alienating. Sometimes separate queues reflect real specialization; sometimes they reflect political or commercial segmentation.

Airports show the segmentation clearly. A passenger entering a terminal may encounter separate security lanes for ordinary travelers, trusted-traveler programs, premium cabins, employees and crew. At the gate, boarding groups convert a crowd into a ranked sequence. Inside some lounges, another service tier begins. The airline does not operate one queue. It operates a portfolio of queues attached to a revenue and loyalty model.

This is where first come, first served begins to fracture.

Chronology works well when claims are roughly comparable and delay consequences are modest. It works poorly when urgency differs drastically. An emergency department that served strictly by arrival order would be simple and grotesque. A person with a minor injury who arrived first should not block immediate care for a person with a life-threatening condition.

The moment urgency matters, time of arrival becomes only one variable.

Triage replaces a universal queue with a classification system. The patient is assessed, assigned a priority, and then usually ordered within that class. This is not the rejection of queues. It is a more complex queue discipline.

The moral justification is powerful: service should reflect clinical need. The administrative burden is powerful too: need must be assessed, categories defined, judgment exercised, reassessment performed and edge cases handled. Priority creates discretion. Discretion creates the possibility of error, bias and gaming.

Simple queues reduce discretion by accepting crudeness. Complex queues improve fit by accepting judgment.

This is a recurring institutional tradeoff.

Civil-service systems, university admissions, grant review, public housing, visas, courts and benefit programs all face versions of it. A pure chronology rule is transparent but may ignore important differences. A multi-factor ranking can respond to those differences but becomes harder to understand and easier to contest.

The problem becomes especially sharp when the criteria are partly subjective.

Suppose a city has a limited number of permits available each month. First come, first served seems neutral, but experienced firms may learn exactly when the portal opens and submit instantly. Wealthier applicants may hire specialists. Automated systems may beat humans. The chronological queue now rewards preparation capacity rather than merely time.

The institution can respond with a lottery. Now technical speed matters less, but luck matters more. It can use merit criteria, but merit requires definition and review. It can auction permits, but ability to pay becomes decisive. It can reserve shares for different categories, but category design becomes political.

Every alternative reveals what first come, first served had been concealing: there is no neutral way to distribute scarcity.

Chronology is a value choice that often presents itself as an absence of choice.

Its appeal partly comes from procedural equality. The same rule applies to everyone who joins. But equal rules can encounter unequal lives.

A day spent waiting is not the same cost for every person. The salaried professional with a laptop may work from a line. The hourly worker loses wages. The parent must arrange childcare. The person with a disability may experience physical waiting differently. The person who lives far away pays travel costs. The person with strong language skills may navigate instructions faster.

The queue can be formally equal and substantively expensive for the least flexible.

This is why some institutions replace waiting with appointments. An appointment does not eliminate scarcity; it schedules the queue into the future. The customer waits in calendar time rather than in a room.

That transformation often improves welfare because it lets people use the interval for something else. Two hours spent physically waiting and two hours spent at home before a precise appointment occupy the same clock but not the same life.

The difference is control.

Uncertain waiting is particularly costly because it prevents alternative use of time. If I know service will begin at three, I can plan the morning. If I am told that service may begin at any point between noon and five, the institution has effectively reserved five hours of my availability while paying for none of it.

This is why narrow service windows feel like respect and vague windows feel like power.

The ability to impose uncertainty on others is a form of leverage.

Organizations experience uncertainty too. A service provider may not know how long the previous case will take, whether staff will be absent, whether equipment will fail or whether demand will surge. Tight appointments can create idle time if customers do not arrive and cascading delay if cases run long. The institution protects itself with buffers and wide windows. Customers protect themselves by arriving early, overbooking alternatives or calling repeatedly. Each side exports uncertainty to the other.

A queue is often a negotiation over who must hold the buffer.

First come, first served places much of the buffer on the customer. Arrive early enough and your place is secure. Reservations move some buffer to the provider, which must honor future commitments even if a more profitable or convenient demand appears later. Deposits and cancellation fees are attempts to rebalance the risk of no-shows.

This makes restaurant reservations, medical appointments and airline tickets more interesting than they first appear. They are not merely conveniences. They are contracts over queue position.

A confirmed ticket says that future capacity has been allocated to you under conditions. Overbooking says the provider believes some confirmed claimants will not appear and is willing to sell more claims than immediate capacity, betting on historical behavior. When the bet fails, a new queue must be created at the gate, often with compensation to induce volunteers to surrender positions.

The system has sold time twice and must repair the conflict.

That repair can be elegant when voluntary prices clear the shortage. It can be ugly when people are involuntarily denied service after believing their place was secure. The emotional reaction again exceeds the minutes involved because the institution violated a claim that felt owned.

The legitimacy of a queue depends heavily on whether commitments are credible.

This is visible in bureaucracies. Applicants tolerate long published processing times differently from constantly shifting estimates. A known fifteen-month process may be painful but plannable. An announced six-month process that repeatedly slips can create greater distress because people organize lives around expectations.

Prediction itself becomes part of service.

A queue that tells the truth about delay may be kinder than a faster queue that repeatedly promises what it cannot deliver.

There is a commercial temptation to underestimate waits. A restaurant may fear that an honest ninety-minute estimate will send customers away. A website may prefer “almost there” to an accurate position. A support system may announce unusually short expectations based on optimistic assumptions. These tactics can reduce abandonment at the cost of trust.

Once people learn that the estimate is strategic, they stop treating it as information.

The queue then loses one of its coordination functions. Customers arrive earlier than necessary, call to check status, submit duplicates and seek informal channels. The institution experiences these behaviors as nuisance demand. The customers experience them as rational responses to unreliable signals.

Trust reduces queue load.

This is an underappreciated operational fact. Reliable information allows people to wait elsewhere, choose substitutes, avoid duplicate requests and plan around scarcity. Opacity can create additional work.

First come, first served works best when position is visible, movement is credible, exceptions are understandable and participation costs are not wildly unequal.

Those conditions are more demanding than the rule's simplicity suggests.

They also explain why physical queues sometimes function better than technologically advanced replacements. A line at least answers three questions immediately: how many people are ahead of me, whether the line is moving, and whether someone is cutting. A digital process that cannot answer those questions may be computationally sophisticated and institutionally primitive.

Yet the physical line has one final problem: it treats time as if it were an abundant currency everyone can spend.

It is not.

The next chapter turns to the price of time itself. Once waiting is recognized as a cost, markets begin trying to sell relief from it. Express lanes, premium support, toll roads, priority boarding, expedited permits, fast passes, delivery subscriptions and private intermediaries all make the same offer in different clothes: pay money so that you can spend less life in the queue.

At that point chronology gives way to exchange.

The line does not disappear. It becomes a market in position.
