# The Queue for Medicine

A medical queue begins with an offense against the simplicity of the line: the person who arrived later may need to go first.

Few people object to this in principle. A child struggling to breathe should not wait behind a routine prescription refill merely because the refill arrived earlier. The difficulty begins when the difference is less obvious, when urgency has to be measured, when expected benefit matters, when resources are scarce for months rather than minutes, and when every priority rule creates people who must wait longer.

Medicine turns queue design into explicit ethics.

Emergency departments use triage because arrival time is an inadequate ordering principle. Patients are assessed according to severity and risk. The queue is continuously reinterpreted as information changes. Someone who appeared stable can deteriorate. A person who looked urgent may prove less so after evaluation.

The sequence is provisional.

This is fundamentally different from a ticket counter. In most retail lines, waiting does not alter the customer's underlying need very much. In medicine, delay can change the case. The queue can make people sicker.

That fact creates a feedback loop: waiting affects urgency, and urgency affects waiting.

A well-functioning triage system must therefore reassess. A patient cannot be classified once and forgotten.

The same principle applies to longer medical waitlists. A specialist referral, elective surgery or transplant listing is not static. Conditions evolve. New evidence appears. Treatments become available. Eligibility changes.

The queue is a living clinical model.

Organ transplantation makes the allocation problem brutally concrete. A donated organ cannot serve every patient who needs one. Compatibility constrains which recipients are possible. Transport time matters. Medical urgency matters. Expected benefit matters. Geographic and logistical factors can matter. Different organs require different allocation policies.

First come, first served would be easy to explain and medically indefensible.

Auctioning to the highest bidder would reveal willingness to pay and violate the moral purpose of the system.

Lottery would treat people symmetrically and ignore clinically meaningful differences.

The actual task is to build a rule that combines relevant medical and ethical considerations under severe scarcity.

No rule can make the tragedy disappear.

This is an important discipline for queue debates. Sometimes criticism assumes a perfect allocation exists somewhere outside the institution. It does not. If ten people need something and only one unit is available, nine people will not receive it now regardless of how elegant the rule is.

The institution is choosing the distribution of an unavoidable loss.

That does not excuse bad policy. It raises the standard for policy. The rule must be defensible precisely because the stakes cannot be made painless.

Medicine therefore develops explicit criteria, appeals, governance and review. The system tries to turn tragic choices into consistent procedure without pretending procedure makes them morally trivial.

This is one of the deepest roles of institutions: to carry decisions that would be unbearable as ad hoc personal judgment.

A clinician can tell a patient, truthfully, that allocation follows a policy larger than any one relationship. That policy can be criticized and revised, but it protects both patients and professionals from pure favoritism.

Rules create distance from personal power.

They also risk creating distance from individual reality.

Every scoring system compresses. A patient becomes variables: age, diagnosis, lab values, severity, compatibility, waiting time, geography, contraindications. The compression is necessary for consistent allocation. It can never contain the whole person.

This is the administrative tragedy of high-stakes queues. Fairness requires abstraction; dignity requires remembering what the abstraction omits.

The problem appears in less dramatic forms throughout health care.

Specialist appointments are scarce. Imaging capacity is finite. Operating rooms, nurses, beds and rehabilitation slots constrain flow. A hospital can have enough surgeons and not enough operating-room time. It can have beds and not enough nurses. It can discharge patients medically and still be unable to move them because post-acute care is unavailable.

The queue migrates through the care pathway.

This is why hospital crowding is a system problem. Adding emergency-department rooms does not solve crowding if admitted patients cannot move to inpatient beds. Adding inpatient beds does not help if staffing is insufficient. Faster surgery can increase pressure on recovery and rehabilitation.

The patient flows through coupled queues.

Healthcare leaders sometimes use the language of bed blockers or delayed discharge, but the phrase can obscure the human situation. An older person may be medically ready to leave acute care and have nowhere safe to go. The hospital bed is occupied not because the patient is at fault but because another service queue is constrained.

A social-care shortage appears as a hospital queue.

This is a recurring institutional pattern: the most visible queue forms downstream from the actual bottleneck.

Fixing the visible queue alone can produce strange incentives. Hospitals may be pressured to discharge faster without adequate community support. Emergency departments may move patients into observation categories. Metrics improve locally while risk moves elsewhere.

Health care punishes station-level optimization.

The unit of analysis has to be the patient journey.

That journey also exposes the burden of uncertain waiting. A delayed elective procedure may not be medically emergent, but it can mean chronic pain, inability to work, anxiety and repeated care. Calling the case “non-urgent” can make the wait sound costless.

Non-urgent does not mean unimportant.

Priority systems need this humility. The bottom category is still made of lives.

A queue can accumulate morally because each individual delay looks tolerable. One postponed operation is not a crisis. Ten thousand postponed operations become a population burden.

The aggregate cost can hide behind the absence of dramatic emergencies.

This is similar to maintenance. Preventive and routine work often waits because emergencies are more visible. Eventually routine delay creates emergencies.

Health systems therefore need capacity for both acute priority and ordinary flow.

If urgent cases consume every available resource, routine cases never recover. If routine schedules are packed too tightly, emergencies cause cascading cancellations. Slack has clinical value.

An empty bed can look inefficient until an emergency arrives.

An unscheduled operating slot can look wasteful until a trauma needs it. Reserve capacity is expensive, and unlimited reserve is impossible. The question is how much uncertainty the system is designed to absorb.

Pandemics made this tradeoff obvious at scale, but the underlying issue is present every day. Hospitals plan for peaks. Ambulance systems position vehicles. Blood banks manage inventories with expiration. Pharmacies manage shortages. Clinics reserve same-day appointments.

Health care is a portfolio of buffers.

Some buffers are physical inventory. Some are staff. Some are empty time. Some are referral agreements. Some are the ability to transfer patients.

Resilience is not one spare bed.

It is a network of alternate service paths.

The patient, however, experiences the network as a sequence of waits.

This creates a moral obligation around communication. If delay is unavoidable, what does the patient know? Is there an estimate? What symptoms should trigger reassessment? Is there a cancellation list? Can the patient travel elsewhere? Will the institution call, or must the patient keep checking?

Information can be clinical safety.

A queue with poor communication may turn stable waiting into dangerous waiting because patients do not know when to escalate.

This is where digital health systems can help and harm. Portals can provide results, scheduling and status. They can also shift monitoring work to patients and create new message queues for clinicians. Make communication easier and arrivals increase.

Lower friction reveals latent demand.

Clinicians may feel that messaging created demand. In many cases it revealed questions that previously went unanswered or required phone calls. The service channel changed the queue, not necessarily the underlying need.

This is a general lesson in access policy. When a queue becomes easier to enter, measured demand often rises. That does not prove the reform failed. It may prove earlier barriers were suppressing demand.

Capacity planning must anticipate this.

Telemedicine offers another version. Remote visits can reduce travel time and expand geographic access. They do not create infinite clinician hours. The queue may become easier to join, increasing demand for scarce specialists.

Access can worsen congestion before capacity adapts.

Again, the queue moves rather than vanishes.

Medicine also contains markets. Prices, insurance networks, provider contracts and reimbursement affect who can access what care. The formal clinical queue sits inside a financial queue.

A patient may be medically eligible and financially blocked. An insurer may require prior authorization. A clinician may wait for approval before treatment. The authorization process is an administrative queue layered onto the medical one.

Its purpose is cost and appropriateness control. Its burden is delay and work.

The key question is whether review meaningfully improves decisions enough to justify the waiting it creates.

Administrative queues are often defended through aggregate savings without fully pricing clinician and patient time. A form takes ten minutes. Multiply it by millions. A phone appeal consumes an hour. Add the staff on both sides. Delay a treatment. The hidden time cost becomes enormous.

This is why administrative simplification can be a health intervention.

The same caution applies: not all friction is waste. Some review prevents error, fraud or unnecessary care. Eliminating every check can increase harm.

The goal is not frictionlessness. It is purposeful friction.

Medicine gives us a vocabulary for this because screening and triage are accepted as necessary. The challenge is to distinguish clinically useful gates from administrative sediment.

Sediment accumulates when rules are added after failures and rarely removed.

One incident creates a form. A compliance concern creates a signature. A billing dispute creates documentation. Each addition has a reason. Over years, the patient and clinician move through a maze.

The queue becomes institutional memory in procedural form.

Some memory is vital. Some is obsolete fear.

Good governance periodically asks whether each step still reduces risk enough to justify its cost.

This is especially important when cost is shifted onto people with little bargaining power. A physician may tolerate paperwork because the payer controls reimbursement. A patient tolerates it because care is needed. The queue can survive not because it is efficient but because exit is difficult.

Monopoly protects delay.

Competition can sometimes pressure service improvement, but health care cannot rely on competition alone. Emergency need, information asymmetry, geography, insurance and provider scarcity limit shopping. Public standards and professional ethics remain central.

This makes queue legitimacy essential.

Patients may accept that someone sicker goes first. They are less accepting of unexplained delays, administrative errors or apparent favoritism.

Fairness in medicine therefore has at least four dimensions: the clinical priority rule, consistency of application, access to the queue, and burden while waiting.

A beautifully calibrated priority score means little if disadvantaged patients cannot get evaluated. Equal waitlist rules mean little if referral patterns differ. An appointment queue can look fair after entry while entry itself depends on geography, insurance or information.

The queue begins before the list.

This is the same lesson we saw in capital and housing. Intake is allocation.

The strongest institutions look upstream. Who never reaches triage? Who abandons? Who cannot take time off? Who lacks transportation? Who cannot navigate language or paperwork? Where does the system convert need into silence?

These questions are harder than measuring average wait because the missing people are not in the data.

Yet they are part of the moral population.

Medicine forces us to acknowledge that a queue can be fair among participants and unjust in who gets to participate.

The next chapter moves into government permissions, where that problem becomes structural. A permit, visa, license or court decision is not merely a service purchased from a provider. The institution often has monopoly authority. There may be no alternate queue.

When the state makes you wait, time becomes law.
