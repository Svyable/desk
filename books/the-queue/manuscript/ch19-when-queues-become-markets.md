# When Queues Become Markets

A place in line becomes an asset the moment someone else is willing to pay for it.

This can happen informally. A friend holds a place. A hired person waits outside a store. A broker secures an appointment. A reseller buys a ticket and sells it later. A software bot watches a release page more persistently than a human can.

The institution created a queue. Participants created a market around the queue.

Secondary markets appear because the official allocation rule and the users' valuations differ. First come, first served assigns scarce access by arrival time. If someone values the access more than the person who arrived first, trade can move the position.

Economically, this can improve allocation. Socially, it can feel like cheating.

That tension runs through ticket resale, reservation markets, licensing, launch drops and scarce appointments.

The first buyer obeyed the rule and earned position. The second buyer pays to acquire it. Is the queue still fair?

The answer depends partly on what the queue was meant to do.

If a concert organizer simply wants seats to go to people who value them most, resale can help discover willingness to pay. If the organizer deliberately priced tickets below market to make them accessible to a broader audience, scalping can defeat the distributional purpose.

The same transaction can be efficient relative to one objective and destructive relative to another.

Markets do not know the institution's moral intent unless rules encode it.

This is why some tickets are transferable and others are linked to identity. Why some reservations require deposits. Why some licenses cannot be resold. Why public benefits are nontransferable. Why spectrum rights, taxi medallions, emissions allowances and landing slots receive careful legal treatment around transfer.

Transferability is a queue-design choice.

Allow it and positions can move toward higher-valued uses. Restrict it and the original allocation rule retains more force.

The choice also changes speculation.

If scarce positions can be resold, people may join the queue not because they want the service but because they expect someone else to want it more later. Demand rises.

The market queue attracts investors.

This is familiar in ticketing and collectible product drops. Bots and professional resellers compete with end users because the initial price is below expected resale value. The original queue becomes a race to capture arbitrage.

Technology changes who can win that race.

A human refreshes a page. A bot can send many requests, use multiple accounts, monitor inventory continuously and react in milliseconds. The allocation rule “first request wins” begins rewarding automation infrastructure rather than enthusiasm.

Chronology becomes capital equipment.

Institutions respond with waiting rooms, lotteries, identity verification, purchase limits and anti-bot measures. The queue evolves into an adversarial system.

Every defense changes incentives again.

A lottery reduces speed advantage but creates many low-cost entries if identity is weak. Strong identity reduces duplication but adds privacy and access concerns. Purchase limits encourage participants to create multiple accounts. Nontransferability reduces resale but also harms legitimate users whose plans change.

There is no frictionless anti-scalping mechanism because the underlying cause remains: official price and market value differ.

Raise the official price high enough and some resale profit disappears. Then the organizer captures more revenue and lower-income buyers face higher access cost.

The queue problem has become a pricing problem.

This is why market-clearing arguments recur. If demand greatly exceeds supply at a posted price, why not raise the price until demand falls?

Because price is never only a technical variable. It determines who participates and what the institution represents.

A musician may want ordinary fans in the room. A public museum may want broad access. A university may want a diverse class rather than the richest class. A national park may allocate permits by lottery because willingness to pay is not considered the right claim on the resource.

The institution can choose not to maximize revenue.

That choice creates scarcity rents someone may try to capture.

One response is to make queue position costly in a nonfinancial way—requiring physical presence, for example. This deters some speculation but burns human time. People camp overnight for products or tickets. The spectacle can create community and marketing, but it also means access goes to those able to spend time.

Time is the anti-bot fee.

Again, the “free” queue has a price.

Online reservations produce a quieter secondary economy. Scarce restaurant tables, public appointments and recreational permits can attract services that monitor cancellations and notify users. Some simply improve information. Others book and resell positions.

Intermediaries flourish when search cost is high.

A person cannot refresh ten websites all day. A service can. It converts attention into software and sells the saved time.

This can be valuable innovation. It can also worsen the original queue by making automated competition necessary for everyone.

The intermediary becomes part of the bottleneck.

The pattern appears throughout markets. High-frequency traders invest in speed because market sequence matters. Search-engine optimization exists because ranking position matters. Delivery platforms compete for courier priority and customer attention. Advertising auctions allocate scarce impressions. App stores and online marketplaces rank products.

Modern capitalism contains markets for position in other queues.

Advertising is a particularly pure example. Consumers have scarce attention. Firms bid to move messages toward the front of the attention queue. The platform sells priority.

Search advertising monetizes intent. Social advertising monetizes predicted attention. In each case, the scarce service is a chance to be noticed.

The auction is a scheduler.

This can be efficient because price helps allocate impressions among advertisers with different values. It can also shape what the user sees in ways that are not identical to user welfare.

The platform is serving two queues at once: users seeking information and advertisers seeking access to users.

Multi-sided markets are coupled queues.

The service rule on one side affects demand on the other. More users attract advertisers. More advertiser revenue funds the platform. Too much advertising degrades user experience. The platform continuously chooses how much scarce attention to sell.

The queue becomes product governance.

Congestion pricing provides a more public example of queue markets. A road with too many cars rations through delay. Charge more at busy times and some trips shift. The price substitutes partly for waiting.

This can increase total welfare if travelers respond and alternatives exist. It can also create distributional concerns.

The crucial insight is that the unpriced system already has rationing. People pay with time.

The choice is not between rationing and freedom. It is between allocation rules.

This framing helps public debate because it prevents the status quo from masquerading as neutral.

A road queue privileges people whose schedules allow waiting. A toll privileges people who can pay. A transit priority lane privileges people willing or able to use transit. High-occupancy lanes privilege shared trips. Each policy defines what claim deserves scarce road space.

Transportation policy is queue ethics with concrete.

Emissions markets use a related logic. A cap creates scarcity in allowable emissions. Tradable permits let firms buy and sell claims under the cap. Instead of a regulator deciding the exact sequence of reductions, price helps allocate where reductions happen.

The queue is not temporal, but the structural idea holds: scarce permission is made transferable.

Markets are powerful when participants possess information the central allocator lacks. A factory knows its abatement cost. A traveler knows how flexible a trip is. An airline knows how it values a slot. Trading can reveal this private information.

But markets work poorly when ability to pay is morally irrelevant, externalities are unpriced, competition is weak or information is badly asymmetric.

A queue market needs boundaries.

That is why organ allocation is not a cash auction, why emergency access cannot be sold purely to the highest bidder, and why public rights are not generally transferable commodities.

The most dangerous sentence in market design is “just auction it” without asking what the institution is for.

The most dangerous sentence in anti-market design is “keep it free” without asking who pays in time.

Both ignore the hidden queue.

The mature question is which scarcity signal produces the best combination of efficiency, fairness, resilience and legitimacy for this service.

Sometimes hybrid systems are strongest.

A city can price congestion and return revenue to residents or fund transit. A ticket seller can use lotteries with purchase limits. A hospital can use clinical priority with waiting time as a tiebreaker. An airport can allocate slots under rules that preserve some entry opportunities. A cloud provider can guarantee baseline quotas and permit paid burst capacity.

Hybrid queues combine claims.

This can make them harder to explain and more robust in purpose.

Complexity should not be celebrated for its own sake. Every rule becomes a gaming surface. But single-variable allocation often fails when the scarce resource serves multiple values.

The system can use price where price is informative, chronology where chronology is legitimate, urgency where urgency matters, and lottery where claims are genuinely comparable.

Plural allocation can be more honest than pretending one principle rules everything.

Markets also teach a lesson about queue property rights. Once people invest in acquiring a position, they resist rule changes.

Taxi medallions, spectrum licenses, airport slots, fishing quotas, permits and other scarce claims can acquire large market value. Reforming the underlying system then changes asset values.

The queue becomes capitalized.

A rule intended merely to manage scarcity creates wealth for incumbents.

This can make policy reform politically expensive. A government may want to expand access, but current holders argue that they paid for rights under the old system. New entrants argue that incumbents received windfalls from artificial scarcity.

Both may have plausible claims.

Institutional design has memory because people build balance sheets around rules.

This is why queue rules should be designed with future change in mind. Licenses can include expiration. Slots can have usage requirements. Permits can be reviewed. Transfer rules can be explicit. Temporary emergency privileges can sunset.

A queue position should not accidentally become permanent sovereignty.

The same caution applies to digital platforms. Early ranking advantages can compound into dominant positions. Sellers with more sales receive more reviews, gain trust, rank higher and receive more sales.

The marketplace queue creates incumbency.

Platforms may need exploration mechanisms that expose new entrants despite weaker histories. Otherwise the ranking system turns past success into perpetual attention.

Again, fairness and efficiency can align. A marketplace that never explores new sellers may miss better products.

Competition requires queue mobility.

The ideal market queue is not one where everyone has the same position. It is one where position responds to relevant value without becoming impossible to challenge.

That is a useful definition of contestability.

The chapter's larger point is simple. When people hate waiting, entrepreneurs will sell ways around it. Some of those markets create real capacity. Some merely redistribute delay. Some reveal useful information. Some exploit opacity. Some convert public scarcity into private rents.

The test is to trace the flow.

Did the innovation add service, reduce arrivals, improve matching, or simply move one customer ahead of another? Who was pushed back? What did the institution intend the original queue to achieve? Can the new priority be challenged?

These questions keep efficiency from becoming a magic word.

The next chapter examines what happens when queue behavior shifts suddenly and collectively. Shortages, bank runs, fuel lines, panic buying and evacuation traffic show that people do not merely wait inside systems. They anticipate one another.

A queue can become dangerous when everyone believes being late will be fatal.
