# The Geopolitics of the Line

States worry about queues they do not control.

A country can have money and still wait for energy, medicine, chips, weapons, food, shipping capacity or industrial equipment if production is concentrated elsewhere. In ordinary times, price and contracts make the system feel like commerce. In a crisis, the queue beneath the market becomes visible.

Who gets the next shipment?

Who is an ally? Who has a long-term contract? Who paid a deposit years ago? Who can threaten retaliation? Who can manufacture a substitute? Who controls export permission? Who owns the transport route?

Scarcity turns supply chains into diplomacy.

This is not new. States have always cared about strategic materials, maritime access and industrial capacity. What changes is the density of modern interdependence. A product assembled in one country can depend on components, software, standards, minerals, tooling and finance from many others.

The queue is distributed across borders.

This makes geopolitical power partly a question of position inside production networks.

A country that controls a difficult-to-substitute bottleneck can influence others without owning the whole chain. A narrow manufacturing capability, processing stage, financial channel, shipping route or technical standard can become strategically important because many flows pass through it.

Power sits at the constrained interface.

The logic resembles a port, only the participants are states and multinational firms.

Consider energy. Import-dependent countries do not merely buy molecules. They buy reliability across time. Pipelines, terminals, storage, shipping, contracts and reserve capacity determine how exposed the system is to disruption.

A cheap supplier can be expensive if losing it creates an impossible queue for substitutes.

This is why diversification has option value even when it raises average cost.

A second supplier may sit idle relative to the cheapest source and still improve bargaining power. Storage may look inefficient until flows stop. Spare import capacity may earn little in normal conditions and become strategic during shortage.

Resilience is the ability to change queues.

The same is true of semiconductors. Advanced chips depend on specialized fabrication, design tools, manufacturing equipment, materials, packaging and expertise. No single label captures the chain.

Policy debates that ask whether a country “makes chips” can miss the actual bottlenecks.

The useful question is which stages can be substituted, at what quality, on what timeline and under whose permission.

A strategic queue is measured in replacement time.

If a component can be sourced elsewhere in two weeks, dependence is different from a component requiring five years to recreate.

Time is the hidden unit of economic security.

Governments respond through stockpiles, subsidies, domestic capacity, allied sourcing, export controls, procurement commitments and research funding. These policies are often debated as if they were simply about economic nationalism versus free trade.

The queue perspective makes the tradeoff more specific.

Global specialization can reduce cost and increase innovation. Concentration can create long replacement queues under disruption. Domestic duplication can improve resilience and waste resources if every country rebuilds every capability.

The right unit is not self-sufficiency. It is recoverability.

How quickly can the system adapt if one node fails?

Alliances matter because they enlarge the pool of trusted alternate capacity. A country does not need to produce everything domestically if it has credible access to partners during scarcity.

Trust shortens geopolitical queues.

But trust is political, not mechanical. Alliances change. Elections change. Export controls change. War changes assumptions. Companies must decide how much of political uncertainty to price into supply strategy.

This is difficult because geopolitical risk is lumpy. Nothing happens for years, then the cost can be enormous.

Average-case optimization underweights regime shifts.

The factory chapter taught us to buy enough slack for variability. Geopolitics asks how much slack to buy for events whose probability is contested and whose consequences are huge.

There is no clean formula.

Probability becomes judgment.

States also create queues intentionally through sanctions and export controls. Restrict access to finance, technology or goods and targeted actors must seek substitutes, wait for licensing or pay more through indirect channels.

The policy weapon is delay as much as denial.

A control need not make acquisition impossible to raise costs. It can slow access, reduce quality, increase uncertainty and force investment in substitutes.

Time becomes strategic friction.

The targeted actor responds by building capacity, stockpiling, redesigning products, creating alternative payment networks or cultivating new suppliers. Over time, the queue can stimulate substitution.

Coercive leverage can erode itself.

This is another reflexive system. The more a chokepoint is used strategically, the stronger the incentive for others to route around it.

The durability of power depends partly on whether bypass is feasible.

This is true for corporate platforms too. Exercise gatekeeping too aggressively and users seek alternatives. Charge too much for scarce access and competitors invest in capacity. A bottleneck is valuable because it is scarce and vulnerable because it is valuable.

Monopoly invites bypass.

Geographic chokepoints are harder to bypass than software interfaces but not impossible. Ships can take longer routes. New pipelines can be built. Rail corridors can expand. Production can move. The substitute may be expensive enough that the chokepoint retains power.

The queue creates an economic moat measured in detour cost.

Sea lanes illustrate this vividly. A canal saves distance. If the canal closes, ships can reroute, but time and fuel rise and effective fleet capacity falls because each voyage takes longer.

A longer route is equivalent to reducing service capacity.

More ships are tied up in transit. Freight rates can rise. Delivery uncertainty spreads.

This is Little's Law on the ocean: longer time in system means more inventory in transit for a given throughput.

A geopolitical disruption can therefore create a global working-capital event.

Goods spend longer on water. Companies hold more inventory. Cash conversion slows. Firms with weaker balance sheets feel the delay first.

The queue distributes geopolitical pain through finance.

This is why global shocks often affect small businesses differently from large multinationals. Large firms can charter transport, negotiate priority, hold more inventory and diversify suppliers. Small firms may accept the common queue.

Scale buys queue options.

Governments face similar asymmetry. Wealthy states can subsidize consumers, secure cargo, finance strategic reserves and support firms. Poorer states face price spikes and longer access delays with fewer buffers.

A global shortage can be formally market-based and materially unequal.

This became obvious during many historical emergencies involving food, medicine, energy or industrial inputs. The states with purchasing power and contracts move first.

Markets clear and politics remembers.

This can alter alliances and development strategy. Countries that felt abandoned during one shortage may pursue domestic capacity later even if it is more expensive.

Queue experience becomes policy memory.

The same dynamic appears inside regional blocs. Shared procurement or reserve systems can reduce internal competition during scarcity. Members pool bargaining power and agree on allocation rules.

A union creates a larger queue and a larger capacity pool simultaneously.

Pooling can improve resilience if rules are credible. If members believe others will defect during crisis, they may hoard nationally.

Trust determines whether the shared queue survives stress.

This is exactly the bank-run problem at state scale: participants cooperate when they believe future claims will be honored.

Institutions reduce the incentive to rush.

Treaties, contracts, monitoring and dispute mechanisms are queue stabilizers.

They do not eliminate national interest. They make some future service claims more credible.

This helps explain why standards and interoperability have geopolitical significance. Compatible systems can source from broader pools. A military using widely available fuel and components may have different resilience from one dependent on proprietary parts. An energy grid connected to neighbors has access to a larger balancing pool, subject to transmission and political constraints.

Compatibility expands alternate queues.

But common standards can also create common-mode dependence. If everyone uses the same vulnerable component, diversification at the supplier level may not protect against a shared flaw.

Resilience needs diversity at the right layer.

This is the same “narrow waist” logic that appears in networks: standardize enough to interoperate, preserve enough implementation diversity to avoid a single failure mode.

Geopolitical queue design is therefore not simply redundancy. It is structured substitutability.

Can another supplier serve the same interface? Can the product accept another component? Can logistics switch modes? Can payment route elsewhere? Can operators maintain the substitute?

A backup that takes two years to activate is not a backup for a two-month crisis.

Activation time matters.

States can rehearse substitution through exercises and small-scale use. Companies dual-source not only to split volume but to keep alternate suppliers qualified. Emergency stockpiles rotate inventory. Resilience requires maintenance.

An unused queue option can decay.

Skills disappear. Contracts lapse. equipment ages. Political assumptions change.

Preparedness is recurring work.

This is why strategic reserves and industrial policies often look wasteful in calm periods. Their purpose is to purchase a different distribution of future waiting.

The debate should therefore be explicit about insurance value.

How severe is the failure being insured against? How likely? How long would substitution take? What is the cost of maintaining the option? Could a smaller intervention create similar resilience?

Without these questions, “national security” can become a label that moves every preferred industry to the front of the public funding queue.

Priority inflation exists in geopolitics too.

If every sector is strategic, the category loses discipline.

Governments must prioritize scarce fiscal and administrative attention. Industrial policy itself has a queue.

The irony is complete: to reduce strategic queues, the state must decide which strategic queues deserve priority.

Good policy therefore needs falsification conditions. What evidence would show that a bottleneck has eased? When should subsidies end? When has private capacity become sufficient? Which dependence is acceptable because substitutes are fast enough?

Temporary scarcity should not automatically create permanent privilege.

The queue should be able to close.

This brings us from international power back to domestic politics. Citizens experience state capacity not mainly through semiconductor strategy or shipping routes but through ordinary waiting: schools, hospitals, benefits, licenses, transit and courts.

A government can project power abroad while making people wait badly at home.
