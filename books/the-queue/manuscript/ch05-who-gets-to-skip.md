# Who Gets to Skip

Priority is a promise that the queue is not really one queue.

The promise can be explicit. Airline passengers receive boarding groups. Hospitals assign triage levels. Cloud platforms define service tiers. Banks classify clients. Courts distinguish emergency motions from ordinary filings. Networks mark traffic classes. Militaries establish rank. Theme parks sell accelerated access. Universities reserve seats or give preference under defined rules. In each case, the institution says arrival order will be interrupted because another attribute matters more.

The attribute may be urgency, payment, seniority, loyalty, membership, legal right, strategic importance, medical compatibility, vulnerability, contractual commitment or institutional convenience.

Once priority exists, the queue becomes a hierarchy.

Hierarchies are not automatically unjust. Some are necessary descriptions of different stakes. If a trauma patient arrives after someone with a sprained wrist, medical urgency should defeat chronology. If a network is under attack, control traffic needed to restore service may deserve precedence over an entertainment stream. If an aircraft faces an emergency, landing sequence should change.

The question is not whether priority is allowed. Every serious system eventually needs it.

The question is what kind of claim can move someone forward, who verifies the claim, whether the rule is visible, and what happens to everyone pushed backward.

Priority always has a shadow queue.

Serve one job first and another job waits longer. The effect may be tiny or severe. A well-designed system knows this and sets limits. A poorly designed system accumulates special cases until “ordinary” service becomes the residue left after everyone important has gone first.

This can happen gradually.

An organization begins with one justified exception. Major outages receive immediate engineering attention. Sensible. Then important customers receive special escalation because their failures are costly. Also sensible. Executives can escalate issues because they see strategic risk. Large sales opportunities receive priority. Regulatory deadlines override planned work. Security issues interrupt everything. A few favored internal teams gain informal access to senior engineers.

Each exception is defensible in isolation.

The combined system can become ungovernable.

Ordinary work never reaches the front because new priority work continuously arrives. Employees begin labeling requests urgent to compete. The priority field loses meaning. Everything becomes P0. The queue no longer orders work; it broadcasts political strength.

This is priority inflation.

It appears wherever labels are cheap and service is scarce. Hospitals guard triage categories because over-classification would destroy the mechanism. Computing systems define quotas because high-priority jobs could starve the rest. Bureaucracies require evidence for expedited handling. Organizations create escalation policies, then discover that people learn the policy.

Any priority rule becomes part of the game.

If faster service is attached to a category, participants have an incentive to qualify for that category. This is not necessarily misconduct. It is often rational adaptation. Companies restructure transactions to meet favorable deadlines. Travelers accumulate loyalty status. Businesses design applications around expedited pathways. Students optimize for admissions categories. Patients seek referrals into specialist systems. Software developers mark work as latency-sensitive.

The institution then tightens definitions. Participants adapt again.

Priority systems become recursive because classification changes behavior, and changed behavior alters the population being classified.

This is one reason simple metrics age poorly. A rule that works when only a few people know or use it can fail once participants reorganize around it.

Airline loyalty programs illustrate the benign commercial version. Frequent travelers are rewarded with status, and status buys smaller queues: earlier boarding, faster check-in, better support, lounge access, upgrade priority. The program helps the airline retain valuable customers. Travelers receive predictable benefits. Nobody needs to pretend the system is egalitarian.

Yet the structure reveals something important about modern markets. Status is valuable partly because ordinary service contains friction.

Remove every line, every uncertainty and every small indignity from the base experience and some premium benefits become less compelling. The hierarchy depends on a gradient.

This does not mean airlines deliberately maximize misery. Operations are constrained, and segmentation can genuinely improve service for high-frequency customers whose time is valuable. The point is subtler: once priority becomes a product, the company earns revenue or loyalty from differences in waiting experience.

The queue enters the business model.

Other institutions cannot be so candid. A public agency usually cannot announce that politically influential applicants go first. If influence changes sequence, it must operate through informal channels: phone calls, relationships, specialized counsel, knowledge of which office to contact, or the ability to frame a request as exceptional.

Informal priority is harder to audit than formal priority.

This makes procedural transparency important. A visible rule can be criticized, measured and revised. An invisible rule produces rumors. People infer that somebody skipped because of connections even when a legitimate reason exists. Trust declines because the institution cannot explain its own sequence.

Opacity magnifies the politics of every exception.

The opposite problem occurs when institutions disclose so much that priority becomes easy to game. If every threshold is public, people cluster just across it. If the rule is hidden, accountability suffers. This is a familiar tension in tax law, admissions, financial regulation, fraud detection and platform ranking.

Queues add a temporal version: people want to know why someone moved ahead, but the institution may need discretion to manage cases.

There is no universal solution. There are better and worse disciplines.

One discipline is to distinguish priority from privilege.

Priority answers a purpose-related question: does serving this claim sooner advance the objective of the system? Privilege answers a power-related question: can this claimant secure earlier service because of position outside the system's stated purpose?

The distinction is easy to state and hard to enforce.

A hospital prioritizing clinical urgency is using purpose-related priority. A donor receiving medically unjustified treatment precedence because of wealth would be privilege. An airline serving premium customers faster is not necessarily privilege in the moral sense because differentiated service is part of the commercial contract. A public court giving faster hearing to a case involving immediate danger may be purpose-related. Quietly accelerating a routine case because a party knows someone in power is different.

The same action—moving ahead—changes meaning with institutional purpose.

Status systems often blur the line because past behavior becomes a claim on present service. Seniority is a good example.

Seniority can reward loyalty, preserve institutional memory and create predictable expectations. It can also protect incumbents at the expense of newcomers. In labor systems, seniority may affect schedules, layoffs, promotions or access to preferred assignments. In organizations, tenure can create informal access to decision-makers. In politics, committee seniority can shape agenda power. In academia, reputation affects whose messages are answered.

A queue based on seniority says that time already invested earns future priority.

This can stabilize cooperation. If people believe loyalty will be rewarded later, they may stay and build specific knowledge. But the rule also compounds advantage. Early position generates better opportunities, which generate stronger credentials, which produce more future priority.

The queue becomes a feedback loop.

Credit markets contain similar dynamics. A borrower with established credit receives faster, cheaper access to capital. That access helps the borrower build assets and survive shocks, further improving credit. A borrower without history faces higher costs, slower processes or rejection. Risk differences are real, but the allocation mechanism can still amplify initial position.

Priority has path dependence.

This is one reason status is so coveted: it protects the holder from future scarcity.

A reservation, a passport, a professional credential, a trusted-traveler membership, a seat on a platform, a credit line, an established vendor relationship, a security clearance, a subscriber tier—these are all, among other things, claims on future queues.

Institutions sell or grant not only present service but queue options.

The most powerful option is sometimes the ability to avoid joining at all.

A senior executive does not submit a request through the public help desk. She sends a message to a person. A wealthy patient does not search the same appointment portal. A large corporate customer has an account manager. A major investor does not wait for the same information channel. A government ministry calls another ministry. The process is relational rather than procedural.

This can be efficient. High-stakes relationships often require dedicated coordination. It can also produce a society in which formal queues are mainly for people without relationships.

That is a dangerous institutional endpoint.

The formal process becomes slower because influential users no longer depend on it. Their exit reduces political pressure to improve it. Private alternatives grow. The people left behind have less leverage. A two-tier system hardens.

This dynamic appears in education, transportation, health care, security, finance and public administration. When elites can reliably purchase or network around a common service, they may become less invested in the quality of the common queue.

Exit changes governance.

Albert Hirschman famously framed organizational decline partly through the responses of exit, voice and loyalty. Queues give the framework a temporal dimension. People with strong exit options do not have to wait. People without exit options must rely more on voice—and voice itself can be slow.

A system can therefore preserve formal equality while stratifying by exit capacity.

That capacity is not only money. It includes knowledge, language, geography, technology, legal status and social capital. A person who knows which form to file can skip months of error correction. Someone who knows a physician personally may get advice that prevents an unnecessary visit. Someone with a flexible employer can take the first available appointment. Someone with broadband can compete for a digital slot. Someone with citizenship can enter a different queue entirely.

The phrase “everyone can apply” says very little about practical queue access.

Good institutions try to reduce these hidden differences. They publish rules. They offer multiple channels. They provide assistance. They audit outcomes. They limit discretionary exceptions. They create appeals. They measure not just average wait but wait by group and case type. They protect ordinary capacity from being entirely consumed by priority demand.

The last point is especially important.

In computing, one way to prevent starvation is to guarantee lower-priority work some service or to raise a job's priority as it waits. Aging recognizes that even a low-priority claim becomes problematic if it waits forever.

Human institutions need versions of aging too.

A legal case that was initially low urgency can become unjust if it sits for years. A routine medical referral can become clinically serious as time passes. An ordinary maintenance task can become an emergency after enough neglect. A visa application can span life events that change its stakes. A customer's unresolved billing problem can become financially damaging.

Delay changes the claim.

This is another form of recursion. Priority determines waiting, and waiting can determine priority.

A queue that does not reassess becomes blind to the consequences it creates.

There is a tragic organizational version. Leaders prioritize visible crises. Routine maintenance waits. As maintenance waits, failure risk rises. Eventually neglected work produces a crisis, which earns priority. The organization learns the wrong lesson: crises deserve resources. In reality, the priority system manufactured some of the crises by starving prevention.

The loudest queue becomes self-justifying.

The same pattern can shape public policy. Emergency responses attract attention and money. Slow prevention produces fewer dramatic moments. Maintenance of bridges, public health systems, court capacity, administrative technology and social infrastructure often competes poorly against urgent demands until deterioration becomes undeniable.

Priority is a theory of what can safely wait.

That theory deserves more scrutiny than most organizations give it.

One useful question is to examine the people or tasks that never receive priority. What is systematically postponed because it lacks a champion? What kind of work has no dramatic deadline? Which customers cannot escalate? Which regions have no influential advocate? Which technical debt never triggers an alert until failure?

The back of the queue is an x-ray of institutional attention.

Another useful question is whether priority is reversible. Temporary emergency powers, temporary service tiers, temporary exemptions and temporary workarounds have a way of persisting. Once a group receives earlier access, removing it feels like a loss even if the original reason has disappeared.

Priority creates constituencies.

This is why queue reform can be politically difficult. Improving the average may require reducing an advantage enjoyed by a smaller group. That group knows exactly what it is losing. The diffuse beneficiaries each gain only a little.

The arithmetic may favor reform while the politics favors preservation.

Markets sometimes solve this by compensating people. Airlines ask for volunteers to give up seats in exchange for benefits. Congestion systems use revenue for transit or rebates. Organizations create transition rules. But compensation itself requires a legitimate way to value the lost position.

The queue turns into bargaining.

We have now moved far from a simple line, but the underlying structure has not changed. Scarce service cannot satisfy all claims at once. Someone goes earlier. Someone goes later. The rule distributing that difference becomes a small constitution.

The best priority systems are explicit about purpose, narrow in scope, resistant to gaming, capable of reassessment, and unwilling to let ordinary service collapse. They make exceptions visible enough to preserve trust without pretending every case is identical.

They also acknowledge something uncomfortable: fairness is not sameness.

Sometimes the fair thing is to let someone skip.

The next chapter turns from the institution to the person inside it. Why does an uncertain twenty-minute wait often feel worse than a known thirty-minute one? Why do occupied waits feel shorter? Why does seeing another line move faster provoke such irritation? Why do people stay in lines that no longer make sense?

To design queues well, we need to understand not only how long people wait but what waiting does to attention.
