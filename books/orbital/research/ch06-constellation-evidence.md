# Chapter 6 evidence notes — The Constellation Bet

This packet supports the first full draft of Chapter 6. The chapter treats the first LEO communications boom as an economic and timing problem, not as proof that the underlying engineering was foolish.

## 1. Iridium — technical success, commercial failure, bankruptcy

**FCC 00-289**  
https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/FCC-00-289A1.pdf

Use for:
- Iridium having about 50,000 subscribers at the end of 1999.
- the system ceasing commercial operations in spring 2000 while Motorola maintained the network temporarily.
- contemporaneous regulatory context around ICO, Globalstar and other mobile-satellite systems.

**FCC DA 02-307**  
https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DA-02-307A1.pdf

Use for:
- Iridium defaulting on debt in excess of $1.5 billion and filing for Chapter 11 protection.
- commercial operations ceasing in spring 2000.
- Motorola preparing to decommission the constellation before a buyer emerged.
- bankruptcy court approval of the successor company's asset bid in November 2000.

**Motorola SEC filing, 2002**  
https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/68505/000091205702031484/a2086369z10-q.htm

Use for:
- new Iridium completing acquisition of the old company's operating assets on December 12, 2000.
- operating assets including the constellation, terrestrial network, real property and intellectual property.
- transition of operation and maintenance toward Boeing.

**Iridium 1998 annual report archive**  
https://ourthing.altervista.org/files/txt/1998AnnualReport.pdf

Use cautiously for:
- financing covenant / milestone structure around subscriber and revenue targets.
- evidence that the company's financing model depended on rapid commercial ramp.

Prefer FCC/SEC sources for bankruptcy chronology. The annual report mirror is useful for milestone detail but should not be the sole source for a major claim.

## 2. Terrestrial mobile competition changed while Iridium was being built

**ITU Telecommunication Indicators Update, 2000 — “The Mobile Cellular Boom”**  
https://www.itu.int/ITU-D/ict/update/pdf/Update_1.pdf

Use for:
- roughly 11 million cellular subscribers worldwide in 1990.
- more than 450 million by the end of 1999; the ITU chart reports 472 million.
- mobile subscriptions roughly doubling every twenty months through the decade.

This is the chapter's strongest evidence for the claim that Iridium's competitive environment changed radically during its development cycle.

**Wired, August 1998 — contemporary Iridium service preview**  
https://www.wired.com/1998/08/beta-20/

Use as contemporary reporting for:
- first-generation Iridium handsets priced around $2,500–$3,000.
- high service cost relative to ordinary cellular.

Do not make a universal claim about every tariff or handset SKU from one magazine item. The manuscript says “contemporary reporting described” rather than presenting one price as timeless.

## 3. Globalstar — another technically operating system whose first owner failed

**Globalstar / SEC 10-Q, 2003**  
https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/933401/000089161803004420/f92214e10vq.htm

Use for:
- Chapter 11 filing on February 15, 2002.
- cumulative losses and debt burden.
- statement that subscriber growth had not been sufficient to sustain operating costs.
- approximately 93,000 commercial subscribers by June 30, 2003.

**Globalstar 2008 Form 10-K**  
https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1366868/000104746908002849/a2181782z10-k.htm

Use for:
- first-generation network being designed and launched in the late 1990s.
- acquisition of Old Globalstar's business/assets after bankruptcy.
- surviving use case as mobile voice/data service beyond terrestrial network coverage.

Guardrail: Iridium and Globalstar had materially different network architectures. Use them as economic rhymes, not as technically interchangeable systems.

## 7. What materially changed by the late 2010s

The manuscript's synthesis is interpretive and should remain identified as such. It combines evidence developed across Chapters 4–6:

- reusable, high-cadence launch and SpaceX control of its own launcher;
- high-rate satellite manufacturing;
- cheaper and more capable electronics;
- electronically steered user terminals becoming plausible as mass-produced products;
- broadband demand becoming ordinary infrastructure demand rather than a narrow premium voice service;
- larger pools of private technology capital;
- an FCC framework actively authorizing multiple NGSO broadband systems.

No single one of these changes “caused Starlink.” The chapter's claim is that several thresholds matured together.

## Drafting guardrails

- Do not use Iridium as a joke about bad forecasting. Its engineering was genuinely formidable and the surviving company found valuable markets.
- Distinguish failure of equity/capital structure from failure of the physical network.
- Avoid claiming that satellite communications had no demand in the late 1990s; the problem was demand relative to cost, debt and the scale of the original business case.
- Do not flatten Iridium, Globalstar, Teledesic and OneWeb into identical systems.
- Keep the “prepaid geography” idea tied to constellation economics: global coverage requires infrastructure over low-revenue regions before those regions justify it.
- Treat Starlink's vertical integration as an odds-changing advantage, not a guarantee of success.
- Do not imply phased arrays, low-orbit broadband or software-defined satellites were invented in the 2010s. The relevant change is manufacturability, cost and system integration at scale.
- The chapter ends before detailed Starlink deployment economics. Chapter 7 should own batch deployment, orbital shells, internal launch demand and the full Starlink flywheel.
