# ORBITAL — TRADE BOOK PROPOSAL SNAPSHOT

## Hook
A generation ago, governments effectively monopolized orbital capability. Today private firms operate the busiest launch systems, the largest satellite networks, commercial crew vehicles, global imaging fleets and increasingly essential wartime and disaster-response infrastructure. *Orbital* explains how that transfer of operational power happened—and what it means when the sky becomes privately run infrastructure.

## Why now
The industrialization of low Earth orbit has crossed from novelty into dependency. Constellations number in the thousands; launch is reaching airline-like cadence compared with historical norms; NASA is preparing a transition from the ISS toward commercial destinations; militaries rely increasingly on commercial communications and imagery; regulators confront congestion, debris and spectrum conflicts; rival national systems are building competing constellations.

The story has reached the stage where readers no longer need a book predicting commercial space. They need a book explaining the system that already exists.

## The book's contrarian argument
Private industry did not conquer LEO by defeating government. Government helped create the market and then became dependent on what the market built.

The decisive innovation was not one rocket or one entrepreneur. It was a stack of mutually reinforcing changes:

1. procurement shifted from ownership toward services;
2. private capital absorbed more development risk;
3. reusable launch enabled repetition;
4. repetition created cadence and learning advantages;
5. manufacturing shifted from bespoke satellites toward fleets;
6. constellations created recurring-revenue businesses that generated their own launch demand;
7. the resulting infrastructure became strategically important to states;
8. governance lagged the scale and speed of deployment.

## Narrative opening
The book opens under a line of freshly launched satellites crossing the night sky, then cuts backward to the bureaucratic decision inside NASA that made the modern commercial architecture possible. The narrative repeatedly returns from spectacle to system: every dramatic launch is connected to contracts, factories, spectrum, logistics and political consequence.

## Desired reader reaction
“I thought this was a story about SpaceX. It is actually a story about how infrastructure changes sovereignty.”

## End-state question
When private companies can sell global communications, persistent observation, human transport and rapid access to orbit—capabilities once associated with major powers—what remains uniquely sovereign about state power in space?
