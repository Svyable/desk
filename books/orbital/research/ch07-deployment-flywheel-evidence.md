# Chapter 7 evidence notes — A Thousand Satellites Is a Different Machine

This packet supports the first full draft of live Chapter 7. It isolates the deployment flywheel: batch launch, partial-service rollout, internal launch demand, fleet turnover, and customer growth. Detailed product-market expansion belongs mainly to the following customer chapter.

## 1. First operational-scale Starlink batch — May 2019

**SpaceX, Starlink Mission webcast / description, May 23, 2019**  
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=riBaVeDTEWI

Use for:
- launch of 60 Starlink satellites.
- the Falcon 9 first stage having previously flown Telstar 18 VANTAGE and Iridium-8.
- initial deployment around 440 km and onboard propulsion toward an operational altitude around 550 km.
- SpaceX framing Starlink as a next-generation global broadband network.

The chapter uses the already-flown booster as the narrative hinge joining reusable launch, satellite manufacturing and constellation deployment.

## 2. Orbital-shell modifications and staged deployment

**FCC 21-48, April 27, 2021**  
https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/FCC-21-48A1.pdf

Use for:
- chronology of SpaceX modifications to the authorized Gen1 constellation.
- December 2019 approval allowing more orbital planes at the 550 km shell and fewer satellites per plane.
- later modification request involving 1,584 previously authorized satellites at 550 km plus additional lower shells.
- regulatory evidence that deployment architecture was changing while the constellation was already being built.

Guardrail: do not imply the FCC designed the deployment strategy or independently validated every company performance claim. The orders record and adjudicate applications, objections and representations.

## 5. 2026 SpaceX filings — mature internal-demand flywheel

**SpaceX Form 10-Q / public filing for quarter ended June 30, 2026**  
https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1181412/000162828026052535/spcx-20260630.htm

Use for:
- 77 Falcon launches in the six months ended June 30, 2026.
- 17 classified as customer launches and 60 as internal launches.
- SpaceX statement that a significant amount of launch capacity is allocated to its Connectivity segment.
- internal Starlink launch costs not recognized as inter-segment launch revenue; instead those costs are capitalized in satellite property, plant and equipment.
- 12.0 million Starlink subscriber service lines as of June 30, 2026 versus 6.0 million a year earlier.
- definition of Starlink subscribers as unique service lines associated with Starlink.com accounts, including personal and some business plans but excluding certain managed enterprise/government contracts.
- Connectivity revenue growth attributed to both consumer-subscriber expansion and government, aviation, maritime and other enterprise business.

This filing is the strongest quantitative evidence for the chapter's mature-flywheel claim.

Important nuance:
- “internal launch” includes internal missions under SpaceX's disclosed definition, not a generic synonym for every Starlink mission in every period.
- 12 million subscribers means service lines under SpaceX's definition, not 12 million individual people.
- do not infer profit from revenue growth alone; the filing also reports higher depreciation and Starlink Kit production costs.

## 7. Deployment flywheel — interpretive synthesis

The manuscript's flywheel is a synthesis, not a company quote:

1. batch launch adds orbital capacity and/or coverage;
2. expanded service permits more customers and use cases;
3. customer and contract revenue supports further network investment;
4. continued internal Starlink demand supports Falcon flight rate;
5. launch cadence and operational learning make further deployment easier;
6. aging assets plus new generations create continuing replacement demand;
7. the loop repeats.

The key evidence comes from the juxtaposition of first-batch reusable launch in 2019, FCC-documented deployment modifications, early subscriber growth, and 2026 disclosed internal-launch / subscriber scale.

## Drafting guardrails

- Do not describe internal Starlink launches as “free.” They consume real launch resources and are capitalized as satellite costs.
- Do not equate satellite count directly with useful capacity. Hardware generations, orbital geometry and traffic distribution matter.
- Do not treat global network capacity as fungible across all locations; congestion is local and orbital geometry constrains where capacity is available.
- Do not say Starlink was fully built at any milestone. The central claim is the opposite: continuous deployment is part of the business model.
- Avoid claiming SpaceX knew demand perfectly. The argument is that rolling deployment shortened the feedback loop between forecast and correction.
- Treat vertical integration as both an efficiency advantage and a concentration/dependency risk.
- Save the detailed “customer everywhere” cases—aviation, maritime, remote work, disaster response, household behavior—for the next chapter.
