# Chapter 9 evidence notes — The Private Astronaut

This packet supports the first full draft of live Chapter 9. The chapter treats commercial human spaceflight as an **unbundling of services**—mission integration, crew transport, destination access, research integration, training and eventually private orbital accommodation—not as a simple tourism story.

## 4. Commercial Crew creates option value beyond NASA

The book's earlier Commercial Crew chapters provide the core evidence: companies own and operate crew systems while NASA buys transportation service and certifies against its requirements.

For this chapter, use that history as an established premise rather than re-explaining the full procurement program.

Interpretive claim:
- private ownership of Crew Dragon allowed the platform to serve NASA and non-NASA missions.
- this is a market-structure consequence of ownership, not proof that government became unnecessary.

Guardrail: NASA certification for NASA missions is not identical to generic regulation of all private missions; FAA launch/reentry licensing and destination requirements occupy different layers.

## 9. Private missions as rehearsals / market formation

The chapter's “rehearsal” argument is interpretive but grounded in repeated missions:

- mission integration procedures are exercised;
- private crews enter NASA training and station operations;
- commercial providers coordinate with NASA, SpaceX and research customers;
- national astronauts can enter through commercial mission structures;
- providers accumulate experience before operating their own destinations.

Axiom itself describes its ISS missions as precursors to Axiom Station; NASA describes PAMs as paving the way for commercial LEO transition.

## 10. Demand-side counterweight

The manuscript deliberately treats commercial stations as an unresolved market, not inevitable success.

Claims to preserve:
- NASA is the obvious anchor customer after ISS.
- other possible customers include national agencies, researchers, industry and private missions.
- the number, duration and willingness-to-pay of non-NASA customers remain uncertain.
- human-rated stations face enormous fixed costs and safety obligations.
- competition is desirable for resilience but difficult to finance in a thin market.

Do not present renderings, MOUs, payload reservations or launch targets as equivalent to a certified, continuously operating station.

## Drafting guardrails

- Avoid reducing PAMs to billionaire tourism.
- Avoid calling Ax-4 wholly “private” in the everyday funding sense; use NASA's private astronaut mission category and explain the hybrid national/commercial crew composition.
- Do not imply NASA has ceased to be operationally or economically important. The thesis is role change, not disappearance.
- Distinguish NASA certification/procurement from FAA launch/reentry regulation.
- Do not claim the post-ISS destination market is settled; schedules and corporate teams are still moving rapidly in 2026.
- Current Vast target is 2027, not the superseded May 2026 target.
- Preserve the asymmetric safety point: fleet-level tolerance useful for satellite constellations does not transfer cleanly to crewed habitats.
- The next part of the book should pivot from commercial convenience/access into strategic consequence, beginning with communications used in conflict.
