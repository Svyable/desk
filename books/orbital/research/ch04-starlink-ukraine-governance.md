# Chapter 4 research note: Starlink in Ukraine — resilience, contracts, and control

## Claim under review

Chapter 4 uses Starlink in Ukraine to argue that a commercial broadband network acquired strategic importance because its distributed satellites, portable terminals, and continuing launch cadence could sustain communications when terrestrial infrastructure was damaged. That is a strong example, but it needs a second layer: the same episode also shows that resilient hardware does not make a commercial service equivalent to sovereign infrastructure. Control can still sit in contracts, terms of service, terminal administration, and the provider's ability to enable or restrict use.

## Primary-source evidence

The strongest recent public record is the USAID Office of Inspector General's August 11, 2025 inspection of the Starlink terminals USAID helped deliver to Ukraine. USAID partnered with SpaceX to provide 5,175 terminals to Ukraine's State Service of Special Communications and Information Protection: USAID procured 1,508 and SpaceX donated 3,667. The OIG describes Starlink as important to both Ukrainian civilian resilience and defense, including restoration of internet access and communications among government officials and emergency services.

Source: USAID Office of Inspector General, “Ukraine Response: USAID Did Not Fully Mitigate the Risk of Misuse of the Starlink Satellite Terminals It Delivered to Ukraine,” August 11, 2025.
https://oig.usaid.gov/node/7845

The report supplies useful civilian detail. It says Starlink provided connectivity during blackouts and damage to critical infrastructure and notes that, in March 2023, Ukraine's Health Ministry reported 590 hospitals and clinics using Starlink terminals to maintain internet access during intense fighting. These facts support the chapter's resilience claim without requiring a sweeping assertion that Starlink replaced Ukraine's communications system.

The Pentagon's own public record also shows a change in institutional relationship. In October 2022, DoD said it had not yet paid SpaceX for Starlink service in Ukraine and was discussing satellite-communications requirements with SpaceX and other companies. By September 2023, the Pentagon publicly acknowledged that it contracted with Starlink for services supporting Ukraine, while withholding operational details because of the systems' critical nature.

That chronology matters. The system's wartime value did not erase the distinction between a company donating or supplying a service at its own discretion and a government buying a defined capability under contract. Strategic dependence tends to pull private infrastructure toward more explicit public arrangements.

## Strongest counterargument / disconfirming evidence

The same USAID OIG report complicates any clean resilience story. USAID did not fully define or enforce conditions governing the terminals it delivered, did not monitor them after transfer, and found that nearly half of active USAID-delivered terminals were present in territories Russia fully or partially occupied. The report therefore treats misuse, theft, and unauthorized military or intelligence use as real oversight risks. It also records that SpaceX's service terms contained location restrictions for parts of eastern Ukraine and that SpaceX restricted some Ukrainian use of Starlink for piloting drones in early 2023.

This is not evidence that the constellation itself was fragile or that commercial satellite communications were a mistake. It is evidence that distributed physical architecture and distributed authority are different things. A network can be hard to destroy physically while access to it remains governable through accounts, contracts, service areas, software controls, terminal custody, and provider policy.

## Editorial implication

A future prose pass should keep Starlink as the chapter's vivid demonstration of commercial resilience but resist the phrase “private infrastructure” if it implies autonomy from public power or provider control. The sharper claim is that Ukraine exposed two forms of resilience at once: physical resilience from proliferation and replenishment, and institutional fragility when a strategically important service depends on contractual authority, corporate policy, and imperfect oversight. That tension gives the chapter a better bridge into its closing argument about why public consequence eventually attracts public governance.
