# The New Eyes

On April 4, 2022, one of the most consequential witnesses in Bucha was hundreds of kilometers above the crime scene.

It had no opinion.

It had no memory in the human sense.

It had an archive.

After Russian forces withdrew from the Ukrainian town, photographs and video showed bodies lying along Yablonska Street and elsewhere. The Russian government denied responsibility and argued that the scene had been staged after its troops left.

That claim created a question of time.

When had the bodies appeared?

A commercial satellite company had an answer stored on servers.

Maxar Technologies had collected high-resolution imagery of Bucha during the Russian occupation. Analysts compared those earlier satellite images with photographs and video taken from the ground after Ukrainian forces returned. Dark objects visible along the road in imagery collected while Russian troops still controlled the town appeared in the same locations as bodies later documented at street level.

The evidence did not come from a declassified spy-satellite program years after the event.

It came from a commercial archive while the war was still being fought.

A private company had photographed the relevant place before anyone knew exactly how valuable the photograph would become.

Orbit had become a timestamp.

This is one of the stranger powers of Earth observation.

A satellite image is usually purchased because somebody wants to know what is happening now.

Its later value may come from proving what had already happened.

The camera does not know which warehouse will burn.

It does not know which army will deny moving.

It does not know which village will become evidence in a war-crimes investigation.

It simply collects.

Then history catches up to the archive.

This is why the commercial Earth-observation revolution is not merely a story about sharper pictures.

It is a story about who is allowed to possess evidence.

For most of the Cold War, the best orbital eyes belonged to states and almost nobody was permitted to know what those eyes could see.

The first successful American photographic reconnaissance satellite, CORONA, began returning images in 1960. Film exposed in orbit was packed into capsules, dropped through the atmosphere and caught in midair by aircraft. The photographs helped the United States count Soviet missiles, airfields and other military infrastructure.

The public did not browse the archive.

The target did not receive a subscription.

The customer was the state.

The secrecy was part of the capability.

A reconnaissance satellite did more than produce information.

It produced privileged information.

That privilege was strategic.

If one side could see what the other side could not, the asymmetry mattered almost as much as the image itself.

Civil Earth observation developed beside this classified world.

Landsat 1 launched in 1972 and began building a repetitive civilian record of the planet for science, agriculture, geology, resource management and environmental monitoring.

The imagery was enormously valuable.

It was not designed to let a newspaper inspect a military convoy vehicle by vehicle.

For decades, the two traditions—civilian Earth science and classified reconnaissance—occupied different levels of detail, access and urgency.

Commercial high-resolution imaging began squeezing into the space between them.

When IKONOS launched in September 1999, it offered one-meter panchromatic imagery for commercial sale. An image once associated in the popular imagination with secret national systems could increasingly become a product.

The distinction was not that private companies had never built reconnaissance hardware.

They had.

Private industry had been woven through national space programs from the beginning.

The change was that the customer could increasingly buy the observation rather than belong to the government that owned the observing system.

That change looked incremental for years.

Then several curves moved at once.

Launch became more available.

Small satellites improved.

Detectors improved.

Data links improved.

Cloud storage became ordinary.

Machine learning made large image archives more searchable.

Commercial constellations increased revisit rates.

Online distribution collapsed the distance between collection and customer.

The same industrial logic that transformed communications transformed seeing.

One exquisite satellite became a fleet.

One image became a feed.

A feed became a database.

A database became an intelligence product.

The old model asked: **Can the satellite see it?**

The new model asks several questions at once.

How sharply can it see?

How often can it return?

How quickly can the image reach the customer?

Can the customer request a new collection?

Can software identify change automatically?

Can the observation be combined with radar, radio-frequency detections, ship transponders, weather, financial records or social-media footage?

And perhaps most politically important:

Can the result be shared?

The war in Ukraine made all of these questions visible.

Before the invasion, commercial imagery providers were already watching the Russian buildup.

In February 2022, Maxar released images showing deployments in Belarus, Crimea and western Russia as Moscow publicly denied that it planned to invade Ukraine and announced partial troop withdrawals.

The images did not tell anyone what Vladimir Putin would decide.

Intent is not a pixel.

But they constrained the plausible stories about physical preparation.

Vehicles were there or they were not.

Helicopters had moved forward or they had not.

Camps expanded or contracted.

Units left garrison.

Aircraft appeared at forward locations.

The commercial camera could not read the Kremlin's mind.

It could make some claims about the world harder to sustain.

After the invasion began, the same market produced images of convoys, airfields, damaged bridges, flooded terrain, fortifications, ships, fires, industrial sites and destroyed neighborhoods.

Newsrooms published them.

Analysts annotated them.

Researchers compared them against videos posted from the ground.

Governments cited commercial data.

Companies sold imagery directly to government customers.

The old relationship between intelligence and publication began to change.

A government often faces a problem when it knows something secret and wants other people to believe it.

Reveal too little and outsiders may doubt the claim.

Reveal too much and the government may expose the sensor, collection method, resolution, timing or analytical technique that produced the intelligence.

Commercial imagery offers a third option.

A state can know more through classified systems while pointing publicly to evidence collected by an unclassified commercial system.

This is intelligence without revealing all the intelligence.

The U.S. National Reconnaissance Office says the advantage plainly.

When it announced its largest-ever commercial electro-optical imagery contracts in 2022, awarding long-term deals to BlackSky, Maxar and Planet, the agency emphasized the unclassified and shareable nature of commercial remote-sensing data. The NRO said commercial imagery could increase transparency, support situational awareness and aid humanitarian missions.

That is an extraordinary sentence when placed beside the history of CORONA.

The agency built to operate some of the most secret machines ever created now values part of the commercial market because the data is less secret.

Shareability became a capability.

A classified image can be extraordinarily powerful inside a secure room.

An unclassified image can move.

It can cross ministries.

It can reach allies without the same clearance infrastructure.

It can be shown to journalists.

It can be included in a diplomatic presentation.

It can be given to disaster responders.

It can be discussed by analysts who will never hold a security clearance.

It can circulate fast enough to become part of the public argument.

The image may be technically inferior to the state's best classified collection and politically more useful for exactly that reason.

This is a subtle inversion.

Secrecy creates advantage.

Shareability creates scale.

Modern intelligence systems need both.

The commercial market widened the second category dramatically.

It also diversified how Earth could be observed.

The word *satellite image* encourages us to imagine one thing: a photograph from very high above.

There is no single commercial eye.

There are different instruments optimized for different jobs.

High-resolution electro-optical satellites behave most like the familiar camera. Under good conditions they can show vehicles, buildings, aircraft, construction, damage and other visible features with remarkable detail.

Current commercial WorldView systems offer imagery in the roughly thirty-centimeter class under favorable collection geometry.

That does not mean the satellite can read a newspaper over someone's shoulder.

Resolution is not magic.

A thirty-centimeter ground sample distance describes the scale at which the sensor samples the ground, not a promise that every thirty-centimeter object becomes perfectly identifiable.

Atmosphere matters.

Angle matters.

Light matters.

Motion matters.

Processing matters.

Interpretation matters.

But the broad progression is unmistakable.

The market can now sell spatial detail that would have sounded extraordinary to a civilian customer a generation earlier.

Planet attacks a different part of the problem.

Its SuperDove constellation is designed as an always-on scanner, collecting a daily record of global change at roughly meter-class rather than exquisite vehicle-level detail. Planet's current filings describe a goal of imaging Earth's landmass every day at up to about 3.5-meter ground sampling distance and adding those observations to an archive whose daily-scanning history extends back to 2017.

That creates another kind of intelligence.

Not: **Show me the finest possible image of this building.**

But: **Show me how this place changed every day for years.**

The archive becomes a sensor of time.

A port fills.

A mine expands.

A reservoir shrinks.

A road appears.

A crop fails.

A forest disappears.

A camp grows.

A line of vehicles moves.

The individual frame may be less detailed.

The sequence can be more revealing.

BlackSky attacks the temporal problem more aggressively at selected places.

Its current public filings describe a constellation and software system designed for repeated monitoring of strategically important locations, with the company's Gen-3 spacecraft offering thirty-five-centimeter-class electro-optical imagery and, under optimal conditions, revisit opportunities that can reach many times per day.

This is not a map-making philosophy.

It is watchstanding.

One system scans the world.

Another watches the door.

Then there is radar.

Synthetic-aperture radar is one of those technologies that makes more sense once you stop calling every orbital observation a photograph.

A radar satellite sends microwave energy toward Earth and measures the return.

It does not need sunlight in the same way an optical camera does.

Cloud cover that can ruin an optical collection may be much less of a barrier.

The resulting image requires different interpretation, but the operational advantage is obvious.

Night happens.

Weather happens.

War does not wait for a clear afternoon.

Commercial SAR companies such as ICEYE, Capella and Umbra turned radar collection into another purchasable layer of visibility.

Ukraine made this market strategically tangible in 2022 when the Serhiy Prytula Charity Foundation signed an agreement with ICEYE that gave the Ukrainian government full capabilities for one in-orbit SAR satellite over the region, operated by ICEYE, plus access to the wider constellation.

The arrangement is worth noticing for its institutional form.

Ukraine did not have to design the radar spacecraft.

It did not have to build the satellite factory.

It did not have to create a launch vehicle.

It did not have to wait years for a national reconnaissance program to mature.

A private fund-raising effort purchased access to an existing orbital intelligence capability.

By 2026, ICEYE said it had expanded its relationship with a customer inside Ukraine's defense ministry for continued high-volume SAR imagery.

This is another example of mass-produced sovereignty.

Not sovereignty itself.

A sovereign function.

A state acquired a form of all-weather orbital observation through contract rather than through a national satellite-development program.

The barrier between *having a space program* and *having space-derived capability* gets lower every time a service can be purchased this way.

The same phenomenon is visible from the other side of the market.

The United States did not respond to commercial Earth observation by abandoning national reconnaissance.

It bought more commercial Earth observation.

This distinction is essential.

The commercial-imagery revolution is sometimes described as democratizing intelligence.

That phrase contains truth and hides too much.

Commercial imagery democratizes access to some collection.

It does not make every intelligence capability equally available.

The U.S. government still operates classified systems with capabilities that are not publicly described in full.

It can combine imagery with signals intelligence, infrared detection, human sources, cyber collection, diplomatic reporting, military sensors and other protected data.

It can assign classified collection priorities that ordinary commercial customers will never see.

It can protect sources and methods.

It can fuse information inside institutions built specifically for national-security analysis.

The intelligence monopoly did not disappear.

A piece of it became a market.

That piece became valuable enough that the state chose to integrate it.

The NRO's 2022 Electro-Optical Commercial Layer contracts were valued in the billions of dollars over their potential duration. The agency summarized its approach with a phrase that deserves to sit beside NASA's service-procurement revolution:

**Buy what we can, build what we must.**

The same logic is spreading across sensing types.

The NRO experimented with commercial radar providers.

It studied commercial radio-frequency sensing.

It contracted with hyperspectral companies.

By 2026, the agency was explicitly expanding toward a multi-phenomenology commercial architecture that could include electro-optical, hyperspectral, radar, radio frequency and lidar capabilities.

In August 2026, it awarded operational Radar Commercial Augmentation contracts to Capella, ICEYE US and Umbra, moving commercial SAR further from experiment toward routine intelligence use.

The intelligence state was becoming a platform customer.

Again the pattern repeats.

Government does not vanish.

Government changes position in the stack.

It still owns unique systems.

It still defines intelligence requirements.

It still decides what can be classified.

It still conducts analysis.

It still has authorities private companies do not.

But where the market can provide useful capacity, government increasingly treats commercial collection as infrastructure to be purchased and fused.

This can improve resilience.

If one national satellite suffers failure, commercial systems may provide alternate collection.

If a crisis suddenly creates enormous demand over one region, multiple private fleets can add capacity.

If allies need evidence, unclassified commercial products may move more easily across policy boundaries.

If a new sensor technology matures in the market, the government can sometimes buy access faster than it could reproduce the entire industrial stack internally.

The state acquires option space.

The companies acquire an anchor customer.

This relationship should look familiar by now.

NASA helped create commercial cargo by becoming an anchor customer for delivery.

The NRO helps shape commercial remote sensing by becoming an anchor customer for observation.

Different mission.

Same institutional physics.

Recurring government demand can finance capability that later has other customers.

Those other customers change the politics of seeing.

A newsroom can license commercial imagery.

A humanitarian organization can document destruction.

A climate researcher can inspect change over time.

A shipping company can monitor ports.

An insurance firm can examine storm damage.

A commodity trader can estimate industrial activity or crop conditions.

A mining company can study infrastructure.

A government without a large national reconnaissance constellation can purchase imagery.

An open-source analyst can sometimes work from images released publicly by providers, governments or media organizations.

The same orbital collection begins serving markets that would never share a classified intelligence system.

This creates an **evidence market**.

The phrase matters because the product is not always intelligence in the full institutional sense.

Sometimes it is evidence waiting for interpretation.

A photograph is not a conclusion.

A radar return is not a policy recommendation.

A thermal anomaly is not proof of intent.

A radio-frequency detection is not a complete electronic order of battle.

The market sells observations.

Customers build meaning on top.

This can distribute analytical power.

It can also distribute analytical error.

The Bucha example worked because the geometry was unusually testable.

Ground-level images showed bodies in identifiable locations relative to curbs, buildings and vehicles. Earlier satellite imagery showed dark objects in corresponding positions during the Russian occupation. Multiple forms of evidence could be compared.

Not every problem is so clean.

A blurry vehicle can be misidentified.

A construction site can look military when it is not.

A change in shadows can suggest movement.

Cloud can hide the important part of an image.

Radar can produce features an untrained observer interprets badly.

A missing object may have moved five minutes before the satellite passed.

A static image can conceal what happened immediately before and after collection.

Camouflage works.

Decoys work.

Underground facilities do not become transparent because a company has an API.

A satellite sees the surface under particular physical conditions.

Intelligence asks what the observation means.

Those are different jobs.

This is the first limit on the democratization story.

Access does not equal literacy.

The second limit is time.

A constellation may revisit quickly and still not be looking at the exact moment something happens.

If a missile launcher emerges, fires and returns to cover between collections, the archive may never contain the decisive frame.

If clouds remain over an area for days, optical systems may produce almost nothing useful.

If a customer discovers an event after it occurs, there may be no high-resolution collection of the relevant location from the relevant minute.

Orbit is repetitive.

It is not omnipresent.

The third limit is tasking.

A satellite has finite attention.

It cannot image every point at maximum resolution at every moment.

Someone chooses priorities.

Commercial providers allocate capacity among customers, standing collection plans, government contracts, weather constraints, orbital geometry and spacecraft health.

A customer can purchase imagery without owning the scheduler.

This means access to the market is not identical to control of the sensor.

Priority itself can be valuable.

The fourth limit is distribution.

Commercial does not mean public.

Some commercial imagery is released openly.

Some is licensed to many customers.

Some tasking can be exclusive or private.

Some government purchases have distribution conditions.

Some imagery sits behind expensive subscriptions.

Some products are delayed.

Some places are subject to legal restrictions.

The evidence market has gates.

The gates include money.

The gates include contracts.

They also include government regulation.

American private remote-sensing operators do not simply launch a camera and sell whatever they like.

The U.S. Office of Space Commerce licenses private remote-sensing systems under 15 CFR Part 960.

The modern licensing framework contains a revealing idea: restrictions depend partly on what comparable data is already available from other domestic or foreign systems.

A system is placed into tiers according to whether substantially similar unenhanced data is already available elsewhere.

The logic recognizes a fact regulators repeatedly encounter in global technology markets.

You cannot preserve scarcity by regulating one domestic company if equivalent capability is already for sale from another jurisdiction.

The frontier of permission moves as the market moves.

A capability once treated as exceptional can become ordinary enough that restrictions relax.

This is governance by competitive reality.

It does not eliminate national-security review.

It makes national-security regulation responsive to diffusion.

The same pressure shaped the history of commercial resolution.

What counts as dangerously revealing changes when foreign providers can sell similar detail.

What counts as an intelligence monopoly changes when private firms in several countries operate comparable sensors.

The sky does not respect national licensing boundaries.

That makes commercial remote sensing a transnational governance problem even before an image crosses a border.

The fifth limit is that the most valuable intelligence often comes from fusion.

A satellite image may show ships leaving port.

Automatic-identification-system data may show which ships claim to be moving.

Radio-frequency sensors may detect emitters.

Weather explains visibility.

Corporate registries explain ownership.

Customs data explains cargo patterns.

Social-media footage shows activity at street level.

A human source explains why the movement matters.

A signals intercept explains the order behind it.

The power lies not in any one layer but in joining them.

Commercial providers increasingly understand this.

BlackSky sells an imagery-and-analytics platform rather than merely frames.

Planet builds time-series tools around a vast archive.

SAR providers offer derived products and monitoring services.

HawkEye 360 and others sell commercial radio-frequency geolocation.

The market is moving from selling pictures toward selling answers.

This transition will make the intelligence boundary even harder to draw.

If a company collects imagery, detects change automatically, fuses third-party data and alerts a customer to an emerging event, where does remote sensing end and intelligence begin?

The legal categories were built around sensors.

The economic product is becoming inference.

This matters because inference scales differently from human analysis.

A human imagery analyst can inspect one target carefully.

Software can compare thousands of sites against their historical baselines.

A machine can ask which airfields gained aircraft overnight.

Which ports accumulated ships unusually quickly?

Which construction sites changed shape?

Which roads gained traffic?

Which facilities emitted heat?

Which locations produced new radio-frequency activity?

The satellite is still constrained by orbit.

The analyst is less constrained by attention.

This is another version of the book's recurring pattern.

Cheap repetition changes the unit of work.

When imagery was scarce, analysts studied individual photographs.

When imagery becomes abundant, the challenge becomes finding the few changes that deserve human attention.

The product shifts from picture to exception.

That shift makes archives more powerful.

Planet's archive, for example, is valuable partly because a customer can ask not only what a place looks like now but what normal used to look like.

Normal is the baseline against which anomaly becomes visible.

A single image tells you there are fifty vehicles in a lot.

A time series tells you there are usually five.

A single image shows a ship at a pier.

A time series shows it arrived after six months of absence.

A single image shows excavation.

A time series reveals the date excavation began.

This is why the archive deserves to be treated as infrastructure, not storage.

The archive creates a limited ability to look backward.

Most sensing systems cannot travel into the past.

A dense commercial archive sometimes can.

Not literally.

But operationally enough to matter.

A reporter learns that a facility may have been damaged last week and checks whether a collection exists.

An investigator learns that a village may have been destroyed months earlier and searches the timeline.

An insurer compares the roof before and after a storm.

An environmental group reconstructs a clearing.

A government reviews how an adversary prepared a site before an event.

The image was captured before the question existed.

That is a peculiar form of option value.

Collect first.

Discover the question later.

Commercial constellations can afford more of this because their economics are built around repeated collection.

The satellite does not have to know every customer in advance.

The archive becomes inventory.

This is the observation equivalent of the constellation logic from earlier chapters.

The individual image becomes less sacred.

The database becomes the machine.

Once the database exists, another political consequence follows.

Official secrecy becomes harder to sustain around visible physical events.

Harder does not mean impossible.

States can move at night.

They can conceal equipment.

They can manipulate narratives.

They can restrict access to ground truth.

They can flood information channels with false interpretations.

They can attack satellites or ground infrastructure.

They can prosecute leakers and intimidate witnesses.

But a government trying to deny the presence of a large deployment, a burned installation, a flooded region or a newly constructed facility may now have to contend with private eyes it does not control.

The cost of denial rises.

This is what happened before the 2022 invasion.

Commercial imagery did not prevent war.

It did not persuade everyone of Western warnings.

It did not reveal Putin's final decision.

It made the physical preparation harder to hide.

That distinction matters.

Transparency is not deterrence by itself.

Evidence is not power by itself.

But the distribution of evidence changes diplomacy.

It changes journalism.

It changes propaganda.

It changes accountability.

It changes how quickly outside observers can challenge an official claim.

The information battlefield gets another sensor.

This can be good for truth and still dangerous for security.

The same commercial visibility available to journalists can be purchased by adversaries.

The same high-revisit imagery that documents human-rights abuses can monitor military bases.

The same radar that helps disaster responders see through storm clouds can track activity during conflict.

The same archive that creates accountability can expose operational patterns.

Dual use is not a side effect of Earth observation.

Seeing is dual use.

This is why governments are both customers and regulators.

They want the industrial capacity.

They want the innovation.

They want the unclassified sharing advantage.

They also worry about what others can buy.

Commercial remote sensing therefore sits in a permanent tension between diffusion and control.

The market wants more customers.

National security sometimes wants fewer.

Foreign competitors make unilateral restriction less effective.

Technology keeps moving.

Regulation follows from behind.

This is the same governance lag we saw in communications, but the control point is different.

Starlink's political power came partly from the provider's ability to determine whether a terminal received service.

Earth-observation power comes partly from determining who can task a sensor, how quickly they receive the collection, what resolution they receive, whether the imagery is exclusive and whether it can be redistributed.

The scarce resource is not connectivity.

It is attention from orbit.

This produces a new kind of customer relationship.

A small state can rent the eye.

A large state can rent many eyes while keeping its own.

A company can rent enough visibility to make a supply chain legible.

A newsroom can rent evidence.

An NGO can rent historical memory.

The operator keeps the spacecraft.

The customer acquires the view.

This is sovereignty as a service in miniature.

The capability once associated with a national reconnaissance program can be bought in slices.

One hour of tasking.

One area of interest.

One archive query.

One alert stream.

One monitoring subscription.

One radar collection through cloud.

One answer delivered through an API.

The capability becomes modular.

Modularity accelerates diffusion.

It also concentrates power in the platforms that own the sensors and archives.

A company with a unique historical archive possesses something a new competitor cannot manufacture overnight.

A company with high revisit over strategic latitudes can sell time, not merely resolution.

A company with automated tasking can sell responsiveness.

A company with government contracts can finance capacity that benefits commercial products.

A company with many commercial customers can build capacity government later buys.

The public-private feedback loop appears again.

Private investment builds sensors.

Government demand validates the market.

Government contracts finance scale.

Scale lowers the marginal cost of other use.

New customers appear.

Their demand finances more sensors.

The state then buys from a richer market.

This is not privatization in the simple sense.

It is co-evolution.

By 2026, the NRO's behavior made that co-evolution explicit.

The agency was not only buying high-resolution optical imagery through its large commercial contracts. It was operationalizing commercial radar, expanding radio-frequency procurement and opening pathways for newer phenomenologies such as hyperspectral and infrared sensing.

Its leaders publicly challenged industry to scale faster because demand for overhead data was rising.

The classified reconnaissance institution had become one of the market's most sophisticated customers.

That should change how we think about the phrase *commercial intelligence*.

The word commercial does not mean strategically peripheral.

Sometimes the commercial layer becomes valuable precisely because it is abundant, fast, shareable and replaceable.

A government system may optimize for exquisite capability and protected access.

A commercial system may optimize for volume and service.

The best national architecture can use both.

This is the lesson the NRO has been signaling for years.

Buy what the market can supply.

Build what only the state can justify or protect.

Fuse the result.

The model looks less like replacing spies with startups and more like cloud computing for reconnaissance.

The state keeps the most sensitive workloads.

It rents enormous amounts of commodity and near-commodity capability from private infrastructure.

The boundary shifts as the market improves.

Yesterday's exquisite capability can become tomorrow's service tier.

That is why the commercial imagery story is not finished.

Thirty-centimeter optical imagery will not be the final threshold.

Radar constellations will grow.

Hyperspectral sensors will improve.

Thermal infrared will become more available.

Radio-frequency sensing will mature.

Automated fusion will make separate sensors behave more like one observational fabric.

The question will become less *Who owns the best camera?* and more *Who can turn the most observations into trusted decisions quickly?*

That competition favors systems, not satellites.

It favors archives.

It favors software.

It favors distribution.

It favors customers who know what to ask.

And it favors organizations that can keep replenishing the machines overhead.

That last condition is easy to overlook because imagery arrives as pixels.

Pixels feel weightless.

The sensors are not.

Every commercial eye is hardware in an unforgiving environment.

Satellites fail.

Electronics degrade.

Orbits decay.

Competitors improve.

Adversaries learn how to interfere.

Demand shifts toward new geometries and new sensing types.

The market that sells persistent observation must continuously replace and expand the physical fleet that makes persistence possible.

This is where seeing reconnects to launch.

During the early Ukraine crisis, BlackSky said it changed spacecraft orbits, launched additional satellites and began collecting over the conflict with the new capacity within weeks.

Whether or not every future crisis allows such speed, the strategic logic is unmistakable.

Observation is more resilient when satellites can be treated as a replenishable fleet rather than irreplaceable monuments.

A country that depends on commercial eyes therefore depends on something beneath the imagery contract.

Factories.

Launch pads.

Rockets.

Spectrum.

Ground stations.

Software.

Capital.

And time.

The new eyes are private.

Their replacement chain is private too.

Once governments begin planning around commercial orbital visibility, the ability to put another sensor overhead becomes part of the capability.

Seeing is not enough.

You have to be able to keep seeing.

The next strategic question is therefore not only who owns the satellites.

It is how fast another one can be launched when the need changes.

The answer is the promise—and the problem—of launch on demand.
