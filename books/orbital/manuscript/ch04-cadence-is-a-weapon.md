# Cadence Is a Weapon

In industrial systems, speed does not merely save time. It changes strategy.

An army that can replace equipment quickly fights differently from one whose losses require years to replenish. A retailer with overnight logistics stocks differently from one supplied by seasonal caravans. A software company that deploys every day can tolerate experiments that would be terrifying if releases occurred once a year.

Launch cadence has the same effect on orbit.

For most of the space age, a satellite failure could become a programmatic tragedy. Spacecraft were expensive, launch opportunities scarce, schedules long. Engineers responded rationally by making satellites highly reliable, redundant and thoroughly tested.

The result was a culture of exquisite machines.

Exquisite machines are wonderful when access is scarce.

They become less obviously optimal when access is abundant.

Suppose a satellite costs hundreds of millions of dollars and its replacement would take five years. You will spend heavily to prevent failure. You may add redundant systems, radiation hardening, exhaustive qualification and layers of review. The satellite becomes expensive partly because launch is expensive and replacement slow.

Now change the environment.

Suppose satellites are built on a production line, launches occur weekly, several dozen spacecraft fit on a rocket, and replacements can be inserted as part of a standing deployment flow.

You still care about reliability.

But you care about the reliability of the **network**, not the immortality of each node.

This is the economic logic that made megaconstellations different from earlier communications satellites.

Traditional geostationary communications satellites orbit roughly 36,000 kilometers above the equator, moving around Earth at the same rate Earth rotates so they appear fixed in the sky. One spacecraft can cover a huge region. The tradeoff is distance: signals travel farther, producing latency, and capacity is concentrated in expensive individual assets.

Low Earth orbit offers the opposite bargain. Satellites are much closer, so latency can be low, but each spacecraft covers less territory and moves rapidly relative to the ground. Continuous service requires many satellites handing traffic from one to another.

For decades, engineers knew this.

The hard part was economics.

A low-orbit constellation can demand hundreds or thousands of satellites plus a launch program capable of deploying and replacing them. Earlier constellation ventures demonstrated both the appeal and danger of the idea. Iridium survived bankruptcy and eventually became an important global communications network, but its painful financial history stood as a warning: orbital elegance does not guarantee terrestrial demand at the required price.

What changed was not one invention.

It was the simultaneous decline of several constraints.

Electronics became smaller and more capable. Satellite manufacturing began borrowing techniques from high-volume industries. Phased-array antennas improved. Software-defined systems became more common. Cloud computing and global internet demand expanded. Private capital became willing, at least episodically, to fund large space ventures. And most critically for SpaceX, the company attempting to build a giant constellation also controlled a reusable launch system.

The customer and the launch provider could become one balance sheet.

This produced a feedback loop no previous constellation operator had possessed at comparable scale.

If outside launch demand softened, internal satellites could use flights.

If satellite demand expanded, launch cadence could expand with it.

Rocket improvements reduced constellation deployment cost. Constellation revenue could support rocket development. Each business strengthened the other.

Vertical integration is often described as a cost strategy. Here it became a strategy of **temporal control**.

SpaceX could decide when its own payloads were ready, when its rockets were ready and how to allocate launch capacity between external customers and internal deployment. It did not need to negotiate every mission across corporate boundaries.

Time belonged increasingly to the integrated system.

This is why cadence is a weapon.

Not a weapon in the narrow military sense, though militaries quickly noticed its value. A weapon in competitive strategy: cadence lets an organization act while others are still planning.

A high-cadence launcher can test hardware faster.

A high-cadence constellation can replace failed satellites faster.

A high-cadence manufacturer can introduce new satellite generations incrementally rather than waiting for a once-in-a-decade fleet refresh.

A high-cadence launch market can attract customers whose business models would never tolerate multiyear uncertainty.

And a country whose commercial sector possesses high cadence gains strategic options whether or not the rockets wear military insignia.

This point became impossible to ignore after Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022.

Commercial satellite imagery helped make troop movements visible to the public. Commercial communications systems became part of wartime resilience. Space capabilities that once would have belonged almost exclusively to states were available through companies, contracts and consumer-derived hardware.

The most famous case was Starlink.

The network was not designed as a military communications program. It was designed as a broadband business. Yet a broadband business built from thousands of replaceable satellites, flat user terminals and frequent launch possessed characteristics militaries had spent decades seeking: distributed architecture, rapid replenishment and reach across damaged terrestrial infrastructure.

A consumer network had stumbled into strategic relevance because its industrial architecture was resilient.

That should change how we think about defense technology.

Military capability is not always produced by military procurement.

Sometimes the state buys access to a commercial system that has become more capable, more distributed and more rapidly refreshed than a bespoke government alternative.

The pattern reaches beyond war.

After hurricanes, wildfires, earthquakes and floods, portable satellite terminals can restore connectivity before ground networks are rebuilt. Airlines can offer broadband over oceans. Ships can stay connected far from shore. Remote mines, farms, research stations and communities can bypass terrestrial buildout.

Every new use strengthens the network's claim to being infrastructure.

And infrastructure creates dependency.

The deeper a commercial orbital system becomes embedded in ordinary life and state operations, the harder it becomes to describe it as merely a private service.

Electric utilities are private in many countries. Banks are private. Telecom carriers are private. Cloud providers are private. Yet governments regulate them intensely because private ownership does not erase public consequence.

Low Earth orbit is arriving at the same argument without having passed through the same century of legal development.

The machines got there first.

The law is chasing them.
