# The Customer Everywhere

At eight in the morning on October 15, 2025, United Flight 2940 left Newark for Houston carrying a new kind of expectation.

The airplane was a Boeing 737-800.

More than 170 customers were aboard.

Two Starlink antennas had been installed on the aircraft.

United presented the event as a first for a major American airline: Starlink connectivity on a mainline flight across both personal devices and seatback entertainment screens.

But the most revealing part of the announcement was not the antenna.

It was the list of things passengers were supposed to do with it.

Watch live television.

Stream.

Work on shared documents.

Play games.

Shop.

Use multiple devices.

In other words, behave normally.

This is how infrastructure wins.

Not when a technology performs a miracle.

When the miracle becomes a complaint if it fails.

A satellite internet connection once carried the glamour of a field expedition. It belonged on a research vessel, a military base, a television truck or a remote outpost where any connection at all felt impressive.

By the middle of the 2020s, the standard was reversing.

A passenger on an aircraft crossing several states did not need to understand which satellite was overhead, which ground station was receiving traffic, which laser links were active, which orbital plane was carrying the connection or how often the session moved from spacecraft to spacecraft.

The passenger had a simpler demand.

Does the Wi-Fi work?

This is a brutal question for a space company because it strips away every excuse that once made space special.

Customers do not care that orbital mechanics is difficult.

They care that the video buffers.

They do not care that a phased-array antenna is tracking a spacecraft moving several kilometers per second.

They care that the call drops.

They do not care that thousands of machines are coordinating overhead.

They care that the payment terminal goes offline.

This is the final transformation from spacecraft to infrastructure.

The technology becomes judged by the ordinary standards of the thing it replaces.

Starlink did not become consequential merely because millions of terminals connected to satellites.

It became consequential because the network entered categories of life where people already had expectations.

Home internet.

Office internet.

Ship internet.

Airplane internet.

Emergency communications.

Industrial connectivity.

Backup connectivity.

The orbital network had to stop asking the customer to admire orbit.

It had to compete with not thinking about orbit at all.

That is a harder market than space enthusiasts sometimes imagine.

A household buying broadband does not buy a constellation.

It buys video calls that do not freeze.

A ship operator does not buy low Earth orbit.

It buys crew communications, cloud applications, weather data, remote support and operational continuity.

An airline does not buy satellites.

It buys an onboard experience customers will notice only when it is worse than the internet in the terminal.

A retailer does not buy a dish.

It buys the ability to process a card payment when the primary terrestrial link fails.

The customer everywhere is not one person.

It is a change in what the network is for.

The first Starlink story was rural broadband.

That story was real.

It was also incomplete.

Remote households were an obvious opening market because terrestrial alternatives were often weakest where population density was lowest. Fiber is extraordinary infrastructure, but fiber has to be physically built to a place. Cable does too. Fixed wireless needs towers. Cellular coverage depends on terrestrial radios, backhaul, power and economics that generally improve where customers are concentrated.

A satellite constellation reverses part of that logic.

The expensive layer is already moving overhead.

The marginal installation can be a terminal.

That does not make rural service cheap in every case. The customer still needs equipment, power, a clear enough view of the sky, an affordable plan and legal permission to operate. Capacity can become constrained. Weather and local obstruction matter. Terrestrial fiber can offer performance and durability that satellite service may not match.

But the geography of deployment changes.

A road is no longer required for the communications network to arrive.

That matters in places where roads themselves are scarce.

In 2023, the Kuskokwim Corporation in Alaska described Starlink as an immediate bridge for communities waiting on longer-term broadband infrastructure. Its Connect TKC program provided equipment and installation to participating shareholder households. The organization later reported serving 450 households across forty-six communities and five states, reaching nearly half of TKC households.

The word **bridge** is useful.

Satellite broadband does not have to defeat fiber to be valuable.

It can arrive before fiber.

It can reach the location fiber may never economically reach.

It can back up fiber.

It can provide enough service to change daily life while a better long-term network remains years away.

The National Telecommunications and Information Administration used similar logic when it announced tribal broadband awards in 2023. One Alaska project planned to purchase and install Starlink for 1,410 homes and nine community anchor institutions as an **interim broadband solution** while serving nine Alaska Native villages.

Interim is not a small word.

Infrastructure debates often collapse into a tournament: fiber versus satellite, public versus private, permanent versus temporary.

Real communities need connections while the tournament is still being argued.

A temporary bridge can have permanent consequences if it lets a student finish coursework, a business process a transaction, a patient attend a consultation or a family handle government paperwork that has migrated online.

But temporary infrastructure can also become sticky.

Once people organize life around a service, replacing it becomes harder politically and practically.

That is why the rural broadband argument around Starlink has never been as simple as “it works” or “it does not work.”

The more useful question is: **works for what, at what price, with what capacity, compared with which alternative, and for how long?**

Alaska itself exposes the tension.

State broadband officials have discussed communities already using Starlink while still treating some of those locations as candidates for more durable high-capacity infrastructure. Under federal broadband programs, satellite service has not always been treated as equivalent to fiber or other qualifying networks for subsidy purposes. Regulators have worried about long-term capacity, speeds, reliability and affordability even while residents use the service because it is available now.

Both realities can be true.

A service can be transformative for the person who lacked broadband yesterday and still be an imperfect substitute for infrastructure a government hopes will last decades.

This distinction is easy to lose when technology becomes political symbolism.

Users live in the present.

Infrastructure policy lives partly in the future.

The ship offers a different view.

A container vessel does not wait for fiber because fiber cannot follow it.

At sea, satellite connectivity is not the bridge to a terrestrial network.

It is the network.

In October 2023, Maersk announced that it would deploy Starlink across more than 330 of its own-operated container vessels after trials on more than thirty ships.

The announcement described speeds above 200 megabits per second and emphasized two classes of benefit that reveal how connectivity becomes embedded.

The first was human.

Crew members could make high-definition video calls, stream media and remain in better contact with people at home during long periods at sea.

The second was operational.

Maersk said higher-speed, lower-latency connections would help move business applications into the cloud and improve remote support and inspection.

These uses look unrelated until you remember what a ship is.

A ship is both a workplace and a temporary home.

Connectivity serves both lives at once.

That creates a kind of infrastructural ratchet.

Before high-bandwidth connectivity, a company designs operations around scarcity.

Applications are local.

Files synchronize occasionally.

Remote support is limited.

Crew communications are constrained.

Processes assume disconnection.

Then bandwidth arrives.

The company moves systems into the cloud.

It changes maintenance procedures.

It centralizes support.

It changes crew expectations.

It installs new sensors and workflows that assume continuous links.

The connection is no longer an amenity added to the old operation.

The operation reorganizes around the connection.

This is when infrastructure acquires power.

Dependency is not created the moment a service appears.

Dependency is created when other systems are redesigned on the assumption that the service will remain.

The same pattern appeared in cruise ships.

In 2024, Carnival said it had completed Starlink deployment across more than ninety ships in its global fleet. Royal Caribbean had already moved fleet-wide after earlier trials. Cruise operators described not only faster guest internet but new operational uses: ship-to-shore communication, equipment monitoring, crew connectivity and digital services that were difficult under older bandwidth constraints.

The passenger may think the product is streaming by the pool.

The operator may be moving pieces of the ship's nervous system into the cloud.

This distinction matters because it changes the consequence of an outage.

An amenity can fail and disappoint people.

An operational dependency can fail and interrupt work.

The deeper Starlink penetrates enterprise operations, the less useful it becomes to think of the service as merely consumer broadband from space.

SpaceX's own 2026 public filings describe the network across construction, agriculture, retail, telecom, hospitality, aviation, maritime and land mobility. The company names field offices, remote worksites, research stations, drilling rigs, rural hospitals, aircraft, cruise ships, trains and hotels as enterprise environments.

There is a rhetorical danger in a list like that.

A company describing its own market will naturally make the market sound enormous.

The useful evidence is not the list alone.

It is the revenue composition around it.

For the six months ending June 30, 2026, SpaceX reported that Connectivity revenue had grown by roughly $2.5 billion from the comparable period a year earlier. About $1.4 billion of the increase came from consumer subscribers. Another roughly $1.1 billion came from government, aviation, maritime and other enterprise businesses.

The customer base was diversifying in money, not only in marketing copy.

That is the customer-everywhere transition.

The constellation does not need every person to want the same product.

It needs enough different people and institutions to want different products from the same physical network.

A home subscription.

A ship contract.

An airline agreement.

A government service.

A backup link for a store.

Connectivity for a remote worksite.

The spacecraft do not care which billing category produced the packet.

This is platform economics appearing in orbit.

One fleet supports many contracts.

One factory replenishes the fleet.

One launch system expands it.

One network-operations layer allocates capacity across many forms of demand.

The customer sees a product category.

The operator sees shared infrastructure.

This is why mobility matters so much.

Traditional broadband is geographically sticky.

A fiber connection belongs to an address.

A cable modem belongs to a service territory.

A cell tower covers a region.

Starlink can follow customers who move through places where terrestrial networks become discontinuous.

Ships.

Aircraft.

Trucks.

Field teams.

Temporary construction sites.

Emergency deployments.

The network's geometry is already moving, so moving customers are not an exception in quite the same way they are for fixed terrestrial infrastructure.

That does not eliminate regulation.

It multiplies it.

International waters are not the same legal environment as territorial waters.

An aircraft crosses national jurisdictions quickly.

In-motion service may be permitted in one country and restricted in another.

Spectrum rights remain national even when satellites ignore borders physically.

Starlink's own support materials warn that maritime and land-mobility use depends on government approvals and that in-motion use can be prohibited in particular countries.

The customer may experience a single network.

The operator experiences a patchwork of permissions.

This will become important later when the book turns to regulation.

For now, notice what this does to the idea of global service.

A constellation can be physically capable of covering a place without being commercially or legally available there.

Coverage is geometry.

Service is permission plus capacity plus price.

That distinction also matters in emergencies.

Satellite systems are often most valuable when terrestrial assumptions fail.

A hurricane cuts power and fiber.

A wildfire destroys towers.

An earthquake damages roads and cables.

A volcanic eruption severs an undersea link.

The normal communications network can fail for the same reason the community needs communication most.

Tonga offered an unusually clear example.

On January 15, 2022, the Hunga Tonga–Hunga Ha'apai eruption and tsunami damaged the undersea cable connecting the country to the outside world. International agencies documented severe communications disruption.

Within weeks, the Tongan government received donated Starlink terminals. By May, communications teams were installing them in Ha'apai and Vava'u. Government departments, schools, public facilities and communities used the links while broader connectivity was being restored.

A Justice Office in Ha'apai described being able to process birth and marriage certificates and complete daily work more effectively. Public Wi-Fi points were installed at government offices, a fire station, an airport and a school.

This is a useful corrective to the cinematic image of disaster communications.

Emergency connectivity is not only rescuers speaking into radios beside collapsed buildings.

It is paperwork.

Certificates.

Licenses.

School access.

Public Wi-Fi.

Routine government continuing while the ordinary network is broken.

Civilization during recovery consists largely of boring processes that still need packets.

This is why the phrase **infrastructure becomes boring** is not an insult.

Boring is the achievement.

Boring means the technology has moved from spectacle into expectation.

The user does not celebrate every successful packet because there are too many successful packets to notice.

The system becomes part of the background against which life happens.

But boring infrastructure has a special political property.

People discover dependence most clearly during failure.

You notice electricity when the lights go out.

You notice payment networks when the card reader stops.

You notice cloud infrastructure when applications vanish.

You notice broadband when work, school, entertainment and communication all fail at once.

An orbital network is moving toward the same category.

The more invisible it becomes during normal operation, the more visible its operator becomes during disruption.

This creates a profound reversal in the politics of commercial space.

The early private space company asked government and investors for permission to try something extraordinary.

The mature infrastructure company may be asked why an ordinary service stopped.

That is a heavier obligation.

It is also one reason pricing matters.

Infrastructure is not universal because it is technically available.

It becomes widely social only when people can afford to use it.

Starlink's customer growth has been accompanied by lower average revenue per consumer service line as the company expanded internationally and introduced lower-priced plans. In the first half of 2026, SpaceX reported roughly 101 percent year-over-year growth in Starlink service lines while average revenue per user declined by more than twenty percent, which it attributed primarily to international expansion and lower-priced plans.

That trade is revealing.

A network designed only for affluent remote users can charge a premium and remain a specialty product.

A network attempting to become global infrastructure eventually confronts lower-income markets, local competitors, regulatory obligations and customers with less willingness to pay.

Scale pulls price downward.

The falling average does not prove affordability everywhere. A terminal can still be expensive relative to local income. Monthly service can still be out of reach. Taxes, import duties and local pricing vary. Some markets may support subsidies or community access; others may not.

The point is not that the affordability problem disappeared.

It entered the business model.

This is what happens when the customer becomes everyone.

The median customer gets less exotic.

And less exotic customers are harder to impress.

A polar researcher may tolerate a complicated setup because the alternative is no connection.

A suburban household comparing fiber, cable, fixed wireless and satellite will not.

An airline passenger will compare onboard Wi-Fi with the experience on another airline.

A shipping company will compare network cost, uptime, support and integration.

A retailer will compare backup options.

The constellation enters ordinary procurement.

That is both the triumph and the danger of commoditization.

Space loses its romance and gains a service-level agreement.

A network that competes in ordinary markets must answer ordinary questions.

How much does it cost?

How fast is it at peak demand?

How often does it fail?

How quickly can a terminal be replaced?

What happens when a country changes the rules?

Can the provider raise prices?

Can the customer switch?

Who owns the data?

What support exists when something breaks?

What happens if the provider itself has an outage, a financial problem or a political dispute?

These questions sound mundane.

They are the questions that turn technology into power.

The customer-everywhere strategy creates a paradox.

Diversification can make the network stronger.

Residential users, airlines, maritime fleets, enterprises and governments create different revenue streams across different geographies.

But diversification can also make the operator more central.

The same network begins appearing inside more unrelated systems.

A farm and an aircraft share infrastructure.

A cruise ship and a government office share infrastructure.

A remote school and a military unit may use variants of the same underlying industrial machine.

One provider becomes present in many domains that previously had separate communications stacks.

This is how platform power grows without anybody declaring a platform.

Customers adopt one useful service at a time.

The concentration appears afterward.

No household signs up because it wants to create a globally important communications company.

It signs up because the terrestrial option is bad.

No shipping line installs terminals because it wants to alter the geopolitics of orbit.

It wants better crew connectivity and cloud access.

No airline offers free Wi-Fi because it wants to make private space infrastructure strategically indispensable.

It wants passengers to choose the airline and enjoy the flight.

Infrastructure power is often an emergent consequence of many reasonable local decisions.

That is what makes it durable.

The network does not conquer customers.

Customers pull it into their lives.

Then they build around it.

There is an important counterweight.

No single network is best everywhere.

Fiber remains extraordinary where it can be economically built.

Terrestrial mobile networks have enormous capacity in dense areas.

Geostationary and medium-Earth-orbit satellite systems retain valuable roles.

OneWeb and other LEO providers serve enterprise and government markets through different architectures and business models.

Ships and airlines can use multi-orbit or multi-provider connectivity strategies rather than betting everything on one constellation.

Carnival, for example, described Starlink as part of a broader multi-provider strategy rather than as the only network aboard its fleet.

Redundancy matters precisely because connectivity becomes critical.

The more valuable the service, the stronger the case for alternatives.

This is an old infrastructure lesson.

A bridge is useful.

Two bridges can be resilience.

The commercial winner in orbit may therefore not be the company that eliminates every competitor.

It may be the company that becomes the default layer even inside systems that retain backups.

Cloud computing offers the analogy.

Large enterprises often maintain multiple providers, private systems or disaster-recovery options.

That does not prevent a dominant cloud platform from becoming deeply important.

Redundancy reduces dependence without eliminating it.

Starlink's enterprise filings contain a revealing phrase: some customers begin with the service as backup and later move it into the primary role.

This is infrastructure adoption in miniature.

First, the network is insurance.

Then it is convenience.

Then it is normal.

Then removing it becomes work.

By the time the customer notices the transition, the technical argument may already be over.

The airline has installed antennas across a fleet.

The shipping company has moved applications into the cloud.

The remote business has reorganized operations around continuous connection.

The community expects online services.

The backup link is carrying primary traffic.

Dependence grows not from a contract clause but from accumulated redesign.

This is why the economics of infrastructure cannot be separated from the politics that follow.

A company selling a novelty can walk away from a market and disappoint enthusiasts.

A company operating infrastructure can walk away and disrupt institutions.

The legal obligations are not automatically the same.

The public expectations will not remain the same either.

That problem becomes much sharper once humans themselves begin buying rides and destinations in orbit.

The commercial transformation does not stop at sending data through private machines.

It begins sending people through them.

The next customer is not holding a terminal.

The next customer is inside the spacecraft.
