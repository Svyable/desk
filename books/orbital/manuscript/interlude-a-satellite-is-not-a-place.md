# A Satellite Is Not a Place

One reason public debate about orbit becomes confused is that we speak about it using terrestrial nouns.

Slot. Lane. Shell. Territory. Parking place.

These metaphors help until they do not.

A satellite in low Earth orbit is not parked. It is falling.

It remains above Earth because it is moving sideways so quickly that the planet curves away beneath it. Gravity is not defeated; gravity is the mechanism. The spacecraft is continuously falling around Earth.

This physical fact creates a different kind of geography.

On land, ownership often attaches to a fixed boundary. At sea, vessels move through shared water. In orbit, useful paths are shaped by altitude, inclination, orbital planes, atmospheric drag, mission requirements and collision risk. Two spacecraft can occupy similar altitudes while moving in different planes and crossing each other's paths at frightening relative velocity.

The resource is not simply empty volume.

It is predictable, safe access to trajectories plus radio spectrum plus the ability to maneuver plus the information needed to avoid everyone else.

That is why “there is plenty of room in space” is both true and unhelpful.

There is plenty of ocean too. That does not mean ports, shipping lanes, fisheries and chokepoints have no scarcity.

Industrialization concentrates activity.

Constellations prefer certain altitude bands for reasons that include latency, drag, coverage and debris lifetime. Earth-observation satellites favor geometries that suit imaging. Crewed stations occupy particular inclinations. Launch vehicles inject payloads into trajectories constrained by geography and safety. Spectrum allocations create another invisible map layered over the physical one.

Low Earth orbit is not filling evenly.

It is urbanizing.

And like all urbanization, the conflict is not primarily about whether more people can physically fit.

It is about coordination.
