# The Night the Sky Became a Network

At first it looked like a mistake in the heavens.

One point of light followed another across the dark, then another, then another, each keeping an almost ceremonial distance from the next. They did not blink like airplanes. They did not wander like satellites usually seemed to wander, solitary and incidental, a moving star spotted only because someone had bothered to look up at the right minute. This was a procession. A dotted line had been drawn across the sky.

People stopped what they were doing.

Some reached for their phones. Some called children outside. Some assumed they were seeing aircraft, missiles, meteors, a military exercise, visitors from somewhere less administratively burdened than Earth. Amateur astronomers supplied the answer online: a fresh batch of communications satellites, still traveling close together after launch, spreading gradually into their assigned orbits.

The uncanny part was not that machines were passing overhead. Machines had been passing overhead since 1957. The uncanny part was the number of them, and the implication contained in the number.

A satellite had once been the sort of object a nation announced.

Sputnik was a metal sphere with four whisker-like antennas and a radio transmitter powerful enough to say almost nothing. It said *beep*, over and over, and that was enough to reorder geopolitics. The first American reconnaissance satellites were state secrets wrapped in engineering. Weather satellites became shared civic miracles. Communications satellites acquired names that sounded like treaty organizations and Greek gods. They were singular machines, expensive enough to deserve biographies.

Now satellites came in batches.

They were stacked for launch, released by the dozen, checked out by software, and inserted into a network whose usefulness depended not on the magnificence of any one spacecraft but on the behavior of the whole. If one failed, the loss was unpleasant but comprehensible. Another would come. Another already had.

This was a different kind of space age.

In the first space age, governments built machines to prove they could do impossible things. In the second, companies built systems intended to make impossible things ordinary.

The difference is easy to miss because the rockets are louder than the institutions behind them.

A booster descends from the sky on a column of fire and lands upright: that is cinematic. A government agency rewrites the terms of a procurement agreement so that a company may own the resulting vehicle: less so. A capsule docks with a space station: magnificent. A contracting officer decides to buy cargo delivery as a service rather than purchase a spacecraft designed to government specifications: unlikely to make anyone's highlight reel.

Yet the paperwork came first.

Long before low Earth orbit filled with privately owned satellites, before rockets began landing on drone ships, before astronauts climbed into capsules belonging to corporations, a quieter revolution took place inside the state that had done more than any other to define the old model. The United States government began experimenting with a dangerous thought.

What if NASA did not have to own the truck?

The question sounds small. It was not.

For most of the American space program, NASA had behaved like an institution responsible not only for deciding where to go but also for specifying, overseeing and ultimately owning much of the machinery required to get there. Contractors built the hardware, certainly. Private industry had been present from the beginning. North American Aviation, Grumman, Boeing, McDonnell Douglas, Lockheed, Rocketdyne, IBM and thousands of suppliers made the Moon landings possible. There had never been a purely governmental space program in the sense that civil servants machined every valve and soldered every circuit board.

But government sat at the center of the architecture. It defined missions, controlled designs, paid the bills and owned the resulting systems. Industry supplied the state.

The new model was stranger.

The state would specify an outcome. Deliver cargo. Carry astronauts. Meet safety requirements. Demonstrate milestones. Then a company would decide how to build the machine, invest some of its own money, retain ownership of the hardware and attempt to sell the resulting service to other customers as well.

This was not deregulation. It was not government getting out of the way. It was government changing where it stood.

NASA would become, in the phrase that matters throughout this book, an **anchor customer**.

That shift created room for a company to fail differently, build differently and eventually fly differently. It helped produce rockets whose most valuable component returned from launch instead of falling into the ocean. Reuse made repetition possible. Repetition made rapid learning possible. Rapid learning made high cadence possible. High cadence made giant constellations economically imaginable. Giant constellations created services that could attract millions of customers, which in turn funded still more launch.

A flywheel began to turn.

Launch built the network. The network paid for launch. The more often the system flew, the better the organization became at flying it. The cheaper and more routine each mission became, the more uses appeared for orbit. And because the company building the rockets also happened to build the largest constellation launched by those rockets, demand could be generated internally whenever outside customers were insufficient.

The heavens acquired something resembling an industrial metabolism.

By August 2026, one privately operated constellation—Starlink—accounted for more than ten thousand active satellites in the lower reaches of low Earth orbit. Under one widely used independent cataloging scheme, those spacecraft represented roughly three quarters of all active payloads in that region. The exact percentage moves as satellites launch and reenter, but the scale of the change does not depend on a particular day's count. A single commercial network now contains more active spacecraft than all of human civilization had operated in orbit for most of the space age combined.

To call this merely “commercial space” is to undersell it.

Commerce is part of the story. So is engineering. So is venture capital. So are charismatic founders, brilliant technicians, government subsidies, public research, military contracts, spectrum licenses and a great deal of concrete poured near beaches.

But the larger story is about **power**.

A company that can launch every few days possesses a different relationship to failure than a country that can launch every few months. A constellation that can replace a damaged spacecraft quickly possesses a different relationship to wartime attrition than a military whose exquisite satellites take years to build. A network with customers on ships, airplanes, farms, battlefields and disaster zones occupies a different political category from a conventional telecom provider whose cables end at national borders.

The old symbols of sovereignty were easy to recognize: flags, warships, embassies, currencies, armies.

The new ones are harder to see because some look like consumer electronics.

A flat antenna on the roof of a farmhouse can connect through a satellite moving at thousands of meters per second, route data through a privately managed network and reach the global internet without touching much of the traditional terrestrial infrastructure below. The same basic system can connect an airliner over an ocean. It can restore communications after a hurricane. It can keep a military unit online after cell towers have been destroyed. It can be switched on in a country, restricted near a border, provisioned for a government or denied to a user.

Infrastructure has always been political. Orbital infrastructure merely makes the politics visible at planetary scale.

And this infrastructure occupies a strange commons.

Low Earth orbit is vast in human terms and crowded in orbital ones. Spacecraft travel so quickly that a paint chip can be dangerous. Satellites cannot simply pull onto the shoulder. Operators receive conjunction warnings and maneuver when probabilities cross thresholds. Different constellations share altitude bands. Dead spacecraft and fragments pass through the same environment. Radio frequencies must be coordinated. Launches must cross airspace. Reentries must cross it again.

The system depends upon rules, but many of the rules were written for an era in which satellites were scarce.

The number of machines changed faster than the institutions.

That is the recurring pattern in this story. First a technology becomes possible. Then it becomes cheap enough to repeat. Then repetition changes behavior. Then behavior changes power. Only afterward does governance discover that the thing it thought it was regulating has become something else.

The transformation of low Earth orbit is therefore not best understood as a race between companies or a duel between billionaires. Those stories are vivid because personalities fit neatly into headlines. But orbit does not care about personalities. Orbit rewards velocity, mass, timing, reliability and continuous correction.

So does industrial power.

The important question is not who loves space most. It is who can keep putting useful mass where it needs to be, who can replace what breaks, who controls the resulting networks, who depends on them and who writes the rules after dependency has already formed.

On a clear night, the answer sometimes passes overhead in a line.

A child sees lights.

An astronomer sees interference.

A military planner sees resilient communications.

An investor sees recurring revenue.

A regulator sees a stack of pending licenses.

An orbital-dynamics engineer sees thousands of future conjunctions.

And somewhere in a control room, no one looks up at all. The satellites are dots on a screen, elements in a database, members of a fleet.

That may be the surest sign that the revolution has succeeded.

The extraordinary has become operations.
