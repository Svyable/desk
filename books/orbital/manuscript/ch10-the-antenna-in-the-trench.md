# The Antenna in the Trench

The first attack on orbital communications came before the tanks crossed the border.

On February 24, 2022, roughly an hour before Russia began its full-scale invasion of Ukraine, a cyberattack struck Viasat's KA-SAT satellite network.

The target was commercial infrastructure.

The effect was military and civilian at once.

Terminals in Ukraine stopped working. Other users across Europe were disrupted. Wind turbines in Germany lost remote communications. Tens of thousands of satellite modems were rendered inoperable.

The attack was a useful warning delivered with terrible timing.

A satellite service could be privately owned, commercially sold and still become part of the opening moves of a war.

Two days later, another commercial satellite network entered the same conflict from the opposite direction.

Ukraine's digital minister, Mykhailo Fedorov, publicly asked Elon Musk to provide Starlink terminals.

Musk replied that Starlink service was active in Ukraine and that more terminals were on the way.

The exchange looked improvised because it was.

A minister asked a chief executive for communications infrastructure on social media while his country was being invaded.

Within days, white Starlink antennas began appearing in a war zone.

They did not look like strategic assets.

That was part of their usefulness.

A military satellite terminal had traditionally been the kind of object that announced itself through rugged cases, specialized operators, procurement programs and classified acronyms. A Starlink terminal looked like consumer hardware because it was consumer hardware: a flat antenna, a cable, a router, power.

In photographs from Ukraine, the equipment appeared on rooftops, beside government buildings, in utility facilities, near vehicles and at field positions. The same basic object that could connect a farmhouse or a recreational vehicle could be carried toward the front.

The antenna in the trench is one of the defining images of the second space age.

Not because Starlink was the first commercial satellite service used by a military.

It was not.

Not because private contractors had suddenly entered warfare.

They had been there for generations.

The change was the scale, speed and architecture of dependence.

A commercial network built for mass broadband customers became operationally important to a state fighting for survival before governments had decided what obligations such a provider should have in war.

This is what infrastructure does when it becomes good enough.

It escapes its original category.

The technical reason begins on the ground.

Terrestrial communications are powerful because they concentrate capacity.

Fiber carries extraordinary volumes of data.

Cell towers cover dense populations efficiently.

Data centers aggregate computation.

Switching facilities route traffic.

The system becomes economical by placing expensive infrastructure where many people can use it.

War turns some of those efficiencies into targets.

A tower can be destroyed.

A fiber route can be cut.

A power substation can fail.

A switching center can be attacked.

A cable landing station can become inaccessible.

A network engineer can repair one break and discover another five kilometers away.

The local infrastructure map begins to matter as much as the communications protocol.

Satellite networks change that map.

A Starlink terminal does not need the nearest cellular tower to survive.

It needs power, a view of the sky and authorization to use the network.

The satellite it talks to is moving.

Another satellite follows.

Another follows that one.

The terminal can be replaced more easily than a buried fiber trunk.

The spacecraft are distributed across thousands of moving nodes rather than concentrated in one local facility.

Later generations of the network can route traffic through inter-satellite laser links, reducing dependence on a nearby ground gateway for every path.

This does not make the network invulnerable.

Nothing in war is invulnerable.

Signals can be jammed.

Terminals can be located and destroyed.

Power can fail.

Satellites can malfunction.

Gateways and backbone connections still matter.

Software can contain weaknesses.

Adversaries can steal hardware.

But the attacker's problem changes.

Destroying one terminal does not destroy the network.

Destroying one satellite does not destroy the network.

Cutting one fiber route does not necessarily isolate the user.

The service can absorb local physical damage because its useful machinery is spread across geography and orbit.

That is the resilience advantage of a proliferated constellation.

The Viasat attack showed another side of the same principle.

Commercial satellite communications can themselves be attacked through software and ground systems. Satellite does not mean magically outside the reach of cyber operations.

The lesson was not that one architecture was secure and another was not.

The lesson was that communications resilience depends on where failure can concentrate.

Starlink distributed many of the physical failure points.

Ukraine needed exactly that property.

The country's terrestrial networks did not disappear in 2022. Ukrainian telecom engineers performed extraordinary repairs under attack. Mobile networks, fiber, microwave links, radio systems and other satellite services continued to carry enormous amounts of traffic.

Starlink mattered because it was another path.

Sometimes another path is the difference between inconvenience and isolation.

The first American government shipments illustrate how quickly the service changed category.

A 2025 inspection by the U.S. Agency for International Development's inspector general reconstructed one part of the early terminal flow. USAID purchased 1,508 Starlink terminals. SpaceX donated another 3,667. Together, 5,175 terminals moved through that USAID-supported effort.

The original purpose was heavily civilian.

Keep government working.

Keep hospitals connected.

Support energy systems.

Help public authorities and essential-service providers reach the internet when ordinary connections failed.

This use alone would have been strategically important.

A state is partly an information system.

Tax records, registries, identity systems, court files, emergency coordination, public payments, logistics and official communications have to continue while buildings are being destroyed.

Ukraine had already moved important state data into cloud infrastructure. Starlink helped officials reach systems that were no longer safely tied to one office or one terrestrial connection.

The country could lose rooms without losing every function performed inside those rooms.

That is digital resilience meeting orbital resilience.

But the same terminals had another use.

War found it immediately.

Ukrainian units used Starlink for battlefield communications.

Commanders could exchange information.

Drone teams could transmit video.

Artillery units could receive targeting information.

Positions separated by damaged infrastructure could join the same digital network.

A commercial broadband service became part of command and control.

The distinction between civilian and military use blurred because communications itself is dual-use.

A packet does not know whether it contains a hospital inventory or a target coordinate.

A router does not know whether the person behind it is a mayor or a platoon leader unless somebody builds policy around that distinction.

This is where the chapter becomes more interesting than a story about heroic technology.

The same architecture that made Starlink resilient on the battlefield made SpaceX unusually powerful over the battlefield.

The network was physically distributed.

Its control was not.

A Starlink terminal does not connect merely because the hardware exists.

The network has to recognize it.

The account has to be authorized.

The location has to be permitted.

The service plan has to apply.

The software has to accept the device.

Coverage policy has to include the geography.

Network management has to route the traffic.

This is the paradox of commercial orbital infrastructure.

The physical network can be difficult for an enemy to destroy and easy for the operator to modify.

Resilience and control are not opposites.

They can be two properties of the same system.

That control became visible almost as quickly as the service became useful.

At first, the funding arrangement itself was unstable.

SpaceX donated equipment and service. Governments donated equipment. USAID purchased terminals. Poland and other allies bought large numbers of terminals and paid subscription costs. Ukrainian agencies distributed hardware. Private donors contributed.

This was a remarkable emergency response.

It was not a durable military procurement architecture.

By the autumn of 2022, the Pentagon was publicly discussing how to support Ukraine's satellite communications and whether government funding should replace some of the extraordinary private contribution.

The underlying problem was obvious.

A military cannot base a critical communications requirement indefinitely on the generosity of a vendor.

And a vendor cannot be expected to discover its wartime obligations through social-media arguments.

Charity is not doctrine.

Donation is not a service-level agreement.

A battlefield dependency needs contracts, authorities, funding, technical requirements, escalation channels and somebody empowered to decide what happens when the military wants the service to do something the provider does not want it to do.

That transition took time.

In October 2022, Pentagon officials said the Department of Defense had not yet paid SpaceX for Starlink service in Ukraine, though discussions were continuing.

By June 2023, the Pentagon acknowledged that it had contracted with Starlink for satellite communications supporting Ukraine.

The terms were withheld for operational-security reasons.

That shift—from donated service to contracted capability—is one of the most important institutional developments in the entire story.

The network did not become less commercial.

The relationship became more governmental.

Contracts are how states try to turn dependence into obligation.

They specify service.

They allocate cost.

They can define support.

They can create remedies.

They can place operational decisions inside chains of authority that do not begin with an executive's personal judgment.

But contracts cannot erase the fact that the provider owns and understands the machine.

The government can purchase availability.

It does not instantly acquire the satellites, software, operations centers, manufacturing lines, launch system and accumulated engineering knowledge required to reproduce the service.

That asymmetry is the source of leverage.

It also explains why the most controversial Starlink episodes matter even when the public record around them is messy.

The first rule for understanding those episodes is to separate different kinds of refusal.

Turning off service that is already functioning is one thing.

Declining to extend service into a new geographic area is another.

Restricting a specific use, such as controlling an offensive drone, is a third.

Blocking an unauthorized adversary terminal is a fourth.

All four can change battlefield outcomes.

They are not the same operational or legal act.

Public debate has repeatedly collapsed them together.

The Crimea story is the best warning.

In 2023, an early account from Walter Isaacson's biography of Musk was widely reported as saying that Musk had ordered Starlink service turned off during a Ukrainian attack on Russia's Black Sea Fleet at Sevastopol.

That account was later corrected.

Isaacson said he had misunderstood the sequence.

Starlink had not been enabled in the relevant area near Crimea. Ukrainian officials asked for coverage to be extended for the operation. Musk refused to enable it.

The distinction does not eliminate the strategic consequence.

A private provider declined to expand a communications service for an intended military operation because its chief executive feared the escalation that might follow.

But the distinction matters enormously if we are trying to understand what power was actually exercised.

The service was not cut during the attack in the corrected account.

A request to change the service map was denied.

This is why chronology is not pedantry in infrastructure history.

The verb determines the institution.

Did the provider disconnect a customer?

Did it refuse a new request?

Did a contract permit the use?

Was the geography licensed?

Was the terminal authorized?

Was an outage intentional, technical or adversarial?

Each answer points toward a different governance problem.

Another episode is both more consequential and more contested.

In July 2025, Reuters reported that during Ukraine's counteroffensive in late September 2022, Musk ordered Starlink coverage shut down over areas including parts of the Kherson region that Ukrainian forces were trying to retake.

Reuters based the account on interviews with people familiar with the order, current and former employees, military officials and others who experienced the outage. Ukrainian sources told Reuters the loss of connectivity disrupted drone operations, artillery targeting and battlefield coordination.

SpaceX called the reporting inaccurate and referred to earlier company statements affirming its commitment to providing service to Ukraine, but did not publicly specify which parts of the account it disputed.

The Pentagon declined to comment on the reported incident.

That is the evidence state.

Not certainty.

Not rumor either.

A serious multi-source investigation, denied in general terms by the company, concerning a decision that would have had battlefield consequences if reported accurately.

The temptation in a book like this is to convert ambiguity into drama.

We should do the opposite.

The uncertainty is part of the argument.

If a private communications provider can make decisions important enough that governments, soldiers and historians later struggle to reconstruct who changed what coverage when, then the institutional problem exists even before every historical dispute is resolved.

Critical infrastructure normally develops formal records because society cannot tolerate important decisions becoming folklore.

Electric utilities have dispatch logs.

Air-traffic systems record clearances.

Military networks have authorities and operating orders.

Telecommunications carriers operate under licenses, lawful-intercept rules, emergency obligations and contracts.

A global commercial constellation was being pulled into war faster than comparable governance could form around it.

SpaceX itself recognized part of the mismatch.

In February 2023, company president Gwynne Shotwell said Starlink had not been intended for weaponization and that Ukraine had used the service in ways the company had not anticipated or agreed to. She said SpaceX had taken steps to limit Ukraine's ability to use Starlink for controlling drones, without publicly describing the technical measures.

Current Starlink terms make the distinction explicit.

The ordinary commercial service is a communications product. Its terms say it is not designed or intended for use with offensive or defensive weaponry or comparable military end uses. The company's acceptable-use rules can also restrict military activities and remote piloting unless separately permitted by contract.

This is not an eccentric position.

Commercial products have terms.

Export controls exist.

Sanctions exist.

Companies distinguish consumer offerings from military products.

The problem is that the battlefield had already discovered a military use before the product category caught up.

The service was too useful to remain what the contract language said it was.

This is a recurring pattern in general-purpose infrastructure.

Trucks carry food until they carry ammunition.

Cloud services host accounting systems until they host military planning tools.

Commercial aircraft carry tourists until governments charter them during evacuations.

Civilian ports become military logistics nodes when war arrives.

Dual use is not an exception.

It is what happens when a broadly useful capability encounters state power.

The question is what institutions do once the second use appears.

SpaceX's answer increasingly included a separate government product.

Starshield takes technology developed through Starlink and packages it explicitly for national-security customers. The company describes Starshield as a secure satellite network for government entities, with communications, Earth observation and hosted payload missions, additional cryptographic protections and government-oriented requirements.

By 2026, the distinction had become operational procurement rather than branding.

The U.S. military was awarding firm-fixed-price task orders for Starshield services under proliferated-low-Earth-orbit satellite contracts. Space Systems Command described a broader effort to acquire PLEO commercial satellite services for the Joint Force.

This is the mature institutional response to the confusion of 2022.

Do not ask a consumer broadband service to become a military network by improvisation.

Buy a military service.

Specify the mission.

Pay for the capability.

Define security requirements.

Create contractual authority.

Build the government interface before the crisis rather than during it.

That sounds like a clean solution.

It is only a partial one.

A contract clarifies who owes what.

It does not remove concentration risk.

If one provider has the largest constellation, the most mature terminals, the highest launch cadence and the deepest operating experience, purchasing the service can deepen dependence even while making the relationship more formal.

The customer gains contractual rights and loses the fantasy that it can replace the system quickly.

This is the infrastructure trap.

The better the service performs, the more operations reorganize around it.

The more operations reorganize around it, the more painful substitution becomes.

That is why governments began thinking about alternatives.

Europe's push toward sovereign and commercially partnered satellite communications gained urgency from the Ukraine experience. Eutelsat's OneWeb network became part of discussions about backup capability. The European Union accelerated work around secure satellite communications and the IRIS² constellation.

The motive was not that Starlink had failed.

It was almost the opposite.

Starlink had demonstrated the value of a capability Europe did not want to depend on entirely from one foreign company.

Successful infrastructure creates competitors through fear as well as opportunity.

The same lesson appeared inside Ukraine.

By 2024, Ukrainian officials were reporting that Russian forces had obtained Starlink terminals through intermediaries and were using them despite SpaceX saying it did no business with the Russian government or military.

This created a new version of the control problem.

The network helping Ukraine was useful enough that its enemy wanted access too.

Now the question was not whether SpaceX should restrict Ukraine.

It was whether SpaceX could distinguish authorized Ukrainian terminals from unauthorized Russian ones without breaking the service Ukraine depended on.

The answer required identity.

Who owns this terminal?

Who is allowed to use it?

Where is it?

Which account pays for it?

Has it been stolen?

Is it moving?

Does it belong on this side of the line?

These questions sound administrative.

On a battlefield, administration becomes targeting logic for connectivity.

In early 2026, Ukraine and SpaceX worked on a whitelist of authorized terminals after Ukrainian officials said Russian forces were using Starlink connections, including on drones. Ukraine then said unauthorized Russian terminals had been deactivated while whitelisted Ukrainian equipment continued operating.

Reuters could not independently verify the full scale of the resulting disruption, and SpaceX did not publicly detail the mechanism.

But the episode shows the control plane in its purest form.

A software-defined service boundary can become part of a front line.

The same provider capability that worries a customer when it can be used to deny service becomes valuable when it can be used to deny service to the adversary.

There is no morally stable position called “never control the network.”

A network in war will be controlled by someone.

The real questions are who, under what authority, according to what rules, with what evidence and with what recourse.

This is why the simplistic version of the Starlink debate is unsatisfying.

One version says a private company should never be able to influence a war.

That is impossible once private infrastructure is important to war.

A telecom carrier influences war by maintaining or losing service.

A cloud provider influences war by deciding which sanctioned customers it can serve.

A shipping company influences war when it accepts or refuses cargo into a conflict zone.

A semiconductor company influences war through export compliance.

An insurance market influences war by changing which vessels can sail.

Private decisions acquire public consequences when private systems become strategic inputs.

Another version says the company owns the network and therefore can do whatever it wants.

That is equally incomplete.

Ownership does not dissolve public law.

Export controls apply.

Sanctions apply.

Contracts apply.

Licenses apply.

Governments can purchase services, impose conditions, diversify suppliers, subsidize alternatives, use emergency authorities and define military requirements.

A company operating strategic infrastructure exists inside a political system whether it prefers the role or not.

The mature question is not whether private power should exist.

It already does.

The question is how private operational power is converted into accountable infrastructure.

Ukraine's Starlink experience suggests several answers.

First, critical services need explicit contracts before they become critical if possible.

The contract cannot wait until a chief executive posts a complaint about the monthly bill.

Second, military uses should be separated from consumer-service ambiguity.

If a government expects offensive, defensive or command-and-control use, it should procure a service designed and authorized for that purpose.

Third, identity and terminal control matter as much as satellites.

A constellation is only useful to the intended side if the provider and customer can distinguish friend from unauthorized user.

Fourth, redundancy is policy, not waste.

A backup system may look inefficient during peace because the dominant provider works.

Its value appears when commercial terms change, political relations sour, technical failure occurs or the provider itself becomes a target.

Fifth, governments need visibility into operational decisions that affect contracted strategic capability.

A service outage caused by jamming, software failure, geofencing, account policy or deliberate provider action may look identical to the soldier holding the terminal.

The institution needs a way to know the difference.

This is observability for sovereignty.

You cannot govern dependence you cannot diagnose.

The sixth answer is the hardest.

Some decisions cannot be delegated cleanly to either the company or the state.

Imagine a request to extend a commercial communications network into a disputed area for an operation that could escalate a war between nuclear powers.

The company has legal obligations and property rights.

The customer has military objectives.

The home government has foreign-policy interests.

The host state has sovereignty claims.

Allies have commitments.

Export law may constrain support.

The provider's engineers possess operational control.

No one actor has all the legitimate authority and all the technical capability.

That is not a bug in the story.

It is the institutional shape of modern infrastructure.

Power is distributed across layers.

The company owns the machine.

The state writes the law.

The military defines the mission.

The customer depends on service.

The ally funds part of it.

The engineer can change the software.

The adversary tries to jam, steal or exploit it.

The result is not privatized war.

It is interdependent war.

That distinction matters.

A company cannot declare peace.

It cannot sign a treaty on behalf of a nation.

It cannot lawfully annex territory.

It does not command Ukrainian forces.

It does not possess the state's monopoly on legitimate coercion.

But it can control a capability that military units have designed operations around.

That is enough to create foreign-policy consequences without making the company a state.

This is the deeper meaning of **infrastructure with a kill switch**.

The phrase is tempting because it sounds like one dramatic red button.

Reality is less theatrical and more powerful.

There may be no single switch.

There are coverage maps.

Account permissions.

Service plans.

Terminal identifiers.

Firmware updates.

Geographic restrictions.

Network-routing policies.

Export-control rules.

Contract classes.

Backend tools.

The power lies in administrative control over a technical system.

That is how modern infrastructure governs.

Not usually by someone pulling a lever in a bunker.

By software deciding which authenticated device receives which service in which place under which policy.

The battlefield made that invisible governance visible.

It also made Starlink a model for militaries watching from elsewhere.

By 2026, the United States was buying increasingly sophisticated proliferated-LEO communications and data-network services. SpaceX's Starshield business was explicitly designed for national security. The U.S. Space Force was awarding major contracts for resilient, high-speed orbital data transport. Britain had begun using Starshield for military operations, according to Reuters reporting. European governments were accelerating their own secure-communications plans.

The lesson had traveled.

The first space age taught militaries to value satellites.

The second is teaching them to value commercial constellations.

There is a difference.

A military satellite program is built around a government requirement.

A commercial constellation is built around a market, then discovered by governments.

Its scale may come from millions of civilian users.

Its replacement tempo may come from consumer revenue.

Its manufacturing system may be financed by ordinary subscriptions.

Its battlefield resilience may be an accidental dividend of an architecture built for broadband economics.

That can produce capabilities governments could not afford to create in the same way on their own.

It can also create dependencies governments did not design deliberately.

This is why the antenna in the trench matters beyond Ukraine.

The antenna is small.

The institution behind it is enormous.

A soldier plugs in a consumer-looking terminal and joins a network whose satellites are manufactured by the week, launched by a vertically integrated rocket fleet, financed partly by civilian customers, managed by a private company, constrained by American law, purchased by allied governments and contested by an enemy.

That is not merely communications.

It is a new stack of power.

The stack worked well enough that by August 2025 the U.S. government approved a possible $150 million foreign military sale to extend satellite communications services for Ukraine's Starlink terminals.

The emergency improvisation of February 2022 had become a formal defense transaction.

This is how strategic infrastructure is born now.

First somebody needs the service.

Then they cannot operate without it.

Then lawyers discover what the service means.

Then procurement catches up.

Then competitors and governments decide they need their own version.

The chronology should make us suspicious of any claim that technology is “just a tool.”

Tools become infrastructure when institutions reorganize around them.

Infrastructure becomes power when interruption changes what institutions can do.

And private infrastructure becomes political when someone has to decide whose interruption counts as legitimate.

Starlink did not make SpaceX sovereign.

It did something more interesting.

It gave a private operator control over one function that sovereigns suddenly needed.

Communications was the first capability to make this visible at planetary scale.

It would not be the last.

The next private orbital system did not carry messages.

It carried evidence.

Commercial satellites were beginning to show governments, journalists and ordinary citizens things that once belonged mostly to intelligence agencies.

The new eyes were already overhead.
