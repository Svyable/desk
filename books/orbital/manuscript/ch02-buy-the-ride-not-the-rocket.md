# Buy the Ride, Not the Rocket

There are two ways to make an expensive machine cheaper.

The first is to make the machine less expensive.

The second is to stop treating each machine as the final product.

Commercial space did both, but the second change came first conceptually. NASA's procurement experiments separated the service from the hardware. Cargo delivery, not capsule ownership, was the object being purchased. That seemingly modest abstraction created the intellectual space in which everything downstream could change.

To understand why, it helps to look at the old aerospace bargain.

The traditional cost-plus contract solved a real problem. When government asks a company to build something that has never existed, neither side can know exactly what it will cost. If the contractor is forced to name a fixed price under extreme uncertainty, it may either refuse the work, inflate the price to absorb risk or fail when unknowns arrive. Cost reimbursement lets the government pursue technically ambitious projects for which the path is uncertain.

The model helped build weapons, spacecraft, laboratories and machines that changed history.

It also created incentives that became notorious.

If allowable costs are reimbursed and profit is applied partly as a fee on top of those costs, reducing expense can be less attractive than it would be in a normal product business. Requirements proliferate. Changes propagate through layers of documentation. Contractor workforces grow around unique programs. The system becomes excellent at producing singular high-performance machines and less excellent at producing cheap repetition.

This is not because aerospace engineers enjoy wasting money. It is because institutions optimize for what contracts reward.

Commercial procurement altered the reward.

A company paid when it completed a milestone had reason to reach the milestone efficiently. A company that intended to own the resulting vehicle had reason to care about operating cost after development. A company contributing its own capital had reason to reject requirements that added expense without adding value. And a government agency that paid for service rather than reimbursing the entire industrial process could, at least in theory, know the price of the outcome in advance.

Risk moved.

Not all of it. Never all of it. Public money remained essential, and public agencies still carried mission risk, political risk and in human spaceflight the ultimate obligation to crew safety. But some technical and financial risk migrated to the provider.

That migration changed behavior inside companies.

The fastest way to see the difference is to compare two sentences:

*Build the spacecraft we have specified.*

*Deliver this cargo safely to the station.*

The first sentence contains a design.

The second contains a problem.

Problems permit invention.

They also permit stupid solutions. That is why requirements do not disappear. NASA still defines interfaces, safety standards, mission needs and verification. The commercial model is not a blank check for improvisation. It is a negotiation over which layer of the system belongs to whom.

This question—*which layer belongs to whom?*—is the hidden engine of the entire commercial space era.

NASA might define where cargo must arrive and what hazards are unacceptable. The company might choose the capsule's internal architecture. The Federal Aviation Administration might license launch. The Air Force or Space Force might operate the range. The Federal Communications Commission might license spectrum. The company might own the ground station. Another firm might manufacture components. A public university might have produced research underpinning the technology decades earlier.

“Private” and “public” are not two boxes. They are layers in a stack.

The revolution occurred when some of those layers were rearranged.

The same rearrangement later reached human transportation.

When the Shuttle retired in 2011, the United States lost the ability to launch its own astronauts from American soil. NASA purchased seats on Russia's Soyuz spacecraft to reach the International Space Station. This arrangement was technically successful and politically awkward.

Dependence has a way of clarifying abstract policy.

For years, arguments over commercial crew funding could be framed as disagreements about acquisition strategy, industrial policy or program risk. But every time an American astronaut rode a Russian rocket, the debate acquired a visible symbol. The United States had built the station, operated much of it and possessed the world's largest aerospace economy, yet it relied on another country for crew transport.

The dependence became more uncomfortable as relations between Washington and Moscow deteriorated.

NASA needed a ride.

Again, the question was whether it needed to own the ride.

The Commercial Crew Program extended the service model toward the most sensitive cargo imaginable: human beings.

Companies would design, own and operate their crew transportation systems. NASA would establish demanding certification and safety requirements and purchase astronaut transportation. The agency selected Boeing and SpaceX for the major 2014 Commercial Crew Transportation Capability contracts.

The contrast between the two providers would become one of the most instructive natural experiments in modern aerospace.

Boeing possessed a century-scale corporate lineage, deep experience with government programs and a central role in American aviation and spaceflight. SpaceX was younger than the International Space Station.

The intuitive prediction was that the established aerospace giant would be the safer schedule bet.

Reality was less respectful of intuition.

SpaceX flew its uncrewed Crew Dragon demonstration mission to the station in 2019, then launched NASA astronauts Bob Behnken and Doug Hurley on Demo-2 in May 2020. The mission restored orbital human spaceflight from the United States after nearly nine years.

The symbolism was obvious: American astronauts, American rocket, American launch site.

The ownership structure was more consequential.

The rocket was not NASA's.

The capsule was not NASA's.

NASA had bought a service from a company whose spacecraft could, in principle, carry customers other than NASA.

This altered the meaning of a human spacecraft.

Apollo capsules were instruments of a national mission. The Shuttle orbiters were a government fleet. Crew Dragon was a vehicle within a business.

That business could fly NASA crews to the station and later fly private astronauts. It could serve institutional customers and wealthy individuals. It could become part of a market for commercial stations that did not yet exist. The same hardware platform could support missions the original public procurement had not specified.

This is what policymakers mean when they talk about “commercialization,” but the word is too bloodless. What had actually happened was that the state had paid to help create an **option space**.

An option space is a set of future actions made possible by an asset.

A government-owned capsule built for a narrow program may have few legitimate futures outside that program. A privately owned capsule certified to carry astronauts to orbit can be pointed at any customer and destination compatible with its capabilities, regulations and economics. Ownership enlarges the owner's option space.

This is why contracting structure eventually becomes geopolitical structure.

The entity that owns the machine can decide how else to use it.

The entity that owns the network can decide whom else to connect.

The entity that owns the launch vehicle can choose pricing, scheduling and internal payload priorities.

The commercial model creates efficiencies partly by giving private actors more freedom. Freedom is not merely an economic variable. It is power.

At first, this power looked benign because the government's interests and the providers' interests were aligned. NASA wanted cargo and crew transportation. Companies wanted contracts and flight heritage. Successful missions benefited everyone.

But aligned incentives during market creation do not guarantee aligned interests after dependency forms.

That comes later in our story.

First, the machinery had to become good enough to create dependency.

And that required something more dramatic than a better contract.

It required teaching a rocket to come home.
