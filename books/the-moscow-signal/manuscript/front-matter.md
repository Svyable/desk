# THE MOSCOW SIGNAL

## The beam was real. The weapon was not.

Sven Hardy Benson

---

This book begins with an inconvenience.

The microwave signal aimed at the American embassy in Moscow was not a rumor. American technicians measured it. Diplomats complained about it. Presidents discussed it. The United States spent millions trying to determine what it meant and what it did.

That is the easy part.

The hard part is that a real signal does not tell us its purpose.

It does not tell us whether the transmitter was intended to activate surveillance equipment, interfere with American collection systems, illuminate something hidden in the building, harass the mission, study human beings, or alter them. It does not tell us whether an effect feared by officials was physically plausible at the measured exposure. It does not tell us whether an illness that occurred in an exposed population was caused by the exposure. It does not tell us what a later intelligence service learned from the American fear.

This book keeps four ledgers.

**Documented.** Measurements, official memoranda, diplomatic protests, program records, congressional investigations, experimental reports and other material whose existence and content can be checked.

**Credible but incomplete.** Accounts or reconstructions supported by serious sourcing but missing enough of the chain that they cannot carry the strongest conclusion alone.

**Disputed.** Claims for which material disagreement remains over causation, mechanism, intent, diagnosis or provenance.

**Speculative.** Models proposed here because they connect facts in a useful way, not because an archive has certified them.

The rule is simple:

**Exposure is not effect. Effect is not intent. Intent is not capability.**

The Moscow Signal matters because those four things were repeatedly made to impersonate one another.
