# The Crypto Laboratory

Sam Bankman-Fried spent years being photographed in shorts.

The costume worked because it told a story. Here was a billionaire who did not look much like the old picture of a banker. He slept little. He played video games during meetings. He talked about probability, expected value, philanthropy, regulation, and the future. FTX bought sports-arena naming rights, hired celebrities, advertised during the Super Bowl, and became one of the best-known cryptocurrency exchanges in the world.

The image was not incidental to the business.

Trust had to live somewhere.

Cryptocurrency was built around an argument that trust could be moved away from institutions. Bitcoin’s design made it possible to transfer a scarce digital asset through a network that did not depend on a central bank maintaining the ledger. The slogan “don’t trust, verify” captured a real technical achievement: some facts about ownership and transaction history could be established through protocol rules rather than by taking a financial institution’s word for them.

Then millions of people deposited crypto assets with centralized exchanges and trusted the people running them.

The irony is useful because it shows the limit of technological rhetoric. A blockchain can verify what happened on a blockchain. It cannot by itself tell a customer whether an exchange has secretly transferred customer assets to an affiliated trading firm, whether a chief executive is lying in an interview, whether a stablecoin’s claimed mechanism behaves as represented, or whether a person in a messaging app is running an investment scam.

Verification moves. It does not disappear.

When FTX collapsed in November 2022, the failure became a public morality play because the founder had become inseparable from the company. The bankruptcy exposed a vast gap between the public story and the handling of customer funds. A federal jury later convicted Bankman-Fried on seven criminal counts. In March 2024, a judge sentenced him to twenty-five years in prison and ordered eleven billion dollars in forfeiture. The Justice Department said he had misappropriated billions of dollars of FTX customer funds, defrauded FTX investors of more than $1.7 billion, and defrauded lenders to Alameda Research of more than $1.3 billion.

The criminal case matters because it removes one common escape route from the analysis.

This was not merely “crypto volatility.”

It was fraud proven in court.

That distinction matters for every technology boom because losses create an incentive to rewrite the category. When prices rise, promoters describe a new economic system. When prices fall, defenders say investors should have understood the risk. Sometimes that is fair. Speculative assets really can collapse without fraud. A stock can decline because a business fails. A cryptocurrency can lose value because demand disappears. A new technology can be overhyped and still be legitimate.

Fraud is different.

Fraud needs a representation.

Somebody says the money is somewhere it is not. Somebody says a system has a property it does not have. Somebody presents a false financial condition, fabricated account, fake return, fake identity, fake endorsement, fake regulator, fake investment platform, or fake explanation for why withdrawals are delayed.

AI is unusually relevant to crypto fraud because it industrializes representation.

It can make the promoter more convincing.

It can generate the website, customer-support scripts, social posts, investor messages, synthetic headshots, translated outreach, fake testimonials, cloned voices, and videos of public figures that appear to endorse an investment. It can sustain a conversation longer and at lower cost. It can personalize persuasion.

Cryptocurrency, meanwhile, can make the payment harder to reverse.

That combination is attractive to scammers.

The FBI’s 2025 Internet Crime Report, released in 2026, showed the scale of the problem. Americans who filed complaints involving cryptocurrency reported more than eleven billion dollars in losses. The report included, for the first time, a separate section on artificial intelligence: 22,364 complaints and nearly $893 million in reported losses involved AI. The FBI described fake social profiles, voice clones, identification documents, and believable videos depicting public figures or loved ones among the tactics used by scammers.

These figures are complaints, not a census of every loss. Some victims never report. A person may not realize that AI was used. Categories overlap. The numbers should not be read as a clean estimate of “AI-caused fraud.”

They are sufficient to establish that synthetic impersonation is no longer a hypothetical edge case.

Cryptocurrency investment fraud has become particularly destructive. The FBI describes long-running confidence schemes in which a stranger makes contact through text, social media, advertisements, dating apps, or other channels, builds trust, and introduces a supposed investment opportunity. Victims are shown fake trading platforms and fake profits. They may be encouraged to borrow, sell assets, or move retirement funds. Withdrawals eventually fail or trigger demands for additional fees.

The industry nickname “pig butchering” became common, though the FBI has increasingly preferred terms such as confidence-enabled cryptocurrency investment fraud because the slang can be demeaning to victims and can obscure the forced labor behind many scam operations.

That last point changes the moral picture.

Some of the people sending fraudulent messages are themselves victims of trafficking into scam compounds, particularly in parts of Southeast Asia. Organized criminal groups use coercion to force workers to carry out online fraud. The person on the other side of the chat may be both perpetrator and captive.

The CEO model of accountability fails completely here.

There may be no legitimate company.

The “investment manager” may be a fake profile. The office may be a compound. The trading platform may show numbers with no connection to a market. The customer-service representative may be reading scripts under threat. The public figure in the video may have no idea the video exists.

Authority is synthetic all the way down.

This is why crypto scams are a laboratory for the wider AI trust problem. They show what happens when the visible surface of an institution can be manufactured cheaply.

A professional website used to imply something about cost. A television advertisement implied access to production and distribution. A polished prospectus implied lawyers, printers, and a company capable of producing it. A foreign accent, poor grammar, or amateur design sometimes exposed a scam because deception was expensive to execute consistently.

Generative tools reduce those frictions.

The scammer can produce better English.

The fake company can have plausible employee biographies.

The “CEO” can post video updates.

The support bot can answer twenty-four hours a day.

The investment memo can contain tables and risk disclosures.

The product can look more regulated than the regulator.

This does not mean people become helpless. It means surface polish becomes weaker evidence.

The crypto boom had already taught this lesson before the current generation of AI.

FTX was polished in a different way. It had real employees, real investors, real celebrity endorsers, real venture-capital backing, real political influence, real trading activity, and real customers. Those facts made trust feel more reasonable. They did not establish that customer assets were being handled as represented.

The failure was not an absence of signals.

It was a misclassification of signals.

Prestigious investors had incentives to believe other prestigious investors had performed diligence. Customers saw famous athletes and institutional branding. Journalists saw a founder who spoke fluently about risk and policy. Policymakers saw a company participating in regulatory discussions. Each signal borrowed credibility from another.

The system became socially overcollateralized with reputation while financially undercollateralized in reality.

Bankman-Fried was the perfect interface until the interface became evidence against the institution.

The Terraform case provides a different version of the same pattern. Terraform Labs and founder Do Kwon promoted TerraUSD, or UST, as an algorithmic stablecoin designed to maintain a one-dollar value through a relationship with another token, Luna, rather than traditional dollar reserves. The system’s technical architecture became part of the marketing story.

After UST lost its peg in 2022, the ecosystem collapsed and roughly forty billion dollars in market value disappeared. The Securities and Exchange Commission sued. In April 2024, a jury found Terraform and Kwon liable for fraud. The SEC said the defendants had deceived investors about the stability of UST and about claims concerning blockchain use in a Korean payments application. A final judgment and settlement imposed more than $4.5 billion in monetary remedies and required Terraform to wind down.

Again, the distinction between technological failure and fraudulent representation matters.

An algorithmic stablecoin can fail without fraud. A company can make a risky design that collapses. The jury verdict concerned specific misrepresentations, not the proposition that every algorithmic financial instrument is illegal.

Public pressure often loses this discipline after a crash.

Everyone who was skeptical appears wise. Everyone who invested appears foolish. The category becomes either vindicated or discredited.

Markets do not work that neatly.

Bitcoin survived FTX because FTX was not Bitcoin. A centralized exchange can fail while the underlying protocol continues producing blocks. Terra’s failure did not prove that every stablecoin is impossible. Fraudulent token offerings did not make cryptographic systems useless. Conversely, the continued existence of useful blockchain applications did not excuse fraud committed under crypto branding.

Technology and legitimacy have to be evaluated separately.

This is difficult during a boom because innovation creates an excuse for weak verification.

Traditional finance is full of friction: audits, custodians, settlement systems, capital requirements, disclosure rules, broker-dealer obligations, bank supervision, licensing, anti-money-laundering controls, and paperwork that innovators often experience as obstacles. Some of those rules are badly designed. Some protect incumbents. Some slow genuinely useful products.

They also exist because financial history is a museum of confidence tricks.

The entrepreneur says the old rule does not fit the new technology.

The regulator has to decide whether the mismatch is real or merely convenient.

Crypto became a political battlefield partly because reasonable people disagreed on that question. Regulators pursued enforcement using securities, commodities, banking, money-transmission, anti-fraud, and sanctions authorities. Industry participants argued that regulation by enforcement created uncertainty and that new statutory frameworks were needed. Political leadership changed, and federal policy changed with it.

Those disagreements should not obscure a simpler fact.

Fraud rules are technologically portable.

Lying to investors does not become innovative because the lie contains the word decentralized.

Stealing customer assets does not become a protocol experiment.

Impersonating a celebrity with AI does not transform deception into generative art.

The hardest cases concern activity that is risky, novel, and honestly described.

Public pressure should preserve that distinction because overbroad backlash creates a strange benefit for scammers. If every crypto project is described as a scam, the word loses information. If every AI-generated investment pitch is assumed false, legitimate companies using synthetic media become indistinguishable from criminals until proven otherwise. Categories become moral labels rather than analytical tools.

Scammers thrive in environments where trust is already polarized.

They can target people who distrust banks with a story about decentralized liberation.

They can target people who distrust crypto with a story about government recovery services.

The FBI has warned about criminals impersonating its own Internet Crime Complaint Center to re-scam people who had already lost money. The fraudster promises to recover the prior loss and becomes the new authority.

Public pressure can create the market for the next scam.

After a collapse, frightened victims search for help. Search results can be manipulated. Fake law firms, recovery companies, regulators, and support accounts appear. The victim’s prior loss becomes data about vulnerability.

AI makes the second approach easier because the scammer can imitate institutional language more convincingly.

This is why anti-fraud education increasingly teaches a behavior rather than a list of visual tells.

Slow down.

Verify through a separate channel.

Do not use the phone number or link supplied by the person who contacted you.

Do not treat urgency as evidence.

Do not transfer assets merely because an apparent authority says your existing account is unsafe.

Those practices work because they change the information path.

A fake bank representative can control the call. The victim calls the number on the back of the card instead.

A fake government agent can control the text. The victim visits the agency’s official site independently.

A fake investment manager can control the dashboard. The victim verifies custody and registration elsewhere.

The principle is channel separation.

AI increases the value of channel separation because multiple signals can now be forged by one actor. Voice, image, text, and documents are no longer independent evidence if they came from the same synthetic pipeline.

The scammer can make them agree.

A convincing video call is therefore not necessarily a second source.

It may be the same source wearing a face.

This has implications far beyond consumer scams. Companies need payment controls that do not depend on an executive voice. Political campaigns need authenticated channels for instructions. Families need verification habits for emergency calls. Investors need custody and disclosure systems that do not depend on founder charisma.

The more convincing synthetic authority becomes, the less authority should depend on convincing presentation.

That sounds bureaucratic because it is.

Dual approval, transaction limits, callback procedures, signed instructions, regulated custody, independent audits, and separation of duties are deliberately less charismatic than a founder.

They are built for the day charisma fails.

FTX became a story about one man because one man had worked so hard to become the story.

The legal record ultimately supplied the less glamorous description: customer money, affiliated entities, false statements, political donations, investments, loans, and accounting.

The difference between those two stories is the difference between reputation and control.

AI will make reputation cheaper to manufacture.

That makes control more valuable.

Crypto learned the lesson first because money turns trust failures into numbers quickly.

The rest of the economy is about to learn it in slower forms.

The phrase “trustless system” is therefore useful only when accompanied by a question: trustless with respect to what?

Bitcoin can reduce the need to trust a central ledger operator about the state of the Bitcoin ledger. It does not remove the need to trust software you install, hardware you purchase, an exchange that holds keys for you, a bridge between systems, the tax adviser explaining your obligations, the person offering an investment, or your own ability to protect credentials.

Every technical reduction in one trust dependency can create demand for another service around it.

Custody is the clearest example. Holding one’s own cryptographic keys preserves a form of direct control. It also imposes a severe operational burden. Lose the keys and there may be no customer-service desk capable of restoring them. Many users therefore choose custodians. The moment they do, old financial questions return.

Where are the assets?

Are customer assets segregated?

Who can move them?

What records exist?

What happens in insolvency?

Who audits the institution?

Which regulator has jurisdiction?

The protocol can be radically new while the custody problem is ancient.

This is why proof-of-reserves discussions after exchange failures were both useful and incomplete. Demonstrating control of certain on-chain assets can provide evidence. It does not automatically reveal liabilities, off-chain obligations, related-party exposures, or the legal rights customers have against the entity. A snapshot of assets is not a full audit of a business.

Verification needs scope.

AI will create the same problem in other industries. A company can publish a model benchmark without revealing operational incentives. It can demonstrate a safety control without showing how often the control is bypassed. It can produce a provenance signal without proving the underlying claim is true.

Crypto teaches a discipline that transfers well: verify the thing the evidence actually verifies.

Nothing more.

The lesson is especially valuable for celebrity and influencer promotion. A famous person’s involvement can verify that the famous person was paid or chose to promote something. It does not verify the investment. Synthetic media weakens even that limited signal because the endorsement itself may be fake.

The rational response is not to distrust every public figure.

It is to stop treating endorsement as diligence.

The same applies to venture capital. A respected fund’s investment establishes that the fund invested. It may imply that some diligence occurred. It does not transfer the fund’s information or risk tolerance to the retail customer. Investors can be wrong. They can accept risks other people cannot afford. They can rely on contractual rights unavailable to ordinary users.

Social proof is information about people.

It is not information about solvency unless the people have actually verified solvency.

This distinction sounds obvious after FTX. During a boom, social proof compounds because every participant can point to the others.

The celebrity points to the company.

The customer points to the investors.

The investors point to the founder and market growth.

The policymaker points to the company’s regulatory engagement.

The journalist points to the prestigious backers.

Everyone possesses a reason to believe somebody else checked.

This is outsourced skepticism.

Fraud thrives inside it.

Artificial intelligence can scale outsourced skepticism by creating even more apparent validators. A fake platform can have analysts, customers, lawyers, executives, media mentions, ratings, and support staff, all generated or controlled by one operation. A victim looking for independent confirmation may unknowingly move through several pages of the same fiction.

Search and AI assistants will therefore need to become more sensitive to source independence. Ten pages repeating a claim are not ten sources if they all derive from one press release or one scam network. A trustworthy synthesis system should care about the graph behind the text.

Human investigators already do.

Who originated the claim?

Who independently verified it?

Who has money at risk?

Who has authority to certify the relevant fact?

These are due-diligence questions that AI can help answer but should not replace.

There is a policy implication too. Regulators should resist the temptation to certify whole technologies as legitimate or illegitimate. Regulatory registration, licensure, or compliance may establish specific facts. It should not become a halo. Criminals routinely exploit the appearance of government approval because citizens confuse an institution’s narrow jurisdiction with a general endorsement.

The same risk arises when legitimate companies advertise compliance. “Regulated” can mean many things. The user needs to know regulated for what.

Financial systems are full of such boundaries because money makes them unavoidable. A bank account, brokerage account, cryptocurrency wallet, payment app, and investment fund may each have different protections and legal structures even when they appear side by side on a phone.

The interface smooths the differences.

The law does not.

Crypto backlash was partly the discovery that digital interfaces can make fundamentally different forms of risk look equivalent.

AI will smooth many more boundaries. A generated answer can make an advertisement look like advice. A chatbot can make a vendor look like a professional. A synthetic executive can make a criminal look like an institution. The presentation becomes continuous while the underlying rights remain discontinuous.

This is why boring verification becomes more valuable as interfaces improve.

Ask what legal entity holds the asset.

Ask what authority the adviser has.

Ask what the regulator actually regulates.

Ask whether the endorsement is authenticated.

Ask what happens if the institution fails.

The person in shorts was easy to remember.

The balance sheet was harder.

Public pressure becomes useful when it learns to prefer the harder object.
