# The Regulators Arrive

Regulation usually arrives after the vocabulary.

First a technology is described by the people building it. Then by investors, journalists, critics, users, workers, and politicians. The words harden. Artificial intelligence becomes a category large enough to contain a spam filter, a medical image classifier, a hiring score, a chatbot, a military system, a recommendation engine, and a foundation model capable of generating software.

Then lawmakers try to regulate the category they have inherited.

This is one reason technology regulation can look clumsy even when the underlying concern is legitimate. The law needs definitions. The political argument arrives with metaphors.

The European Union’s AI Act was an ambitious attempt to turn the category into a risk architecture. The law entered into force in August 2024 and began applying in stages. Most prohibited-practice rules and AI-literacy duties began applying in February 2025. Obligations for providers of general-purpose AI models began applying in August 2025. On August 2, 2026, the AI Office and national authorities gained enforcement powers over provisions already in application, and Article 50 transparency requirements began applying to covered AI interactions and synthetic content. But the calendar had already changed again: Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, the Digital Omnibus on AI, entered into force in July 2026 and pushed the main high-risk-system rules for Annex III uses to December 2, 2027, while rules for high-risk systems embedded in regulated products are scheduled for August 2, 2028.

This staggered implementation tells a more useful story than the phrase “Europe regulates AI.”

The Act is not one switch.

It is a calendar of obligations.

Some uses are prohibited. Some systems face transparency duties. General-purpose model providers have obligations of their own. High-risk uses receive a separate regulatory structure. Enforcement is divided among European and national institutions. Technical standards, codes of practice, guidelines, amendments, and later deadlines fill in parts of the legal framework over time.

That complexity attracts criticism from both directions.

Industry argues that compliance can become costly, slow deployment, create uncertainty, and disadvantage European companies against less regulated competitors. Civil-society groups argue that exceptions, delayed implementation, weak enforcement, or narrow definitions may leave people exposed. Governments worry about strategic competition. Open-source communities worry that rules written around major firms may fall badly on smaller developers.

No comprehensive regulatory system avoids tradeoffs by being comprehensive.

It merely locates them in more places.

The United States took a different path.

Federal agencies applied existing law to specific uses while presidential policy shifted with administrations. The Biden administration’s 2023 executive order emphasized safety, civil rights, government standards, and reporting across a broad set of AI issues. President Donald Trump revoked that order in January 2025 and directed a policy focused on removing barriers to U.S. AI leadership. The White House’s July 2025 AI Action Plan organized more than ninety federal actions around accelerating innovation, building infrastructure, and international leadership.

By 2026, the contrast with Europe was politically clear even though the operational reality remained more complicated.

The United States did not become an AI-law vacuum.

The Federal Trade Commission still had consumer-protection law. The Federal Communications Commission still regulated communications. Employment discrimination remained illegal. Securities fraud remained illegal. Health and financial systems remained governed by sector-specific law. States and cities enacted their own rules. Courts continued hearing disputes. Procurement rules constrained government use.

The country had less of a single AI code and more of a regulatory patchwork.

Patchwork is often used as an insult.

Sometimes it is a feature.

A health regulator understands clinical workflows better than a new central AI agency might. A financial regulator understands custody, capital, disclosure, and market manipulation. An employment agency understands discrimination law. A communications regulator understands robocalls and carrier duties. The same model can appear in all those sectors, but the legal interest changes with the deployment.

Sectoral regulation asks what the system is doing to people.

Horizontal AI regulation asks what kind of system it is.

A mature framework may need both.

The risk of sectoral regulation is inconsistency. The same underlying technology can face very different requirements depending on industry. Gaps appear where no regulator has obvious jurisdiction. Agencies can lack technical expertise. Companies operating nationally face state-by-state requirements.

The risk of horizontal regulation is abstraction. Rules can become detached from actual workflows. A model provider may be required to produce extensive documentation that does not answer the hospital’s question about who reviews a denial. A risk category may place unlike systems together because they share a technical feature.

The public-pressure question is not which architecture is philosophically pure.

It is which one produces recourse.

Rite Aid’s facial-recognition case shows why existing institutions can matter. The Federal Trade Commission alleged that the retailer deployed facial-recognition surveillance across hundreds of stores without reasonable safeguards, leading to false matches and customers being wrongly accused of shoplifting or other wrongdoing. The 2024 court order prohibited Rite Aid from using facial recognition for security or surveillance for five years and imposed safeguards if it later returned to biometric surveillance.

The case was not built around a general theory of artificial intelligence.

It was built around unfair practices, biometric surveillance, vendor oversight, data security, and concrete harms.

That specificity made the remedy legible.

Stop using the technology for a period.

Notify affected people in certain circumstances.

Delete certain data and algorithms.

Implement safeguards.

Have senior executives oversee information security.

The public can understand what changes after the enforcement action.

Compare this with a company announcing a responsible-AI principle.

Principles matter most before enforcement. Orders matter after.

The difference is coercive authority.

A regulator can require something the company would not have chosen voluntarily.

This is why industry self-governance and law should not be treated as substitutes. A company can create sophisticated risk frameworks because it knows its systems best. Government can impose minimum duties because the company’s incentives are not identical to the public’s.

The relationship is adversarial and complementary.

Regulators need company expertise.

Companies need external constraints to make some commitments credible.

This is particularly true for competitive races. A company may genuinely prefer stronger safety testing but fear that delaying a product gives a rival an advantage. A binding rule can coordinate competitors around a floor. The rule becomes valuable to the company precisely because it removes the option to defect.

This is the strongest argument frontier AI executives can make when they request regulation.

The weakest version is asking government to codify practices large incumbents already perform, while making entry more expensive for everyone else.

The difference depends on regulatory design.

Licensing is the clearest example. A license can ensure that certain high-risk activities occur only under supervision, as society does with aviation, medicine, securities, nuclear facilities, and other domains. It can also become a moat. If obtaining the license requires enormous legal expense, compute disclosures, or compliance infrastructure, only incumbents survive.

The policy should therefore ask whether the regulated activity is dangerous because of scale, capability, use, or merely because it is labeled AI.

Thresholds matter.

So do exemptions.

So does the ability to revise the rule when the technology moves.

The EU AI Act attempts this through differentiated categories and special treatment for general-purpose models. The U.S. has relied more heavily on sectoral authorities and policy instruments. Neither model has reached a stable endpoint.

That instability is not evidence of failure.

It is what early governance looks like.

The mistake is expecting regulation to predict the technology.

Good regulation often governs obligations rather than implementations.

Do not discriminate unlawfully.

Do not deceive consumers.

Maintain records.

Protect sensitive data.

Provide notice when a synthetic interaction creates material risk of deception.

Allow qualified human review for certain consequential decisions.

Test systems before using them in defined high-risk contexts.

Report serious incidents.

These obligations can survive changes in model architecture better than rules tied to a particular technical method.

The political conflict appears when obligations themselves are contested.

How much transparency is enough?

What qualifies as high risk?

Should open-source model developers bear the same duties as commercial deployers?

When does safety testing become compelled disclosure of trade secrets?

Can the federal government preempt state AI laws?

Which restrictions burden speech?

How should national-security systems be treated?

What does due process require when a model informs a government benefit decision?

These are not questions engineers can optimize away.

They are governance.

Public pressure can improve the answers by supplying cases regulators would otherwise miss. Patients describe prior-authorization failures. Workers describe automated discipline. Artists document unauthorized replicas. Businesses report fraud patterns. Researchers find discriminatory outputs. Journalists expose internal policies.

The regulator translates stories into categories.

That translation is dangerous because categories can distort.

If ten vivid stories produce a broad prohibition, society may overreact. If a company demands statistically perfect proof before any intervention, society may wait through avoidable harm. Regulation operates between anecdote and certainty.

This is why pilots, reporting duties, sunset clauses, staged implementation, and regulatory sandboxes can be useful. They create ways to act without claiming omniscience.

The European AI Act’s phased calendar is partly an acknowledgment of this problem. Rules need standards, institutions, and implementation guidance before enforcement can be meaningful. Companies need time to adapt. Regulators need staff.

A law without administrative capacity is a press release.

The same is true in the United States. Congress can prohibit a practice, but an agency needs investigators, technical expertise, lawyers, data, and procedures. State attorneys general need resources. Courts need cases. Rules need regulated entities capable of understanding what is required.

Public anger tends to focus on the statutory moment because legislation is visible.

Implementation is where the law becomes real.

This creates a paradox for politicians. They receive credit for passing a bill and blame for regulatory burdens. The slow work of building a competent agency produces fewer headlines. Technology companies therefore spend heavily on the implementation stage, where definitions and guidance can determine actual cost.

Public-interest groups need to be there too.

Otherwise the most sophisticated participants in the rulemaking process will be the organizations being regulated.

This is not proof of corruption. It is an information asymmetry.

The regulated company has engineers, lawyers, economists, lobbyists, product data, and a direct financial stake in every paragraph. An ordinary applicant harmed by a hiring tool has a job search.

Democratic regulation needs institutions that aggregate the applicant’s interests without requiring the applicant to become a professional rulemaker.

Unions, consumer groups, civil-rights organizations, industry associations, academic researchers, standards bodies, and public-interest technologists can help fill that role.

Public pressure becomes durable when it creates administrative expertise.

That is less exciting than a protest.

It matters more ten years later.

The deepest regulatory lesson of the AI backlash is that responsibility should follow control.

Model providers control some things.

Deployers control others.

Professional users control others.

Infrastructure providers control others.

Government controls legal permissions and public deployments.

The affected person controls least of all.

A regulatory system should not solve that asymmetry by assigning every obligation to the largest company simply because it is visible. Nor should it fragment responsibility until every actor can point elsewhere.

Shared systems need allocated duties.

A provider may owe documentation and testing.

A deployer may owe validation in context and a meaningful appeal.

A professional may retain responsibility for a signed decision.

An intermediary may owe authentication or anti-fraud controls.

A regulator may owe procedural rights and oversight.

This is not elegant.

Accountability rarely is.

The desire for one AI regulator, one global standard, one safety test, or one liability rule comes partly from the human preference for a single place where responsibility lives. The technology resists because general-purpose systems spread across institutions that already have their own law.

The regulator arrives and discovers the same problem as the public.

There is no single machine to regulate.

There is a chain of decisions.

The best rule is the one that makes each link visible enough that no participant can honestly say the chain belonged to someone else.

The next regulatory problem is not writing the rule.

It is deciding how the rule learns.

Technology policy often imagines regulation as a moment of classification. A legislature defines a category. An agency publishes a standard. The regulated company either complies or does not.

AI systems complicate this because the object can change after approval. A model provider updates weights. A deployer changes a prompt. A vendor adds retrieval from a new database. A business turns a recommendation into an automated action. A user population changes. A model that performed acceptably at launch can drift in context even if the model itself is unchanged.

Static compliance can therefore coexist with dynamic risk.

The regulatory system needs change control.

When does a modification require retesting?

Who decides whether a new model version is materially different?

What happens when a third-party vendor silently updates a service used in a high-stakes workflow?

Can a hospital or employer remain compliant if it cannot freeze the version it validated?

Do contracts require advance notice of material changes?

These questions sound technical until a regulator tries to enforce the rule against a system that no longer exists in the form originally inspected.

Versioning becomes legal infrastructure.

The same is true of incident reporting. A regulator that only inspects before deployment sees the institution on its best day. Post-market monitoring matters because real users discover combinations that predeployment testing misses.

This is familiar from medicine, aviation, finance, and product safety. Approval is not the end of supervision. Adverse events, recalls, suspicious activity reports, audits, examinations, and mandatory notifications create feedback after the system enters the world.

AI needs analogous mechanisms calibrated to actual risk.

Not every wrong chatbot answer belongs in a government database. Serious failures in consequential systems may.

The design question is how to define reportable incidents without flooding regulators with noise or encouraging companies to classify serious events downward.

One answer is to tie reporting to consequences rather than embarrassment.

Did the event cause or create a substantial risk of physical harm?

Did it produce a material financial loss?

Did it affect a large class of people?

Did it expose protected data?

Did it circumvent a required safeguard?

Did the same failure recur after the organization said it was fixed?

Consequence-based reporting is imperfect. It gives the rule an object beyond the adjective “AI.”

Regulators also need the ability to compare institutions.

A single company may tell a plausible story about its error rate. An agency overseeing a sector can see whether one deployer produces ten times the appeal rate of peers, whether one model version appears repeatedly in incidents, or whether one vendor’s documentation fails across multiple customers.

This comparative view is a form of public intelligence unavailable to individual consumers.

It is one of the strongest reasons to fund competent agencies.

A patient sees one denial.

A regulator can see ten thousand.

An employee sees one vendor.

A regulator can see the same vendor across employers.

A company sees its own fraud losses.

A financial supervisor can see the attack pattern across institutions.

Scale can make regulators smarter if the data arrive in usable form.

It can also make regulators dangerous if they become overconfident in averages.

The person harmed by a rare failure still needs a route to individual remedy. Aggregate oversight and case-level recourse serve different functions. One detects patterns. The other corrects particulars.

This is why a mature AI regulatory architecture needs both.

The state itself creates a further accountability problem when it becomes the deployer.

Governments will use AI for translation, case management, fraud detection, intelligence analysis, tax administration, benefits, licensing, procurement, law enforcement, and internal productivity. The public should resist a convenient asymmetry in which government imposes strict duties on private actors while treating its own deployments as exceptional.

The government may legitimately need different rules in national security or law enforcement. Different does not mean unaccountable.

Public deployment raises some of the strongest due-process questions because the citizen often cannot exit the relationship. A customer can choose another retailer. A person cannot choose another tax authority.

The harder exit becomes, the stronger the case for notice, records, review, and appeal.

Government procurement can become a powerful regulatory tool precisely because public agencies are buyers as well as rule makers. A procurement contract can require logs, audit access, version notice, incident reporting, data restrictions, accessibility, and termination rights before a general statute reaches every market.

This is regulation through purchasing power.

It can improve standards because vendors often prefer one robust product to maintaining a separate government version. It can also entrench large suppliers that are able to navigate procurement complexity.

Again, design creates distributional effects.

Small-company exemptions deserve the same nuance. A startup with ten employees cannot maintain the compliance department of a global platform. That does not mean a startup should be able to deploy a dangerous system without controls merely because it is small.

The burden should scale with risk and capacity where possible.

A low-risk small business using AI for internal drafting should not inherit the compliance architecture of a frontier laboratory.

A small company operating a high-stakes medical or financial system may still need serious safeguards because the patient or customer does not experience harm at startup scale.

Risk is not measured by headcount alone.

The regulatory system therefore needs proportionality without loopholes.

This is hard to legislate in a sentence. It is what competent administration is for.

The public often wants regulation to resolve distrust immediately.

A statute cannot do that.

The institution earns trust when people see that rules are enforced against allies as well as enemies, that regulators can admit mistakes, that small actors are not crushed merely because compliance is easier for incumbents, that serious harms produce remedies, and that useful innovation is allowed to continue.

Good regulation is not maximum restriction.

It is credible discrimination among risks.

That may be the most difficult task because AI has become a single cultural category while governance needs many categories underneath it.

The regulator arrives after the backlash and has to do the unpopular work of separating them.

Which anger points to fraud?

Which points to bad service?

Which points to discrimination?

Which points to a labor bargain?

Which points to a privacy problem?

Which points to a dangerous capability?

Which points to ordinary technological change that the law should not stop?

The answer will never satisfy everyone.

That is not the standard.

The standard is whether the institution can show its work, revise the rule, and give the affected person somewhere to go.

Regulation is public pressure after it has learned to keep records.
