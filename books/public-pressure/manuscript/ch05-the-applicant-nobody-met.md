# The Applicant Nobody Met

The rejection email is polite because politeness is cheap.

Thank you for your interest. We appreciate the time you invested. We received many qualified applications. We have decided to move forward with other candidates.

The applicant may never know whether another human being read the résumé.

That uncertainty has existed for years. Large employers were using applicant-tracking systems and automated filters long before generative AI became a household term. The basic problem is scale. A desirable job can attract hundreds or thousands of applicants. Recruiters cannot give every application a slow reading. They search, rank, filter, test, and route.

Software is the obvious tool.

What changed with the AI backlash was not the discovery that computers participate in hiring. It was the growing realization that hiring technology can do more than organize a queue. It can shape who gets considered at all.

An employer may use software to place ads, scan résumés, score assessments, recommend candidates, conduct video interviews, schedule interviews, detect supposed fraud, rank employees for promotion, or identify workers considered likely to quit. Different products use different techniques. Some are simple rules. Some use machine learning. Some vendors advertise sophisticated inference about personality, skills, or fit.

The affected person usually sees one thing: a portal.

This makes automated hiring a useful case study in public pressure because the person excluded may have almost no evidence that exclusion occurred.

A patient denied coverage receives a denial. A worker fired by an app notices the loss of access. An applicant filtered out at the top of a funnel may receive nothing that distinguishes an algorithmic screen from ordinary competition.

The harm, if there is one, is invisible by design.

Civil-rights law does not become optional merely because the decision occurs invisibly. The U.S. Department of Justice has warned that hiring technologies can discriminate against people with disabilities. The Equal Employment Opportunity Commission has emphasized that existing federal employment-discrimination law applies when employers use automated systems. New York City’s Local Law 144 added a more explicit local requirement: certain automated employment decision tools cannot be used unless they have undergone a bias audit, the audit information is made public, and required notices are provided.

The laws differ in scope and legal force. Together they reveal the institutional anxiety.

The employer buys a tool to reduce human inconsistency.

The law asks whether the tool has industrialized it.

This is not a theoretical concern. Predictive hiring often learns from history because history is the available data. A company may want to identify applicants who resemble successful employees. That sounds reasonable until the composition of successful employees reflects decades of unequal access, accommodation failures, occupational segregation, informal networks, or biased promotion.

The model can become very good at reproducing the organization that already exists.

No racist instruction is required. No sexist variable needs to be named. A system can use proxies. It can learn patterns correlated with protected characteristics. It can optimize for retention in a workplace that has been hostile to certain groups. It can interpret a disability-related speech pattern or movement as low performance if a video tool was built around a narrow behavioral norm.

Automation changes the form of discrimination because intent becomes less central to the public story.

People tend to imagine bias as a person with a prejudice. Law has never been limited to that simple picture. Certain employment practices can be unlawful because of their effects even when discriminatory intent is not established. Automated systems make the difference easier to understand. A model has no personal hatred. It can still create a systematically unequal result.

The chief executive is far from the decision. So is the software developer at the vendor. Responsibility exists anyway.

An employer chose to use the tool.

That sentence is the accountability anchor.

Vendors sometimes market automation as if purchasing software transfers responsibility. It does not. The Department of Justice guidance on disability discrimination is explicit that an employer can be responsible when it uses another company’s discriminatory hiring technology. The choice to outsource a decision process is still a choice about how the employer makes decisions.

This principle will become more important as companies adopt general-purpose AI systems built by outside providers. A manager may paste résumés into a model, use an AI assistant to summarize interviews, or ask a system to rank candidates. The employer may not control the model’s training data. It still controls whether the model receives authority.

Authority is the key distinction.

A recruiter can use software to find a missing date on a résumé without delegating judgment. A tool can help schedule interviews without deciding who deserves one. An AI assistant can summarize notes while a hiring committee reviews the underlying material. These are different uses from a system that removes candidates from consideration.

Public policy often fails when it treats them as one category.

The phrase “AI in hiring” can refer to nearly everything between spell-checking and autonomous selection. A blanket ban would sacrifice useful tools. A blanket permission would ignore high-stakes delegation. The better question is where the technology alters opportunity.

Opportunity is difficult to audit because the counterfactual person is missing.

If a model rejects an applicant who would have become an exceptional employee, nobody may ever know. The employer cannot measure the productivity of the person it never hired. This creates a measurement trap. Systems are often evaluated using outcomes among selected candidates, but selection itself determines whose outcomes become visible.

The pipeline teaches the model with its own decisions.

This is a version of the feedback problem that appears throughout automated institutions. A police deployment algorithm sends officers to places where it expects crime, generating more recorded incidents there. A recommendation system shows people content they are likely to engage with, then uses the engagement as evidence that the recommendation was right. A hiring system selects candidates who resemble successful incumbents and then observes that selected candidates resemble successful incumbents.

The loop can be self-confirming.

Public pressure enters when outsiders ask for a view of the people who disappeared.

Bias audits are one attempt. They examine selection rates across demographic groups or test other aspects of tool behavior. Audits can reveal patterns that individual applicants cannot see. They also have limits. The result depends on what data exist, which groups are measured, how the tool is defined, how the audit is conducted, and whether the tested deployment matches the system in actual use.

An audit is evidence, not absolution.

This distinction matters because “audited” can become a new form of marketing language. A company may treat the completion of a required process as proof that the system is fair. The meaningful question is what the audit found and what changed afterward.

The same problem appears in model safety. Companies run evaluations and publish selected results. Regulators ask for testing. Users hear that a system has been “red teamed.” These practices can be valuable. They can also become ceremonies unless failures create consequences.

Accountability requires a link between measurement and authority.

If the audit finds a problem, who can stop the deployment?

That question is much harder than whether an audit exists.

Applicants face another problem: they may not know they need an accommodation. A person with a disability can ask for reasonable accommodation in a hiring process, but the request requires enough information to identify the barrier. If the employer uses a video assessment that analyzes speech or facial behavior without clearly explaining what is being measured, the applicant may not know what to request.

Transparency therefore has to arrive before the decision.

A notice after rejection is too late if the technology itself made the application inaccessible.

This is a useful general rule for high-stakes AI. Disclosure has value only when it enables action.

Telling a person that AI was used somewhere in the process may satisfy curiosity. It does not necessarily create control. A useful disclosure explains enough about the role of the system that the person can make a meaningful choice, challenge an error, or request an alternative process.

The difference is between decorative transparency and operational transparency.

Decorative transparency says, “We use AI to improve your experience.”

Operational transparency says, “This assessment contributes to the decision, these are the abilities it is designed to measure, you may request an accommodation, and a human reviewer can reconsider the result.”

The second form is harder because it exposes the institution to contest.

That is precisely why it matters.

Organizations often fear that explanations will create more appeals. They are correct. If people understand how decisions are made, some will challenge them. Appeals cost money. Human review costs money. Documentation costs money.

Opacity is efficient if the affected person bears the cost of mistakes.

Public pressure changes that allocation by making opacity expensive in other ways.

A lawsuit can be expensive. A regulatory investigation can be expensive. A discrimination finding can be expensive. Losing trust among applicants can be expensive. So can employee dissatisfaction when workers discover that an opaque system is influencing schedules, performance ratings, promotions, or termination.

The economic case for accountable automation is partly a matter of moving costs earlier.

Pay for review before scandal.

Pay for accommodation before litigation.

Pay for validation before a pattern becomes institutional.

This is not a guarantee against criticism. It is a way of reducing the number of grievances that have no internal route.

The hiring case also reveals why the CEO-as-interface model has limits. A chief executive may publicly promise fairness while the real decisions are distributed among procurement, human resources, hiring managers, outside vendors, and compliance teams. The company can mean the promise and still fail to operationalize it.

High-level values are weak controls.

A company that says it values diversity needs to know whether its selection tools change selection rates. A company that says it values disability inclusion needs to test whether its application process works with assistive technology. A company that says humans make final decisions needs to know whether humans routinely rubber-stamp model recommendations.

The public does not need another principle until the institution can answer those questions.

This is why worker involvement matters. The people closest to the workflow often know where nominal safeguards fail. A recruiter knows whether managers trust a score too much. An applicant-support specialist knows which accommodation requests are common. A hiring manager knows whether “recommendation” really means “do not override unless you want a meeting.”

Public policy often discusses AI governance as a conversation among engineers, executives, lawyers, and regulators. Workers belong in it because they operate the exceptions.

Exceptions reveal the true system.

A formal process tells you what should happen. An exception tells you who has power.

If a recruiter can override a score without punishment, human discretion is real. If a manager can request a second review, the workflow has resilience. If an applicant can get a human assessment after a technical failure, accessibility is more than a statement.

The backlash against automated hiring is partly a backlash against discovering that many organizations cannot describe their own exception paths.

This can create overreaction. Not every automated screen is sinister. A rule that checks whether a nurse has a required license is not a moral crisis. A system that prevents an applicant from submitting duplicate applications may be useful. A scheduling assistant can save everyone time.

The danger is categorical thinking in both directions.

Critics say, “A machine should never decide who gets a job.” Employers say, “Automation makes hiring more objective.” Both claims are too large.

The right answer depends on what the machine does, what evidence it uses, how performance is validated, which humans remain accountable, and what happens when the result is contested.

This is less satisfying than a slogan.

It is also more governable.

The applicant nobody met exposes a structural difference between public pressure and private error. The applicant may never know that the system made a mistake. There is no dramatic denial to post. The person simply disappears from the employer’s future.

That makes statistical oversight unusually important.

Individual appeal cannot detect harms that individuals cannot observe.

Institutions need aggregate checks: selection patterns, accommodation requests, error analysis, audits, vendor testing, and comparisons across versions. Regulators need the power to ask for evidence when a pattern suggests discrimination. Researchers and journalists need enough access to test claims. Workers need protection when they report problems.

Public pressure in this domain therefore looks less like a viral crowd and more like institutional surveillance of the surveillant.

Someone has to watch the system that watches applicants.

The irony is that employers often adopt hiring automation because human hiring is already flawed. Interviews reward confidence that may not predict performance. Résumés encode access to prestigious institutions. Referral networks reproduce social networks. Recruiters are inconsistent. Managers fall in love with candidates who resemble themselves. Human judgment is not a clean baseline.

The case for technology is real.

So is the requirement that improvement be demonstrated rather than assumed.

If an automated tool reduces one form of bias while creating another, the tradeoff should be visible. If it predicts job performance better than a résumé screen, that is useful evidence. If it merely reduces recruiter time, the company should not market efficiency as fairness.

The language matters because AI systems arrive surrounded by adjectives: objective, intelligent, predictive, data-driven, unbiased, scalable.

These words can cause people inside organizations to defer before they have seen the evidence.

Public pressure can perform a valuable function by making the adjectives expensive.

Show the test.

Show the error.

Show the appeal.

Show who can stop the system.

The employer does not owe every applicant a job. It owes applicants compliance with the law and a process that does not hide discriminatory practice behind a vendor’s interface.

That is a smaller promise than fairness in the abstract.

It is also a promise an institution can actually keep.

The applicant nobody met may never become a public critic. The person may assume somebody else was better qualified and move on.

That silence is not evidence that the system worked.

In automated institutions, the quietest people are often the ones the system removed before they could complain.

The employer’s most difficult evidentiary problem begins with procurement. A hiring team can evaluate a candidate because the candidate is in front of it. Evaluating the tool requires information from a vendor whose commercial incentive is to make the tool look useful. Procurement therefore becomes an accountability function whether or not anyone in procurement thinks of the job that way.

What exactly is the product predicting?

How was the target variable defined?

Which populations were represented in development data?

What validation exists for jobs like ours rather than jobs in general?

What happens when the employer changes the workflow around the product?

Can we obtain enough information to investigate a complaint later?

Can the vendor change the model without telling us?

Can we export logs if the relationship ends?

A contract that answers none of these questions can turn an employment decision into a black-box dependency before the first applicant sees the portal.

Vendor claims deserve the same skepticism employers apply to candidates. “Science-backed,” “objective,” “predictive,” and “bias reducing” are propositions, not product categories. A buyer should ask what study supports the claim, who conducted it, what outcome was measured, and whether the result survives in the buyer’s actual setting.

This matters because validity can be local. A system that predicts performance in one role may not predict it in another. A test that works well when applicants have stable broadband may behave differently when administered on older phones. A model trained on one language or labor market may fail when used elsewhere.

Scale encourages organizations to forget this because software looks portable.

The interface works everywhere.

The evidence may not.

The same issue appears after deployment. Suppose a company buys a screening tool, validates it responsibly, and finds acceptable results. Two years later the labor market changes, the applicant pool changes, the company redesigns the job, and the vendor updates the model. The original validation has become historical evidence.

Automated hiring therefore needs monitoring, not merely predeployment certification.

A bias audit performed once is a photograph. Employment systems are movies.

This is one reason audit requirements can disappoint activists who expect them to produce a binary answer about fairness. The most valuable function of an audit may be creating a repeated measurement practice. Once a company knows it must look again, the system is less likely to become invisible infrastructure.

The measurement should include the funnel, not just the final hire.

Who saw the job advertisement?

Who began the application?

Who completed it?

Who passed each screen?

Who received an interview?

Who received an offer?

Who accepted?

Who remained long enough to generate the “successful employee” outcome used in later models?

Each stage can filter differently.

A company that studies only final hires can miss the mechanism that produced them.

This also reveals why accommodations should not be isolated in a compliance office. Accessibility is part of system quality. If an assessment excludes qualified applicants because it measures an irrelevant physical, sensory, or linguistic characteristic, the employer is not merely creating legal risk. It is degrading the search for talent.

The argument for accommodation is therefore both legal and operational.

A strong hiring system measures what the job actually requires.

That sounds obvious until proxy variables arrive. Employers often use degrees, prior employers, gaps in employment, commuting distance, assessment speed, communication style, or other signals because they correlate with something valuable. Each proxy can also encode unrelated social structure.

AI can discover proxies humans never would have chosen explicitly.

This is part of its power.

It is also why explanation matters at the feature and workflow level. A model can be predictive without the employer having a defensible reason to use every predictive signal. Employment law and organizational ethics can reject some correlations even when they improve prediction.

Prediction is not permission.

That sentence travels beyond hiring.

A bank may predict default and still be restricted in which information it can use. An insurer may predict health outcomes and still face coverage rules. A government may predict fraud and still owe due process. The fact that a variable improves a model does not automatically make it legitimate input to institutional power.

Hiring makes this unusually clear because employers routinely decide which qualifications count. AI does not remove the normative choice. It buries it one layer earlier, in data and target construction.

Public pressure should therefore ask employers to describe the job before defending the model.

What capability is actually needed?

Why is this assessment relevant to it?

What evidence connects the score to performance?

What alternative routes exist for people the assessment does not measure well?

These questions protect applicants and improve hiring at the same time.

The final difficulty is that most people who are excluded will never complain. The company cannot treat complaint volume as the main safety signal. It needs affirmative sampling and audit.

Take rejected candidates at random and examine whether the recorded reason is supportable.

Test the system with applicants using assistive technologies.

Compare outcomes when recruiters see model recommendations and when they do not.

Study override behavior.

Look for sudden distribution changes after a vendor update.

These practices cost money because accountability costs money.

The alternative is a labor market in which invisible filters shape opportunity while every rejected person assumes the decision was made by somebody else.

The applicant nobody met should not need to become a plaintiff before the employer learns whether its own gate works.
