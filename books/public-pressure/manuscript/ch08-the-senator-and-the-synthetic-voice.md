# The Senator and the Synthetic Voice

The call sounded like the president.

Two days before the New Hampshire presidential primary in January 2024, some voters received a robocall carrying what appeared to be Joe Biden’s voice. The message urged them to save their votes for the November election rather than participate in the primary. Biden had not recorded it.

The voice was generated with artificial intelligence.

That much made the episode feel new. The rest was almost antique.

A political operative wanted to influence voter behavior. A script was written. A list of phone numbers was assembled. A telecommunications provider carried the calls. Caller identification was manipulated. Election law, consumer-protection rules, communications regulation, and civil-rights law already contained tools for dealing with deception and voter interference.

The AI did not invent the scheme.

It made the impersonation cheap enough to fit inside it.

The Federal Communications Commission eventually fined political consultant Steve Kramer six million dollars for orchestrating the illegal spoofed robocalls. The agency said the campaign used an unauthorized deepfake of Biden’s voice and caller-ID spoofing to spread election misinformation to prospective New Hampshire voters. Lingo Telecom, which transmitted the calls, separately reached a settlement with the FCC that included a civil penalty and compliance obligations. New Hampshire prosecutors also brought state charges against Kramer.

The enforcement sequence matters because it answers a question that often becomes confused in AI politics.

Does a new technical method require an entirely new legal system?

Sometimes it does. New technology can expose gaps. But the first question should be more ordinary: what conduct occurred?

The conduct in New Hampshire included deceptive robocalling and spoofing in an election context. The law already had opinions about those things.

In February 2024, shortly after the incident, the FCC issued a declaratory ruling confirming that the Telephone Consumer Protection Act’s restrictions on calls using an artificial or prerecorded voice apply to AI-generated voices. The ruling did not create a general ban on voice synthesis. It interpreted an existing category—artificial or prerecorded voice—to include a newer way of producing one.

This is the kind of legal adaptation that rarely receives the attention of a grand AI bill because it looks almost boring.

Boring is useful.

If every AI-enabled wrong is treated as categorically unprecedented, existing law becomes weaker than it needs to be. Fraud becomes “AI fraud.” Harassment becomes “AI harassment.” Discrimination becomes “algorithmic discrimination.” Election interference becomes “deepfake election interference.” The adjective can make institutions hesitate while everyone waits for a specialized statute.

The victim experiences the noun.

A voter was deceived.

A consumer was impersonated.

A worker was discriminated against.

A person lost money.

Technology can change the evidence, scale, cost, and available defenses without changing the underlying interest society is protecting.

This principle is important in politics because synthetic media arrived inside a field already built on strategic representation.

Campaigns select flattering photographs. Advertisements cut quotations. Opposition research assembles unflattering facts. Satire impersonates politicians. Actors portray presidents on television. Political cartoons distort appearance on purpose. Speechwriters place words in candidates’ mouths with permission. Surrogates tell stories that simplify opponents. Modern democracy has never required political communication to resemble a neutral laboratory report.

The public knows that campaigns persuade.

Deepfakes create a different problem because they can counterfeit the evidence on which persuasion rests.

A voter can disagree with a quotation and still assume that the person said it. A synthetic recording attacks that assumption.

This is why the most useful policy distinction is not real versus manipulated in the abstract. Almost all media is manipulated in some sense. The distinction is whether the manipulation materially misrepresents the identity, speech, or conduct of a real person in a context where the audience is likely to treat it as evidence.

That sounds straightforward until satire arrives.

Political satire depends on exaggeration, imitation, parody, and obvious fabrication. A law drafted too broadly can suppress protected speech. A rule requiring labels on every synthetic alteration might cover ordinary editing. A prohibition on realistic depictions can run into the First Amendment. A platform trying to detect deepfakes can remove legitimate commentary or fail to detect malicious material.

The difficult policy work begins after everyone agrees that deceptive impersonation is bad.

The New Hampshire call provides a relatively easy case because the mechanism was direct. The message purported to come from Biden and told voters not to participate in an imminent election. There was no need to solve the entire philosophy of synthetic media to ask whether robocall and caller-ID laws applied.

Harder cases live online.

A video may be authentic footage with synthetic audio. A photograph may be real except for an inserted object. A campaign may generate an image of a hypothetical event and label it. A satirist may create an obvious parody. A foreign influence operation may blend real documents with fabricated narration. A candidate may falsely claim that genuine damaging footage is AI-generated.

That last possibility is the liar’s dividend.

When believable fakes exist, real evidence becomes easier to deny.

This may become the more consequential political effect of synthetic media. A democracy does not need every voter to believe a fake. It only needs enough uncertainty that partisans can select whichever reality is useful.

The old evidentiary shortcut—“I saw the video”—weakens.

That is not the same as saying video becomes useless. Courts, news organizations, campaigns, platforms, and researchers already authenticate material through sources, metadata, corroboration, chain of custody, geolocation, original files, witness testimony, and other methods. Synthetic media makes these practices more important.

The public-pressure problem is that verification is slower than accusation.

A fake can be produced before breakfast. Authentication may require specialists. By the time a news organization establishes that a clip is false, millions of people may have seen it. Corrections often travel through a different audience than the original claim.

AI therefore changes the temporal structure of political accountability.

The accused person must answer before the evidence is settled.

Politicians are already unusually exposed to this because they are expected to respond to public events quickly. Silence can be interpreted as admission. A campaign that waits for forensic verification may surrender the news cycle. A campaign that immediately declares “deepfake” can exploit uncertainty even if the material is genuine.

The public becomes the appeals court before the record exists.

One response is provenance: systems that attach information about where media came from and what edits occurred. Standards such as Content Credentials can help establish a chain of information about creation and modification when tools and platforms participate. Watermarking can provide signals for certain generated content. Cryptographic signatures can authenticate official material.

None solves the whole problem.

A provenance system can establish that a participating device signed a file. It cannot prove that the scene itself was honest. A real camera can record a staged event. A signed campaign video can contain misleading statistics. A malicious actor can strip metadata. Screenshots and re-encodings can break signals. Open-source tools may ignore industry standards.

Truth cannot be reduced to a file format.

Still, provenance can change the burden of proof.

An official campaign could publish authenticated versions of important speeches. News organizations could preserve originals. Government agencies could sign emergency communications. Platforms could distinguish known-origin material from files whose origin is unclear without automatically declaring the latter false.

That is a more modest promise than deepfake detection.

Detection tries to infer how a file was made from the file itself. Provenance tries to preserve evidence of how it was made.

Both have value. Provenance has the advantage of not requiring tomorrow’s detector to recognize tomorrow’s generator perfectly.

The political system also has a social provenance layer: trusted intermediaries.

Election officials issue notices. Campaigns confirm whether a recording is theirs. Journalists contact participants. Political parties authenticate communications. Courts take evidence under rules. Opposing campaigns can sometimes agree that a fabrication is fabrication even while fighting over everything else.

Public trust in these intermediaries is uneven, which is exactly why synthetic media is politically attractive.

A fake does not have to convince everyone. It can deepen suspicion of the institutions that authenticate it.

This turns AI into an accelerant for an older crisis of authority.

The person creating the fake says the media are lying.

The target says the fake is misinformation.

The platform says it is enforcing a policy.

The partisan sees censorship.

The regulator sees an election-integrity problem.

The free-speech advocate sees a vague rule capable of abuse.

All of them can be describing a real risk.

The solution is not to pretend those risks are equivalent in every case. It is to design narrow interventions around conduct that can be described precisely.

Unauthorized robocalls using a cloned candidate voice are precise.

Knowingly distributing nonconsensual intimate deepfakes is precise.

Fraudulently impersonating an official to obtain money is precise.

A broad ban on “misinformation” is not.

The narrower the conduct, the easier it is to preserve political disagreement without licensing deception.

This lesson applies to disclosure too. A label can be useful when it answers a question the audience cares about. “This ad contains an AI-generated depiction of a candidate” conveys more than a generic badge saying “AI was used.” The second could apply because an editor used a transcription tool.

Disclosure should follow the material deception risk.

The public does not need to know every time a campaign used spell-checking.

It does need to know when a candidate appears to say something the candidate never said.

The same logic should constrain platforms. Content moderation around political deepfakes often becomes controversial because platforms are private institutions exercising speech power at enormous scale. Removing content may limit a malicious operation. It may also remove reporting, satire, or evidence of the manipulation itself.

Contextual labels, distribution limits, archival preservation, and rapid appeal can sometimes be more proportionate than deletion. In other cases, such as direct voter suppression or fraud, speed may matter enough to justify stronger action under platform rules or law.

There is no single “AI misinformation policy” that fits all speech.

The category is too large.

Public pressure tends to erase these distinctions because political coalitions want symmetrical rules. If our opponent used a fake, ban fakes. If our satire was removed, protect satire. If a platform labels us, punish platform bias. The principle often changes with the victim.

A durable rule has to survive partisan rotation.

That is one of the best tests of AI election policy.

Would you want the same authority used by the other party?

Would you want the rule enforced when the deepfake targets your candidate rather than theirs?

Would you accept the evidentiary standard if your own speech were accused of being synthetic?

This is not a call for false equivalence. Parties and candidates do not necessarily behave identically. It is a design constraint on state power.

Technology companies face the same constraint in private form. A platform policy written after one scandal will eventually apply in another political context. A rushed rule becomes a precedent.

The New Hampshire case also reveals the importance of infrastructure providers that are not household names.

The public sees the politician whose voice was cloned and the consultant who arranged the call. The call still had to pass through a telecommunications chain. FCC enforcement against Lingo Telecom focused partly on caller-ID authentication obligations and provider practices. The system had seams where accountability could attach.

This is a recurring pattern in digital governance.

Scams need payment rails.

Bots need platforms.

Robocalls need carriers.

Deepfakes need distribution.

Cryptocurrency fraud needs exchanges or wallets at some stage.

AI models need compute and interfaces.

The visible bad actor is not the only point where society can intervene.

Intermediary regulation is powerful because it can raise the cost of abuse across many actors at once. It is dangerous for the same reason. If a carrier, payment processor, cloud provider, or platform becomes responsible for policing too broad a category of conduct, private companies can become de facto regulators without due process.

The design question is where the intermediary has enough information and control to act reliably.

A telecom provider can authenticate caller ID more readily than it can decide whether every political message is true.

A payment platform can identify certain transaction patterns more readily than it can adjudicate every investment dispute.

A model provider can limit obvious impersonation features more readily than it can determine the legality of every downstream parody.

Assigning responsibility close to the controllable mechanism makes enforcement more credible.

This is how public pressure can mature. It begins with the visible offense: somebody cloned the president. It ends by asking which systems made the offense cheap, scalable, and believable, and which of those systems can reduce abuse without controlling legitimate speech.

That is a harder question than whether AI is dangerous to democracy.

It is also one democracy can answer in pieces.

The political class initially reacted to synthetic media with the language of emergency. That reaction was understandable. Elections operate on deadlines. A false claim released the day before voting cannot always be corrected by litigation six months later.

But emergency language can become its own risk. Governments are most tempted to regulate speech broadly when they believe the public cannot safely evaluate it.

A better approach preserves the voter as an adult.

Give the voter evidence about origin.

Punish direct fraud and intimidation.

Require useful disclosure in defined contexts.

Protect satire and criticism.

Create rapid remedies when election timing makes ordinary process useless.

Hold distribution infrastructure to duties it can actually perform.

Then let political argument remain political.

AI will make campaigns stranger. Candidates will use synthetic translations, personalized messages, rapid video production, data analysis, and automated outreach. Some uses will improve access. A candidate can speak to voters in languages the candidate does not personally speak, provided the translation is disclosed and accurate. People with disabilities can benefit from synthetic communication. Small campaigns can produce media once available only to large ones.

The same tools can deceive.

That is not a contradiction unique to AI. It is the normal structure of communication technology.

Printing can publish a constitution or a libel.

Radio can broadcast an emergency warning or propaganda.

Television can document a war or stage a spectacle.

Synthetic media lowers the cost of plausible fabrication while also lowering the cost of legitimate production.

The political task is to govern the abuse without granting incumbents ownership of reality.

The senator and the synthetic voice therefore belong in the same chapter because both are forms of representation.

The senator is authorized to speak for constituents through an election.

The synthetic voice pretends to be authorized to speak for a person through resemblance.

One authority is institutional.

The other is counterfeit.

AI makes the counterfeit more convincing.

It does not make the difference between them unknowable.

The work of public pressure is to insist that institutions preserve the evidence.

Election administration adds another layer because the most dangerous synthetic message may not be persuasion at all. It may be procedural instruction. A fake endorsement asks a voter what to believe. A false message about when, where, or whether to vote can interfere with the act of voting itself.

That difference should shape both urgency and remedy.

Election officials already maintain authoritative information about polling places, eligibility procedures, deadlines, and ballot administration. The synthetic-media response can strengthen those channels rather than build a parallel truth ministry. When a false procedural claim circulates, the answer can point voters to the office with actual authority over the procedure.

This is authority matching again.

A campaign is authoritative about whether it created an advertisement.

An election office is authoritative about how an election is administered.

A court is authoritative about a judgment.

A platform is authoritative about whether an account was verified under its own process.

A news organization is authoritative about whether it published a report.

The public gets into trouble when one authority is asked to certify another domain.

A social-media company should not become the final interpreter of state election law merely because a disputed post appeared on its service. It can route users toward official information, preserve evidence, enforce rules against impersonation, and respond to clearly unlawful conduct. The legal interpretation still belongs to institutions designed for it.

The same division helps campaigns prepare before a crisis. Candidates can establish verified channels for official audio and video. Staff can preserve original files and production records. Campaigns can decide in advance how they will respond to suspected synthetic material. They can identify outside forensic experts rather than finding them after a clip goes viral.

This is incident response for political speech.

Security teams have long learned that the worst time to design a breach process is during the breach. Elections need the same precommitment because the decision window is short.

What standard will the campaign use before calling something fake?

Who is authorized to make that statement?

Will the campaign publish the evidence supporting its denial when doing so does not compromise security?

How will it correct itself if the authenticity judgment changes?

These questions protect the target from deepfakes and the public from false deepfake claims.

The liar’s dividend becomes smaller when denial itself has a procedure.

News organizations can make similar precommitments. A newsroom receiving explosive audio late in a campaign should know what minimum authentication it requires before publication, what it can report while verification continues, and how it will label uncertainty. Competition makes this difficult because another outlet may publish first.

The synthetic era therefore increases the value of institutional reputation earned before the crisis.

A publication known to wait for verification can afford to say “we do not yet know.” A campaign known to authenticate its own media can rebut a fake more credibly. An election office known for clear public communication can correct procedural deception faster.

Trust becomes a stock accumulated before the run on truth.

Platforms occupy the hardest position because they operate at the speed and scale of the attack. A platform may have minutes to decide whether a manipulated clip should be removed, labeled, downranked, or left alone. Each choice affects speech and distribution.

The most defensible systems separate functions.

Automated tools can detect likely synthetic or coordinated material and trigger review. Human specialists can evaluate high-impact cases. Rules can distinguish impersonation from satire and procedural voter suppression from ordinary political falsehoods. Appeals can reverse mistaken interventions. Records can preserve removed material for later scrutiny.

No process will eliminate partisan accusations of bias.

The goal is not to make everyone believe the platform is neutral.

It is to make decisions reproducible enough that critics can argue about the rule rather than invent the rule after every case.

There is also a positive use of synthetic media that election regulation should not accidentally erase. A campaign may use a candidate-approved synthetic voice to translate a speech into another language or make material accessible to voters who would otherwise not receive it. The communication can be authentic in authority even if synthetic in production.

That distinction is important.

The candidate did not literally pronounce the words in that language.

The candidate did authorize the message.

Synthetic does not mean counterfeit.

Authorization is the missing variable in many political deepfake arguments.

A cartoon of a candidate does not claim authorization. A parody communicates its own distance. A campaign-approved synthetic translation carries authorization even though the signal is generated. A cloned robocall designed to make voters believe the candidate spoke when the candidate did not is counterfeit authority.

This is why provenance systems should be able to express more than “AI was used.” They should help establish who authorized the artifact and how it changed.

The voter still has to judge the message.

The infrastructure merely restores the proper speaker.

That is enough to preserve an essential democratic distinction. Politics is allowed to manipulate attention, emotion, narrative, and imagery within broad constitutional limits. It should not be allowed to make a person participate in speech they did not authorize and then demand that the public solve the forgery under deadline.

The New Hampshire call was small compared with the fears surrounding synthetic election interference. Its importance lies partly in that modest scale. The legal system did not need to wait for a national catastrophe before learning that an old robocall rule could reach a new kind of voice.

That is how resilient institutions should respond to AI.

Adapt at the seam where the conduct changed.

Preserve the larger freedoms around it.

And make the counterfeit prove its authority before the public has to prove it false.
