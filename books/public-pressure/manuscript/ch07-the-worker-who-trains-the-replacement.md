# The Worker Who Trains the Replacement

The most unsettling automation project may be the one that begins by asking for your help.

Document the process. Label the examples. Correct the output. Explain the exception. Record the call. Rate the response. Write the template. Build the knowledge base. Show the system how an expert does the job.

None of these requests necessarily means the worker will be replaced. They may make the worker more productive. They may reduce repetitive work. They may improve training for new employees. They may make a dangerous job safer or a frustrating process faster.

The worker cannot know from the request alone.

This uncertainty gives workplace AI backlash a different emotional structure from earlier waves of automation. A factory worker could often see the machine arriving. A robot occupied floor space. A new production line required installation. Office automation is less visible. It arrives as software already sitting in the tools employees use.

The first version is an assistant.

The second version may be a benchmark.

The third may become a staffing plan.

Workers have always transferred knowledge into organizations. Apprentices teach apprentices. Procedure manuals preserve expertise. Software codifies processes. A business that could only operate while one employee remembered every step was fragile long before AI.

What generative systems change is the amount of unstructured human work that can be absorbed into software.

A call-center employee’s conversations can become training data. A lawyer’s documents can become exemplars for drafting tools. A programmer’s code review can become a signal for code models. A support team’s resolved tickets can become a knowledge base that an AI assistant uses to answer future customers. A designer’s revisions can teach a system what the organization prefers.

The work produces the data needed to change the work.

This is not automatically exploitation. Learning from past performance is how organizations improve. The question is who has bargaining power over the productivity gain.

That is the labor question underneath most AI job forecasts.

Public debate likes to count jobs. How many disappear? How many are created? Which occupations are exposed? The International Labour Organization’s 2025 update estimated that one in four workers globally were in occupations with some degree of exposure to generative AI, while emphasizing that transformation of jobs was more likely than full automation for most occupations. Clerical work remained especially exposed.

The word exposure is useful because it avoids pretending that a technical capability maps directly onto unemployment.

A model can perform a task without eliminating the job that contains the task. Jobs are bundles. The worker may do twenty things, some automatable, some complementary to automation, some changed by regulation or customer expectations. Employers may use saved time to reduce headcount, increase output, lower prices, improve service, or assign more work.

Technology does not choose among these outcomes.

Management does.

Markets, labor institutions, and regulation constrain the choice, but somebody eventually decides what to do with the productivity gain.

This is why workers often distrust statements that AI will “free people from drudgery.” The claim describes a technical possibility as if it were an employment contract.

A tool can save two hours of work.

The worker wants to know who owns the two hours.

If the employer raises the output target by exactly two hours, the worker experiences acceleration rather than liberation. If staffing is reduced and the remaining employees supervise more automated systems, the work may become cognitively denser. If the worker receives training, higher pay, or a shorter week, the gain is shared differently.

The same model can support all three futures.

Public pressure therefore has to reach beyond safety and bias into distribution.

Responsible-AI frameworks often focus on whether systems are accurate, secure, explainable, privacy-preserving, and nondiscriminatory. Those are important. A perfectly accurate system can still be used to intensify work.

Algorithmic management predates generative AI. Warehouses, delivery networks, ride-hailing platforms, call centers, and retail operations have long used software to assign tasks, measure performance, set routes, forecast demand, and discipline workers. Employees may experience the manager as a dashboard even when human supervisors remain formally responsible.

Generative AI extends management into domains that once relied more heavily on professional discretion.

A system can review sales calls. It can score whether required phrases were used. It can summarize performance. It can recommend coaching. It can draft an employee evaluation. It can monitor code contributions. It can analyze written communication. It can estimate whether a worker is likely to leave.

Each capability can be defended individually.

Together they can create a workplace in which the employee is continuously interpreted by systems the employee cannot inspect.

This is where worker backlash becomes a privacy problem as well as a job problem.

The employment relationship already contains asymmetrical power. An employer can require use of tools that a consumer could simply refuse. “Consent” has a different meaning when the alternative is not getting paid.

A company that tells employees they consented to AI monitoring by accepting a policy update may satisfy a formal notice requirement while missing the social reality.

Meaningful worker participation requires more than a click.

The U.S. Department of Labor’s 2024 principles and best practices for AI and worker well-being emphasized worker input, transparency, human oversight for significant employment decisions, protection of labor rights, and training. The principles were not a universal binding code, and federal AI policy shifted with the change of administration in 2025. Their significance lies in the problem they identified: workplace AI cannot be governed only as a product decision.

Workers are stakeholders in the system because the system measures them, changes their tasks, and may alter their economic security.

That sounds obvious. Organizations routinely forget it.

Technology procurement often begins with a buyer and a vendor. The buyer asks about price, integration, security, features, service levels, and return on investment. The people whose workflow will change may enter late, when implementation needs training.

By then, the most important choice has already been made.

A worker who objects can look anti-innovation because the contract is signed.

Public pressure can intervene earlier if workers have institutions capable of receiving information before deployment. Unions can bargain over workplace technology. Works councils in some jurisdictions have consultation rights. Professional associations can create norms. Employee groups can raise internal concerns. Whistleblowers can disclose hidden practices, at considerable personal risk.

The common feature is procedural standing.

The worker needs a place in the decision before becoming the object of it.

This is not an argument that employees should veto every software purchase. Organizations need the ability to change. A company that required unanimous approval before automating routine work would eventually become uncompetitive and might destroy the jobs it was trying to preserve.

The relevant question is narrower: which changes alter rights, compensation, surveillance, staffing, evaluation, or professional responsibility enough that workers deserve information and bargaining power?

That question does not have one answer across industries.

A hospital adopting an AI note-drafting tool creates different risks from a warehouse changing pick-rate algorithms. A newspaper using an AI transcription service is different from using generated articles to reduce reporting staff. A software company giving engineers a coding assistant is different from scoring engineers by the amount of AI-generated code they accept.

The governance must follow the work.

This is another reason broad AI principles often disappoint. The actual conflict occurs at the task level.

Who reviews the machine’s work?

Does review time count in the productivity estimate?

Who is liable for an error introduced by the system?

Can the employee refuse an unsafe recommendation?

Are model outputs used in performance evaluations?

Can worker data be used to train future systems?

Does the employer own prompts and corrections produced during work?

Will productivity savings affect staffing?

These are not futuristic questions. They are ordinary labor-management questions with new technical inputs.

The worker who trains the replacement fears a specific sequence. First, expertise is captured. Then the system becomes good enough for less experienced workers to perform more of the task. The organization reduces the number of experts. Eventually the remaining experts are asked to supervise a system trained on the work of people who are no longer there.

Sometimes this is exactly what productivity improvement looks like.

Civilization is full of skills that became less economically valuable because tools changed. Typesetting, telephone switching, bookkeeping, navigation, drafting, machining, and countless other occupations have been transformed by technology. A society cannot guarantee that every current task remains a paid job.

That is not the same as saying the transition is nobody’s responsibility.

The distribution of gains is a political and institutional choice even when the technological change is real.

One response is retraining. The word has become a ritual because it is easier to say than to implement. Training works best when there is a real adjacent demand for labor, the worker has time and money to acquire the skill, and employers recognize the credential. Telling a fifty-year-old administrative worker to “learn AI” is not a labor policy.

A more useful approach begins with the work that remains.

If AI drafts, humans may need to verify. If AI classifies, humans may handle exceptions. If AI translates, humans may supervise high-stakes nuance. If AI codes, engineers may spend more time on architecture, testing, security, and product judgment. If AI handles routine customer questions, human agents may receive a larger share of angry, ambiguous, or unusual calls.

Automation often leaves the hardest cases behind.

That can make the remaining job more valuable and more exhausting at the same time.

Consider customer support. A chatbot may resolve simple requests cheaply. This is good for the customer who needed a password reset. The human agent now receives fewer easy calls and more cases involving billing disputes, emotional customers, policy exceptions, or system failures. If management measures the human agent against the old average handling time, the apparent performance may deteriorate even though the agent is doing harder work.

The automation changed the case mix.

Metrics that ignore the change punish the humans for the machine’s success.

This pattern will recur across professions. Junior tasks disappear first because they are structured and repetitive. The remaining work requires more judgment. But junior tasks were also how people learned.

A law firm that automates basic research still needs senior lawyers later. A software company that automates simple coding still needs architects. A newsroom that automates routine briefs still needs experienced reporters. Where do the experienced people come from if the training work disappears?

AI can create an apprenticeship problem.

Institutions that focus only on immediate productivity may consume the pipeline that creates future expertise.

Workers feel this problem before executives measure it. Senior employees know which apparently trivial tasks taught them the domain. A model can perform the task without needing the apprenticeship. The organization still needs humans who understand when the model is wrong.

This creates a strange interdependence.

The better AI becomes at routine work, the more important it may be to deliberately preserve human learning opportunities in the remaining workflow.

That is not nostalgia. It is resilience.

A system with no human competence outside the automation cannot recover when the automation fails, changes vendors, encounters an unusual case, or is attacked.

The public pressure around jobs therefore contains a risk-management argument that companies often miss. Worker knowledge is not merely a labor cost. It is operational redundancy.

Eliminating it can make quarterly economics look better while increasing long-run fragility.

The same principle applies to professional ethics. Doctors, lawyers, engineers, accountants, journalists, and other professionals carry duties that do not disappear when software assists them. If organizations use AI to accelerate output while retaining human sign-off, they must allow enough time for the sign-off to be real.

A human in the loop who cannot realistically disagree is decoration.

Workers recognize decorative oversight quickly. They are the ones asked to click approve.

This is why internal backlash matters. Employees can expose the difference between public policy and actual operation. A company may advertise human review while workers report impossible workloads. It may claim a model is advisory while performance systems reward compliance. It may promise privacy while expanding monitoring.

Executives should treat those reports as system telemetry.

Instead, organizations often treat dissent as a culture problem.

Some dissent is self-interested. Workers have incentives to resist changes that threaten their positions. Unions can protect inefficient practices. Professionals can use ethics arguments to defend monopolies. None of that makes worker evidence irrelevant.

A person can be self-interested and correct about a risk.

Companies are self-interested too.

Good governance assumes incentives on all sides and builds procedures that force evidence into the disagreement.

Pilot the tool. Measure productivity. Measure error. Measure workload. Track turnover. Ask whether exceptions increased. Compare staffing scenarios. Let workers challenge metrics. Preserve the ability to roll back.

The important feature is reversibility.

AI adoption is often framed as inevitable. Inevitability is a powerful management tactic because it makes negotiation sound like delay. The technology may indeed be inevitable at the industry level. A particular deployment, vendor, metric, or staffing change is not.

Institutions should be able to distinguish technological direction from managerial preference.

Public pressure becomes destructive when it refuses every change. Corporate power becomes unaccountable when it describes every preference as technological necessity.

The worker caught between them needs a more honest sentence.

This tool can do part of your job.

We do not yet know what that means for the job.

That uncertainty is uncomfortable, but it creates room for bargaining, experimentation, and adaptation. False certainty creates panic on one side and arrogance on the other.

The worker who helps train a system deserves to know what the organization is trying to accomplish with it. That does not mean the worker owns the company’s strategy. It means expertise should not be harvested under one promise and deployed under another without consequence.

Trust at work is partly a contract about the future.

AI makes that contract easier to break because capability can change between annual reviews.

The institutions that survive the backlash will be the ones that learn to renegotiate faster than the technology changes the work.

The employee does not need a guarantee that nothing will change.

The employee needs evidence that change is something people are still allowed to discuss.
