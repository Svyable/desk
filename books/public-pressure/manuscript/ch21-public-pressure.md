# Public Pressure

The crowd wants a name.

The institution wants a process.

The machine wants nothing.

That is where the age of artificial intelligence becomes politically interesting.

Technology companies can build systems that write, rank, classify, recommend, imitate, predict, and act. Health insurers can automate parts of claims administration. Employers can screen applicants. political campaigns can generate speech and images. banks can detect fraud. criminals can counterfeit voices. creators can produce faster. unions can bargain over new tools. regulators can apply old law and write new rules. courts can decide what happens when automated interfaces make representations or when training practices collide with copyright.

None of these systems abolishes the question of responsibility.

They make it easier to hide.

The hiding does not always look like deception. Often it looks like scale.

The company is too large for one person to know everything.

The model is too complex for one explanation to capture every output.

The law is distributed across agencies.

The supply chain crosses vendors.

The decision was partly automated.

The policy came from somewhere else.

The data were acquired years ago.

The customer agreed to terms.

The employee used the tool voluntarily.

The system only recommended.

The executive was not involved.

Each statement may be true.

Together they can create an institution in which no one appears answerable for an outcome everybody helped produce.

Public pressure is the social response to that disappearance.

It does not begin with AI.

People have always resisted distant authority. They have protested prices, dangerous products, discrimination, corruption, war, monopoly, pollution, bad working conditions, financial fraud, medical abuse, and political exclusion. They have formed unions, consumer leagues, shareholder campaigns, parties, reform groups, professional associations, newspapers, churches, neighborhood organizations, and movements.

They have also formed mobs.

The difference is not whether people are angry.

The difference is what anger becomes.

A boycott becomes information about consumer willingness to exit.

A strike becomes bargaining power.

A lawsuit becomes evidence under procedure.

A hearing becomes a public record.

An election becomes delegated authority.

A regulation becomes a rule.

A threat remains a threat.

A murder remains a murder.

AI does not change those distinctions.

It makes them easier to blur because the same network carries evidence, propaganda, organizing, fraud, parody, journalism, harassment, whistleblowing, marketing, and synthetic media.

The public sees them in one feed.

This book began with the killing of Brian Thompson because the reaction exposed the danger of confusing an institution with a person.

The criticism of the health-insurance system did not become illegitimate because a chief executive was murdered.

The murder did not become legitimate because many people had criticisms of the health-insurance system.

Both facts survive at once.

That is the discipline public pressure requires.

Hold the institution accountable without converting a human being into a symbolic target whose ordinary rights no longer matter.

Hold the individual accountable for actual authority and conduct without assigning every system failure to personal intent.

This sounds moderate only because extremes are rhetorically simpler.

It is harder in practice.

The public prefers villains because villains create causal clarity.

Companies prefer systems because systems diffuse blame.

The useful analysis lives in the space between them.

Who chose the system?

Who set the incentives?

Who had discretion?

Who received the warning?

Who could have stopped the action?

Who benefited?

Who can correct it now?

These questions pull responsibility out of abstraction without pretending one person controls everything.

That is the central method of the book.

Follow control.

The method works across very different cases.

In health insurance, the meaningful question is not whether an algorithm existed but how its recommendation interacted with coverage rules, reviewer incentives, clinical judgment, appeal rights, and timing.

In hiring, the question is not whether software was used but whether it materially shaped opportunity, whether the employer validated the tool, whether applicants received meaningful notice, and whether discriminatory effects could be detected.

In creative work, the question is not whether models learn like people but which works were used under what rights, how outputs affect markets, and whether creators retain consent, compensation, attribution, or bargaining power in defined contexts.

In the workplace, the question is not whether AI performs tasks but what management does with the saved time, how workers are measured, whether expertise survives, and who participates in redesigning the job.

In politics, the question is not whether media can be synthetic but whether voters are deceived about who actually spoke, whether authenticity can be established, and whether rules preserve satire and disagreement.

In crypto fraud, the question is not whether blockchain technology is good or bad but whether people lied about assets, custody, stability, returns, or identity, and which controls could have exposed the lie earlier.

In voice cloning, the question is not whether resemblance can be perfect but why an organization gave resemblance authority over money.

In AI governance, the question is not whether the company has principles but whether the principles create bounded authority, records, independent review, appeal, compensation, and a person or body capable of being held accountable.

These are narrower questions than the cultural argument about artificial intelligence.

That is their strength.

A society can disagree about the long-run future of AI and still agree that a patient deserves a meaningful appeal.

It can disagree about copyright doctrine and still negotiate a labor contract.

It can disagree about open-source models and still punish impersonation fraud.

It can disagree about whether frontier models create catastrophic risk and still require companies to maintain records of consequential incidents.

It can disagree about whether AI will create more jobs than it destroys and still measure what happens to workers during deployment.

Institutional questions make pluralism possible.

They let people govern together without resolving every philosophy first.

This is especially important because AI has become a symbolic object far larger than the systems themselves.

For some people, it represents progress, abundance, scientific acceleration, accessibility, and tools that make individuals more capable.

For others, it represents layoffs, surveillance, synthetic culture, concentrated corporate power, environmental cost, plagiarism, scams, political deception, and decisions made by systems nobody can question.

Both lists contain real possibilities.

Neither is a forecast.

The backlash becomes productive when it converts fear into observable demands.

Show the error rate.

Show the appeal.

Show the energy use.

Show the licensing terms.

Show who authorized the deployment.

Show whether workers were displaced or augmented.

Show what the audit found.

Show the rule that governs the agent.

Show the record when something goes wrong.

Evidence cools symbolic conflict by moving disagreement toward things that can change.

It does not eliminate politics.

People will still disagree over what error rate is acceptable, what compensation is fair, what data use should be lawful, how much automation a workplace should accept, which risks justify regulation, and how much control companies should have over public speech.

Those disagreements are the politics.

The point is to stop allowing “AI” to substitute for them.

The same discipline applies to executives.

Technology CEOs have become unusually visible because their companies are building systems that affect broad areas of life and because many founders intentionally present themselves as interpreters of the future. That visibility creates legitimate scrutiny.

It also creates a temptation to mistake charisma for governance.

A brilliant founder cannot personally audit every output.

A responsible chief executive cannot promise zero harm.

A hostile chief executive does not prove every employee is malicious.

The useful demand is institutional.

Build a company that remains accountable after the founder leaves the stage.

A serious board.

Independent reporting channels.

Clear decision rights.

Auditable commitments.

Product controls.

Incident review.

Appeals.

Compensation.

Regulatory compliance that exists outside public relations.

A leader proves responsibility by creating structures that do not depend entirely on the leader being responsible.

Politicians face the same test.

AI creates enormous incentives for theatrical policy. A hearing can produce a viral confrontation. A sweeping bill can produce headlines. An executive order can announce national strategy. The harder work is administrative capacity.

Who enforces the rule?

Can the agency hire people who understand the system?

Does the law fit the sector?

Can a small company comply?

Is there a complaint process?

Do citizens know where to go?

Can a court review the decision?

What evidence is preserved?

A state capable of governing AI will look less cinematic than the technology it governs.

That is normal.

Civilization runs on boring controls.

Double-entry bookkeeping is boring.

Building codes are boring.

Audit trails are boring.

Appeal deadlines are boring.

Licensing registries are boring.

Two-person payment approval is boring.

Standards documents are boring.

They are the accumulated response to earlier forms of public pressure.

Somebody was injured.

Somebody was defrauded.

Somebody discovered the hose did not fit.

Somebody found that the company’s books could not be trusted.

Somebody learned that a judge needed a record to review.

The procedure is the fossil of the scandal.

AI is now producing its own fossils.

The digital-replica clause.

The deepfake disclosure.

The automated-hiring audit.

The model-risk framework.

The AI interaction notice.

The incident log.

The right to a human appeal.

The rule that a chatbot speaking on a company’s site is not a separate sovereign.

Some of these mechanisms will fail.

Some will become bureaucratic rituals.

Some will be captured by incumbents.

Some will impose more cost than benefit.

Public pressure does not end when a rule is written.

It moves into evaluation.

Did the rule work?

This may be the least glamorous and most important political question in the book.

Movements like beginnings.

Institutions like announcements.

Results require return visits.

A bias audit that changes nothing after finding disparity is theater.

An appeal process that never reverses a decision is theater.

A safety framework whose threshold never blocks a launch is theater.

A disclosure label that users do not understand is theater.

A hearing without follow-through is theater.

The test is consequence.

Public pressure should be able to point to the altered default.

This is also how companies can earn relief from pressure.

A culture that never allows an institution to demonstrate improvement eventually teaches institutions to conceal rather than correct. If every error permanently defines the company, admitting errors becomes irrational.

Accountability needs an exit.

The company fixes the process.

The regulator verifies compliance.

The affected people receive remedy.

The public record reflects the correction.

Critics remain free to argue that the institution’s larger business model is harmful.

The specific case can still close.

This is the difference between accountability and vengeance.

The same principle should govern people.

Public life has become unusually good at creating permanent searchable records of human error. AI will make those records easier to summarize and retrieve. A person’s worst moment can become the first paragraph of every future assessment.

This creates a new pressure toward informational mercy.

Not forgetting.

Proportion.

A convicted fraudster’s conviction remains relevant to financial trust. A corrected professional mistake may not deserve equal weight forever. A politician’s official record should remain available. A private person misidentified in a viral post should not carry the accusation after it is disproven.

The right to correction will become more valuable as memory becomes cheaper.

So will the right to provenance.

In a synthetic media environment, people need to know not only what information exists about them but where it came from and whether it was authenticated. Companies need records of what their systems actually did. Governments need records of which decisions were official. Journalists need source chains. Courts need evidence. Movements need confidence that the spectacular artifact is not poison.

Public pressure depends on a common world of facts.

AI makes that world more expensive to maintain.

That cost is worth paying.

The alternative is not a world without pressure.

It is a world where pressure is governed by whoever manufactures the most believable evidence first.

That would reward exactly the actors least deserving of power.

The scammer.

The propagandist.

The harasser.

The institution willing to fabricate its own innocence.

The politician willing to deny authentic evidence.

The company willing to describe every failure as model behavior beyond human control.

Accountability requires resistance to all of them.

This is why the phrase public pressure should not be romanticized.

Pressure is force.

Force needs direction.

A hydraulic system without valves destroys seals. A political system without channels turns frustration into spectacle or coercion. Institutions exist partly to route pressure into decisions that can be reviewed.

Elections route pressure.

Courts route pressure.

Markets route pressure.

Unions route pressure.

Regulators route pressure.

Boards route pressure.

Journalism routes pressure.

Appeals route pressure.

Public protest matters most when these channels are blocked, captured, or insufficient.

Then protest can reopen them.

The mistake is treating protest itself as the final institution.

A crowd can reveal urgency.

It cannot run every appeal.

A viral thread can expose a pattern.

It cannot adjudicate every case.

A CEO can apologize.

The company still needs a process.

A politician can promise action.

The agency still needs staff.

An AI model can summarize the dispute.

Someone still has to decide.

That final someone is the subject of the book.

Not one person.

A chain of answerable people and institutions.

As artificial intelligence becomes more capable, society will delegate more decisions because delegation is what useful technology is for. The benefit is scale. The danger is distance.

The person affected by the decision can become farther from the person capable of changing it.

Public pressure is what fills the distance when institutions fail to provide another route.

The best AI governance will therefore not be measured by how little criticism exists.

It will be measured by how rarely criticism has to become a crowd before somebody with authority listens.

That is a demanding standard.

It applies to companies, governments, unions, activists, journalists, courts, researchers, and the public itself.

Power should explain itself.

Criticism should prove what it can.

Serious decisions should be appealable.

Synthetic identity should not equal authority.

Automation should not erase principals.

Violence should remain outside legitimate pressure.

Correction should remain possible.

The person nobody is watching should receive the same rule as the person with an audience.

These are not principles for machines.

Machines do not need legitimacy.

Institutions do.

The pressure will keep coming.

That is not a failure of democracy or markets. It is evidence that people still expect systems with power over them to answer back.

The question for the AI age is whether they will have to shout.
