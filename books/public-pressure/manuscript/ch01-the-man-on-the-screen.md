# The Man on the Screen

At 6:45 on a December morning in Manhattan, a corporate title became a public object.

Brian Thompson, the chief executive of UnitedHealthcare, was walking toward an investor conference when he was shot outside a hotel. The killing on December 4, 2024, was quickly treated by police as a targeted attack. Within hours, his photograph was everywhere. So was the logo of the company he ran. The ordinary machinery of a murder investigation—camera footage, witness accounts, bullet casings, travel records, a search for the shooter—began running beside a second process that no police department controlled.

People started talking about health insurance.

Some expressed shock and sympathy. Some described personal battles over claims, prior authorization, bills, networks, denials, rehabilitation, drugs, and appeals. Some reacted with a coldness that startled people who were used to public condolences after a killing. Others crossed the line into celebration. The ammunition recovered at the scene was reported to have words written on it that echoed the vocabulary of insurance criticism. That detail became a symbol before investigators had established a full motive.

The murder did not create anger at the American health-insurance system. It exposed how much of it was waiting.

That distinction is important because the reaction was often discussed as if the internet had spontaneously become cruel. The cruelty was real. It was also attached to an existing structure of grievance. Millions of people interact with health insurers at moments when they are sick, frightened, exhausted, in pain, caring for somebody else, or trying to understand a bill that seems to have arrived from another civilization. A dispute about coverage may be a routine administrative event inside a company. Outside the company, it can be the most consequential paperwork in a family’s life.

When an institution repeatedly meets people at moments like that, it accumulates stories.

Most stories do not become lawsuits. Most do not become news. They exist as conversations with relatives, angry calls from parking lots, screenshots of portal messages, letters saved in kitchen drawers, and memories of somebody saying that a procedure was not authorized. Some of those complaints reflect genuine errors or bad incentives. Some reflect the complexity of benefits that people did not understand. Some are disagreements about medical necessity. Some are caused by employers, government rules, hospital billing, pharmacy-benefit design, or other actors that the patient experiences simply as “insurance.” The system is sufficiently complicated that blame can be both sincerely felt and badly assigned.

Complexity does not dissipate anger. It often concentrates it.

A patient rarely knows the actuary who priced a product, the committee that wrote a utilization rule, the contractor that built a model, the regulator that approved a plan, the employer that selected it, or the executive who signed off on an automation program. The patient does know the company name on the card. If the company has a chief executive, that person can become the human form of a system whose real causal structure is much larger.

This is one of the central changes in modern corporate life. Large institutions have always had leaders. But leaders now exist inside an attention system that makes them unusually visible. Their faces appear on earnings calls, social feeds, podcasts, conference stages, congressional hearings, internal videos, magazine covers, and investor presentations. Founders cultivate audiences. Chief executives explain strategy directly to employees and customers. Politicians summon them to hearings precisely because a complicated company can be made legible by putting one person behind a nameplate.

The arrangement is useful when things are going well. It makes leadership feel concrete. The chief executive can tell the story of the company in a way that a legal entity cannot.

It works in reverse too.

A logo cannot feel shame. A recommendation engine cannot answer a senator. An automated claims system cannot be embarrassed by a newspaper investigation. A holding company cannot apologize to a parent. Public pressure therefore searches for a person.

That does not mean the person is a fair target for everything the organization has done. It means the public has discovered a compression scheme.

A chief executive is a compression scheme for responsibility.

The same pattern appears across technology. Mark Zuckerberg became the face of Facebook’s effects on privacy, political communication, child safety, content moderation, and competition even when the decisions at issue involved thousands of employees and years of product development. Sam Altman became a human proxy for arguments about generative AI, safety, employment, copyright, energy, corporate governance, and the future of intelligence. Elon Musk’s companies are unusually inseparable from his own public identity. In politics, presidents are held responsible for fuel prices they only partially influence and economic conditions that result from many institutions. A mayor can become the face of a subway failure caused by deferred maintenance decisions across decades.

Public life needs representatives. It also mistakes representation for causation.

The health-insurance reaction after Thompson’s killing showed both impulses at once. People used a single person to talk about an industry. Some used the event to describe policies they believed were harmful. Some used it to vent. Some turned a death into a meme. The moral boundary matters because public anger can reveal information without justifying what people do with it.

Violence is not an appeal process.

That sentence is simple because it should be. A society that allows political, economic, or administrative grievance to become a license to kill does not create accountability. It replaces institutions with fear. The most powerful people may buy better security. Everyone else becomes easier to intimidate. The result is not democratic pressure. It is private coercion.

Still, a condemnation of violence cannot carry the rest of the analysis. If the only lesson drawn from the reaction is that people should behave better online, the underlying institutional information is lost. Something unusual happened when a killing generated such a large volume of discussion about denied care. The reaction indicated that many people experienced the insurer not as a neutral financial intermediary but as an adversary whose internal logic they could not see.

That perception had already become part of a more specific dispute over automated decision-making.

A class-action complaint filed in 2023 against UnitedHealth Group, UnitedHealthcare, and naviHealth alleged that a predictive system known as nH Predict was used to push Medicare Advantage patients out of post-acute care before treating physicians believed they were ready. The companies disputed the characterization. UnitedHealth said the tool was a guide and that coverage decisions were based on Medicare criteria and plan terms. The litigation has not established at trial that the allegations are true. In February 2025, the federal district court found that the Medicare exhaustion requirement applied but waived exhaustion on futility grounds; it dismissed five of the seven state-law causes of action as preempted while allowing claims for breach of contract and breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing to proceed. By March 2026 the case was in discovery, where a magistrate judge ordered production of certain materials about the development and use of nH Predict and relevant government investigations while limiting other requests. As of late August 2026, the putative class action remained active.

The distinction between allegation and finding matters. So does the shape of the allegation.

The plaintiffs were not mainly complaining that a computer existed. They were complaining that an output had become difficult to argue with.

That is a different problem from ordinary automation. Hospitals have used software for decades. Insurers use statistical models because insurance itself is a statistical business. Scheduling, coding, fraud detection, network management, pharmacy systems, and clinical decision support all depend on computation. The relevant question is not whether there is an algorithm somewhere. There is almost always an algorithm somewhere.

The question is what authority the algorithm acquires.

A model may begin as advice. A company may then build performance targets around it. Managers may prefer consistency. Employees may learn that deviations are questioned. Appeals may be slow. The model can become institutionally stronger than its formal description. Nobody has to issue a memo saying that software outranks a doctor. Incentives can produce that outcome more quietly.

This is one reason public anger increasingly attaches to AI. People are often less concerned with whether a system meets a technical definition of artificial intelligence than with whether a machine-generated conclusion can alter their life without a human being who appears able to reconsider it.

The public vocabulary is rough. People call statistical scoring, rules engines, machine learning, optimization, and generative systems “AI” interchangeably. Engineers may find that imprecise. From the outside, the shared feature is easier to describe: a decision appears to have been made somewhere else.

Somewhere else is the geography of modern frustration.

A driver is deactivated by a platform and receives a form response. A job applicant is rejected without knowing whether a human saw the application. A musician hears a synthetic voice that sounds like a colleague. A writer learns that models may have been trained on books that took years to produce. A voter receives a call that sounds like a politician. A customer watches a bank transfer leave because an employee heard what seemed to be the chief executive’s voice. A patient is told that a stay is no longer covered.

In each case, there may be a real human decision behind the machine. The system may even be working as designed. The affected person often cannot see either fact.

Opacity changes the psychology of accountability. When people can identify a clerk, supervisor, doctor, editor, banker, or local official, they may disagree with the decision but understand where it lives. When the decision appears to emerge from a distributed technical process, responsibility becomes abstract. The person affected has to search upward.

Eventually the search reaches somebody whose name is known.

The chief executive is rarely involved in an individual claim, moderation decision, hiring screen, ad recommendation, or model output. But executives choose systems. They approve priorities. They hire the people who build policy. They answer to boards and investors. They can change incentives that no customer-service worker can change. So the instinct to look upward is not irrational.

It becomes irrational when every harm is treated as personal intent.

That error is easy to make because the modern corporation often encourages the opposite idea when selling itself. Companies speak in the first person. “We believe.” “We care.” “We built.” The brand adopts a voice. Leaders give interviews in which they explain what “we” are trying to accomplish. Marketing humanizes the institution because people prefer to trust people.

The institution cannot then be surprised when anger is humanized too.

This is not an argument for executive anonymity. It is an argument for a better map of responsibility.

A useful accountability system distinguishes at least four things: the person who has symbolic authority, the people who made the operational decision, the system that constrained their choices, and the institution that can provide remedy. Those categories overlap, but they are not identical.

Consider a denied medical claim. The symbolic authority may be the chief executive. The operational decision may involve a clinician employed by the insurer. The system may include benefit language, Medicare rules, a prediction tool, utilization criteria, and performance metrics. The remedy may require an internal appeal, an external review, a government process, a lawsuit, or a policy change. Public anger tends to collapse all four categories into the company name.

AI increases the temptation because it adds another layer that can be blamed without being questioned. “The algorithm did it” sounds explanatory while often explaining very little. Which algorithm? Trained on what? Optimizing for what? Used by whom? With what override rights? Audited how? Compared against what baseline? Who can reverse the result?

Those questions are harder than blaming either the machine or the executive. They are also where accountability begins.

The strongest public-pressure campaigns eventually learn this. They start with a symbol and then move toward a mechanism. Organized labor did not respond to generative AI by merely denouncing studio executives. Writers and performers negotiated contract language about when AI could be used, how digital replicas required consent, what counted as literary material, and how compensation would work. Regulators did not respond to AI-generated robocalls by scolding the technology. The Federal Communications Commission interpreted existing law so that AI-generated voices in robocalls fell under restrictions on artificial or prerecorded voices. New York City did not outlaw automated hiring. It required certain bias audits and notices.

These measures can be criticized. They may be incomplete. But they represent a transition from pressure to design.

That transition is the difference between a crowd and a constituency.

A crowd knows what it hates. A constituency develops a theory of change.

The online reaction to Thompson’s murder mostly remained a crowd event. It had no single demand. It mixed grief, disgust, dark humor, policy criticism, personal testimony, ideological argument, and in some corners an ugly fascination with the killer. It was not a movement in the organized sense.

But institutions watched it.

Executives across industries were reminded that public hostility can become physical risk. Corporate security practices received new attention. Health-care leaders condemned the killing. Commentators asked why the reaction had been so different from the response to other high-profile murders. The event forced a discussion that the victim did not choose and could not answer.

This is another danger of personalization: the dead become available for symbolism.

Once a person stands in for an institution, facts about the person may matter less to the crowd than what the institution represents. A stranger can become an avatar. That is morally dangerous because avatars do not have families. People do.

The inverse danger belongs to the institution. An organization can respond to a frightening public reaction by treating every critic as part of the threat. That collapses another distinction. The patient who has spent months appealing a denial is not equivalent to someone issuing a death threat. The journalist investigating a business practice is not equivalent to a harasser. The regulator asking for documents is not part of a mob. A company that fails to distinguish them may use security as another form of opacity.

Accountability requires separating legitimate pressure from illegitimate coercion in both directions.

The rest of this book will return to that boundary repeatedly because AI makes it easier to cross without noticing. Synthetic media can fabricate evidence against a public figure. Automated accounts can make a small campaign look enormous. Recommendation systems can reward the most inflammatory version of a complaint. Large language models can make it easier to draft thousands of letters, comments, reviews, or messages. Voice cloning can impersonate an executive. Image generation can put a politician into a scene that never occurred.

The public sphere is gaining more capacity to apply pressure while losing some of its old confidence in the evidence used to justify pressure.

Institutions face the same contradiction. They gain more capacity to decide while often making decisions harder to inspect.

That symmetry is what makes the current moment different from an ordinary consumer backlash. The company has models. The crowd has models. The politician has models. The scammer has models. The regulator has models. Everyone can produce more analysis, more persuasion, more messages, more classifications, more documentation, and more noise.

Scarcity moves elsewhere.

It moves toward trusted evidence, legitimate authority, accountable discretion, and meaningful appeal.

A company cannot solve that problem by putting a friendlier chief executive on television. A regulator cannot solve it by requiring a disclosure nobody reads. A platform cannot solve it by labeling everything uncertain. A public cannot solve it by choosing a villain quickly enough.

The work is slower. Responsibility has to remain attached to decisions as the decisions become more automated.

The morning Brian Thompson was killed, a human being died and an institution acquired an even larger symbolic life. The public reaction showed how dangerous the gap between those two facts can become.

The man on the screen was a person.

The system behind him was larger.

A serious theory of public pressure has to be able to see both at the same time.

There is another reason the reaction matters. Public hostility does not distribute itself according to an organizational chart. It accumulates where ordinary people can see, speak, and imagine leverage. That makes reputation a kind of informal jurisdiction. A company can be legally compliant and still politically vulnerable because legality answers only some of the questions people are asking. It can also be widely disliked while behaving lawfully in the specific case that triggered the anger. Public pressure does not wait for these categories to align.

The mismatch is easiest to see in complaints about health care because the American system contains so many actors with overlapping incentives. Insurers receive premiums and pay claims. Employers often choose plans. Hospitals negotiate rates. Physicians decide treatment while sometimes owning facilities or practices with financial interests. Pharmacy benefit managers sit between drug manufacturers, insurers, pharmacies, and patients. Federal and state governments finance, regulate, or directly administer large portions of the system. A person can be harmed by the interaction among these parts without any single participant controlling the whole outcome.

That makes moral storytelling difficult.

It also makes institutional mapping indispensable.

Imagine two families describing an apparently identical denial. One is experiencing an insurer applying a plan exclusion that the employer chose. The other is experiencing a utilization decision inside a Medicare Advantage plan. The letters may look similar. The proper remedies may be entirely different. A public campaign that treats both as evidence of the same mechanism may mobilize anger efficiently and reform nothing.

The map has to reach the level where a rule can move.

Who wrote the benefit language? Who can amend it at renewal? Which regulator has jurisdiction? What does federal law require? What clinical evidence was considered? Was the appeal internal or independent? Did the person know the deadline? How many comparable decisions were reversed? These questions sound bureaucratic because accountability is often bureaucratic after the slogan has done its work.

This is why the emotional power of an individual story should be treated as an entry point rather than a conclusion. The story reveals where to look. It does not by itself tell us what we will find.

The discipline matters most when the story is unbearable.

A family facing illness has no obligation to become an expert in insurance architecture before describing what happened. The burden shifts when journalists, politicians, lawyers, activists, researchers, and authors generalize from the case. They have the time and institutional role to separate the patient’s experience from the mechanism that produced it.

This separation protects the patient too. If a critic exaggerates the role of a particular algorithm and the company disproves the exaggeration, the underlying failure can disappear beneath the fact-check. A weak causal claim gives a strong institution an easy defense.

Accuracy is therefore not politeness toward power.

It is leverage.

The same principle applies to the chief executive. A leader may have no knowledge of the individual case and still have responsibility for the system producing many such cases. That is a different claim and often a stronger one. The CEO’s relevant decisions are likely to concern staffing, incentives, capital allocation, vendor selection, risk tolerance, compliance, reporting, and the authority given to managers below. Those are structural decisions. They can be investigated without pretending the executive personally pressed a denial button.

This is a more demanding form of accountability because it asks for evidence about governance rather than biography.

How are appeals reported upward? Which metrics reach the board? Are denial reversals treated as quality information or merely administrative cost? What does executive compensation reward? What happens when a clinical team disagrees with an efficiency initiative? Does an internal control have the authority to delay deployment? A person at the top can reasonably be asked these questions because they concern the architecture of the institution rather than one unknowable transaction.

They also create the possibility of a meaningful answer.

A leader can change a metric. A board can require reporting. An insurer can change an appeal workflow. A regulator can change a rule. An employer can choose another plan. A legislature can alter statutory rights. These are forms of power that exist before and after the person becomes a symbol.

The public reaction after Thompson’s killing showed what happens when many people no longer believe those ordinary channels are responsive. Their conclusion may be too broad. Their anger may attach to the wrong node. But an institution should still ask why so many people expect that only extraordinary attention produces an answer.

That question is different from asking whether the public behaved well.

It is a question about institutional latency.

How long does a grievance have to sit before someone with authority encounters it?

Organizations routinely measure latency in software. A slow service is visible because the dashboard shows it. Grievances have latency too. A complaint moves through intake, triage, first review, appeal, external review, legal escalation, media attention, executive escalation, and sometimes regulation. The institution may know the timing at each step and still fail to understand what the total wait feels like from outside.

AI can shorten some of that latency. It can summarize files, retrieve policy, translate documents, route cases, and identify inconsistent decisions. It can also create more first-stage decisions so quickly that the human review queue expands. The same technology can reduce and increase frustration depending on which side of the workflow receives the investment.

That is why the ratio between decision capacity and appeal capacity deserves attention.

An institution that doubles automated decisions while leaving appeals unchanged has changed more than productivity. It has changed the relative power of the first answer.

The political consequences will not appear immediately. Most people accept most decisions. The pressure accumulates among the exceptions until one event provides a focal point. Then years of stories can attach themselves to a morning that had nothing to do with most of them.

This is how symbols become dangerous.

They carry more grievance than one person can answer and more guilt than one person can bear.

A better system does not wait for the symbol.

It makes enough of its own decision chain visible that people can direct pressure toward a rule, an office, a contract, a regulator, or an appeal before anger needs a face.

The point is not to protect CEOs from criticism.

It is to make criticism accurate enough to change something larger than a CEO.
