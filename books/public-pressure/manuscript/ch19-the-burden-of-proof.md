# The Burden of Proof

The most expensive sentence in the synthetic era may be: prove it.

The company says the system did not discriminate.

Prove it.

The critic says the recording is fake.

Prove it.

The insurer says a clinician made the decision rather than the model.

Prove it.

The model provider says the training data were lawfully obtained.

Prove it.

The employee says the safety process existed only on paper.

Prove it.

The politician says the video is fabricated.

Prove it.

The burden of proof is not one rule. Courts assign burdens according to legal doctrine. Regulators have statutory authorities. Companies set internal standards. Journalists use editorial practices. Scientists use evidence conventions. Online audiences use whatever standard survives the morning.

AI strains all of them because it changes the relative cost of production and verification.

A false document can be generated in seconds.

A forensic analysis may take hours.

A model can produce ten thousand plausible claims.

A human fact-checker reads one at a time.

An automated decision system can reject one million applications.

One applicant may need weeks to discover what happened.

The side producing action can scale faster than the side demanding reasons.

This is the core asymmetry of public pressure in an AI-mediated world.

The answer cannot be to require perfect proof before institutions act. Companies need to block suspected fraud. Platforms need to respond to threats. Doctors need to make decisions under uncertainty. Governments need to administer programs without litigating every case first.

Nor can the answer be to let scale lower the evidentiary standard indefinitely.

An institution should not be allowed to make more consequential decisions merely because explaining each one is expensive.

Scale should increase recordkeeping, not erase it.

This is the principle that connects the book’s cases.

If an insurer uses a model in coverage decisions, the organization should preserve enough information to reconstruct the decision.

If an employer uses automated screening, it should know what the system measures and whether outcomes differ systematically across protected groups.

If an AI company makes claims about safety, it should have evaluations and governance records behind the claims.

If a campaign publishes synthetic media, it should be able to establish authorship and disclosure.

If a financial institution accepts an executive instruction, it should be able to show how the instruction was authorized rather than merely recognized.

If a regulator imposes a penalty, it should show the legal basis and evidence.

The burden of proof belongs closest to the party with control over the evidence.

This is not an absolute legal rule. It is a governance intuition.

The person who could not see inside the system should not be expected to reconstruct the system from outside before being allowed to complain.

This problem appears throughout civil-rights law, consumer protection, product liability, employment, and administrative systems. Information asymmetry is not new. AI expands it because model behavior can depend on data, versions, prompts, settings, retrieval systems, and downstream workflows that the affected person cannot observe.

A user may know only the output.

The company knows the pipeline.

That should matter when designing evidentiary duties.

Consider an applicant who suspects automated discrimination. The applicant knows demographic identity and rejection. The employer knows whether a tool was used, how candidates were scored, who advanced, what validation was performed, and what the aggregate selection rates look like.

Requiring the individual to prove the statistical pattern before gaining access to any information can make the right theoretical.

At the same time, the employer should not have to expose every applicant’s private data publicly to answer one complaint.

The solution is institutional access to evidence: regulators, auditors, courts, or other authorized reviewers capable of examining sensitive information under rules.

This is accountable opacity again.

Not everything must be public.

Something must be inspectable.

The same principle applies to frontier AI companies. Some safety testing involves information that could enable misuse. Some model details are trade secrets. Some security findings should not be published in full. A demand for radical transparency can create real harm.

Secrecy still needs a witness.

An independent evaluator, regulator, board committee, or trusted auditor can see information the general public cannot. The credibility of the system then depends partly on the independence and competence of that reviewer.

This is how society handles other sensitive domains. Courts seal records while still reviewing them. Financial auditors inspect private books. nuclear regulators inspect facilities. Security clearances permit oversight of classified activity. Privacy regulators can examine data-processing practices without publishing personal data.

AI does not require inventing oversight from nothing.

It requires deciding which analogies fit.

The burden-of-proof problem becomes more difficult with generative media because the accused party may be asked to prove a negative.

“This video of you is fake.”

Show the original.

What if there is no original because the entire artifact was synthesized?

Show where you were.

What if the event supposedly occurred in private?

Ask the platform.

What if the account is anonymous and the file has been re-encoded repeatedly?

The accusation can become epistemically sticky. Lack of disproving evidence is treated as suspicious.

This reverses ordinary caution.

The person fabricating the claim should bear the primary burden of establishing authenticity when the medium is easily synthesized and the accusation is serious.

That norm will be difficult to maintain because people prefer evidence that confirms prior beliefs. A damaging recording of an unpopular executive will be accepted more quickly by critics. A damaging recording of a favored politician will be dismissed more quickly by supporters.

Synthetic media gives both sides a technical vocabulary for motivated reasoning.

“It’s obviously AI.”

“It looks too real to be AI.”

Neither is a method.

Public institutions need stronger authentication practices so the burden does not fall on visual intuition.

News organizations can demand original files and source chains for extraordinary claims.

Platforms can preserve provenance where available.

Campaigns and governments can sign official media.

Courts can use expert evidence and chain-of-custody rules.

The public can learn to distinguish “unverified” from “false.”

That last distinction matters.

Uncertainty is a legitimate status.

Online pressure hates uncertainty because uncertain claims do not mobilize cleanly. The statement “this recording may be fake; verification is ongoing” is emotionally weaker than either “caught” or “hoax.”

A serious information culture needs the middle category.

The same is true for claims about AI systems.

A critic finds a biased output. Does that prove the system is generally biased?

No.

Does one good benchmark prove the system is safe?

No.

Does a lawsuit prove wrongdoing?

No.

Does dismissal prove the underlying practice was good?

Not necessarily.

Evidence arrives with scope.

Public pressure improves when it preserves the scope.

This sounds modest. It is radical in an attention economy that rewards universal conclusions from local facts.

The problem is especially acute with model evaluations. AI companies and researchers publish benchmark results that compress complex behavior into numbers. These numbers are useful. They can also acquire more authority than the evaluation deserves.

A model scores well on a reasoning benchmark.

That does not prove it reasons reliably in a hospital workflow.

A safety evaluation finds no successful attack under tested conditions.

That does not prove the attack is impossible.

A bias test shows parity across one measured group.

That does not prove fairness across every context.

Metrics are evidence under a test distribution.

The burden is to connect the test to the claim.

This is where corporate communication often overreaches. A result becomes an adjective: safe, responsible, robust, fair, secure.

Adjectives travel without methodology.

Public pressure should ask companies to replace adjectives with claims narrow enough to test.

What failure rate was measured?

Under what conditions?

Against which baseline?

What uncertainty remains?

What did the test not cover?

What happens if the result degrades after deployment?

These questions do not make the public anti-innovation.

They make corporate language auditable.

The same standard should apply to critics.

A viral thread showing ten model failures should say how the prompts were selected. Were they random? Adversarial? Repeated until a bad output appeared? Are the outputs reproducible? Did the model version change? Is the behavior unique to one system or common to alternatives?

A critic does not need a peer-reviewed paper before raising a problem.

The strength of the conclusion should match the strength of the evidence.

This reciprocity is essential because the AI backlash can otherwise become a competition in selective demonstration.

Companies select their best examples.

Critics select their worst.

The public sees two different products.

Independent evaluation exists to sample outside both incentives.

That makes third-party testing a public good.

Universities, nonprofit labs, auditors, standards organizations, journalists, government evaluators, and users can all contribute. Their independence will vary. Funding matters. Access matters. Methodology matters.

There will be no perfectly neutral evaluator.

The goal is plural evidence.

Several groups using different methods can reduce the chance that one institution defines reality for everyone.

This is another reason open systems matter, though openness creates tradeoffs. Open models permit broader independent research and experimentation. They can also make certain safeguards easier to remove. Closed models can maintain stronger centralized controls but force outsiders to trust provider-selected information.

The burden-of-proof framework avoids turning openness into a moral absolute.

Ask what claim is being made and what evidence outsiders need to evaluate it.

A provider that keeps weights closed may compensate with richer evaluations, access for independent researchers, incident reporting, and regulatory inspection.

An open-source developer may provide full weights while having little capacity to monitor downstream misuse.

Different architectures produce different accountability obligations.

The same is true in health care. A proprietary model can still be governed if clinicians, institutions, and regulators have enough information to validate its use. A fully transparent formula can still be discriminatory if nobody measures outcomes.

Transparency is a means.

Proof is the goal.

There is another burden in public pressure: proving intent.

People often want to know whether a company meant to harm, deceive, discriminate, or replace workers. Intent is morally intuitive because it maps institutions onto persons.

Institutional harm often does not require malicious intent.

A threshold chosen to reduce cost can predictably increase false denials without anyone wanting a particular patient to suffer. A hiring model can reproduce inequality without a developer holding discriminatory views. A recommendation system can amplify inflammatory content because engagement predicts retention, not because executives prefer social conflict.

This does not make intent irrelevant. Fraud and criminal law can depend on states of mind. Deliberate deception is different from negligence.

It means public accountability should not stop when it fails to find a villainous email.

Incentives can be evidence.

Design can be evidence.

Repeated warnings can be evidence.

Failure to correct known problems can matter.

A company can be responsible for systems it chose without anyone inside resembling a cartoon villain.

This is a more mature form of criticism because it focuses on controllable causes.

The reverse danger is inferring intent from outcome automatically.

A model performs worse for one group, therefore the company intended discrimination.

A company lays off workers after deploying AI, therefore the AI project was secretly designed only to eliminate jobs.

A politician benefits from a deepfake, therefore the campaign created it.

These inferences may sometimes be true.

They require evidence.

Public pressure becomes credible when it can say “we do not know” without losing its demand for action.

We do not know whether the disparity was intended. We know it exists and requires investigation.

We do not know who created the fake. We know the distribution system can preserve evidence.

We do not know whether the model caused the denial. We know the insurer should disclose the role it played in the workflow.

Uncertainty can define the next procedural step.

That is better than filling the gap with motive.

The burden of proof also belongs to advocates of AI when they make large promises.

The technology will create more jobs than it destroys.

Prove it over time.

AI will democratize expertise.

Measure who gains access.

Automation will remove drudgery.

Show what happened to workload.

Models will improve medical care.

Run clinical evaluations.

AI will personalize education.

Measure learning rather than engagement.

These claims may become true in important ways. They should not receive a lower evidentiary standard because the future is attractive.

Optimism is also a claim.

The best response to backlash is therefore not reassurance.

It is proof proportional to power.

The larger the institution, the more people affected, the harder the decision to reverse, and the more opaque the mechanism, the stronger the institution’s burden to preserve evidence.

This does not mean every company must prove innocence before innovating.

It means power should arrive with records.

If the system changes lives, keep enough of the system to explain what happened.

If the company makes a promise, make it measurable.

If the critic makes an accusation, preserve the evidence.

If the government acts, state the authority.

If the crowd does not know, say so.

AI will make plausible stories abundant.

Public pressure will make stories consequential.

The institutions that remain trustworthy will be the ones capable of turning stories back into evidence.

That requires evidence to survive long enough to be used.

The synthetic era creates a record-retention problem before it creates a courtroom problem. A model changes. Logs roll over. A vendor deletes prompts. A platform removes a post. An employee leaves. The user’s browser history disappears. Months later, somebody asks what happened.

The institution may be unable to answer honestly because the evidence no longer exists.

This is not always negligence. Privacy law can require deletion. Security practice can favor minimizing sensitive logs. Storage costs are real. Trade-secret and employee-privacy concerns matter.

The solution is not infinite retention.

It is evidence escrow proportionate to consequence.

For defined high-stakes actions, preserve the minimum record needed to reconstruct authority and material facts for a defined period.

The model version.

The relevant policy version.

The material input fields.

The source documents or references used.

The human approval if one occurred.

The action taken.

The reason category.

The later correction.

Sensitive content can be protected, access-controlled, hashed, encrypted, or held by a trusted intermediary depending on context. The design problem is to preserve contestability without building a surveillance archive.

This is evidence engineering.

The field will grow because ordinary software observability is not the same as legal or institutional observability. Engineers log what helps debug the system. Accountability requires records that help reconstruct the decision that mattered to a person.

The overlap is large and not complete.

A model evaluation needs similar discipline. The result “92 percent” is less useful without the test set, version, scoring method, system prompt, tool configuration, date, and known limitations. A benchmark should have provenance too.

Otherwise the organization can reproduce the marketing claim without being able to reproduce the result.

Reproducibility becomes a burden-of-proof control.

This is particularly important when models are updated frequently. A public demonstration from January may say little about the product in August. A safety evaluation may refer to a checkpoint users never saw. A critic may reproduce a failure only after the provider has changed the system.

Both sides need version discipline.

When was the claim true?

Under which configuration?

Can the artifact be recreated?

This is not pedantry. It is how a moving target becomes evidence.

There is also a useful distinction between legal burdens and epistemic confidence.

A court may require one party to prove a claim under a specified legal standard. A scientist may express probability. A journalist may publish once evidence crosses an editorial threshold. A company may suspend an account based on a fraud score because waiting for courtroom certainty would expose other users.

These thresholds need not be identical.

Public arguments become confused when people borrow the language of one domain for another.

“No conviction” becomes “nothing happened.”

“High model confidence” becomes “proven.”

“A regulator opened an investigation” becomes “guilty.”

“A company found insufficient evidence to discipline” becomes “the allegation was false.”

Each conclusion exceeds the procedure that produced it.

The honest label is the status the process actually established.

Unverified.

Substantiated internally.

Found liable.

Convicted.

Dismissed on procedure.

Settled without admission.

Measured under a defined test.

Suspected under a risk threshold.

The vocabulary should carry the burden.

AI assistants will increasingly summarize these statuses for people who never read the underlying record. That creates a design obligation for information systems: preserve procedural qualifiers.

“Was accused of” is not a decorative phrase.

“Was convicted of” is not interchangeable with it.

“Court dismissed the case for failure to exhaust remedies” is different from “court found the allegations false.”

A system that compresses away these distinctions creates defamation risk, political distortion, and bad institutional memory.

Burden of proof becomes a summarization problem.

Another useful governance principle is information forcing.

The party with unique control of material evidence should sometimes have a duty to produce enough of it for review when a defined threshold is met.

The applicant cannot know the employer’s aggregate selection rates.

The patient cannot know the insurer’s override patterns.

The customer cannot know which model version answered the question.

The employee cannot know what the board was told.

Requiring the outsider to prove the hidden fact before obtaining any access creates circular rights.

This does not mean open-ended discovery for every complaint. The trigger can be legal, regulatory, contractual, or procedural. The point is to prevent opacity from becoming a burden-of-proof weapon.

A right is weak when the evidence needed to invoke it is controlled entirely by the party accused of violating it.

Independent auditors and regulators are information-forcing institutions.

They can examine data unavailable to the public, protect confidentiality, and decide whether the concern is supported enough for further action.

Their credibility depends on access.

An auditor allowed to inspect only company-selected examples is not meaningfully independent.

A regulator without technical capacity can possess formal power and practical blindness.

A court facing destroyed logs can have jurisdiction over an event nobody can reconstruct.

Proof requires infrastructure.

The same applies to public critics. A movement making serious claims should preserve its sources rather than relying on screenshots stripped of context. Journalists already know the value of notes, recordings, source documents, and chain of custody. Activists operating in a synthetic environment will need some of the same habits if they want their evidence to survive challenge.

This is not professionalization for its own sake.

It is defense against easy fabrication.

The person who can produce the original file, document the source, explain the uncertainty, and narrow the claim gains power precisely because everyone else can generate plausible artifacts cheaply.

The synthetic era does not eliminate evidence.

It raises the return on evidence discipline.

A plausible story used to be costly enough that plausibility carried information.

Now plausibility is abundant.

The burden of proof is what makes abundance expensive again.
