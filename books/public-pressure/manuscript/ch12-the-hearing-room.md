# The Hearing Room

Congress puts chief executives at tables designed to make them look smaller.

The room is ceremonial. Senators sit above the witness line. Clocks control answers. Nameplates identify the people who have been compressed out of organizations with tens of thousands of employees. Cameras turn every exchange into potential national media. Behind the witnesses sit lawyers, staff, advocates, families, lobbyists, reporters, and other people whose presence changes what the testimony means.

A hearing is government by staging as well as government by evidence.

That is not necessarily a criticism.

Democratic institutions need rituals for confronting private power. A committee can compel attention that an ordinary customer, employee, or journalist cannot. It can obtain documents, place statements in a public record, expose contradictions, and create the political conditions for legislation or enforcement.

The hearing room is one of the places where a corporation becomes a person because law requires somebody to answer.

AI arrived there quickly.

On May 16, 2023, only months after ChatGPT became a mass-market phenomenon, Sam Altman sat before a Senate Judiciary subcommittee alongside IBM executive Christina Montgomery and New York University professor Gary Marcus. Senators asked about jobs, election manipulation, copyright, safety, licensing, competition, and the risks of increasingly capable models.

The speed was unusual. Major technologies often become objects of congressional expertise only after years of diffusion. Generative AI moved from technical research communities into hearings while many lawmakers and companies were still learning what consumer uses would matter.

That uncertainty shaped the event.

Altman did something technology executives are not always known for doing in Washington: he asked for regulation.

The request made him sound less like a defendant and more like a participant in institutional design. He discussed the possibility of a federal agency, licensing or registration for sufficiently capable systems, safety standards, testing, and other forms of oversight. The exact proposals could be debated. The political effect was clear.

The CEO was volunteering to enter the regulatory conversation.

This produced two opposite reactions.

Some people treated it as evidence of responsibility. A leader of a frontier AI company was acknowledging that the technology could create serious risks and that voluntary corporate policy would not be sufficient.

Others saw regulatory capture arriving before the regulator. If the most capitalized companies help design licensing rules, those rules can become barriers that smaller competitors cannot afford. A company with lawyers, safety teams, compute, and regulatory staff may survive obligations that exclude open-source developers or startups.

Both interpretations can be true at once.

A technology can require regulation and incumbents can benefit from the way regulation is written.

This duality is central to public pressure against technology CEOs. The public asks powerful companies to accept constraints. When the companies agree, critics suspect they are choosing the constraints they can dominate.

The suspicion is healthy if it produces better rules rather than paralysis.

Regulation should be evaluated by mechanism.

Who is covered?

At what capability or use threshold?

What evidence must be produced?

Which regulator decides?

Can small firms comply?

What happens to open models?

What rights do affected people receive?

Does the rule create accountability or merely paperwork?

A hearing can surface these questions. It cannot answer them by itself.

The limits of hearings became even clearer in January 2024 when the Senate Judiciary Committee convened the chief executives of Meta, TikTok, Discord, Snap, and X for a hearing on online child sexual exploitation. Families sat in the room. Lawmakers confronted the executives with accounts of severe harm. At one point Senator Josh Hawley pressed Meta chief executive Mark Zuckerberg to apologize to families. Zuckerberg stood, turned, and spoke directly to them.

The moment was emotionally legible.

It was also institutionally ambiguous.

An apology creates no statutory duty. It does not reveal how a recommendation system is configured. It does not establish causation in any individual tragedy. It does not determine the right liability rule for platforms. It does not resolve tradeoffs among privacy, age verification, encryption, parental control, speech, and law enforcement.

Yet to dismiss the apology as theater would miss the hearing’s function.

Power had to face the people describing harm.

Organizations often protect leaders from the emotional texture of their systems. Metrics arrive aggregated. Policy teams summarize. Legal departments frame exposure. A hearing collapses some of that distance. The executive cannot respond only to a dashboard because a parent is in the room.

That exposure can matter even when it is not adjudication.

The danger is mistaking exposure for adjudication.

Congressional hearings reward clear confrontation. A senator has limited time. The witness has incentives to qualify. The clip that travels is usually the sharpest exchange, not the most accurate technical explanation. A complex causal problem becomes a yes-or-no question.

“Will you commit today?”

“Do you agree your company harmed children?”

“Should this technology be licensed?”

“Can you guarantee this will never happen?”

These questions are politically useful because they force priorities into the open. They are analytically risky because complex systems rarely produce honest guarantees.

A skilled executive learns hearing grammar.

Acknowledge concern.

Reject the premise if legally dangerous.

Point to investment in safety.

Offer to work with the committee.

Emphasize scale.

Describe tradeoffs.

Avoid conceding a fact that litigation may later use.

The language can be sincere and still feel evasive because it is optimized for a room where every sentence has several audiences.

The senator is performing too.

This is not cynicism. Politics requires public communication. The problem begins when the performance becomes the policy.

AI makes this temptation stronger because the subject is difficult. A dramatic exchange about a chatbot hallucination is easier to understand than a discussion of evaluation design. A chart showing model size is easier than a debate over marginal risk. A CEO saying “we need regulation” is easier than writing rules across sectoral agencies.

The hearing produces attention faster than institutions produce capacity.

That gap has defined much of U.S. AI policy.

Federal agencies have used existing authorities. The FTC has pursued unfair or deceptive practices. The EEOC and Justice Department have warned that civil-rights law applies to automated employment. The FCC has applied robocall rules to AI-generated voices. Banking, health, securities, and consumer agencies have issued guidance or enforcement actions touching algorithmic systems.

Meanwhile presidents have issued executive orders with sharply different policy emphases. Congress has considered many bills without enacting one comprehensive national AI code comparable to the European Union’s AI Act.

The result is not an absence of law.

It is legal pluralism.

A chief executive summoned to Congress may therefore face one national political audience while operating under dozens of existing legal regimes, state laws, foreign laws, contractual duties, and sectoral regulators.

The phrase “regulate AI” hides this complexity.

Regulate which use?

A model summarizing a public document is not a model deciding eligibility for benefits. A chatbot writing a poem is not a medical device. An automated ad system is not a military targeting system. A voice clone used for an audiobook with consent is not a voter-suppression robocall.

General-purpose models complicate the picture because one system can participate in all these domains downstream. That is why policymakers debate whether obligations should attach to model providers, deployers, high-risk uses, capability thresholds, or combinations of them.

The hearing room naturally focuses on the provider because the provider has a famous CEO.

The deployment may be where the harm actually occurs.

This creates a political asymmetry.

Congress can summon the leader of a major AI laboratory. It is harder to summon every employer using the model badly, every hospital integrating it carelessly, every local agency purchasing an automated tool, or every scammer exploiting an API.

Visible suppliers can become accountability magnets for downstream decisions they did not control.

They can also use that argument to understate upstream responsibility.

A general-purpose provider controls training, base capabilities, access policies, certain safeguards, documentation, monitoring, and business terms. It may have unique information about model behavior. A deployer controls context, data, workflow, authority, and recourse.

Serious regulation has to allocate responsibility between them.

The hearing room likes a single witness.

The system has several.

This is why public pressure should use hearings as discovery rather than verdict.

A good hearing establishes questions that later mechanisms can answer.

What did the company know?

What internal tests existed?

When did leaders learn about a risk?

What resources were allocated to mitigation?

What promises were made to users?

What data are retained?

What would the executive support as law?

What would the company oppose?

The record can then inform legislation, litigation, journalism, procurement, shareholder action, or future oversight.

The worst hearing produces only humiliation.

Humiliation is addictive because it makes power appear reversed. For five minutes, the senator controls the billionaire’s microphone. A viral clip can create the sensation that democratic accountability occurred.

The billionaire leaves.

The product remains.

The question is what changed.

This standard should apply to critics as much as companies. A hearing that generates a policy proposal may be productive even if no executive looks defeated. A hearing that exposes a false statement may matter even if the moment is not dramatic. A bipartisan request for documents can be more consequential than a partisan speech.

Public pressure needs a theory of follow-through.

The same is true for company responses. “We take this seriously” should be treated as the beginning of a record. What happened next? Was a team created? Was a metric published? Was the product changed? Did the company reverse a decision? Did the board receive information? Did the regulator receive a report?

A commitment should create a future fact that can be checked.

Without that, accountability becomes an endless sequence of present-tense statements.

AI companies have learned to publish system cards, model reports, safety frameworks, preparedness processes, responsible-scaling policies, transparency reports, and usage policies. These documents are meaningful when they describe tests, thresholds, decision rights, and incidents with enough specificity to constrain future behavior.

They are public relations when they can explain any outcome after the fact.

The hearing room can test the difference.

If a company says it will not deploy beyond a certain risk threshold, lawmakers can ask who measures the threshold. If the company says humans remain in control, they can ask which humans. If the company says users can appeal, they can ask how many do and how long it takes. If the company says a model was extensively tested, they can ask what categories of failure were found.

Concrete questions force abstract commitments into operational form.

This is where CEOs are genuinely useful witnesses.

They can commit institutional resources.

An engineer can explain a model. A policy specialist can explain a rule. A CEO can say the company will stop shipping under specified conditions, submit to an audit, fund a remediation program, or support a law that changes its own incentives.

If the CEO cannot make those commitments, the hearing has discovered something about where power really lives.

Perhaps the board decides.

Perhaps investors constrain the company.

Perhaps a parent corporation controls the relevant resource.

Perhaps the CEO has more symbolic than practical authority.

That information matters too.

The public often imagines chief executives as monarchs because technology founders can possess unusual voting control and public visibility. Corporate law still creates boards, fiduciary duties, shareholder structures, contracts, and other constraints. The actual governance varies company by company.

The OpenAI crisis of November 2023 made this dramatically visible. The board fired Altman. Employees and partners reacted. Altman returned. A new board was formed. An external review later described a breakdown of trust and concluded that the prior board had acted within its authority but on an abridged timeline without a full inquiry or opportunity for Altman to address concerns.

The episode showed that formal authority can collide with practical legitimacy.

A board had the power to remove a CEO.

It discovered that employees, investors, and partners had power over what happened next.

The hearing room is built on the same constitutional insight.

No institution should possess only one source of legitimacy.

Companies have boards and markets.

Governments have branches, elections, courts, agencies, and federalism.

Workers have contracts and collective action.

Consumers have exit, complaint, law, and publicity.

Journalists and researchers provide outside scrutiny.

Public pressure is healthiest when these systems overlap rather than when one viral constituency becomes sovereign.

This is why the spectacle of a senator confronting a CEO should neither be mocked nor worshiped.

It is one checkpoint.

The CEO should have to answer.

The senator should have to legislate or oversee.

The regulator should have to enforce within law.

The court should have to evaluate evidence.

The public should have to decide whether the result was enough.

Distributed accountability is slower than outrage.

That slowness is frustrating.

It is also how a society prevents the person with the loudest microphone from becoming the only judge in the room.
