# A Right to Appeal

The most important button in an automated system may be the one that says the system is wrong.

Not a feedback icon.

Not a thumbs-down that improves a model someday.

A real appeal.

A real appeal changes authority.

The person who made or inherited the first decision is no longer the final judge. New evidence can enter. A different reviewer can reconsider the result. The institution has to preserve enough of the original process to explain what happened. Time limits matter. The reviewer needs power to reverse the outcome.

Many organizations have something called an appeal that does not meet these conditions.

A customer clicks “contact support” and receives the same automated answer in different words. A gig worker challenges a deactivation and is told the decision was reviewed, without learning by whom or on what evidence. A patient sends more records and waits beyond the period when the care would have mattered. A platform user appeals a removal and receives a generic confirmation. An employee asks why a performance score changed and is told the scoring system is proprietary.

These processes can be sincere attempts at review.

From outside they feel like loops.

AI makes the distinction between appeal and loop more important because it increases the number of decisions an institution can make faster than it increases the number of people available to reconsider them.

Automation creates a review debt.

Every system deployed at scale implicitly creates a future queue of exceptions.

If management budgets only for average-case automation, the queue becomes the place where accountability fails.

This is why “human in the loop” is such an incomplete phrase. Which loop? Before the decision? After? On every case? Only when the model is uncertain? Is the human qualified? Does the human have time? Can the human see the source evidence? Can the human override the model without punishment? Does the person affected have access to that human?

The phrase describes topology, not power.

A human can be in the loop and still be a rubber stamp.

A right to appeal is stronger because it describes a relationship of authority.

The first decision can lose.

This should become one of the central institutions of the automated state and automated corporation.

Not every AI output needs an appeal. Nobody needs a tribunal because a music recommender played the wrong song. Low-stakes errors can be corrected through ordinary product feedback. The right becomes more important as decisions affect access to employment, health care, credit, housing, education, government benefits, insurance, professional opportunities, account access, or other consequential interests.

The higher the stakes, the less acceptable it is for “the system says no” to be the end of explanation.

This principle does not require the first decision to be human.

In some contexts, an automated first decision may be faster and more consistent. A system can approve ordinary cases immediately and route unusual ones for review. It can identify obvious fraud. It can prefill decisions that trained staff verify. The efficiency gains may be substantial.

The accountability condition is what happens near the boundary.

Most systems are easy in the middle.

A highly qualified applicant clearly meets a license requirement. A claim clearly fits a benefit. A transaction clearly matches ordinary behavior. A piece of content clearly violates a narrow rule. The value of human judgment appears where facts are incomplete, rules conflict, context matters, or the model has not seen enough similar cases.

Institutions often design automation around averages and then treat boundary cases as user inconvenience.

Those cases are where legitimacy lives.

A person does not evaluate a system by its average accuracy when the person is the error.

This creates a political problem. An organization may correctly report that a model performs better than prior human decision-making overall. The affected person still has a legitimate claim if a correctable error caused serious harm.

Aggregate performance and individual recourse are not substitutes.

A 99 percent accurate system used one hundred million times can produce one million errors.

The number is only illustrative; real systems rarely have one clean accuracy metric. The point is structural. Scale converts small rates into populations.

An institution should therefore ask not only, “How often is the model right?” but “How many people can be wronged before we notice?”

Appeals are sensors.

They are often treated as costs.

Every reversed decision contains information about the first process. The reason for reversal can reveal missing data, ambiguous policy, model drift, poor documentation, user misunderstanding, employee training problems, or a recurring exception the product did not anticipate.

A company that analyzes appeals can improve the original system.

A company that merely clears the queue wastes its best failure data.

This suggests a useful metric: reversal rate by decision category, reviewer, model version, population, and reason.

The metric should be interpreted carefully. A low reversal rate can mean the first process is excellent. It can also mean appeals are inaccessible or reviewers defer too heavily to the original decision. A high reversal rate can mean healthy correction or a broken first stage.

Metrics need context.

The institution should combine them with sampling.

Randomly review some decisions that were never appealed.

That is especially important where people may not know they were harmed. Automated hiring is an obvious example. A rejected applicant may not know an AI system materially influenced the result. The applicant cannot appeal an invisible decision.

A right to appeal therefore has two companions: notice and audit.

Notice tells the person enough to know that a consequential decision occurred and, where relevant, that automation played a material role.

Appeal lets the person challenge it.

Audit protects people who do not or cannot appeal.

Together they create a basic accountability triangle.

None requires publication of every model parameter.

This matters because transparency debates often become stuck on source code. Critics ask to see the algorithm. Companies reply that code is proprietary, technically complex, or security-sensitive. Both can be correct while missing the practical question.

The person usually needs a reason, not a repository.

Why was this decision made?

What facts mattered?

Which rule applied?

What can I correct?

Who can change the result?

This is reason-giving rather than total transparency.

Administrative law has wrestled with versions of this problem long before AI. Governments exercise power through procedures because affected people need notice, reasons, records, and opportunities to challenge decisions. The exact constitutional and statutory rights vary by jurisdiction and context. The broader institutional insight travels well.

Power becomes more legitimate when it explains itself enough to be contested.

Corporations are not governments and should not automatically inherit every public-law procedure. A streaming service does not need a constitutional hearing before recommending a different movie. Private organizations do, however, exercise forms of quasi-public power when they control access to essential services, work, markets, or communications.

The appropriate procedure should match the consequence.

This is proportional due process.

A small account restriction might receive a quick internal review.

A permanent employment deactivation might warrant evidence, human reconsideration, and a route to independent arbitration or regulatory complaint.

A medical coverage denial may need rapid clinical review because delay itself is harm.

A government benefits decision may require formal statutory appeal rights.

The design cannot be one-size-fits-all.

The principle can.

Meaningful adverse decisions should have meaningful review.

This would change how AI systems are procured.

Today a buyer may ask a vendor about model accuracy, security, privacy, uptime, integration, and cost. A recourse-oriented buyer asks additional questions.

Can we reproduce the output later?

Can we know which model version produced it?

Can the system show the relevant inputs?

Can a reviewer change the result?

Can we configure escalation thresholds?

Can we export logs if we switch vendors?

What happens if the vendor sunsets the model?

Does the contract preserve evidence needed for an appeal?

These questions make appeal an architectural requirement rather than a customer-service afterthought.

The cost becomes visible before deployment.

That is important because cheap automation can look less cheap once recourse is included.

A system that processes ten million cases for pennies each may still require a well-staffed exception team. A product manager might view that as reducing the business case.

It is part of the business case.

The alternative is externalizing the cost of errors onto users until enough of them organize, sue, leave, or attract regulators.

Public pressure is the invoice for underfunded recourse.

This is especially clear in government. Automation is attractive to agencies facing backlogs and staffing shortages. A model or rules system can process eligibility, fraud, taxation, licensing, or benefits faster. The public may benefit enormously from shorter waits.

But an agency that automates the first decision without expanding appeal capacity can create a strange state: fast rejection, slow justice.

The citizen experiences technological progress as administrative asymmetry.

Speed belongs to the institution.

Delay belongs to the person.

A right to appeal should therefore include a right to timely appeal where timing affects the substance.

This cannot mean every dispute is resolved instantly. Complex cases take time. The rule should ask whether the institution’s process preserves the value of the remedy.

A hospital authorization decided after the scheduled procedure date may be useless.

A content appeal decided after an election may be useless.

A payroll dispute decided after eviction may be technically correct and practically late.

Time is part of due process.

This is one reason automated systems need triage that considers harm rather than publicity.

An institution may naturally prioritize the complaint from a lawyer, celebrity, journalist, or regulator because escalation risk is high. A just process prioritizes urgent stakes even when the person has no audience.

AI can help identify urgency if the criteria are designed carefully.

That is a productive use of automation in accountability itself.

A model can classify incoming complaints, extract deadlines, identify medical or financial emergencies, route language translations, and summarize files for reviewers. It can reduce the administrative cost of recourse.

The same technology that creates more decisions can help review them.

This is an underappreciated possibility.

AI backlash often assumes oversight must be entirely human to be legitimate. There is no reason for that to be true. An appeals system can use automation to organize evidence while preserving human authority for the consequential judgment. It can compare similar cases, detect inconsistent outcomes, and flag unusual decisions.

The important constraint is independence.

An appeal system should not merely ask the same model the same question again.

If the first decision depends on a model, the review should introduce genuinely new information or authority: a different model, a different data source, a qualified human, an independent unit, or an external body.

Otherwise repetition masquerades as reconsideration.

This is common in automated support. The user appeals, the same system rephrases the policy, and the institution records that an appeal occurred.

Procedural metrics can be gamed.

A company can report that 100 percent of appeals receive human review while giving reviewers twenty seconds per case. A regulator can require explanations and receive generic templates. A platform can offer appeal buttons that rarely change outcomes.

The right must be substantive enough to resist box-checking.

One method is outcome testing.

Can reviewers reverse decisions?

Do they ever?

Are reversals implemented promptly?

Are patterns of reversal fed back upstream?

Do independent audits show that reviewers consider new evidence?

Does the organization protect reviewers from retaliation when they disagree with automated recommendations?

These questions measure power rather than presence.

Another method is adversarial testing.

Give the appeals process known erroneous cases and see whether it corrects them.

Organizations routinely test cybersecurity by simulating attacks. They can test recourse by simulating mistakes.

This creates a red team for bureaucracy.

Can a person with limited English navigate the process?

Can a person without legal counsel identify the reason for a denial?

Can someone using assistive technology submit evidence?

Can a gig worker reach an empowered reviewer?

Can a patient obtain review before care becomes moot?

Can an applicant correct an erroneous credential record?

The test should resemble the lives of users rather than the expectations of designers.

Public pressure can demand this kind of evidence.

It is more useful than asking whether a company “cares about accountability.”

The company can show that appeals are resolved within defined times, that reversal patterns trigger review, that high-stakes cases receive qualified humans, and that people can correct data.

This gives the public something to inspect.

It also gives the company a way to demonstrate improvement.

That reciprocity matters because trust cannot be built by permanent suspicion alone.

A company that improves recourse should receive credit even if its first decisions remain imperfect. No institution will eliminate all error. The goal is to make error survivable.

This may be the most important design principle in the book.

Do not ask only how to prevent the machine from being wrong.

Build the institution that knows what to do when it is.

AI systems will become more capable. They will make fewer mistakes in many domains. That success can paradoxically make appeal harder because humans defer more to systems that are usually right.

High reliability creates automation bias.

The rare person challenging the output sounds less credible because everyone knows the model performs well.

This is why recourse cannot depend on the reviewer’s vague willingness to entertain exceptions. It needs defined authority and evidence standards.

An aviation system that works almost all the time still has emergency procedures.

A payment network that works almost all the time still has dispute processes.

A court system that trusts lower courts still has appeals.

Reliability and recourse are complements.

The better AI becomes, the more socially important the remaining errors may feel because they will be harder to explain as ordinary imperfection.

The person will hear, “The model is right 99.9 percent of the time.”

The response should be: “Then reviewing this one unusual case should not threaten the system.”

That is the cultural change institutions need.

Appeals are not attacks on automation.

They are what lets automation become legitimate enough to scale.

The right to appeal is therefore not a concession to backlash.

It is the infrastructure that prevents backlash from becoming the appeal.

The architecture needs one more element: an appeal clock.

Different harms run on different time.

A credit-report error may matter before a mortgage closing. A content removal may matter before an election or product launch. A worker deactivation may matter before rent is due. A medical authorization can become meaningless after the treatment window. A school-placement decision may matter before enrollment closes.

A generic promise to review within thirty days can therefore be fast for one case and absurdly slow for another.

Institutions should classify appeals by the harm clock, not only by internal queue age.

This is not a demand for instant review of everything. It is triage based on irreversibility.

A temporary stay can sometimes preserve the value of the appeal while the institution investigates. Continue a benefit briefly. Pause an irreversible deletion. Preserve access to earned funds. Prevent a disputed record from propagating while review occurs. Keep evidence from being destroyed.

The law uses stays and injunctions because a later victory can be useless after the contested action has already done the damage.

Automated institutions need the same intuition.

A right to appeal without a way to preserve the disputed interest can become a right to receive an apology after the loss is permanent.

Appeal independence also needs a stronger test than organizational charts.

A team can be formally separate and still share the same incentives, data, model output, supervisor, and performance targets as the first decision-maker.

Fresh eyes require some meaningful difference.

The reviewer may see evidence the original system did not.

The reviewer may use a different decision rule.

The reviewer may have authority outside the business unit whose metric benefited from the original result.

The reviewer may be a licensed professional whose duty runs to a patient or client rather than a cost target.

The reviewer may be an external arbitrator or regulator.

Independence is a reduction in correlated judgment.

That is the same principle used in auditing and security.

The person appealing also needs access to the state of the case.

What information does the institution believe?

Which field is wrong?

Which document is missing?

Which policy version applies?

Which model or rule materially contributed?

An appeal cannot be meaningful if the person is arguing with a hidden record.

This does not mean exposing trade secrets or another person’s private information. It means providing enough of the decisional state that the appellant can correct the institution’s premises.

Many disputes that look ideological are data-quality disputes.

The address was stale.

The credential was mismatched.

The treatment record did not arrive.

The fraud system linked the wrong device.

The applicant profile duplicated somebody else.

The generated summary omitted a qualifying fact.

A good appeal process makes correction cheaper than argument.

This suggests another useful metric: time to truth.

How long from the first adverse decision until the institution has the relevant accurate facts?

Resolution time can be longer because policy may still be contested. Time to truth isolates a simpler failure. Did the organization make it easy to repair the record?

The distinction matters because appeals often become slow while parties exchange the same incorrect information repeatedly.

A system designed around record correction can resolve many cases before they become legal disputes.

Appeal queues also create an operational temptation: optimize throughput instead of quality.

Once the backlog becomes visible, management measures closures per reviewer, average handling time, and cases older than a threshold. These metrics are reasonable. They can recreate the same automation pressure that caused the problem.

Reviewers learn to finish cases quickly.

Complex cases become performance liabilities.

The organization has automated the first decision and Taylorized the appeal.

Quality assurance has to sample the reviewers too.

Were reasons individualized?

Was new evidence considered?

Did reviewers have enough time?

Are particular reviewers or teams unusually reluctant to reverse?

Do high-volume periods correlate with lower reversal or higher re-appeal rates?

The appeals system is a decision system and deserves its own governance.

An ombuds function can help where the institution needs somebody authorized to look across queues rather than merely decide individual cases. The ombuds office can identify recurrent policy confusion, inaccessible notices, model versions associated with disputes, and areas where formal appeal rights do not produce practical relief.

The office should not become another powerless mailbox.

It needs access, independence, and a route to leadership or an external authority.

This is the pattern throughout the book.

A channel without power becomes theater.

Appeals can also reveal when the wrong unit of correction is the individual.

If five thousand people appeal the same failure, the institution should stop requiring five thousand adjudications.

Identify the cohort.

Pause the flawed rule.

Rerun affected decisions.

Notify people who never appealed.

Correct the system upstream.

The mature appeal process knows when to transform a case into an incident.

This is where automation can become accountability’s ally. Logs can identify all decisions produced under a bad configuration. Models can cluster similar complaints. Workflows can send corrections at scale. Humans can validate the remedy and authorize it.

The institution that can automate the adverse decision can often automate much of the restoration too.

Failure to do so is rarely a technical limitation alone.

It is a priority.

The right to appeal therefore ends where a broader duty begins.

Once the institution knows a systematic error exists, recourse should no longer depend on who had enough time, literacy, anger, or money to click the button.

A good appeal system corrects the appellant.

A great one learns who else never got to appeal.
