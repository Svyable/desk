# When Pressure Works

A successful backlash eventually becomes difficult to see.

The picket line comes down. The hearing room empties. The lawsuit becomes a docket entry. The viral post disappears beneath new outrage. The executive stops apologizing. What remains is a clause, a procedure, a product change, a rule, a budget, a dashboard, a new person with authority, or an old practice the institution no longer performs.

This is how pressure should be judged.

Not by the volume it creates.

By what becomes different after the volume is gone.

The 2023 Hollywood labor disputes provide unusually clear examples because the result can be read in contracts. Writers and performers entered negotiations with broad fears about artificial intelligence. The slogans were necessarily larger than the provisions. Writers worried that studios could use AI-generated material to undermine authorship, compensation, and employment. Performers worried about digital replicas, synthetic performances, consent, and reuse.

The settlements did not resolve every future use of AI.

They created enforceable boundaries inside particular labor relationships.

For writers, AI-generated material could not be treated as literary material in a way that displaced the contractual status of human writers. Companies could not require writers to use AI, while writers could use it under agreed conditions. Disclosure rules addressed AI-generated material supplied to writers. Broader training questions remained contested.

For performers, bargaining produced provisions around consent and compensation for digital replicas and related synthetic uses.

These are imperfect, industry-specific outcomes.

That is what makes them valuable.

Public pressure worked because the movement had three things an online backlash often lacks: an organized constituency, a counterparty capable of agreeing, and a mechanism for turning demands into enforceable text.

The strike was pressure.

The contract was governance.

This distinction should become a standard test for AI activism.

What is the conversion mechanism?

A boycott may change sales. A petition may change a policy. A shareholder campaign may change a board decision. A lawsuit may obtain damages or an injunction. A regulatory complaint may trigger investigation. A union can bargain. An election can replace officials. A procurement campaign can change what a government buys. An industry standard can make a practice difficult to sustain.

Without a conversion mechanism, pressure can remain expressive indefinitely.

Expression matters. It creates solidarity, signals values, surfaces information, and changes what leaders believe is politically possible. But expression alone is fragile. The institution can wait for attention to move.

The AI backlash has already produced examples of conversion across several domains.

The New Hampshire deepfake robocall produced enforcement under existing communications law, a multimillion-dollar FCC fine, carrier consequences, state charges, and clearer federal interpretation that AI-generated voices fall within restrictions on artificial or prerecorded robocalls.

The Rite Aid facial-recognition case produced a five-year prohibition on the retailer’s use of facial recognition for security or surveillance, along with required safeguards and data-related obligations.

Hollywood labor action produced contract terms.

Automated-hiring concerns produced local rules such as New York City’s bias-audit and notice requirements, alongside federal civil-rights guidance reminding employers that use of a vendor does not erase employer responsibility.

These outcomes differ in quality and scope. Some may prove too weak. Some may impose costs without enough benefit. Some will be revised.

They share a useful property.

Someone can verify whether the institution complied.

Verification is what makes pressure durable.

A company promising to “do better” creates no external fact unless better is defined. A company promising to publish an annual audit creates a date and a document. A regulator ordering deletion creates a requirement. A union clause creates a grievance process. A law creates duties and remedies.

The public can return later.

This is the difference between reputation management and accountability.

Reputation management aims to reduce current pressure.

Accountability creates future exposure if the commitment is broken.

Institutions often prefer the first because it preserves flexibility. Critics should prefer the second when the issue is serious enough to justify constraint.

This does not mean every controversy needs law.

Law is an expensive way to govern small mistakes. Companies can create meaningful voluntary commitments when those commitments are specific, measured, and costly to abandon. Industry standards can work when participants have incentives to maintain interoperability or trust. Insurers can improve appeals without legislation. AI companies can publish safety frameworks and submit systems to external testing.

The question is whether the commitment survives a change in leadership, market conditions, or public attention.

A promise attached only to a chief executive’s reputation is weak institutional memory.

A process embedded in contracts, systems, board reporting, or regulation is stronger.

Pressure works when it changes the default.

This is more powerful than changing an exception.

A viral complaint often produces exceptional service. The customer gets a refund after the chief executive’s office calls. The patient gets the denial reversed after a newspaper asks questions. The suspended account is restored after a famous person posts about it.

These corrections may be deserved. They do not improve the system if the next ordinary person faces the same barrier.

The institution has learned how to manage attention, not how to reduce error.

A successful pressure campaign asks what happened to the next person.

Did the form change?

Did the model change?

Did the threshold change?

Did the support team gain authority?

Did the appeal time shrink?

Did the data source change?

Did the company begin tracking reversal rates?

Did a new contract clause apply to every covered worker?

This is an operational definition of reform.

The next case should be less dependent on luck.

This standard also protects institutions from endless symbolic punishment. If a company changes the relevant process, critics should be able to recognize the change even if they still dislike the company. Otherwise public pressure becomes identity politics around brands: one corporation is permanently evil, another permanently responsible.

Institutions need a path to earn trust back.

Accountability without rehabilitation becomes vengeance.

The same is true for individuals. Executives, politicians, doctors, programmers, regulators, and workers make mistakes. Some commit misconduct. Some should lose positions or face legal consequences. Others should be able to correct a decision and continue.

A public sphere in which every error becomes permanent personal identity creates incentives to hide errors.

That is bad for AI safety because learning requires admission.

The best incident-reporting cultures in aviation, medicine, security, and other high-risk domains distinguish among bad luck, ordinary error, negligence, reckless behavior, and intentional wrongdoing. The distinctions are imperfect, but they matter because a system that punishes every reported mistake maximally will receive fewer reports.

AI institutions need the same maturity.

Public pressure should be severe enough that organizations take failures seriously and proportionate enough that organizations can reveal them.

This is difficult online because the crowd has no sentencing phase.

A screenshot arrives. People react. Context arrives later. The harshest participants can dominate. There is no formal procedure for saying the issue has been remedied.

The punishment can continue because attention has memory while institutions change invisibly.

This is one reason formal mechanisms matter even to companies. A regulator can close an investigation. A court can dismiss a claim or enter judgment. A union can settle a grievance. A board can complete a review. These endpoints do not erase criticism, but they create authoritative milestones.

The crowd rarely has one.

Pressure also works better when it targets an actor with control.

This sounds obvious and is frequently ignored.

People angry about AI-generated spam may target the model provider when the distribution platform controls reach. Artists may target users when the commercial client commissioned the imitation. Workers may target the tool when management chose the productivity target. Consumers may target a call-center worker who has no authority to change the policy. Voters may target a local official for a federal rule.

Misallocated pressure creates theater and leaves the mechanism intact.

The useful question is always: who can change the next decision?

That may be a CEO.

It may be a procurement manager.

It may be a regulator.

It may be a software vendor.

It may be Congress.

It may be a union bargaining committee.

It may be the person receiving a transaction request who can insist on a second approval.

Control is often lower in the hierarchy than symbolism.

This is why operational research matters to activism. A movement that understands the system can identify leverage points other people miss.

Environmental movements learned to target permitting, finance, supply chains, and standards rather than only individual polluters. Consumer-safety advocates learned to use recalls, liability, testing, and federal standards. Labor movements learned that bargaining units and contract clauses create more durable power than moral appeals to employers.

AI activism will mature the same way.

The first phase is cultural: make the problem visible.

The second is institutional: identify the decision path.

The third is procedural: create a rule that changes it.

The fourth is empirical: check whether the rule worked.

This sequence prevents backlash from becoming permanent ideology.

A campaign should be capable of discovering that its preferred intervention failed.

Suppose a disclosure rule produces so many labels that users stop noticing them. That is evidence.

Suppose a bias audit misses discrimination because the categories are poorly chosen. Revise it.

Suppose a model-provider restriction pushes abuse to open tools without reducing harm. Change strategy.

Suppose a human-review requirement creates rubber-stamping and longer delays. Measure it.

Public pressure that cannot update becomes another institution defending itself.

This is the humility activists often demand from technology companies and should practice themselves.

A cause does not become empirically correct because its moral concern is legitimate.

Pressure works when it is falsifiable.

This may sound technocratic for a book about anger. It is how anger survives contact with reality.

The strongest reform movements in history combined moral force with institutional competence. Civil-rights organizers understood courts, federal agencies, voter registration, local power, churches, media, labor, and law. Consumer advocates learned technical standards. Environmental groups learned permitting and administrative procedure. Disability-rights activists converted dignity claims into architectural, employment, communication, and accommodation requirements.

The public did not need every participant to become an expert.

The movement needed expertise somewhere.

AI backlash will require its own institutional experts: people who understand model evaluation, employment workflow, insurance appeals, provenance standards, telecommunications, copyright licensing, platform policy, identity systems, and administrative law.

This is less glamorous than having the right opinion about artificial general intelligence.

It is more likely to change Tuesday afternoon for somebody dealing with a bad system.

Pressure works when it gives ordinary people leverage without requiring extraordinary attention.

That is the standard worth keeping.

A patient should not need a senator.

An applicant should not need a journalist.

A worker should not need a walkout to correct a broken evaluation.

A creator should not need millions of followers to enforce consent.

A consumer should not need a viral post to make a company honor the representation its chatbot made.

A voter should not need technical expertise to know whether an official campaign message is authentic.

The purpose of public pressure is not to make every dispute public.

It is to create institutions that can handle disputes before publicity becomes the only functioning appeal.

This suggests another test of successful reform: does it reduce the need for itself?

A good fire code produces fewer catastrophic fires and therefore fewer campaigns after catastrophe. A reliable refund process produces fewer viral complaints. A functioning whistleblower channel produces fewer leaks. A meaningful AI incident process produces fewer surprises at hearings.

The movement that improves the system may lose visible relevance because the system stops generating the same grievance.

That is success, not obsolescence.

Organizations have trouble recognizing this because they often measure pressure by volume. Fewer complaints can mean improvement. It can also mean people gave up.

The institution needs independent indicators.

Are decisions being reversed less often because the first decision improved, or because appeals became harder?

Are workers reporting fewer concerns because conditions improved, or because they fear retaliation?

Are creators filing fewer complaints because licensing works, or because enforcement is too costly?

Metrics need interpretation.

This returns us to AI itself. Institutions will increasingly use models to summarize complaints, detect emerging themes, prioritize cases, and predict which issues will escalate.

That can improve responsiveness.

It can also automate the institution’s ability to ignore quiet people.

A complaint-triage model trained on past escalation may learn that famous users, lawyers, journalists, and politically connected people are more expensive to disappoint. The system can rationally prioritize reputational risk over underlying harm.

Public pressure can corrupt recourse if the institution optimizes for pressure rather than justice.

The answer is to separate severity from visibility.

A good complaints system asks how serious the underlying problem is, not only how likely the person is to cause trouble.

This sounds elementary.

It is not how incentives naturally work.

Executives receive escalations precisely because somebody caused trouble. Organizations then infer that trouble identifies importance. AI can encode the inference at scale.

A mature accountability system deliberately protects cases with low publicity potential.

Random audits help.

Independent reviewers help.

Published service standards help.

Automatic review of high-stakes denials helps.

Whistleblower protection helps.

External ombuds offices can help.

The design should make justice less correlated with audience size.

This is the deepest measure of whether public pressure worked.

Did it increase the power of the public only when the public is watching?

Or did it change how the institution treats the person nobody is watching?

The first outcome is spectacle.

The second is governance.

The AI backlash will produce a great deal of spectacle because the technology itself is spectacular. Models talk. Images appear from text. Voices return from a few seconds of recording. CEOs make claims about intelligence and civilization. Politicians describe races with geopolitical stakes. Scammers create impossible scenes.

The institutional response does not need to match the spectacle.

It can be a checkbox that actually carries authority.

A second signature.

A disclosure that means something.

A contract clause.

A right to a human review.

A log retained long enough to reconstruct a decision.

A regulator with jurisdiction.

A refund because the company’s bot spoke for the company.

Small mechanisms can govern large technologies.

That is what pressure looks like when it works.

The final test is maintenance.

Reform has a half-life.

The people who negotiated the clause leave. A company reorganizes. A regulator changes leadership. A vendor replaces a product. The crisis that made everyone attentive becomes a paragraph in an annual report. The control remains on paper while the incentives that produced the original problem slowly return.

Successful pressure therefore needs an owner after victory.

Who checks the rule next year?

Who pays for the audit?

Who receives the report?

Who can reopen the issue if the metric worsens?

When does the commitment expire?

Which part survives a merger, acquisition, model migration, or change in political administration?

These are maintenance questions, and movements are often structurally weak at answering them because mobilization rewards novelty.

A campaign attracts energy by saying something must change now.

Governance requires somebody to say the same thing in a quieter room three years later.

The most durable reforms therefore create maintenance institutions rather than merely victory statements.

A union creates stewards and grievance procedures.

A regulator creates examinations, reporting cycles, and enforcement staff.

A court order can create continuing obligations.

A company can put a metric into board reporting or internal audit.

A standard can create periodic certification.

A procurement contract can make compliance a renewal condition.

The mechanism survives because somebody has a job that depends on remembering it.

This suggests a useful concept for public pressure: the conversion ratio.

How much of the attention became a durable change in default behavior?

A million angry posts that produce one temporary exception have a low conversion ratio.

A smaller campaign that produces a rule applied to every future case may have a high one.

The ratio cannot be reduced to a number because the value of reforms differs enormously. The concept is useful because it asks activists and institutions to distinguish energy from yield.

Where did the pressure go?

Into a person’s resignation?

A new policy?

A budget?

A technical control?

A legally enforceable right?

A practice that can be audited?

A change that expired after the news cycle?

This is the accounting system movements rarely keep.

They should.

The institution should keep it too because the same record can reveal which public complaints predicted real operational problems and which did not. Over time, the organization can improve how it listens.

Another test is displacement.

A pressure campaign can succeed locally while moving the underlying harm elsewhere.

A platform bans a category of synthetic impersonation and the scammers move to encrypted messaging.

A jurisdiction imposes strict rules and the risky service moves offshore.

A company eliminates one automated screening tool and managers reproduce the same bias informally.

A labor contract protects one bargaining unit while contractors absorb the displaced work.

A model provider closes access to one misuse while open systems remain available.

The local rule may still be worthwhile.

Victory should include checking the next channel.

This is why mature reform requires system boundaries broad enough to detect substitution without becoming so broad that every adjacent problem is claimed as the same campaign.

Specificity remains the discipline.

The goal was not “end bad things involving AI.”

The goal was to change a defined mechanism.

Did the mechanism change?

Did the harm fall?

What substituted for it?

What new cost appeared?

These questions create something movements rarely articulate clearly: an exit condition.

What would count as enough?

A company cannot respond to criticism if no possible action can close the claim. A regulator cannot administer a rule if compliance never changes the moral status of the regulated actor. A movement cannot learn if every partial success is automatically redefined as failure because the larger world remains imperfect.

An exit condition does not mean permanent approval.

It means specifying what resolution looks like for this dispute.

The denied patient receives timely review and the systemic error is corrected.

The performer receives the contractual protection that was demanded.

The deceptive robocall is punished and carriers adopt the required controls.

The hiring system is audited, the identified flaw is fixed, and outcomes are monitored.

The campaign can then move from escalation to watchfulness.

This is not surrender.

It is institutional confidence.

The crowd proved it could create pressure.

The institution proved it could convert pressure into a durable rule.

Both should be capable of recognizing when that conversion occurred.

Otherwise backlash becomes a permanent identity rather than a tool.

Public pressure works when it can stop shouting without losing the change it won.
