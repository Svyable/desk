# When Pressure Breaks

A society needs ways to make powerful people uncomfortable.

It also needs limits on what discomfort can become.

The two statements belong together because institutions often invoke the second to weaken the first. A corporation facing organized criticism can describe activists as dangerous. A politician can call scrutiny harassment. A public figure can treat every hostile question as a threat. Security language can become a way to avoid accountability.

The reverse mistake is worse.

A legitimate grievance can become a moral permission slip for cruelty.

The killing of Brian Thompson made this boundary impossible to treat as theoretical. The public reaction contained anger at health insurers, personal testimony about denied claims, dark humor, political argument, and in some corners celebration of a murder. Afterward, companies reevaluated executive security and law-enforcement agencies paid more attention to threats against corporate leaders.

The institutional grievance and the individual killing became tangled because the victim was the chief executive of a company many people associated with painful experiences.

Untangling them requires a rule that should survive every ideology.

Violence against a person is not a substitute for due process against an institution.

The reason is not only moral.

It is structural.

Political violence selects targets by vulnerability, not responsibility. The executive with the least security may be easier to reach than the person with the most power. A front-line employee may be attacked for a policy created elsewhere. A judge can be threatened because somebody dislikes a ruling. An election worker can be harassed for administering a process. A scientist can receive threats because a rumor attached a name to a controversy.

Fear reallocates public life toward whoever is most willing to intimidate.

That is the opposite of accountability.

The AI era increases the risk because targeting information and persuasive media become cheaper to create. A false accusation can acquire synthetic screenshots. A fake recording can make a person appear to confess. An address can be circulated alongside a generated narrative. Bots can make a fringe accusation look socially validated. Harassment can be translated and personalized at scale.

The cost of accusing falls faster than the cost of disproving.

This asymmetry is the dark side of the accountability tools described throughout the book.

The same model that helps a patient summarize insurance documents can help a harasser summarize a target’s personal history.

The same image tool that helps a campaign produce legitimate media can fabricate a scene.

The same search capability that helps journalists connect public records can be used maliciously to intensify a campaign against an individual.

Technology does not erase the distinction between investigation and targeting.

Institutions need to preserve it.

Doxxing illustrates the boundary. The word is used loosely, sometimes to describe publication of any personal information and sometimes to describe intentional exposure of private identifying information for harassment. Journalists may publish an official’s business relationships because they are relevant to public accountability. A citizen may identify the person who holds a public office. Those acts are not equivalent to circulating a family member’s private contact information with an invitation to intimidate.

Context and intent matter.

So does necessity.

Public pressure should ask whether a piece of personal information is necessary to establish the public claim.

The chief executive’s compensation may be relevant to an argument about incentives.

The chief executive’s child’s school is not.

A regulator’s official conduct may be relevant.

The home address of a career employee usually is not.

The distinction becomes harder when information is publicly searchable. Availability is not the same as justification for amplification.

AI collapses that practical difference by making obscure public records easier to aggregate. Facts that once required hours of searching can be assembled in seconds. A person can become legible to a hostile crowd in a way the individual records never made possible.

Privacy law has long struggled with aggregation. The synthetic era makes aggregation an ordinary capability.

This should change norms around responsible criticism.

A serious public campaign minimizes irrelevant personal exposure.

It identifies institutional roles rather than families.

It preserves evidence.

It corrects false claims.

It distinguishes allegations from findings.

It gives targets a meaningful opportunity to respond when factual disputes matter.

These are not courtesies to powerful people.

They are safeguards against making the campaign itself unaccountable.

The public may resist this because procedural restraint looks weak next to moral certainty.

Certainty is emotionally efficient.

A complicated institution produces a harm. A visible leader is selected. Every fact about the leader is interpreted through the harm. The person becomes a villain. Once that happens, evidence has only one permitted direction.

This is how accountability turns into scapegoating.

A scapegoat can be genuinely powerful.

That does not make the mechanism safer.

The CEO may have approved harmful policies. A politician may have voted for a damaging law. A founder may have deceived customers. A convicted fraudster may have caused enormous losses. Public condemnation can be appropriate.

The boundary is not niceness.

It is proportionality and evidence.

Fraud should be called fraud when established.

An allegation should be called an allegation when unresolved.

A bad policy should be criticized as policy.

A crime should be prosecuted as crime.

A person should not acquire infinite moral liability because the institution is unpopular.

This principle protects ordinary people more than elites.

Chief executives can hire security. Politicians can receive police protection. Companies have lawyers and communications staff. The employee mistakenly identified online, the local official, the teacher, the election worker, the small-business owner, or the person with the same name as somebody in a viral post may have none of those resources.

Synthetic evidence makes mistaken identity more dangerous.

The mob can be wrong at scale.

This is not hypothetical in the broader internet era. Public crowds have repeatedly misidentified suspects after crimes, spread false accusations, and subjected innocent people to harassment. AI adds tools for producing more convincing supporting material and for sustaining attacks automatically.

A system that rewards speed creates incentives to accuse before verification.

Platforms participate because outrage is engagement. Their recommendation systems can turn a small dispute into a large audience. This does not mean an algorithm “caused” every harassment campaign. It means distribution design influences how quickly a campaign escapes its original context.

Platforms face a difficult tradeoff.

If they intervene aggressively, they may suppress legitimate criticism of powerful people. If they wait for certainty, coordinated harassment can become irreversible. If they build policies around protected classes only, other forms of targeted abuse can remain. If they define harassment too broadly, public officials can use the rules against scrutiny.

The solution is not a single content rule.

It is a layered response based on conduct.

Threats can be treated differently from criticism.

Repeated unwanted contact can be treated differently from public commentary.

Publication of sensitive personal information can be treated differently from analysis of public corporate filings.

Impersonation can be treated differently from parody.

Coordinated inauthentic activity can be treated differently from many real people arriving at the same opinion.

The categories will still produce hard cases.

Specificity improves the argument.

Public pressure breaks in another way when punishment becomes economically unlimited.

A person can lose employment, contracts, friends, professional standing, platform access, and personal safety because of one disputed event. Sometimes the event is serious enough to justify many consequences. Sometimes each institution acts independently based on the same viral evidence, producing cumulative punishment nobody designed.

The crowd has created a distributed sanction system.

No single actor sees the total penalty.

This resembles the distributed-responsibility problem inside corporations. Everyone can say they made one reasonable decision.

The aggregate becomes extreme.

A society with no formal right to rehabilitation can create informal life sentences.

This matters for AI because synthetic evidence increases uncertainty precisely where permanent punishment is most dangerous.

Institutions should be slower to impose irreversible sanctions when the underlying evidence is easy to fabricate and difficult to authenticate.

That does not mean refusing to act until absolute certainty. Employers and platforms often need to make interim decisions. A credible threat may require immediate suspension or security measures.

Reversibility becomes the key.

Temporary restrictions while evidence is verified are different from permanent public condemnation.

A review process can restore access.

A correction can update the record.

A platform can preserve content for investigation even if it removes it from distribution.

An employer can distinguish administrative leave from a finding of misconduct.

Pressure is safer when institutions can change their minds.

Crowds are bad at changing their minds because membership is fluid. The people who amplified the accusation may not see the correction. Nobody owns the obligation to notify them.

This is the correction asymmetry.

The original claim has a protagonist and emotion.

The correction has a paragraph.

AI can help here too. Platforms can attach later context. Search systems can surface updates. News organizations can link corrections to original stories. Companies can maintain incident pages with status changes. Provenance systems can preserve the history of claims.

But technical design cannot make people care about being wrong.

That is a norm.

A healthy public-pressure culture celebrates correction as evidence of seriousness rather than weakness.

Activists should want to remove false examples from their case because weak evidence gives institutions a reason to dismiss strong evidence.

Journalists already operate under versions of this norm. Courts formalize it. Science depends on revision. Political movements are more tempted to treat every retraction as ammunition for opponents.

AI raises the cost of that temptation.

There will be more plausible false evidence.

Movements that cannot retract will become easy to poison.

An adversary can feed a campaign a spectacular fake, wait for amplification, then expose it. The entire cause acquires a credibility problem. This is a classic disinformation tactic made easier by synthetic media.

Epistemic discipline becomes movement security.

Verify before escalating.

Preserve the original artifact.

Separate what is known from what is inferred.

Do not use a fake merely because it expresses something you believe is spiritually true about the target.

That last temptation is powerful. People sometimes defend fabricated content by saying it captures a larger truth. Satire can legitimately use invention when the invention is recognizable as satire. Evidence cannot.

A movement that cannot distinguish art from evidence will eventually accuse an innocent person.

Pressure also breaks when it becomes totalizing.

An AI company makes a mistake, therefore every employee is complicit.

A health insurer denies a claim, therefore every denial is malicious.

A cryptocurrency exchange commits fraud, therefore every cryptographic asset is a scam.

A politician uses AI deceptively, therefore every synthetic campaign tool is illegitimate.

Total categories eliminate learning.

They turn empirical questions into tribe markers.

This is especially dangerous because public opinion about AI is already shaped by a large gap between experts and the general public. Pew Research Center found in surveys fielded in 2024 that 51 percent of U.S. adults were more concerned than excited about increased AI use, compared with 15 percent of surveyed AI experts. The public was much more likely than experts to think AI would personally harm them and to expect fewer jobs. Yet both groups showed substantial concern about inaccurate information, impersonation, bias, and weak oversight.

The gap is not proof the public is ignorant or experts are captured.

It is information about different positions in the system.

Experts see capabilities and technical possibilities up close.

The public experiences uncertainty about jobs, institutions, identity, and control.

A productive political system needs a way for these perspectives to correct one another.

Backlash becomes destructive when either group denies the other standing.

“People are afraid because they do not understand the technology” is sometimes true and politically useless as a complete explanation.

“Experts support AI because they are paid by the industry” is sometimes relevant and equally useless as a universal claim.

Distrust cannot be argued away by insulting the distrusting person.

It has to be reduced through performance.

Companies need to show responsible behavior.

Governments need to show competent oversight.

Critics need to show accurate evidence.

Movements need to show that their remedies work.

This reciprocity is the opposite of mob logic.

The mob says only the target has obligations.

Accountability says every participant does.

The powerful actor has greater duties because power creates greater capacity for harm. The critic still has duties to truth and proportionality. The platform has duties created by distribution power. The government has duties created by coercive authority.

Public pressure becomes legitimate through this network of obligations.

It breaks when one participant claims moral exemption.

That is why the chapter began with the need to make powerful people uncomfortable.

Comfort is not the standard.

A CEO may deserve a hostile hearing.

A politician may deserve to lose an election.

A company may deserve a boycott.

A fraudulent founder may deserve prison after conviction.

A regulator may deserve public criticism for failure.

An AI lab may deserve employees resigning in protest.

These are forms of pressure inside institutional boundaries.

The boundary is not that criticism must be polite.

It is that criticism remains attached to evidence and remedy rather than becoming a license to intimidate human beings outside process.

Violence destroys this distinction fastest because it makes fear the enforcement mechanism.

Doxxing, threats, fabricated evidence, and coordinated harassment can move in the same direction without physical harm occurring.

They privatize coercion.

A democratic society should be suspicious when corporations try to escape scrutiny by calling it coercion.

It should be equally suspicious when crowds try to justify coercion by calling it scrutiny.

Public pressure works only while both remain different things.

The distinction becomes especially difficult for institutions responsible for security because they have to act before uncertainty disappears.

A company receives a threat against an executive. Is it rhetorical rage, fantasy, a joke in bad taste, or preparation for violence?

A university receives thousands of messages about a controversial researcher. Are they criticism, coordinated harassment, or both?

A platform sees users publishing public-record information about an official. Is the purpose accountability or intimidation?

Security teams cannot wait for philosophical certainty.

They can still preserve the difference between risk management and retaliation.

That requires role separation.

The security team assesses credible threats.

The legal team evaluates duties and evidence.

The public-affairs team does not get to label criticism dangerous merely because it is damaging.

The executive being criticized should not unilaterally determine which critics become security subjects.

The person under investigation should have some route to challenge consequential errors where disclosure would not create additional risk.

These separations matter because security authority is powerful precisely when it is opaque.

Companies may legitimately keep threat assessments confidential. They should not use confidentiality to convert uncomfortable scrutiny into a category that nobody outside the company can examine.

The same is true for governments. Law-enforcement monitoring of threats can protect public servants. Political surveillance of lawful dissent can suppress democracy. The boundary depends on conduct, authority, and oversight rather than whether officials feel endangered by criticism.

Public pressure therefore needs a security constitution.

Threats are investigated.

Criticism remains criticism.

Private information unrelated to the public claim is minimized.

Evidence of imminent harm receives rapid escalation.

Political disagreement does not become a risk score by default.

The state and the company both need checks on the people making these classifications.

Synthetic media creates another problem: evidentiary quarantine.

When a spectacular recording, screenshot, or document emerges, the pressure system wants immediate circulation. A safer norm is to distinguish evidence worth preserving from evidence ready for amplification.

Preserve the file.

Record where it came from.

Ask for the original.

Check whether the source has independent corroboration.

Do not destroy or edit the artifact in ways that make later verification impossible.

Then decide what can responsibly be claimed.

This is not a demand that ordinary citizens become forensic laboratories. It is a norm for institutions and movements making consequential accusations.

The higher the requested sanction, the stronger the verification burden should become.

A claim worth investigating can justify inquiry.

A claim used to fire somebody, revoke a license, destroy a professional relationship, or trigger a criminal accusation needs more.

This is proportional evidence.

The concept is useful because online culture often treats publication and judgment as one event. Somebody posts the artifact and the social network begins sentencing immediately.

A healthier system separates critic, investigator, and adjudicator.

The critic raises the claim.

The investigator tests it.

The adjudicator decides the consequence.

One person can sometimes occupy more than one role, but the roles should remain conceptually different.

The distinction protects both directions of power. A corporation should not get to investigate and acquit itself automatically. A crowd should not get to accuse and punish automatically.

This is ordinary procedural wisdom applied to an attention system that tries to collapse every step into one feed.

Distributed punishment needs similar restraint.

An employer, platform, professional association, payment provider, publisher, and conference organizer may each make an independently defensible decision based on the same allegation. The combined effect can be economic disappearance.

No single institution intended that outcome.

That does not mean the outcome is automatically unjust. Some conduct legitimately affects many relationships. A fraud conviction can matter to employers, regulators, investors, and customers at once.

The danger is when all institutions are acting on the same unresolved evidence while assuming somebody else verified it.

The result is evidentiary leverage without evidentiary ownership.

A practical norm would ask each institution imposing a serious sanction to identify the basis it is relying on.

A court finding?

A regulator’s order?

Its own investigation?

A credible external investigation?

A viral allegation?

The answer does not have to be public in every case. It should exist.

This creates accountability for cumulative sanctions without requiring one central authority to control every private decision.

Corrections need a distribution system too.

If a platform meaningfully amplified a false allegation, attaching a correction only to a later post may not reach the audience that saw the original. Search systems can preserve stale accusations after the source has retracted them. News stories can remain prominent even after acquittal or correction because the original event was more newsworthy.

No system can guarantee equal attention for correction.

Institutions can reduce the asymmetry.

Update the original page.

Link the correction to the initial claim.

Change summaries and search snippets where appropriate.

Notify people directly affected by an institutional error.

Preserve the history rather than silently rewriting it.

The goal is not forgetting.

It is informational due process.

A record should be capable of changing state when the facts change.

This matters more as AI assistants become intermediaries to public memory. A model asked about a person may summarize years of articles without understanding that an early allegation was later disproven unless the information environment encodes the correction clearly.

Cheap memory can preserve cheap accusation forever.

Responsible information systems need status, provenance, and revision.

The same principle applies to movements themselves. A campaign should maintain a correction log for material factual claims when stakes are high. That sounds bureaucratic because truth maintenance is bureaucratic.

Which claims were withdrawn?

Which were narrowed?

Which remain allegations?

Which were established by a court, regulator, audit, or independent investigation?

A movement capable of maintaining that ledger becomes harder to discredit with one planted fake and easier for outsiders to trust when it makes a new claim.

The burden is worth carrying because legitimacy is cumulative.

Pressure breaks when winning today becomes more important than being credible tomorrow.

It also breaks when institutions use the existence of bad actors to delegitimize everyone applying pressure.

One threat does not turn a boycott into violence.

One fabricated post does not make every patient story false.

One harasser does not transform journalism into harassment.

Collective action should not inherit infinite liability for the worst person who claims association with it any more than a corporation should escape responsibility by blaming every failure on one rogue employee.

The standard should remain control.

What conduct did the movement encourage, tolerate, correct, or condemn?

What conduct did the company enable, reward, or ignore?

Responsibility follows what an actor could reasonably shape.

This symmetry keeps the boundary stable.

Power does not gain immunity by pointing to mobs.

Mobs do not gain legitimacy by pointing to power.

The purpose of public pressure is to make authority answerable.

The moment pressure becomes a private authority that no one can answer back to, it has reproduced the problem it claimed to solve.
