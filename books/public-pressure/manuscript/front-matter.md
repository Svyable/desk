# Public Pressure

*AI, Anger, and the Revolt Against Institutions That Decide for Us*

Sven Hardy Benson

Working draft — August 2026

There is a temptation, when a new technology arrives inside an old institution, to tell the story as if the technology replaced the institution. It rarely does.

A health insurer that uses prediction software is still an insurer. A political campaign that uses synthetic media is still trying to persuade, mislead, mobilize, or survive. A technology company training a model on other people’s work still operates through contracts, capital, labor, copyright, procurement, and reputation. A crypto promoter with an AI-generated voice is still running a confidence game. The machinery changes. The incentives usually arrive first.

This book is about what happens when that machinery becomes fast enough, cheap enough, and opaque enough that the people on the receiving end begin looking for a person to hold responsible.

They find the chief executive. The founder. The senator. The regulator. The doctor whose name appears on a denial. The celebrity whose face has been cloned. The customer-service worker reading what the system allows them to say. Sometimes they find the right decision-maker. Often they find a symbol. Occasionally they find a stranger who had little to do with the event at all.

Public pressure is not new. Boycotts, strikes, hearings, investigative journalism, lawsuits, shareholder campaigns, elections, petitions, protests, whistleblowing, satire, and ordinary complaint are old instruments. So are mobs, rumors, threats, scapegoats, frauds, and political theater. What is changing is the speed with which these instruments can be aimed, copied, automated, and turned back on the institutions that use automation themselves.

Artificial intelligence sits in both positions. It is an object of anger and a tool for producing anger. It helps institutions classify, rank, predict, deny, recommend, price, monitor, moderate, and communicate. It also helps outsiders search records, summarize documents, generate campaigns, impersonate executives, manufacture evidence, flood comment systems, translate grievances, and coordinate attention. The same general-purpose capability can make bureaucracy more legible and public life less trustworthy.

The central argument of *Public Pressure* is narrower than “AI is causing a backlash.” Distrust was already present. Many of the institutions in this book had earned criticism long before generative models became consumer products. What AI does is change the pressure gradient. It reduces the cost of institutional action, the cost of imitation, the cost of organizing a response, and sometimes the cost of making a mistake at scale. It also widens the gap between the person affected by a decision and the person who can explain or reverse it.

That gap is where pressure accumulates.

The book therefore treats backlash as an accountability problem rather than a mood. Who made the decision? Who benefited? Who can inspect the evidence? Who can appeal? Who can change the rule? Who has to answer when a system fails? What happens when the public’s chosen target is not actually the person with the relevant authority? And what happens when legitimate grievance mutates into cruelty, fantasy, or violence?

Those distinctions matter. The killing of a corporate executive is not consumer advocacy. A threat is not an argument. A false accusation does not become true because an institution has behaved badly elsewhere. The fact that a crowd is angry can be socially important without making the crowd correct.

The reverse is also true. Institutional language can become a way of avoiding the substance of a complaint. “The model,” “the process,” “the policy,” “the market,” and “the system” are often useful descriptions. They can also make agency disappear. A system was purchased. A threshold was selected. A data set was accepted. An appeal path was designed. A business model was funded. An executive signed something. A regulator chose a jurisdiction. A legislature wrote a statute or declined to write one.

Responsibility may be distributed. It is rarely absent.

This is a work of narrative and analytical nonfiction, not a catalog of villains. It distinguishes allegations from findings, public anger from evidence, and technological possibility from documented use. The cases are here because they reveal mechanisms: how opaque decisions become personal, how reputational pressure travels, how organized labor translates fear into contract language, how regulators convert scandal into rules, how scammers borrow the prestige of technology, and how institutions can design recourse before the crowd tries to design it for them.

The subject is public pressure. The deeper subject is whether a society that delegates more decisions to machines can keep human responsibility attached to the decisions that still matter.
