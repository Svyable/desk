# The Customer Becomes a Crowd

A complaint used to have an address.

You wrote a letter. You called a number. You went back to the store. If the problem was large enough, you might contact a newspaper, a regulator, a lawyer, a union, a consumer group, or an elected official. The complaint could travel, but somebody had to carry it.

Now a complaint can wake up already public.

A customer records a malfunction. A worker posts a screenshot. A patient describes an insurance denial. A creator compares an AI output with an illustration style. A voter receives a strange robocall. Somebody else adds context. Somebody removes context. A third person finds an old executive interview. A fourth turns it into a clip. Within hours, thousands of people who were never part of the original event are arguing about what it means.

The individual complaint has become a network object.

This is not simply social media. Social media is the current infrastructure for something older: the conversion of private grievance into collective leverage.

Boycotts predate the word boycott. Workers have struck for centuries. Consumers have organized against prices, working conditions, discrimination, dangerous products, and political causes. Investors have coordinated votes and campaigns. Religious groups have pressured merchants. Newspapers have exposed business practices. Governments have used public hearings to turn scattered stories into evidence of a pattern.

The difference now is transaction cost.

Collective action has always had a coordination problem. People may share a grievance without knowing one another. They may disagree on goals. They may have little time. Organizers need names, meeting places, printing, distribution, money, volunteers, information, and some way to decide whether other people are participating. A boycott works better if enough people believe other people are boycotting too.

Networks reduce many of these costs.

AI reduces some of them again.

A person can summarize a long policy document, translate a complaint, generate a form letter, search a public filing, compare terms, extract dates, draft a petition, build a simple website, or produce a video script with tools that were either unavailable or expensive a few years earlier. None of these actions guarantees truth. They do make organization cheaper.

Cheap organization changes which complaints become pressure.

Consider consumer advocacy before the internet. Ralph Nader’s 1965 book *Unsafe at Any Speed* became a landmark in the politics of automobile safety because it converted technical design questions and accident data into a public argument. The work did not succeed because one customer was angry with one car. It succeeded because a specific set of claims became legible enough for journalists, legislators, lawyers, and consumers to treat them as a public issue.

The pressure eventually contributed to a broader environment in which federal automobile-safety regulation expanded. The mechanism was not merely outrage. It was evidence plus organization plus institutional leverage.

That combination remains powerful.

What the network era changes is the number of people who can attempt it.

The classic consumer advocate required access to researchers, publishers, editors, legal expertise, and mass media. A modern critic may still need all of those things to establish a difficult claim. But the first move is much cheaper. A person can publish immediately. Other people can test the claim. Specialists can appear from elsewhere. Documents can circulate without a newspaper deciding they are important.

This produces genuine democratic value.

It also produces a great deal of nonsense.

The same system that lets a nurse explain a billing practice lets a stranger confidently misread a medical code. The same system that lets a security researcher document a software failure lets a conspiracy theory accumulate screenshots. The same tools that help a worker search an employment policy can help somebody manufacture a fake memo. Networked complaint improves the ability of truth to travel and the ability of error to recruit.

Artificial intelligence makes both processes more efficient.

The central public-pressure problem is therefore not voice. Voice is abundant. Verification is scarce.

This is a reversal from much of the twentieth century. When access to publication was expensive, the major accountability question was often who could get heard. Gatekeepers could exclude legitimate complaints. Editors, broadcasters, political parties, professional associations, and large organizations controlled distribution.

The internet weakened those gates. The result was not a clean market of ideas. It was a new problem: attention became the gate.

Attention favors properties that are not identical to truth. Novelty helps. Anger helps. surprise helps. Visual evidence helps even when misleading. A clear villain helps. A short causal story helps. A claim that confirms an audience’s expectations travels more easily than a technically accurate explanation that distributes responsibility across four institutions and three statutes.

Public pressure therefore has a narrative bias.

It wants a beginning, a harm, a culprit, and a remedy.

Real systems often refuse to provide them in that order.

A health-insurance dispute may involve a hospital that coded a service incorrectly, a plan rule the employer chose, a utilization-management policy, a federal regulation, and an appeal deadline. A social-media harm may involve user behavior, recommendation design, moderation policy, age verification, parental supervision, criminal actors, and legal limits on platform liability. An AI model may generate a false statement because of training data, inference behavior, a third-party wrapper, prompt design, retrieval errors, or a user who deliberately elicited it.

The crowd asks, “Who did this?”

The answer may be a graph.

Graphs are poor protest signs.

This tension encourages institutions to underestimate public anger because the public’s causal story is imperfect. A company may correctly point out that a viral complaint omitted relevant details and then incorrectly conclude that there is no underlying problem. Critics may notice one real failure and then overgeneralize it into a theory that every decision is corrupt.

The most consequential pressure campaigns survive this stage by becoming more precise.

Labor unions are good at this because they have institutional memory for converting broad fear into enforceable terms. A writer can say, “AI will take our jobs.” That is emotionally clear but contractually useless. A bargaining team has to ask narrower questions. Can AI-generated material be treated as source material? Can a company require a writer to use an AI tool? Must AI-generated material be disclosed? Does training on writers’ work violate existing rights? Who receives credit? What is the compensation structure?

The Writers Guild of America’s 2023 agreement did not answer every question about artificial intelligence. It translated anxiety into clauses.

SAG-AFTRA’s 2023 bargaining did something similar for performers. The slogan-level fear was easy to state: a studio could scan an actor and reuse a digital version indefinitely. The contractual problem required distinctions among creating a digital replica, using it, obtaining informed consent, compensating performers, digitally altering performances, and using synthetic performers not based on a specific person.

The union had to take a crowd concern and turn it into a specification.

That is what mature public pressure does.

It specifies.

A consumer movement identifies a defect. A regulator defines a prohibited practice. A court determines whether a claim fits a cause of action. A contract assigns rights. A standards body defines an interface. A legislature describes who must do what, by when, and with what exception.

The move from anger to specification is difficult because specificity creates disagreement inside the coalition.

Everyone can agree that “AI should be fair.” Fairness has competing definitions. Everyone can agree that “people should control their likeness.” What counts as control when a performer has already licensed a character? Everyone can agree that “algorithms should be transparent.” Full transparency can expose trade secrets, private data, or security mechanisms. Everyone can agree that “deepfakes should be labeled.” Labels become harder when synthetic elements are minor or when malicious actors ignore the rule.

Vague pressure is socially easy. Governance is where the factions arrive.

This is why some campaigns prefer to remain at the level of outrage. The coalition is stronger when nobody has to reveal what they would actually accept.

Technology companies sometimes exploit this by treating disagreement among critics as evidence that no action is justified. The argument runs: experts disagree, users want different things, regulation might have unintended consequences, therefore we should continue experimenting.

That can be prudent in a genuinely uncertain domain. It can also be a strategy for converting uncertainty into permission.

Public pressure works best when it can preserve uncertainty without surrendering agency.

A rule can be provisional. An audit can be repeated. A threshold can be revised. A regulation can apply only to high-risk uses. A contract can expire and be renegotiated. A pilot can be limited. Accountability does not require pretending the future is known.

It requires refusing to treat uncertainty as nobody’s responsibility.

AI creates a special challenge because the same technology that makes public pressure easier can counterfeit public pressure itself.

An organization receiving ten thousand comments once had some reason to infer that many people took the time to write. That inference is weaker now. A single person can generate variants. A political campaign can automate outreach. Bots can imitate support. A company can hire an agency to create the appearance of grassroots sentiment. A critic can do the same.

The number of messages becomes less informative about the number of minds behind them.

This will matter enormously for democratic institutions whose consultation systems were designed around costly human participation. Public-comment periods, petition thresholds, constituent email, customer reviews, app ratings, support tickets, letters to boards, and community forums all use volume as a weak signal of importance.

When language becomes cheap, volume becomes cheap.

Institutions will be tempted to respond by discounting mass participation. That would recreate the old gatekeeping problem in a new form. A person should not have to prove biological authorship before complaining about a utility bill. People with limited literacy may reasonably use an assistant to express a real grievance. Translation tools let more people participate. Disabled users may depend on synthetic speech. AI assistance can make public processes more accessible.

The relevant unit is not the document. It is the accountable participant.

That sounds simple until identity and privacy collide. A regulator may want to know that a comment comes from a real resident without publishing the resident’s identity. A shareholder platform may need to know that a vote is authorized. A consumer-review system wants to distinguish buyers from farms of fake accounts. A political campaign wants supporters to contact representatives without turning participation into a surveillance database.

Public pressure is moving toward a provenance problem.

Who is behind this signal?

The answer does not always need to be public. It does need to exist somewhere trustworthy enough for the signal to carry weight.

This is an area where AI backlash and AI infrastructure begin to merge. The public complains about synthetic content, and institutions respond by building systems for authentication, disclosure, watermarking, identity, provenance, and detection. Those systems themselves create power. Whoever controls the verification layer can decide which voices are treated as real.

There is no purely technical escape.

The older forms of collective action had their own verification systems. A picket line consisted of bodies. A union had membership rolls. A town meeting occurred in a place. A shareholder had a record date and a number of shares. A newspaper editor knew the source even when the source remained anonymous to readers. A petition had signatures. These systems were imperfect, exclusionary, and vulnerable to fraud, but they attached public pressure to some costly fact.

Digital networks loosened the attachment.

Generative AI loosens it further.

This is one reason the phrase “online mob” is both useful and dangerous. It captures the speed and emotional contagion of networked criticism. It can also become a way to dismiss legitimate coordinated pressure simply because coordination occurred online. A thousand patients describing similar experiences may constitute evidence worth investigating. A thousand anonymous accounts repeating the same false rumor may not.

The difficult work is distinguishing them without requiring every person to become a public figure.

Institutions that do this badly create incentives for escalation.

If a normal support channel resolves ordinary problems, people use it. If normal support produces scripted replies while a viral post produces an executive call within hours, users learn to go viral. If a labor concern is ignored until a strike, workers learn the value of a strike. If regulators respond only after a scandal, companies learn that pre-scandal compliance is optional and activists learn that scandal is the route to policy.

Every institution teaches the public how to pressure it.

Some teach patience.

Some teach spectacle.

A platform that quietly corrects errors after appeal reduces the value of public shaming. A company that publishes meaningful incident reports reduces the value of leaks. A regulator that explains how complaints are triaged reduces the temptation to flood the system. A legislature that holds substantive hearings rather than only viral confrontations improves the incentive to produce evidence.

These are not public-relations improvements. They are pressure architecture.

The phrase matters because public pressure is often treated as an external weather system: a company launches a product, then “faces backlash.” That wording makes the reaction sound separate from the design choices that shaped it.

But institutions influence the form of backlash through their own recourse systems.

A system with no visible appeal exports appeal into public space.

The customer becomes a crowd because the customer discovers that the crowd has jurisdiction.

This is not always fair to the institution. Some disputes really are too complex for social media. Some customers are wrong. Some employees lie. Some activists manipulate evidence. Some public campaigns demand impossible or contradictory things.

A serious accountability system must be able to say no.

The purpose of recourse is not universal agreement. It is to make disagreement inspectable.

That is a higher standard than customer satisfaction. It means a person can learn what rule was applied, what evidence mattered, what authority exists to review the decision, and where the dispute ends if the answer remains no.

The crowd becomes most powerful when institutions cannot provide that closure.

Then every unresolved case can be recruited into a larger story. One denial becomes evidence of a policy. One biased output becomes evidence of a model. One leaked email becomes evidence of a culture. One scam becomes evidence that an entire asset class is fraudulent. The story may be right. It may be wrong. The unresolved cases provide fuel either way.

AI systems generate cases faster.

They can make more decisions, produce more content, interact with more users, and operate at a scale that human institutions then struggle to review. A small error rate applied to a massive system can produce a large population of angry people. If those people can find one another cheaply, the organization encounters something it did not see on its dashboard: correlated grievance.

That is the new pressure equation, if one insists on a formula, though it is better understood as a sequence.

Automation creates more decisions. Opacity makes some decisions harder to understand. Networks let affected people discover one another. Generative tools lower the cost of organizing and expression. Attention systems reward the strongest narratives. Public pressure searches for a person or institution capable of changing the rule.

At each stage, distortion is possible.

At each stage, so is accountability.

The customer who once had an address now has an audience.

The institution that once controlled the complaint channel no longer controls the route by which complaints become public facts.

That is an extraordinary transfer of power.

It is also the beginning of a new problem: when everyone can assemble a crowd, the crowd itself has to learn how to prove what happened.

The mechanics of a modern pressure campaign are worth following more slowly because they explain why some controversies vanish and others become durable. The first stage is usually discovery. One person publishes an experience. The important fact may not be that the post reaches millions. It may be that ten people with the same obscure problem find one another.

Similarity creates a hypothesis.

The second stage is collection. People compare dates, screenshots, letters, account statuses, model outputs, contracts, or bills. A pattern can emerge. At this point a campaign faces its first serious epistemic test: are these actually the same problem? People are very good at grouping events that feel similar. Institutions are very good at pointing to the differences. Both tendencies can be correct.

The third stage is translation. Somebody who understands the system gives the pattern a name that connects it to an institutional mechanism. A lawyer identifies a statutory right. A worker identifies a performance metric. A doctor identifies a utilization rule. A security researcher identifies a software behavior. A journalist identifies a procurement contract. The complaint becomes more legible because the campaign has found the seam where public experience meets institutional architecture.

The fourth stage is leverage.

A regulator can investigate. A union can bargain. A customer can leave. Investors can ask questions. A politician can hold a hearing. A court can issue an order. A company can change a product voluntarily. Without this stage, evidence can circulate indefinitely while the underlying system continues.

The fifth stage is memory.

This is the stage movements often neglect. What changed? Was the change temporary? Did a new policy survive the news cycle? Did the company quietly reverse it six months later? Did the regulator publish compliance information? Did the contract create a repeatable remedy?

A movement that cannot remember reform cannot distinguish success from fatigue.

AI can lower the cost at every stage. It can help cluster complaints, extract common terms, translate technical documents, find relevant regulations, and track commitments. This is potentially a major redistribution of institutional literacy. People who once needed professional researchers can arrive at a meeting with a reasonably organized file.

The risk is that the same tools can produce false coherence. A model asked to identify common themes will identify common themes whether or not the underlying cases truly belong together. It can summarize a disputed allegation in declarative language. It can omit the inconvenient exception. It can transform five anecdotes into a confident theory because synthesis is what the user requested.

Campaigns need the equivalent of a model card for their own claims.

What evidence is included?

What evidence is missing?

What would contradict the pattern?

Which cases were excluded and why?

Which claims are direct observations and which are inferences?

No social movement will produce such a document for every grievance. The habit matters more than the format.

The same discipline helps institutions respond better. A company receiving coordinated complaints should not begin by asking whether a campaign is “organic.” That question is relevant if manipulation is suspected, but it can become a convenient distraction. The first question should be whether the underlying evidence identifies a repeatable failure.

A legitimate complaint does not become illegitimate because an advocacy organization amplified it.

A false complaint does not become true because ten thousand real people repeated it.

Origin and accuracy are different properties.

This will become harder as AI-assisted participation spreads. A person may use a model to draft a letter because writing is difficult, English is a second language, or a government form is intimidating. That letter is still the person’s complaint. Another person may generate five thousand letters from one account to create the appearance of five thousand complainants. The text can be equally polished.

Institutions need signals tied to participation rather than authorship.

A public-comment system might verify that unique residents or affected parties submitted comments while allowing them to use any writing assistance they want. A customer review platform might verify transactions rather than linguistic style. A regulator might weight detailed evidence differently from sheer volume while still reporting how many people expressed concern.

The goal is not to preserve handwriting as proof of democracy.

It is to preserve the relationship between voice and constituency.

This has consequences for companies that use AI to answer public pressure. An organization can now generate individualized responses at massive scale. That sounds like better service. It can also create synthetic listening: every customer receives a personalized paragraph while nobody with authority sees the pattern.

The response becomes warmer as the institution becomes less responsive.

That would be a particularly perverse outcome of generative AI.

The technology can create the feeling of a human hearing the complaint while routing the complaint away from human attention.

Companies should design the opposite. Use AI to handle language and repetition, then aggregate recurring substantive issues upward. The system should make patterns more visible to people with authority rather than making each complainant feel individually contained.

The metric should not be how many messages were answered.

It should be how many recurring failures were resolved.

This is what separates customer service from institutional learning.

A crowd is often treated as a threat because it is unpredictable. The better view is that a crowd is expensive telemetry. The institution can complain about the noise, or it can improve the sensor.

Some complaints will still be wrong.

Some campaigns will still be manipulative.

Some demands should still be refused.

But the company that builds a real route from complaint to correction reduces the incentive for every future customer to recruit an audience first.

The customer becomes a crowd when the crowd is the only channel that appears to listen.

The mature institution makes the crowd less necessary by proving that one person can still reach someone with authority.
