# The Writer in the Training Set

The first insult was not that the machine could write.

The first insult was that it appeared to have read everything.

A novelist spends years assembling sentences. An illustrator develops a visual vocabulary slowly enough that other artists can recognize it. A photographer travels, waits, selects, edits, licenses, archives. A screenwriter learns what survives a room, a budget, a production schedule, a note, an actor, a cut. The work enters the world through a legal and economic system built around copies, licenses, commissions, salaries, royalties, residuals, contracts, and attribution.

Then a model arrives that can produce something adjacent to the work in seconds.

The public argument quickly becomes philosophical. Is the model learning the way a person learns? Is training more like reading or more like copying? Is style protectable? Does a prompt make a user an author? Is a generated image a derivative work? How much human control is necessary for copyright? Which training uses qualify as fair use? What evidence would show memorization rather than statistical learning?

Those questions are real and difficult.

The backlash began somewhere more concrete.

Creators wanted to know whether their work had been used, whether permission mattered, and whether the system built from that work could reduce the market for the people who made it.

The technology industry often answered at the wrong level.

Developers explained that models do not store a simple library of documents and retrieve one whenever prompted. They described training as statistical optimization over enormous data sets. They pointed to transformative uses, public web access, licensed data, synthetic data, or legal doctrines they believed permitted training. They emphasized the social value of systems that can assist with education, software, medicine, accessibility, research, and creativity.

Much of that may be relevant to the law.

It did not answer the creator’s economic question.

If my work helped build a system that can compete with me, what happened to the bargain?

This became one of the most powerful forms of AI public pressure because it united several grievances that are usually separate: copyright, labor, identity, attribution, consent, and technological displacement.

The U.S. Copyright Office received more than ten thousand comments after opening its artificial-intelligence study in 2023. That number alone does not resolve any legal issue. It demonstrates how many constituencies believed the rules were unsettled enough to justify intervention. The Office eventually organized its report into separate parts on digital replicas, copyrightability, and generative-AI training.

The separation is useful.

Public argument often combines distinct problems because they feel emotionally similar.

A model generating a song that resembles a living performer’s voice is a different legal problem from a model training on copyrighted recordings. A person using a system to imitate an illustrator’s style raises different issues from reproducing protected elements of a specific work. A company replacing an employee with generated output is a labor problem even if the training was lawful. A model output may lack sufficient human authorship for copyright protection even if no infringement occurred during its creation.

The crowd says, “They stole our work.”

The law asks several narrower questions.

This mismatch can make legal outcomes feel morally evasive. A court may decide a claim based on pleading, standing, market harm, substantial similarity, or a specific statutory right. The creator may believe the larger practice remains unjust. Technology companies can then point to a procedural or narrow legal victory as validation of an entire business model.

Both sides are tempted to overread the case.

The more durable response came from institutions that could negotiate terms rather than wait for doctrine to settle everything.

The Writers Guild of America entered its 2023 strike with artificial intelligence as one of several major issues. The public version of the fear was vivid: studios would use AI to generate scripts and hire writers cheaply to fix them, or train systems on writers’ work until the systems could reduce demand for writers themselves.

The resulting agreement did not ban AI.

That matters.

The WGA established that AI-generated material could not be treated as literary material under the agreement. A company could not hand a writer AI-generated material and use it to downgrade the writer’s compensation or rights as if the machine were the prior author. Writers could use AI with company consent but could not be required to use it. Companies had disclosure obligations around AI-generated material provided to writers. The Guild also reserved its position regarding the use of writers’ material for training.

This is a more sophisticated outcome than “writers defeated AI.”

The union protected the economic status of human authorship inside a particular labor contract while leaving broader legal questions open.

The distinction is an example of public pressure becoming institutional pressure.

A picket sign can say “AI is not a writer.” A contract has to define what follows.

SAG-AFTRA faced a related but different problem. Performers are not only workers. Their faces, voices, movements, and performances can become inputs. Digital-replica technology makes identity economically separable from the person in a new way. A studio can already reuse recorded performances under existing rights. AI raises the possibility of generating new material that appears to contain a performer who was never physically present for that scene.

The 2023 agreement created provisions around consent and compensation for digital replicas, among other AI-related terms. Again, the union did not solve the philosophy of synthetic identity. It negotiated jurisdiction over specific uses.

The practical brilliance of collective bargaining is that it can be local in the face of uncertainty.

A legislature writing national law may need a definition that survives across industries. A union can define what a producer must do under a contract for covered productions. It can return to the table later.

This makes labor an important model for AI governance. The technology can change faster than statute. Contracts can adapt through recurring bargaining if workers have enough leverage.

Most workers do not.

That is why creator backlash became unusually visible. Famous actors, bestselling authors, prominent visual artists, recording artists, and major unions already possessed audiences or institutions capable of amplifying their concerns. The freelance translator, junior copywriter, stock photographer, voice actor, game artist, or local journalist may face similar pressures with much less bargaining power.

AI therefore exposes a class distinction inside creative work.

The person whose likeness is valuable enough to negotiate separately can demand consent. The person whose work is one row among millions in a training corpus may not know it was included.

This is where the training-data debate becomes economically important even apart from the ultimate copyright rules.

Training at scale converts many small pieces of human effort into a shared model capability. The value of any single input may be impossible to measure. That is true in other systems too. A search engine depends on websites. A language depends on generations of speakers. A map depends on roads built by somebody else. Knowledge production is full of reuse.

Markets solve some of these problems through licensing. Law solves others through exceptions. Social norms solve others by permitting quotation, imitation, learning, commentary, indexing, and inspiration without a payment for every influence.

Generative AI stresses the boundaries because the reuse is industrial.

Scale changes perception.

A student reading fifty novels to learn how fiction works does not usually threaten the market for the fifty novelists. A model trained on millions of books may become part of a product designed to generate prose for millions of users. The analogy to human learning captures something about pattern acquisition while missing the industrial economics.

The analogy to copying captures something about data ingestion while missing the transformations inside training.

Neither analogy settles the issue.

This is why the U.S. Copyright Office’s work on AI training became so important. The Office had to analyze a technology that did not fit comfortably inside everyday metaphors. Its 2025 pre-publication report on generative-AI training examined legal and policy questions without pretending that every training practice was identical.

That last point deserves emphasis.

“Training data” is not one practice.

A company can license a curated collection. It can use public-domain material. It can train on its own data. It can use data made available under terms that permit certain uses. It can scrape publicly accessible content. It can use user submissions under contractual provisions. It can acquire data from brokers. It can generate synthetic data. It can combine all of them.

Public backlash often treats the entire mixture as one moral event.

Companies encourage that simplification when they speak about “the open internet” as if availability were equivalent to permission.

The real governance problem requires provenance.

What went in?

Under what right?

For what use?

What was retained?

What can be removed?

What happens when a data source included material that the source itself had no right to provide?

These are ordinary supply-chain questions applied to information.

The technology industry is learning that data has a supply chain.

This has strategic consequences. Companies with strong licensing relationships may be able to distinguish themselves from competitors. Publishers, stock-media companies, news organizations, music companies, and platforms can negotiate access. Smaller creators may join collective licensing systems. Some sites block crawlers. Governments may require disclosures or transparency for certain model providers.

A backlash can therefore create a market.

This has happened repeatedly in the history of intellectual property. New copying technologies create conflict, courts and legislatures draw boundaries, industries develop licensing structures, and business models change. The result is rarely a permanent peace. Radio, photocopying, cable television, home video, digital music, search, and streaming all rearranged who could copy what and who got paid.

AI is different in technical details and similar in political form.

The first generation of companies benefits from ambiguity.

The next generation builds infrastructure around the rules that survive.

Creators are not guaranteed to like those rules. Some uses may remain lawful without payment. Some licensing markets may concentrate power in publishers or platforms rather than individual artists. Collective systems can become bureaucratic. Opt-out mechanisms can be hard to enforce. Technical measures can fail.

Public pressure does not guarantee a creator-friendly outcome.

It guarantees that the input side of AI can no longer be treated as invisible.

The same is true of output.

When a model can produce a persuasive imitation of a person’s voice or appearance, the relevant right is not always copyright. A person does not own every visual style associated with them. Identity can implicate publicity rights, privacy, consumer protection, fraud, contract, labor law, and new digital-replica statutes.

This fragmentation frustrates people because the harm feels singular: somebody made a fake me.

The legal system sees a bundle.

The U.S. Copyright Office’s digital-replica report recommended a federal law addressing unauthorized digital replicas, reflecting the concern that existing state and federal protections were uneven. Congress later enacted the TAKE IT DOWN Act in 2025, targeting certain nonconsensual intimate imagery, including digitally forged material. States have also acted in areas such as election deepfakes and performer rights.

The law is assembling category by category.

That is probably how AI governance will proceed more generally.

There will be no single moment when “the AI law” arrives. Employment rules will govern hiring. Health law will govern clinical and insurance uses. Copyright will govern certain uses of protected works. Consumer-protection law will govern deception. Communications law will govern robocalls. Securities law will govern fraud involving investments. Contracts will allocate rights inside industries. New statutes will fill gaps that become politically intolerable.

This matters for public pressure because movements often demand one answer from a system that only has jurisdiction in pieces.

The creator asking, “Can they do this?” may receive five answers.

That feels like failure. Sometimes it is simply federalism and legal architecture.

The more important institutional question is whether the pieces leave a practical remedy.

A right that costs more to enforce than the work is worth may exist mainly for large rightsholders. A notice system that requires creators to identify every model that might have used their work may place impossible burdens on individuals. A licensing market controlled by intermediaries may pay pennies while claiming legitimacy. A disclosure that lists categories of data without allowing verification may satisfy a rule without creating trust.

Public pressure should therefore judge remedies by their usability, not only their existence.

This is where the writer returns.

The working writer does not spend the day debating whether gradient descent is analogous to human learning. The writer wants to know whether the next job exists, whether the studio can call machine output “source material,” whether a book was ingested without permission, whether a client expects the same work for less money because AI can produce a draft, and whether authorship still creates leverage.

AI backlash in creative industries is partly a fight over status.

If generated material becomes abundant, what becomes scarce?

Human provenance may become scarcer. Trusted reputation may matter more. Live performance may matter more. Bespoke work may matter more. Audiences may value the knowledge that a particular person made something. Or they may not care enough to sustain current employment levels.

No contract can repeal economics.

It can shape the transition.

That is the quiet achievement of the 2023 labor agreements. They did not freeze technology. They created bargaining positions inside it.

This suggests a broader principle for public pressure. When a technology is too general to ban and too consequential to ignore, the most useful demand may be procedural power.

Consent.

Disclosure.

Compensation.

Attribution.

The ability to say no in specified circumstances.

The ability to revisit the terms.

These rights are less dramatic than demanding that a model forget the entire internet. They are also more likely to survive contact with an industry.

The technology company would prefer to talk about innovation in general. The creator would prefer to talk about a specific work.

Governance has to hold both scales at once.

A society can benefit from models trained on enormous bodies of culture and still decide that certain inputs require licenses. It can permit broad learning uses and still protect a performer from an unauthorized commercial replica. It can encourage new creative tools and still refuse to let employers use machine output as a way to erase contractual credit.

The hard part is drawing boundaries that do not depend on pretending the technology is either a person or a photocopier.

It is neither.

It is infrastructure built from human-produced information and capable of producing new information at industrial speed.

Once that is clear, the political argument becomes less mystical.

Who contributes?

Who pays?

Who can refuse?

Who gets named?

Who carries the risk when the market changes?

The writer in the training set is not merely afraid of a clever machine.

The writer is asking whether a new industrial system can absorb the evidence of human labor while making the laborer disappear.

Public pressure begins by making the laborer visible again.

Visibility, however, does not solve bargaining power. A novelist can discover that a book was included in a data set and still have no practical way to negotiate with a model developer. A photographer can place a notice on a website and still struggle to know whether a crawler honored it. A freelance illustrator can object to style imitation without possessing a legal right in style as such. Information can make the grievance precise while leaving the creator economically weak.

This is where collective institutions may reappear in forms that do not resemble traditional unions.

Publishers can license catalogs. Stock-media companies can negotiate access to libraries. Rights organizations can aggregate claims. Professional associations can establish contract terms. Platforms can provide standardized metadata about permitted uses. New intermediaries can emerge whose business is proving provenance and collecting payment.

These institutions create their own risks. The intermediary that negotiates for creators can become another gatekeeper. A licensing collective may favor works that are easy to identify and monetize. A publisher may receive bargaining power that the individual author never gains. Technical standards may privilege large repositories capable of implementing them.

The existence of a market is not evidence that the market distributes value fairly.

Still, collective bargaining solves a real transaction-cost problem. A model developer cannot negotiate separately with every creator whose work might be useful. A creator cannot negotiate separately with every model developer. Rights aggregation exists in other creative industries because bilateral bargaining does not scale.

AI may force new versions of familiar institutions rather than an entirely new economic order.

The important design question is whether the creator retains enough information and choice to evaluate the bargain.

Opt-out systems are often proposed because they preserve broad access unless a rightsholder objects. They are attractive for scale and difficult for creators who do not know a system exists, do not know whether their work is included, or have to repeat the request across many companies.

Opt-in systems give permission a stronger role but can make large-scale training far more expensive and may exclude works whose owners cannot be located. Public-domain, orphaned, licensed, and user-generated material create additional categories.

There is no frictionless consent architecture.

That does not mean all architectures are equivalent.

A company can make data practices easier or harder to inspect. It can maintain source records. It can publish meaningful categories. It can respect machine-readable preferences where legally and technically appropriate. It can create channels for creators to report memorized outputs or disputed uses. It can negotiate licenses where the market supports them.

These choices determine whether “we trained on the internet” functions as an explanation or an evasion.

The output side requires equal care because creators often recognize harm first through resemblance. An artist sees an image that looks uncannily like their work. A performer hears a voice that sounds like theirs. A writer sees a passage that resembles a book. Recognition is evidence worth investigating. It is not always proof of infringement or data inclusion.

Models can reproduce broad conventions shared across a field. Users can prompt for recognizable characteristics. Training data may include lawful references, licensed material, or works by many creators with overlapping styles. The legal conclusion cannot be read directly from aesthetic discomfort.

The discomfort still contains information about the market.

If customers can obtain “something like” a creator cheaply enough, demand may change even where no output is legally infringing. Copyright law does not guarantee every business model against technological substitution. Labor and contract therefore remain essential parts of the response.

This is why the WGA and SAG-AFTRA outcomes matter beyond Hollywood. They show that a creative worker can preserve bargaining rights around technology even when copyright does not answer the employment question.

A voice actor can negotiate the scope of a replica license.

A writer can preserve credit rules.

A game performer can negotiate use of scans.

A publisher can negotiate access to a catalog.

A customer can pay a premium for verified human work.

Different markets can attach value to provenance in different ways.

The public debate often assumes a single future in which synthetic abundance drives creative value toward zero. That is not inevitable. Abundance can increase demand for curation, trusted identity, live performance, reputation, and work whose provenance is itself part of the product.

The question is whether those new forms of value compensate the people whose old market shrinks.

Markets do not promise that they will.

Policy cannot promise it either without freezing technological change.

What institutions can do is make transitions less one-sided. They can protect against deceptive replicas, enforce contracts, maintain attribution where required, create bargaining channels, make data sourcing more legible, and preserve rights to negotiate new uses.

This is less emotionally satisfying than a declaration that human creativity is sacred and therefore economically safe.

Creative work has never been economically safe.

Printing displaced scribes. Recorded music changed live performance. Photography changed portraiture. Television changed radio and film. Digital distribution changed newspapers, music, and publishing. New tools create audiences while destroying some old margins.

The ethical question is not whether change occurs.

It is whether powerful actors can use technological novelty to bypass every institution through which creators previously had standing.

That is the real fear inside the phrase “training on my work.”

The creator is not only asking whether a machine saw the work.

The creator is asking whether seeing became a way of removing the creator from the next transaction.

A legitimate AI economy does not need to answer that question with universal payment for every influence.

It does need an answer better than disappearance.
