# The Insiders Revolt

The employee protest starts with an information advantage.

Workers know which promise is real.

They know whether a policy is enforced, whether a safety team has resources, whether a deadline moved after a launch was announced, whether a model is advisory in name and mandatory in practice, whether the sales pitch resembles the internal test results, and whether executives who speak confidently in public are arguing privately about the same uncertainty.

That knowledge gives insiders unusual power in technology companies because the products are difficult for outsiders to inspect.

It also gives them unusual vulnerability.

A customer can criticize a company and leave. An employee’s health insurance, immigration status, stock compensation, professional network, future references, and identity may all be tied to the workplace. The person raising a concern has more information than the public and more to lose by sharing it.

AI companies have therefore become laboratories for a form of public pressure that begins inside the institution and moves outward only when internal governance fails.

This is not entirely new.

Engineers have resisted projects they considered unsafe or unethical for decades. Scientists have signed petitions. Workers have organized over surveillance, discrimination, military contracts, climate impact, harassment, and political donations. Technology culture sometimes imagined employees as unusually free agents because skilled workers could change companies easily. That freedom was never evenly distributed, but it created a norm that technical labor could have moral opinions about what it was building.

Google’s Project Maven controversy in 2018 became an early modern example.

The company had worked on a U.S. Department of Defense project involving machine learning applied to drone video. Thousands of Google employees signed a letter objecting to the work. Some employees resigned. Google later announced AI principles that included restrictions on weapons and certain surveillance uses while saying it would continue working with governments and militaries in other areas such as cybersecurity, training, recruitment, veterans’ health care, and search and rescue.

The importance of the episode is not that the employees permanently settled Google’s defense policy. They did not. Corporate policies evolve, government relationships evolve, and Google updated its AI principles years later.

The episode showed that employees could make the intended use of a technical capability part of the company’s labor politics.

The worker was no longer asking only, “What am I paid to build?”

The worker was asking, “What is this company entitled to ask me to build?”

Generative AI intensifies the question because general-purpose systems make purpose less stable.

An image-recognition model built for a defined military program has an identifiable project. A foundation model can be used for coding, education, persuasion, cyber operations, scientific research, customer service, intelligence analysis, and countless other tasks. The same base capability can travel into contexts the original researchers never saw.

Employees concerned about downstream use therefore have to argue about policies, access, safeguards, customers, and governance rather than one product.

That is harder.

It also places more power in executive judgment.

A policy can say the company will not support certain harmful uses. Somebody still has to decide whether a customer or deployment crosses the boundary. The most consequential decisions may occur under commercial or national-security pressure, when public principles become inconvenient.

Insiders can see those moments.

This is why internal dissent is valuable even when the dissenter is wrong.

A company developing powerful systems should want disagreement to surface before deployment rather than after a public failure. Safety engineers, security teams, legal teams, researchers, product staff, and front-line employees each see different classes of risk. A culture that treats objections as disloyalty destroys its own early-warning system.

The problem is that disagreement has costs.

Every organization contains people who object for bad reasons, misunderstand tradeoffs, dislike deadlines, protect turf, or simply prefer their own judgment to management’s. A company cannot treat every internal concern as a veto. Leaders are paid to decide after disagreement.

The governance question is whether dissent receives process.

Can an employee escalate outside the direct management chain?

Can a safety team stop a launch under defined conditions?

Does the board receive unfiltered risk information?

Are whistleblowers protected when they report suspected illegality?

Can researchers publish negative results?

Do exit agreements or nondisparagement clauses chill legitimate reporting?

Who decides when confidentiality protects the company and when it protects a failure?

These questions became unusually visible in the AI industry because companies often made safety part of their public identity.

A firm that sells shoes can have internal product-safety disputes without making existential-risk governance part of its brand. Frontier AI laboratories have publicly discussed catastrophic misuse, autonomous cyber capability, biological risk, alignment, and the possibility of systems more capable than their creators in important domains. Their employees therefore have grounds to compare internal practices against unusually ambitious public commitments.

When the gap appears large, employee departures become public evidence.

They are still only evidence.

A resignation does not prove the person’s claims. Employees can disagree honestly. A former worker can have incomplete information or mixed motives. Public audiences tend to overvalue insiders because proximity feels like certainty.

Proximity is not certainty.

It is access to a different slice of the system.

The 2024 departures from OpenAI’s superalignment effort became a case in point. Jan Leike, who had co-led the team, publicly said after resigning that he had disagreed with leadership over priorities and believed safety culture and processes had taken a back seat to product work. OpenAI’s leadership disputed broader narratives that the company had abandoned safety and reorganized its safety work. The public could not see all internal resource decisions.

The useful fact was not that one side must have been entirely right.

The useful fact was that resource allocation had become the contested object.

Safety principles are easy to endorse at the level of mission.

Compute allocation is a budget.

Headcount is a budget.

Launch authority is governance.

A policy becomes real when it consumes scarce resources or constrains revenue.

Insiders know where that consumption occurs.

This suggests a test for corporate AI commitments: ask what the company is willing not to do.

A principle that never changes a shipment date, customer contract, hiring plan, or product capability may not be a constraint. It may still guide ordinary decisions, but its limiting force has not been demonstrated.

The public often demands a dramatic example of a company sacrificing profit for safety. That can create incentives for performative sacrifice. A better approach is to make decision rules auditable over time.

What risks trigger review?

Who participates?

What thresholds block deployment?

How are overrides recorded?

Does the board see them?

Are incident reports retained?

Can external assessors test claims where secrecy allows?

These are governance questions rather than morality plays.

Employees become especially important where external verification is impossible.

A regulator may not have access to model weights or internal evaluations. A customer may not know a dangerous capability was discovered before launch. A journalist may depend on sources. A board member may receive information selected by executives.

Internal channels are therefore part of the public accountability infrastructure even when the public never learns about them.

A good whistleblowing system is successful when most valid concerns do not become leaks.

The employee reports internally.

The organization investigates.

The problem is corrected.

The worker is not punished.

No public scandal occurs.

This success is difficult to observe because the absence of scandal looks like nothing happened.

Companies underinvest in invisible success for the same reason they underinvest in reliability: prevention produces no dramatic artifact.

Public pressure can change that by treating speak-up systems as governance features worth evaluating before a crisis.

Boards can ask for data on internal concerns, retaliation allegations, escalation times, and remediation. Regulators can protect whistleblowers. Investors can ask how safety disagreements reach directors. Employees can negotiate policies through labor institutions where relevant.

These mechanisms matter more as AI companies grow.

A startup can operate through dense personal trust. Everyone knows the founders. Critical discussions occur in rooms where the decision-makers are present. Scale creates layers. The chief executive hears summaries. Product groups develop local incentives. Information becomes political.

The company begins to resemble the institutions it once promised to disrupt.

This is where the founder mythology becomes a governance risk.

A founder may be technically sophisticated, charismatic, mission-driven, and correct more often than the people challenging them. None of those properties substitutes for mechanisms that allow the organization to detect when the founder is wrong.

The more central the leader is to fundraising and public identity, the harder internal challenge can become.

People do not merely disagree with the boss.

They disagree with the person investors, media, and customers treat as the company itself.

The social cost rises.

This is why the OpenAI board crisis was so revealing even though the later independent review said the 2023 removal of Sam Altman did not arise from product safety, security, development pace, finances, or statements to investors and customers. The crisis exposed how difficult it can be for formal governance to act against a leader who has accumulated enormous practical legitimacy.

The prior board had legal authority.

It lacked a durable coalition.

Employees threatened to leave. Microsoft had leverage as a major partner. Customers and investors faced uncertainty. Within days Altman returned, most of the board changed, and the company began another round of governance reform.

This does not prove the board was right or wrong to act when it did. The later review found a breakdown of trust, an abridged process, and insufficient opportunity for Altman to address concerns, while concluding the board had broad authority.

The episode is more useful as institutional evidence than as personality drama.

Formal power is not enough.

An oversight body needs process, information, legitimacy, and a plan for what happens after it uses power.

The same is true for employee revolt.

A petition can stop a project. A wave of resignations can make a company change course. A leak can trigger oversight. These are emergency mechanisms.

They are poor substitutes for normal governance.

Workers should not have to threaten collective resignation every time a safety dispute reaches leadership. A board should not have to fire a CEO suddenly because ordinary feedback channels have broken down. The public should not depend on anonymous leaks to learn whether a company follows its own policy.

The goal is not more revolt.

It is more legitimate disagreement before revolt becomes necessary.

This creates obligations for employees too.

Insiders possess information the public cannot easily verify. That power can be abused. Selective leaks can create misleading narratives. Confidentiality can protect users, security, trade secrets, and legitimate research. A worker who dislikes a business decision is not automatically a whistleblower in the legal or moral sense.

Public pressure should therefore distinguish protected reporting of wrongdoing or serious risk from ordinary internal politics.

The distinction may be contested.

That is why channels matter.

An employee needs somewhere to bring evidence that is more trustworthy than a social-media thread and less captured than the direct manager.

A regulator needs a way to receive credible reports without turning every employment dispute into an investigation.

A board needs access to dissent without inviting endless end-runs around management.

These are difficult design problems because they intentionally create competing lines of authority.

Competing authority is the feature.

Modern technology companies often celebrate speed, alignment, and small teams. Those traits are useful for building. Governance sometimes needs the opposite: delay, dissent, redundancy, and somebody authorized to say no.

The conflict is not between innovation and bureaucracy in the abstract.

It is between the coordination system optimized for progress and the checking system optimized for error.

A company needs both.

Public pressure from employees becomes dangerous when it tries to govern every product decision through internal popularity. Companies are not legislatures. Engineers are not elected merely because they write code. Workers have legitimate interests and specialized knowledge, but customers, shareholders, governments, and other communities have interests too.

Employee conscience is one source of pressure, not supreme authority.

The strongest worker interventions recognize this. They do not say, “We are employees, therefore we decide.” They bring information that management or the public has reason to treat seriously: a legal risk, a safety finding, a conflict with stated policy, an undisclosed capability, a discriminatory effect, an operational impossibility.

Evidence travels farther than moral status.

This principle should apply to executives too.

A CEO saying “trust us” is not evidence.

A safety researcher saying “trust me” is not evidence.

An employee petition saying “everyone knows” is not evidence.

The institution needs records.

The future of AI governance will depend heavily on whether companies can preserve those records while moving quickly. Which tests were run? Which exceptions were approved? What did staff recommend? What did management decide? When did the board learn? What changed after an incident?

This is auditability as organizational memory.

Without it, every crisis becomes a contest of recollection and reputation.

The insider then has to become a public figure to be believed.

That is exactly the dynamic the book has been tracing from the outside.

Opaque systems create pressure for a face.

Inside the company, opacity creates pressure for a dissenter.

The CEO becomes the interface for authority.

The whistleblower becomes the interface for doubt.

Neither should have to carry the whole institution.

A mature company builds channels where evidence can move without requiring either person to become a symbol.

That is what the insiders are really asking for when the revolt is at its best.

Not the right to run the company.

The right to prove that the company can hear itself.

There is another reason to protect internal challenge: the organization’s official reporting system is itself a model of reality.

Executives do not see the company directly. They see dashboards, briefings, incident classifications, product reviews, legal summaries, risk registers, forecasts, and carefully chosen examples. As a company grows, leadership becomes dependent on abstraction in exactly the way a large model depends on compressed representations of a wider world.

The abstraction can drift.

A red-team finding becomes “low severity” because the exploit required technical skill. A customer complaint becomes “isolated” because support tickets are categorized separately. An employment concern becomes “performance management.” A launch exception becomes “temporary.” Each label may be defensible. Together they can remove the pattern from executive sight.

Insiders often revolt because they believe the compression has become dishonest before they believe the underlying company has become malicious.

The safety researcher says the benchmark does not capture the failure.

The policy employee says the public commitment does not match the exception process.

The support worker says the supposedly rare error appears every week.

The salesperson says customers are being told something the product cannot reliably do.

The dissent is an attempt to restore resolution.

This suggests that boards should care about what might be called dissent coverage.

Which important functions have a route to directors that does not depend entirely on the executive team? Which classes of concern are summarized quantitatively, and which can be brought as narrative evidence? Does the board see only the number of incidents, or examples of the most consequential ones? Are terminated investigations visible? Are disagreements between product and safety teams recorded or merely resolved in meetings and forgotten?

A board that receives only consensus is not necessarily governing a harmonious company.

It may be receiving a pre-filtered company.

The same logic applies below the board. A launch review should not reward the team that produces the most confident deck. A safety objection should have an owner and an expiration condition. If management decides to proceed despite the objection, the decision should record who accepted the risk and what evidence would cause reconsideration.

This turns dissent into a reversible institutional artifact rather than a personal feud.

The value becomes clearer after time passes.

Suppose the concern was wrong. The record can show that the organization considered it, tested it, and proceeded for reasons that held up. That protects management from retrospective claims that every warning should have stopped the project.

Suppose the concern was right. The record shows where the system failed to listen and gives the organization something more useful than a morality play about who was brave.

Either outcome improves learning.

This is one reason nonretaliation is an insufficient whistleblower policy. A company can avoid firing dissenters and still make speaking up economically irrational. The worker can lose desirable projects, promotion opportunities, access to leaders, conference invitations, or the informal status from which influence comes. Retaliation can be granular enough that no one event looks decisive.

Organizations serious about dissent need to examine career outcomes as well as formal complaints.

Do people who raise substantiated concerns remain promotable?

Can they return to operating roles after an investigation?

Are managers rewarded for surfacing bad news early, or only for avoiding bad news?

Does the person who delays a launch receive any credit if the delay prevents a later incident?

These are incentive questions.

A culture gets the warnings it pays for.

There is a parallel problem with employee exits. A resignation is often the final form of internal feedback, yet companies may classify it mainly as attrition. The exit interview becomes an HR artifact rather than risk intelligence. In a frontier technology company, clusters of departures from a particular function should be treated as evidence requiring interpretation, not automatically as proof of wrongdoing and not merely as a recruiting problem.

Who is leaving?

What expertise is leaving with them?

Do their concerns share a mechanism?

Were the concerns previously raised internally?

Did the company disagree on substance or fail to provide a credible process?

The answers may reveal nothing alarming. The discipline of asking is still valuable.

Employee voice is also unevenly distributed inside the company. Famous researchers, senior engineers, and executives can resign publicly and be heard. Contractors, data labelers, moderators, administrative staff, visa-dependent employees, junior researchers, and workers in regional offices may see serious problems while having far less leverage.

An institution that relies on public resignation as its safety valve therefore hears mostly from people wealthy enough to resign.

That is not a representative sensor.

The internal system should deliberately protect low-status information.

Anonymous reporting can help but creates verification problems. Ombuds offices can help but require independence. Worker councils or unions can help in some contexts because they aggregate claims and reduce individual exposure. Routine skip-level reviews can surface patterns. Independent audits can interview workers without management present.

There is no perfect channel.

Redundancy is the answer.

The company should be harder to fool internally than externally.

That standard sounds demanding because it is. A firm building systems that can act across millions of users should not depend on one charismatic leader’s ability to intuit whether everything below is fine.

The most responsible founder is the one who builds a company capable of proving the founder wrong.

The most responsible employee is not the one who wins every internal argument. It is the one who can place evidence into a process that survives losing the argument today and can be revisited tomorrow.

The most responsible board is not the one that intervenes constantly. It is the one that can distinguish ordinary disagreement from evidence that management’s map of the company has stopped matching the territory.

This is the institutional form of humility.

The AI industry talks often about whether models know what they do not know.

Companies need the same capability.

The insiders revolt when the organization’s uncertainty has nowhere else to go.
