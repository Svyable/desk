# Chapter 14 — AI Act versioning and implementation drift (September 2026)

## Why this note exists

Chapter 14 argues that regulation is not finished when a legislature classifies a system. The legal object changes too: deadlines move, implementation guidance arrives, enforcement channels open, and operational duties become clearer or narrower after the original statute is enacted. The EU AI Act now supplies a concrete example worth preserving for a future prose pass.

## Primary-source check

The European Commission's current AI Act implementation timeline, checked September 3, 2026, incorporates the Digital Omnibus amendments and gives these material dates:

- Article 50 transparency rules and enforcement for applicable provisions began on August 2, 2026.
- The transition for certain pre-existing synthetic-content systems under Article 50(2) runs to December 2, 2026.
- Member States should have at least one AI regulatory sandbox operational by August 2, 2027.
- Annex III high-risk-system rules apply from December 2, 2027.
- Annex I high-risk systems embedded in regulated products apply from August 2, 2028.

Source: European Commission, AI Act Service Desk, "Timeline for the Implementation of the EU AI Act": https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/eu-ai-act-implementation-timeline

The Commission's July 31, 2026 enforcement announcement separately confirms that the AI Office and national authorities began enforcing applicable AI Act provisions on August 2, 2026, including the new transparency rules. It also points to complaints, whistleblower, and downstream-provider channels, making the implementation machinery more concrete than the generic phrase "the AI Act applies."

Source: European Commission, "Commission starts enforcing AI Act rules and new transparency requirements on 2 August," July 31, 2026: https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/commission-starts-enforcing-ai-act-rules-and-new-transparency-requirements-2-august

## The useful versioning detail

The Commission's Article 57 service-desk page still displays the original statutory instruction that a national AI regulatory sandbox be operational by August 2, 2026. The Commission's current implementation timeline, which explicitly incorporates the Digital Omnibus amendments, now gives August 2, 2027.

Source: European Commission, AI Act Service Desk, Article 57: https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-57

This is not evidence that the law is unknowable or that the Commission is uniquely confused. It is a small example of a general implementation problem: readers can encounter an older provision-level rendering and a newer consolidated implementation calendar at the same time. For fast-moving regulation, version provenance becomes part of legal accuracy.

## Strongest counterargument

Moving a deadline is not necessarily evidence of regulatory failure or capture. The Commission describes the AI Omnibus as a simplification measure intended to give firms clearer obligations, extend implementation time where support tools and standards are not ready, broaden testing opportunities, and expand access to regulatory sandboxes. A delayed date can therefore represent administrative sequencing rather than retreat.

Source: European Commission, "AI Omnibus enters into force," July 27, 2026: https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/ai-omnibus-enters-force

The opposite risk also remains: repeated delay can weaken protection if institutions use implementation complexity as a permanent reason not to enforce consequential duties. The evidence here does not establish which description will dominate in practice.

## Editorial implication

The strongest Chapter 14 formulation is narrower than "regulation cannot keep up with AI." The more durable claim is that modern technology regulation itself needs change control. A responsible institution must know which legal version, deadline, guidance document, system version, and deployment context it is acting under.

That is also a useful counterweight to the chapter's discussion of static compliance. The versioning problem exists on both sides of the regulator's desk: the model can change after validation, and the applicable rule can change after enactment.

## What would make this implication false?

If consolidated official implementation tools reliably supersede older renderings in practice, regulated actors can identify the controlling obligations without material ambiguity, and deadline changes rarely affect compliance decisions, then this is mostly a documentation nuisance rather than a meaningful governance problem. The chapter should not inflate one stale-looking service-desk page into a general indictment of EU administration.
