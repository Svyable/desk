# Research Brief

## Thesis under test

*Public Pressure* asks what happens when increasingly automated institutions make consequential decisions at scale while the people affected struggle to identify who can explain, reverse, or own those decisions.

The strong version of the backlash thesis — that artificial intelligence is creating a new revolt against technology companies and other institutions — is too simple. Distrust of insurers, corporations, politicians, banks, employers, media, and technology firms predates generative AI. The version worth testing is narrower:

> AI changes the pressure gradient around institutional power. It lowers the cost of automated action, synthetic persuasion, impersonation, organizing, and scale while often widening the distance between the person affected by a decision and the person capable of explaining or reversing it. Public pressure intensifies where consequential authority becomes less contestable.

The book should survive evidence that many AI systems improve decisions, reduce bias, lower costs, expand access, help workers, or create effective new forms of recourse. It should also survive evidence that some public backlashes are based on false claims, partisan incentives, technological confusion, or misallocated blame.

The target is not a theory in which CEOs, algorithms, regulators, workers, or crowds are always right. The target is a map of responsibility that remains useful when all of them have partial information and self-interest.

## Core empirical tests

1. **Personalization of institutional power:** When and why do CEOs, founders, politicians, doctors, regulators, and other visible figures become human proxies for distributed systems?
2. **Automation and practical authority:** When does a model remain genuinely advisory, and when do incentives, workflow, targets, or interface design make its output effectively binding?
3. **Contestability:** Can a person affected by a consequential decision learn the relevant reason, correct material facts, reach an empowered reviewer, and obtain timely reversal?
4. **Public pressure as information:** When do boycotts, strikes, hearings, lawsuits, petitions, whistleblowing, and public campaigns reveal real institutional failures rather than simply amplify attention?
5. **Public pressure as distortion:** When do harassment, synthetic evidence, misidentification, coordinated inauthentic behavior, threats, or violence convert accountability into coercion?
6. **Synthetic authority:** How much do voice cloning, deepfakes, generated documents, fake profiles, and AI-assisted scam operations weaken appearance, fluency, and familiarity as signals of legitimate identity?
7. **Existing law versus AI-specific law:** Which AI-enabled harms are already reachable through fraud, consumer-protection, employment, communications, copyright, health, securities, administrative, and other existing legal regimes?
8. **Conversion mechanisms:** Which forms of public pressure produce enforceable contracts, orders, procedures, audits, appeal rights, compensation, or other changed defaults?
9. **Worker distribution:** When productivity gains from AI arrive, who receives saved time, higher output, reduced staffing, changed training paths, or new supervisory burdens?
10. **Burden of proof:** As production of plausible text, audio, images, and organizational surfaces gets cheaper, which institutions bear the cost of preserving evidence and authenticating consequential acts?
11. **Cost allocation:** When automated systems are wrong, who pays for review, delay, correction, compensation, and downstream damage, and how does that allocation change incentives upstream?
12. **Veto capacity:** Which people or bodies have practical authority to pause, constrain, or reverse automated action, and do those vetoes remain usable when they conflict with revenue, speed, hierarchy, or political pressure?

## Falsification and counter-evidence

Every chapter should preserve evidence that pushes against the book’s central argument.

- AI may reduce rather than increase opacity when it produces better explanations, translates bureaucracy, summarizes evidence, or makes public records easier to understand.
- Automated decision systems can outperform inconsistent human judgment and may reduce some forms of discrimination, delay, fraud, or arbitrary treatment.
- A high-profile public backlash can be factually wrong, morally disproportionate, manipulated, or aimed at a person with little control over the relevant decision.
- Human review is not automatically better. Humans can rubber-stamp, discriminate, fatigue, ignore evidence, or make less accurate decisions than models.
- Existing law may already regulate conduct effectively enough that broad AI-specific regulation adds cost without improving recourse.
- AI-specific laws can create incumbent advantages, compliance theater, speech burdens, or definitions that age badly.
- Public pressure can cause useful reform, but it can also reward spectacle over quiet severity and make institutions optimize for reputational risk rather than justice.
- Deepfake and synthetic-media risks do not imply that all generated media should be restricted; translation, accessibility, satire, creative production, and legitimate automation can benefit from the same tools.
- Worker resistance may correctly identify hidden risks, but workers and unions can also defend inefficient practices or resist changes that create larger social benefits.
- Corporate safety claims can be self-serving, but activist claims, insider allegations, and viral examples also require evidence and should not receive automatic credibility.
- Compensation can improve incentives but poorly calibrated liability can over-deter beneficial uses or favor incumbents able to absorb legal cost.
- Veto rights can prevent irreversible mistakes but can also become ceremonial, captured, excessively cautious, or slow enough to destroy the value of legitimate automation.

## Evidence lanes

### I. Health insurance and consequential automation

Use Medicare Advantage prior authorization, post-acute-care disputes, CMS rules, congressional investigations, litigation concerning nH Predict, and broader evidence on prior-authorization burden to test when software acquires practical authority inside a regulated workflow.

Key distinction: allegations about a tool are not findings that the tool independently made a denial. Preserve company responses, procedural outcomes, applicable Medicare rules, and evidence about human discretion.

### II. Hiring and employment screening

Use EEOC and DOJ disability guidance, New York City Local Law 144, vendor-validation literature, disparate-impact doctrine, and academic work on algorithmic hiring. Separate simple automation from tools that rank, score, or eliminate candidates.

Key distinction: employer responsibility is not outsourced merely because the employer bought the tool from a vendor.

### III. Creative labor, copyright, and digital replicas

Use the WGA and SAG-AFTRA agreements, U.S. Copyright Office AI reports, federal and state digital-replica law, and copyright litigation. Keep training, data acquisition, output copyrightability, style imitation, digital replicas, and labor displacement analytically separate.

### IV. Workers and algorithmic management

Use ILO exposure research, Department of Labor worker-AI principles, NLRB and labor literature where relevant, and empirical research on monitoring, productivity, workload, and task change. Do not translate technical task capability directly into job-loss counts.

### V. Politics and synthetic media

Use the New Hampshire Biden robocall, FCC enforcement, state election cases, the FCC’s TCPA declaratory ruling, Content Credentials/C2PA material, and election-authentication research. Preserve First Amendment and satire concerns when discussing disclosure or removal rules.

### VI. Crypto fraud and synthetic confidence

Use the Bankman-Fried criminal judgment, Terraform/Do Kwon verdict and settlement, FBI IC3 reports, SEC and DOJ materials, and scam-compound reporting from government and international organizations. Distinguish protocol risk, ordinary market loss, securities-law disputes, and fraud established in court.

### VII. Impersonation and voice cloning

Use FTC impersonation rules and fraud data, FBI alerts, FCC robocall rules, Hong Kong police deepfake-video-conference case material, and research on voice-clone detection and provenance. Treat channel separation, authorization, and payment controls as independent defenses from synthetic-content detection.

### VIII. CEO hearings and internal governance

Use congressional hearing records involving AI and technology CEOs, OpenAI’s published governance review, public employee letters, and documented internal-governance changes. Do not infer the truth of internal disputes merely from a resignation or board action.

### IX. Regulation and enforcement

Compare the EU AI Act’s phased implementation with U.S. sectoral enforcement and changing federal policy. Use the FTC Rite Aid facial-recognition case, FCC enforcement, civil-rights guidance, state/local laws, and current European Commission implementation materials.

### X. Litigation, recourse, proof, and remediation

Use *Moffatt v. Air Canada*, copyright cases, health-insurance litigation, biometric enforcement, insurance and remediation practice, and other disputes to test how ordinary legal duties attach to automated systems. Track procedural posture carefully: complaint, motion, dismissal, verdict, settlement, final order, and appeal are different evidentiary states. Track whether remedies repair only the visible claimant or the broader affected cohort.

## Source discipline

- Prefer statutes, regulations, court opinions, final orders, official enforcement releases, government datasets, collective-bargaining agreements, company filings, original academic research, and contemporaneous primary records.
- Keep allegations, agency allegations, trial findings, settlements without admission where applicable, verdicts, and final judgments distinct.
- Use exact dates for fast-moving AI regulation and policy. Re-check current European Union and U.S. federal implementation immediately before Shelf release.
- For litigation, record procedural posture and do not treat dismissal on exhaustion, standing, or pleading grounds as adjudication of the underlying factual allegation.
- For public-opinion surveys, record field dates, question wording, sample definition, and whether respondents are general public, experts, workers, voters, or another subgroup.
- For fraud-loss data, distinguish reported complaints from total incidence and avoid summing overlapping categories as if they were independent.
- For job-impact claims, distinguish task exposure, occupational exposure, measured displacement, employer intent, productivity, and forecasted employment effects.
- For model-governance claims, distinguish a published policy from evidence that the policy constrained a real deployment.
- For compensation claims, distinguish legal damages, contractual payments, refunds, insurance recovery, reinstatement, correction, and voluntary remediation rather than treating every remedy as interchangeable.
- For veto claims, distinguish nominal authority from evidence that a person or body could stop or delay a consequential action without retaliation.
- Do not infer malicious intent from adverse outcomes without evidence. Incentives and foreseeable effects can matter independently of intent.
- Do not infer legitimacy from professional polish, famous investors, executive reputation, regulatory engagement, or the use of advanced technology.
- Never quote a public figure, executive, worker, regulator, researcher, or litigant without checking the original source.
- For every major claim, keep a note titled **What would make this false?** and preserve counterevidence rather than smoothing it away.

## What would make the book’s thesis false?

The argument would need substantial revision if evidence shows that large-scale AI deployment consistently makes consequential decisions more explainable and appealable; that affected people can reliably identify accountable decision-makers; that automated systems reduce rather than increase the gap between institutional action and recourse; that synthetic media produces little practical degradation of identity signals because authentication infrastructure adapts quickly; or that public backlash is primarily explained by transient cultural panic rather than recurring failures of contestability.

The manuscript should be willing to reach that conclusion if the evidence points there.
