# 1 — The MiG in Nevada

The first useful thing about a captured flying saucer is that it would have to be captured.

This sounds stupid until you notice how often the verb disappears.

In the stories, an object comes down. Men arrive. A perimeter forms. The thing is moved somewhere. After that, grammar changes. The object *is studied*. Materials *are analyzed*. Technology *is reverse engineered*. Programs *are compartmented*. The verbs go passive exactly where the interesting engineering begins.

An airplane will not let you do that.

An airplane has mass and fuel and tires. It leaks. Its batteries die. Its hydraulic fluid is wrong for your supply system. Somebody has to discover the torque value for a fastener whose manual is written in a language the mechanic may not read. Somebody has to decide whether a cracked part can be fabricated locally without changing the failure mode. Somebody has to sign for the hangar. Somebody has to fly the thing after everyone has finished being clever on the ground.

In Nevada, the United States did this with Soviet MiGs.

Not replicas. Not American fighters painted red. The airplanes.

The National Museum of the United States Air Force now describes the story without coyness. In the late 1960s the Air Force secretly acquired and tested MiGs under programs including HAVE DOUGHNUT and HAVE DRILL. Later, under Constant Peg, the 4477th Test and Evaluation Squadron—the Red Eagles—flew MiG-17s, MiG-21s and MiG-23s against American fighter crews. A secret airfield at the Tonopah Test Range supported the operation. The same remote field later carried another secret in the opposite half of the clock: MiGs by day, F-117 training at night.

That last detail is almost too perfect. It sounds written after the fact. It is nevertheless part of the Air Force museum's official history.

From 1977 to 1988, the Red Eagles flew more than 15,000 sorties and trained almost 6,000 American aircrew.

Fifteen thousand.

That number is where this book starts.

Because a genuine secret technology program is not merely a secret. It is an organism.

It eats.

It requires land, fuel, bodies, maintenance, transport, money, rules, accidents, replacements, schedules, institutional protection and a reason to continue tomorrow. The more valuable the object, the more intensely people will want to use it. The more alien the object is to the operator's normal technical ecosystem, the more support it may require. Secrecy can suppress the name of the organism. It has a harder time abolishing its metabolism.

## The airplane Israel loaned America

One of the cleanest threads begins outside Nevada.

In August 1966, an Iraqi Air Force captain defected to Israel in a MiG-21, an acquisition arranged in advance by Mossad. The aircraft was eventually loaned to the United States. According to declassified material assembled by the National Security Archive, it arrived for U.S. exploitation in January 1968. The Air Force gave the Soviet fighter an American designation, YF-110, and flew it at Area 51 under HAVE DOUGHNUT.

Pause over the absurdity of that sequence.

A Soviet-designed fighter built for an adversarial military, flown out by an Iraqi pilot in an Israeli intelligence operation, crossed another national boundary into an American black program and acquired a temporary American identity so that American specialists could learn what the enemy's machine actually did.

This is retrieval and exploitation in the literal sense.

It is also better than a metaphor for modern crash-retrieval claims because it gives us a control case. Here is a genuinely sensitive foreign object. Here is intelligence liaison. Here is concealment. Here is a restricted test site. Here is technical exploitation. Here is a program that most citizens did not know existed while it mattered.

And here, crucially, is what happened next.

They flew it.

They did not spend thirty years staring at it in a warehouse and reporting, through anonymous intermediaries, that its principles remained beyond physics.

The distinction is not a cheap shot. A non-human artifact, if such a thing existed, could be incomparably harder to understand than a MiG. It might lack familiar interfaces, fuels, fasteners, materials, design assumptions or even a recognizable division between structure and mechanism. A broken object can be harder than a working aircraft. A fragment can be harder than either. Reverse engineering has no guaranteed timetable.

But difficulty changes the expected traces. It does not erase them.

That is the first idea I want to name: **the exploitation wake**.

When an institution acquires a valuable foreign object, the object perturbs the institution around it. People are assigned. Facilities are modified. New measurement problems appear. Procurement becomes strange. Training changes. Safety problems emerge. Terminology develops. Contractors may be pulled in. Knowledge leaks sideways into tactics, requirements, countermeasures or design—even when the original hardware remains secret.

The wake can be weak. It can be delayed. It can be hidden under cover names. It can be split across compartments so no single person sees the whole shape.

But a program that does things has consequences from doing them.

## Touching the adversary

Before Constant Peg, American pilots could train against American aircraft flown as Soviet surrogates. Aggressor squadrons learned adversary tactics and used U.S. machines to represent the threat. This was useful. It was not the same thing.

A MiG had its own geometry.

Its own acceleration. Its own visibility. Its own radar. Its own bad habits. Its own places where an American pilot's intuition would be wrong.

The Air Force museum describes Constant Peg's training progression with a wonderful phrase: **buck fever**. The first exposure could simply be a radar intercept and formation flight, intended to remove the shock of seeing a real MiG nearby. Later exposures moved into one-on-one maneuvering and then larger fights using Soviet tactics.

There is an epistemology hiding inside fighter training.

A technical report can tell you a turn rate. An intelligence estimate can draw a performance envelope. A pilot flying against the aircraft discovers something else: which part of the envelope matters when two machines and two nervous systems are trying to kill each other.

Foreign materiel exploitation converts an object into several kinds of knowledge at once.

There is **descriptive knowledge**: dimensions, materials, frequencies, component layouts.

There is **functional knowledge**: what the system can actually do.

There is **failure knowledge**: what breaks, saturates, overheats, stalls, jams or lies.

There is **operational knowledge**: what a human adversary can make the system do under realistic conditions.

And there is **derivative knowledge**: what your own force should change because you now know these things.

This ladder matters when somebody says a recovered technology has been "exploited."

Exploited how?

If the answer never gets more specific than *studied*, the verb is carrying too much weight.

## The maintainer problem

The glamorous version of Constant Peg is a fighter pilot seeing a MiG in the Nevada sky.

The evidentiary version is a mechanic trying to keep it there.

The Air Force's own account is blunt. Replacement parts and expertise were scarce. Aircraft often arrived in poor condition. Maintainers had to pioneer methods for keeping a small, irreplaceable foreign fleet airborne. The work was dangerous. Three members of the Red Eagles died in aircraft or ground accidents.

This is what I mean by metabolism.

A secret aircraft cannot be sustained by classification markings. It needs a technical culture.

That culture produces what I will call **maintenance truth**.

Maintenance truth is often less glamorous than intelligence truth and more difficult to fake. If a machine flies, somebody knows which seal fails. If a material is extraordinary, somebody knows how it behaves under a drill bit, heat cycle, vacuum chamber, tensile test or microscope. If a propulsion system works, somebody eventually confronts its energy source, waste heat, control regime and failure modes. If the object cannot be operated, that inability itself becomes a technical result with a history of attempted methods.

This is not an argument that every secret leaves a public invoice.

It is an argument that claims should become more physically specific as they become more technologically ambitious.

The phrase *reverse engineering program* should increase our demand for mundane detail, not decrease it.

## The black hangar fallacy

There is a temptation on both sides of anomalous technology arguments.

Believers sometimes invoke secrecy as a solvent. Missing evidence dissolves because the program is black.

Skeptics sometimes invoke scale as a solvent in the opposite direction. A large program could never stay secret, therefore the program cannot exist.

History is rude to both positions.

Constant Peg existed.

Thousands of aircrew passed through its training. Soviet aircraft flew from a secret Nevada base. The program lasted roughly a decade. It remained sufficiently protected to perform its purpose.

So: **large, consequential secret programs can exist.**

But the same history also shows why "it was secret" is not the end of the inquiry. Constant Peg had aircraft, pilots, maintainers, sorties, facilities, deaths, training outputs and eventually declassified records and surviving hardware. Its secrecy controlled access to the pattern. It did not eliminate the pattern.

Call the mistake on either side the **black hangar fallacy**: imagining that classification either makes any hidden program plausible or makes any substantial hidden program impossible.

Neither follows.

The correct question is comparative.

If the alleged program were real, what kind of wake should it produce given its duration, scale, object count, technical difficulty, security model and claimed output?

Then: which parts of that wake could plausibly remain classified, which would likely cross into adjacent systems, and which traces do we actually observe?

That is a much harder question than asking whether the government lies.

Of course governments lie.

The interesting part begins after that.

## The receipt

Modern allegations about recovered anomalous craft occupy several different evidentiary classes and should not be blended.

It is documented that the United States has formal mechanisms for highly classified programs, foreign materiel acquisition, technical exploitation and compartmented access. It is documented that actual foreign aircraft were secretly acquired and exploited. It is documented that test ranges can host programs unknown to the public for years.

Those facts belong in Class 1.

Named former officials and intelligence personnel have made public claims about hidden UAP retrieval or reverse-engineering activity. Their positions, statements and any official handling of their complaints can be documented where records exist. The underlying extraordinary claims remain testimony unless independently substantiated.

That belongs in Class 2 or Class 3 depending on the claim and corroboration.

The proposition that some recovered object is of non-human origin is not established by the existence of historical foreign-materiel programs. Constant Peg does not prove a saucer program any more than a submarine proves a sea monster.

That inference is forbidden here.

What Constant Peg gives us is better: a way to ask sharper questions.

Where did the object come from?

What is the chain of custody?

What physical inventory exists?

What measurements were made?

What did those measurements rule out?

What new capability, tactic, requirement, material process or scientific problem followed?

What was tried and failed?

Who maintained the thing?

What infrastructure did it require?

What accidents occurred?

What adjacent programs should have been perturbed?

Which documents ought to exist even if their contents remain classified?

Which claimed outputs can be observed outside the compartment?

This is the **artifact test**.

Not *show me the alien*.

Show me the life of the object inside the institution.

## Reverse engineering is a gradient

The phrase reverse engineering has suffered from movies.

It suggests a binary state. First we do not understand the thing. Then scientists in a secure laboratory take the panels off. Later we have lasers.

Real exploitation is messier.

You can measure without understanding. You can imitate a component without understanding the system. You can understand a mechanism without possessing the manufacturing process. You can duplicate a material but not its economics. You can reproduce performance in a laboratory and fail to turn it into a reliable fielded system. You can learn enough from an enemy machine to defeat it without learning how to build one.

Constant Peg's greatest value was not that the United States became the Soviet Union.

It was that touching the adversary's machine collapsed uncertainty.

The same should be true, eventually and unevenly, of any real exploitation effort.

A claimed program that has held revolutionary hardware for decades but generates no stable measurements, no constrained physics, no derivative engineering, no identifiable technical failures and no independently inspectable material is not impossible.

It is expensive in assumptions.

Secrecy is one assumption.

Extreme technical difficulty is another.

Perfect compartmentation is another.

Absence of usable derivative knowledge is another.

Absence of physical leakage is another.

Absence of institutional wake is another.

Stack enough of them and the explanation becomes a machine built entirely to protect itself from evidence.

That does not make it false. It tells us what kind of claim we are holding.

## A machine can be secret. Physics is harder.

By 2017, one of the MiG-23s flown by the 4477th had been declassified and transferred to the National Museum of the United States Air Force.

There it sits.

A formerly secret enemy aircraft turned into an exhibit.

I like the object because it is an antidote to two stupid certainties at once.

The first certainty says governments could never secretly obtain and operate adversary technology on American soil for years. The airplane says they did.

The second says the historical reality of secret exploitation makes any modern retrieval allegation more likely to be true. The airplane says nothing of the sort.

It says only that real objects have biographies.

They come from somewhere. They move through custody. They acquire names. People measure them. People break them. People learn from them. Institutions reorganize around what they learn. The secret may be extraordinary. Its metabolism is usually embarrassingly ordinary.

This book will follow that metabolism.

We will go from a defecting pilot to a borrowed MiG, from technical exploitation to operational training, from test ranges to maintainers, from foreign materiel to the much larger mythology of reverse engineering. We will look at what classification can genuinely conceal and what physical programs have difficulty hiding. Then we will turn the framework onto the claims people most want either to believe or destroy.

Not to settle them by analogy.

To make them pay their evidentiary bills.

A retrieved object is not a conclusion.

It is the first receipt.
