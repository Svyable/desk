# Evidence Matrix

## Class 1 — verified / documented

- A large coordinated Soviet removal of German technical specialists and family members occurred in the Soviet occupation zone in the night of 21–22 October 1946.
- More than two thousand specialists were moved; total persons including families exceeded six thousand in the standard archival/scholarly estimates.
- Selection and planning began months before the October operation.
- Specialists came from multiple sectors including aviation, missiles/armaments, communications, optics, chemistry, ship systems and related industries.
- Soviet ministries prepared workplaces and reorganized German design groups after transfer.
- German rocket specialists worked at Podlipki and Gorodomlya and produced reconstruction work plus later design studies.
- Zeiss specialists and Jena machinery were transferred to Soviet optical production; returned-source intelligence described Germans installing equipment and training Soviet workers.
- German aviation specialists were assigned to Soviet plants and design bureaus; Russian document collections preserve ministerial orders governing these groups.
- German missile specialists were progressively repatriated in the early 1950s.
- U.S. Paperclip policy was being expanded in 1946 in parallel with Soviet exploitation of German expertise.

## Class 2 — credible but incomplete

- Exact specialist and family totals vary by source and counting method.
- The precise degree of voluntariness differed among individuals and moments; the mass October removals are strongly supported as coercive, while some earlier and later employment arrangements were more mixed.
- Specific German technical proposals influenced Soviet choices through training, comparison, and avoided dead ends even where no direct design lineage can be demonstrated.
- Some specialist groups enjoyed materially privileged living conditions relative to nearby Soviet populations while remaining unable freely to leave.
- Returned German specialists provided Western intelligence with broadly useful but compartment-limited accounts of Soviet programs.

## Class 3 — disputed / not established

- That “Osoaviakhim” was the authentic internal Soviet codename for the October operation.
- That later Soviet strategic missiles, especially the R-7, were essentially German designs.
- That any one German engineer or team can be assigned decisive causal credit for Sputnik.
- That all German specialists were Nazis in a meaningful political or criminal sense.
- That all moved specialists were treated identically, or that conditions can be summarized by either “prison camp” or “ordinary employment.”
- That visual similarity between a German proposal and a later Soviet weapon establishes direct design ancestry.

## Class 4 — speculative synthesis

- Osoaviakhim can be read as an unusually large state experiment in transferring tacit knowledge together with capital equipment.
- The Soviet decision to move families functioned partly as labor-force stabilization and leverage, whether or not planners described it in those terms.
- Compartmentation produced an “expertise firewall”: it reduced dependence and espionage risk while limiting the creative value of foreign teams.
- The declining need for German specialists and their repatriation is a better measure of successful assimilation than any single copied artifact.
- Modern industrial-policy debates can use Osoaviakhim as a warning that factories and machines are not equivalent to communities of practice.
