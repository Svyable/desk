# Cooperation Under Coercion

A coerced engineer can still care whether the turbine works.

This is one of the hardest facts in the Osoaviakhim story because it ruins the clean psychological categories. Some German specialists resisted Soviet authority and wanted to go home. Some complained, slowed work, or violated rules. Archival records also praise initiative and diligence. Technical work can remain intrinsically absorbing even when the employment relationship is unjust.

Russian archival material from Soviet design organizations records management judgments about specific German specialists: who worked conscientiously, who showed initiative, who was absent without permission, who had become unproductive. The tone is bureaucratic rather than dramatic. That ordinariness is revealing. Within months, forced relocation had become a personnel-management problem.

Managers could use salary, housing, status, access to better goods, and professional responsibility as incentives. German specialists could use expertise as bargaining power. A person who knows a unique system is harder to replace than a laborer. This did not equalize the relationship, but it created negotiation inside coercion.

Professional identity mattered too. Engineers often judge themselves through solved problems. A person may dislike the institution paying him and still dislike a bad design more. Soviet administrators benefited from this. So did American programs employing German specialists under different conditions.

The danger is romanticization. Professional pride is not consent. A deported specialist who produced excellent work did not retroactively volunteer to be deported. At the same time, assuming that all work produced under coercion must be intentionally poor would misread the evidence and the psychology of technical labor.

The distinction helps explain why forced knowledge transfer can succeed in bounded tasks. If the state already knows what problem it wants solved, can provide tools, and can evaluate the answer, expert labor may remain productive despite coercion. Open-ended research is harder. It depends more on trust, information exchange, autonomy, and the freedom to pursue unexpected results.

This may be one reason German teams were most valuable in reconstruction, design studies, training, and comparison. Those tasks could be specified. The Soviet state did not need to grant the foreigners full strategic context. It could compartment them.

Cooperation under coercion thus created a ceiling. The state could obtain serious engineering work. It could not easily create the same kind of institutional loyalty or long-horizon creative ownership that comes from integration.

By the early 1950s the Soviet system increasingly judged that the value of German specialists had fallen. Their knowledge had been absorbed, their access remained a security concern, and native programs had advanced. Repatriation was not generosity. It was evidence that the balance sheet had changed.

The Germans had become less indispensable. That was the Soviet objective from the beginning.
