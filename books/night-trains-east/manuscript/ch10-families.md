# Families as Infrastructure

The specialist was not moved alone because the specialist was not, in practice, an isolated unit of labor.

Family removal is one of the clearest differences between a simple recruitment program and Osoaviakhim’s coercive architecture. Spouses and children were awakened, packed, transported, and resettled with the technical personnel. Contemporary American diplomatic reporting from Berlin recorded Soviet public claims that German engineers had gone to the USSR under agreements and that families were supplied with food and transport for personal effects. That official story was designed to normalize the event. Other reporting and later scholarship describe surprise and compulsion.

Both kinds of evidence are useful. The propaganda language shows what Soviet authorities thought needed defending: voluntariness, decent treatment, orderly transport. If the event had been universally understood as an ordinary labor contract, those points would have required less emphasis.

Families performed a technical function whether planners described it that way or not. An engineer separated indefinitely from spouse and children has a strong incentive to escape, sabotage, refuse, or collapse. Moving the household reduced that pressure while increasing Soviet leverage. Housing families together also created communities capable of reproducing German language and social life inside Soviet settlements.

Children attended schools or improvised educational arrangements. Adults built social circles. Births, illnesses, friendships, and deaths occurred inside a geopolitical program whose official objective was technical production. The history becomes strange when viewed at this scale: Cold War technology was being transferred through kitchens, schoolrooms, and marriages as well as laboratories.

The families also complicate the moral ledger. Some specialists had worked voluntarily for Nazi military industries. Some had party affiliations. Others were technocrats whose political commitments varied. Their children had no technical role and no responsibility for wartime weapons. Yet the operation treated the household as collateral infrastructure attached to a valuable expert.

Soviet material conditions after the war were harsh. Foreign specialists could receive salaries and provisioning that looked privileged beside the lives of ordinary Soviet citizens. That relative privilege later became part of German memory. It should not be used to erase coercion, just as coercion should not be used to fabricate conditions worse than the evidence supports.

The most accurate description is uncomfortable: many specialists and families lived materially tolerable or even comparatively comfortable lives while being denied the freedom to decide where those lives would occur.

That distinction matters because modern discussions of forced labor often assume brutality must be visible in hunger, beatings, or camps. Osoaviakhim shows another mechanism. A state can compel movement, restrict return, pay well, provide housing, and still exercise coercive control.

Families were therefore part of the engineering system. They helped make long-duration extraction of expertise sustainable. The Soviet state moved the household because it understood, perhaps more clearly than some modern management theory, that a worker’s productive capacity is embedded in a life.
