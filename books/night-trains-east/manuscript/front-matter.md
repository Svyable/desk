# Night Trains East

## Operation Osoaviakhim and Stalin's Forced Technology Transfer

Sven Hardy Benson

In October 1946, Soviet authorities removed more than two thousand German technical specialists from the Soviet occupation zone and moved them east with thousands of family members. Machinery went too. The familiar English-language shorthand calls it the Soviet answer to Operation Paperclip. That is true enough to be misleading.

The more revealing story is an industrial one. Moscow attempted to transplant not only scientists but design bureaus, production knowledge, machine tools, optical metrology, rocket experience, aviation teams, family life, and the tacit memory that makes a complex system reproducible.

This book follows the transfer from lists made in occupied Germany to Gorodomlya, Jena's displaced optical expertise, aviation plants around Kuibyshev, Western intelligence interviews with returnees, and the Soviet decision to keep German experts useful but separate.

It uses four evidence classes throughout: documented facts; credible but incomplete claims; disputed or unestablished claims; and speculative synthesis. The categories matter because the subject sits at the intersection of national myth, Cold War intelligence, memoir, and postwar moral accounting.

The argument is not that Soviet science was secretly German. It is more interesting than that. The Soviet state correctly understood that advanced technology lives in people as well as blueprints. It then discovered that people are not machines, and knowledge cannot be moved without changing it.
