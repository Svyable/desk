## A note on names and transliteration

The conventional English title “Operation Osoaviakhim” is retained because it is searchable and widely recognized. German sources often use *Aktion Ossawakim*. Russian is commonly written as `Операция «Осоавиахим»`. German scholarship has raised a substantial archival caution: the label may not have been the operation's authentic internal Soviet codename. The manuscript therefore treats the name as conventional rather than proven.

Russian place and institution names are transliterated for readability. Where competing English spellings exist—Gorodomlya/Gorodomliya, Kuibyshev/Kuybyshev—the manuscript uses the form most common in the cited English or German literature and preserves searchable variants in research notes.

## Evidence classes

**Class 1 — verified/documented:** supported by primary records or strong convergent archival scholarship.

**Class 2 — credible but incomplete:** plausible and supported but missing decisive documentation, complete context, or independent verification.

**Class 3 — disputed/not established:** claims that circulate in memoir, intelligence reporting, popular history, or nationalist accounts without enough evidence to be presented as fact.

**Class 4 — speculative synthesis:** the author's interpretive argument, explicitly separated from documentary fact.

## Working conclusion

The trains of October 1946 were an attempt to turn technological capability into movable property. They succeeded enough to accelerate Soviet learning and failed enough to prove that expertise could not be reduced to freight.

The receiving institutions mattered more than the seizure alone. Once Soviet engineers, technicians, managers and factories could reproduce and extend what had been learned, the foreigners became unnecessary. That transition—from indispensable specialist to repatriated security risk—is the real measure of the transfer.
