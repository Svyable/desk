# What the Drawings Leave Out

Engineering drawings are precise partly because they hide the history that produced them.

A finished drawing shows a dimension. It does not show the prototype that cracked before the dimension changed. A process sheet gives a temperature. It may not explain that the furnace reads ten degrees high on humid days. A specification names a material. It does not preserve the argument in which three engineers discovered that the officially acceptable substitute vibrated apart.

This is tacit knowledge, though the phrase can make something ordinary sound philosophical. It is simply the large body of practical understanding that exists in habits, memory, judgment, and shared routines rather than formal documents.

Osoaviakhim is one of the twentieth century’s largest demonstrations that states know tacit knowledge exists even when they do not use the term. Soviet planners moved specialists with machinery. They moved teams rather than only directors. They kept groups together long enough to install equipment, reconstruct systems, train Soviet workers, and explain designs.

The receiving state was trying to capture the undocumented half of technology.

That task becomes harder as systems become more complex. A simple machine can be reverse-engineered from parts. A missile program is a network of laboratories, suppliers, test procedures, instrumentation, manufacturing controls, and organizational routines. Copy the artifact and you still may not possess the capability.

The Soviet experience with captured German technology repeatedly turned copying into redevelopment. Materials changed. Production methods changed. Requirements changed. Native engineers had to understand which properties were essential and which were historical accidents of German factories.

The German specialists accelerated that distinction. Their deepest value was often explanatory. They could say why a design looked strange.

The same principle appears in modern semiconductor fabs, aircraft engines, nuclear plants, and machine-learning systems. Export controls often focus on equipment and software because those can be counted. Human experience is harder to control. A machine tool crossing a border is visible. Ten engineers carrying a decade of failure memory are not.

Osoaviakhim therefore belongs in the history of economic security, not only the history of rockets. It shows why industrial policy that counts factories without communities of practice can disappoint. It also shows why coercion is a crude instrument for acquiring human capital. A state can relocate expertise physically. It cannot guarantee the quality of knowledge exchange.

The Soviet program succeeded where the problem was bounded and the knowledge could be demonstrated, taught, or tested. It was weaker where creativity required trust, open information, or durable institutional belonging.

The drawings left things out. So did the trains.
