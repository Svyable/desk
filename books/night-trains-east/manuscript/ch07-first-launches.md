# First Launches

The Soviet Union did not need a German rocket to prove that rockets existed. It needed to turn captured German achievement into a reproducible Soviet capability.

By 1947 V-2-derived vehicles were being tested at Kapustin Yar. Western intelligence later tracked the process closely. A 1957 estimate noted that the USSR had acquired complete V-2 missiles, parts, and production facilities and had test-fired V-2s as early as the fall of 1947. German specialists contributed to reconstruction and improvement work while Soviet teams built their own competence.

The distinction between test and production is important. A state can launch a captured missile without understanding how to manufacture another hundred. Repeated production requires materials, tooling, inspection, supply chains, process control, trained operators, and a failure-analysis culture. The deepest transfer problem was therefore not aerodynamic theory. It was reproducibility.

German experience helped compress that learning curve. Returned specialists later described participation in early trials and proposals for improving control and accuracy. Intelligence reporting must be treated carefully: defectors and repatriated engineers knew what they had seen, but not necessarily the full Soviet context. Their testimony is strongest when used to reconstruct their own work and weakest when used to infer hidden decisions elsewhere in the Soviet system.

The first launches also made limits visible. A copied system exposes mismatches between industrial bases. German wartime materials may be unavailable. Soviet substitutes may behave differently. Drawings assume machine tools and gauges with particular characteristics. A component redesigned for Soviet production might alter mass, vibration, or thermal behavior elsewhere. Copying becomes engineering.

That is where the foreign specialist is most valuable. A person who remembers why a part was designed a certain way can tell the difference between an arbitrary German choice and a constraint that should not be violated. Without that memory, every dimension looks equally sacred.

The Soviet program moved quickly toward indigenous derivatives. The R-1, heavily based on the V-2, became a bridge rather than an endpoint. Later systems increased range and changed architecture. By the time Soviet missile development reached the R-7, the technological ancestry still included lessons absorbed from Germany, but the system itself belonged to a different engineering generation.

This makes arguments over credit feel oddly small. Industrial knowledge is cumulative. A victorious state extracted knowledge from a defeated one. Soviet engineers learned from it, recombined it with their own work, and created capabilities the Germans had not possessed. The morally important fact of coercion and the technically important fact of later Soviet innovation are both true.

Kapustin Yar is where that double truth stopped being abstract. Hardware flew. Some of it failed. Measurements came back. Engineers adjusted. The Soviet Union did not simply inherit a rocket. It inherited a head start and then had to build an institution capable of using it.
